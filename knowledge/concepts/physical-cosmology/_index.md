# Cosmology III: physical and observational cosmology

The real universe and the evidence for its contents and history, taking the Robertson-Walker kinematics and Friedmann dynamics as given: standard candles, magnitudes and the cosmic distance ladder, Type Ia supernovae and the discovery of accelerating expansion, dark energy and its alternatives, the observational case for dark matter and its candidates, the hot big bang and thermal history (radiation era, matter-radiation equality, phase transitions, photon-to-baryon ratio, baryon asymmetry), big bang nucleosynthesis and the baryon density, recombination and the cosmic microwave background with its dipole, anisotropies, angular power spectrum, polarization and E/B modes, baryon acoustic oscillations, Lambda-CDM and the Hubble tension, structure formation from density perturbations to first stars and reionization, and inflation (horizon and flatness problems, inflaton, Hubble friction, slow roll, reheating, primordial perturbations and gravitational waves), plus cosmic strings as early-universe relics.

| Concept | Tier | Summary | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [[olbers-paradox]] Olbers' paradox | foundation | If the universe were infinite, eternal, static and uniformly filled with stars, every line of sight would end on a stellar surface and the night sky would blaze. The dark sky is explained mainly by the finite age of the universe, which limits how far light has travelled to us, with cosmological redshift dimming distant sources further. | — |
| [[uniqueness-of-the-universe]] Uniqueness of the universe | foundation | Cosmology studies a single object that cannot be repeated or experimented on, so its models rest on extrapolating locally tested physics together with simplifying assumptions such as the cosmological principle. This limits what can be tested statistically and explains why symmetry postulates play such a large role. | — |
| [[apparent-magnitude]] Apparent magnitude and distance modulus | foundation | Astronomers measure brightness on a reversed logarithmic scale, m = constant - 2.5 log10(flux), so fainter objects have larger magnitudes; the difference between apparent and absolute magnitude, m - M, is the distance modulus, a logarithm of the luminosity distance. The reversed sign and the factor 2.5 are common sources of confusion. | — |
| [[standard-candle]] Standard candle | core | A class of astronomical sources whose intrinsic luminosity is known from physics or from calibration, so that the measured flux gives the luminosity distance. Any evolution of the class over cosmic time biases the distances, which is why ordinary galaxies make poor candles. | — |
| [[cepheid-period-luminosity-relation]] Cepheid period-luminosity relation | core | Cepheid variable stars pulsate with periods tightly correlated with their average luminosity, a relation Henrietta Leavitt found from stars at a common distance in the Magellanic Clouds. Once calibrated by parallax, measuring a Cepheid's period gives its luminosity and hence its distance. | — |
| [[cosmic-distance-ladder]] Cosmic distance ladder | core | Distances to far objects are built in overlapping steps: geometric parallax fixes nearby stars, which calibrate Cepheids, which calibrate brighter indicators such as Type Ia supernovae in more distant galaxies. Each rung inherits the systematic errors of the rungs beneath it. | — |
| [[type-ia-supernova]] Type Ia supernova | core | A white dwarf, typically fed by a companion, ignites runaway carbon burning near the Chandrasekhar mass and is completely disrupted. Because the progenitor mass is nearly fixed, peak luminosities are similar (and standardizable), making these explosions bright standard candles visible to redshifts of order one. | — |
| [[magnitude-redshift-diagram]] Magnitude-redshift (Hubble) diagram | core | A plot of distance modulus against redshift for standard candles: the low-redshift slope measures the Hubble constant, and the curvature of the relation near z of order one measures the deceleration parameter and so whether expansion is speeding up. To first order beyond linear, the correction term is proportional to (1 - q0) z. | — |
| [[dimensionless-hubble-constant]] Dimensionless Hubble constant (little h) | core | Writing H0 = 100 h km/s/Mpc (or H0 = 70 h_70 km/s/Mpc) lets formulas carry the uncertainty in the Hubble constant as an explicit factor; h is about 0.7. The symbol h must not be confused with a gravitational-wave strain amplitude or with Planck's constant. | — |
| [[accelerating-expansion-of-the-universe]] Accelerating expansion of the universe | core | Type Ia supernova surveys in 1998-99, later confirmed by the CMB and galaxy surveys, showed that distant supernovae are fainter than a decelerating universe allows, so the expansion rate is increasing today. By the acceleration equation this requires a dominant component with pressure below minus one third of its energy density. | — |
| [[dark-energy]] Dark energy | core | Dark energy is the name for whatever drives the present acceleration and makes up roughly 70 per cent of the cosmic energy budget. In its simplest form it is a cosmological constant, a fluid with constant density and pressure equal to minus that density, but it could instead be vacuum energy, an evolving scalar field, or a sign that gravity is modified on large scales. | — |
| [[quintessence]] Quintessence | advanced | An alternative to a strict cosmological constant in which dark energy is a slowly rolling scalar field, so its density and equation-of-state parameter can change with time. It uses the same scalar-field machinery as inflation, applied to the late universe. | — |
| [[averaging-problem-in-cosmology]] Averaging problem in cosmology | frontier | Because the Einstein equations are nonlinear, the spacetime produced by a smoothed-out matter distribution need not equal the average of the true lumpy spacetime. Fitting a Robertson-Walker model to an inhomogeneous universe could therefore bias inferred parameters, a possible loophole in the inference of dark energy. | — |
| [[temperature-redshift-relation]] Temperature-redshift relation | core | Radiation energy density falls as a^-4 and a black body has energy density proportional to T^4, so the radiation temperature scales as 1/a: T = T0 (1 + z). The universe was a thousand times hotter when it was a thousand times smaller, which makes temperature a clock for the early universe. | — |
| [[hot-big-bang-model]] Hot big bang model | core | The picture in which the universe expanded from an extremely hot, dense state and cooled as it grew, since radiation temperature falls inversely with the scale factor; as it cooled, nuclei, then atoms, then stars and galaxies formed. Its pillars are the Hubble expansion, the cosmic microwave background and the light-element abundances. | — |
| [[cosmic-microwave-background]] Cosmic microwave background | core | Nearly perfect black-body radiation at 2.725 K that fills the universe, released when the early plasma became neutral and transparent and redshifted by a factor of about 1100 since. Predicted by Alpher and Herman and found by Penzias and Wilson, it is the key evidence for a hot big bang, shows isotropy to about one part in 100,000, and fixes the radiation density. | — |
| [[preservation-of-black-body-spectrum]] Preservation of the black-body spectrum under expansion | core | As the universe expands every photon frequency falls as 1/a, and a thermal spectrum stays exactly thermal with a temperature that also falls as 1/a, because the ratio of photon energy to temperature is unchanged and the frequency-cubed factor dilutes exactly like inverse volume. This is why radiation released at decoupling is still a near-perfect black body today. | — |
| [[cmb-dipole]] CMB dipole and the cosmic rest frame | core | Our motion relative to the frame in which the CMB is isotropic Doppler-shifts it into a dipole pattern, slightly hotter ahead and cooler behind; Planck finds the Solar System moving at about 370 km/s. The CMB frame is physically distinguished by the matter content of the universe, though not privileged by the laws of physics. | — |
| [[thermal-history-of-the-universe]] Thermal history of the universe | core | Because temperature scales as 1/a, the early universe can be labelled by temperature or typical particle energy instead of time, and read as a sequence: free quarks, then free nucleons, nuclei in a radiation-dominated plasma, a matter-dominated plasma, and finally neutral atoms with free-streaming light. Physics at each stage is set by which reactions are fast compared with the expansion, and the picture is laboratory-tested up to about TeV energies. | — |
| [[temperature-time-relation]] Temperature-time relation in the radiation era | core | During radiation domination a grows as t^(1/2) and T falls as 1/a, so T is proportional to t^(-1/2); numerically the temperature is about 10^10 K, or roughly 1 MeV, when the universe is one second old. This turns the thermal history into a clock for nuclear and particle processes. | — |
| [[matter-radiation-equality]] Matter-radiation equality | core | Radiation density falls as a^-4 and matter density as a^-3, so they were equal when the scale factor was the present ratio of radiation to matter density parameters, about 3 x 10^-4 of today (redshift of a few thousand, tens of thousands of years after the big bang). Before it the expansion was radiation-dominated, after it matter-dominated, which changes both a(t) and how structure grows. | — |
| [[cosmological-phase-transition]] Phase transitions in the early universe | advanced | As the hot universe cooled it is thought to have passed through symmetry-changing transitions: separation of the strong force near 10^15 GeV, electroweak symmetry breaking by the Higgs field near 100 GeV, and confinement of quarks into hadrons at a few hundred MeV, around 10^-5 to 10^-4 s. Before confinement matter was a quark-gluon plasma. | — |
| [[cosmic-string]] Cosmic string | advanced | A hypothetical line-like topological defect formed in an early-universe symmetry-breaking transition and stretched to astronomical length. It has tension equal to its mass per unit length, leaves flat space around it with a missing wedge (deficit angle) that would create double images, and its oscillations and cusps would emit gravitational-wave bursts. | — |
| [[photon-to-baryon-ratio]] Photon-to-baryon ratio | core | There are roughly a billion photons for every baryon in the universe, a number fixed after electron-positron pairs annihilated at around one second and conserved since; it measures the entropy per particle. Its size explains why rare high-energy photons could keep hydrogen ionized until the temperature fell well below the ionization energy. | — |
| [[matter-antimatter-asymmetry]] Matter-antimatter asymmetry | advanced | The present universe contains essentially no antimatter, so the early plasma must have held a tiny excess of matter, about one extra particle per billion photons, which survived annihilation. Producing it (baryogenesis) requires baryon-number, C and CP violation out of equilibrium, and its cause is still unknown. | — |
| [[big-bang-nucleosynthesis]] Big bang nucleosynthesis | core | In the first few minutes, as the plasma cooled through tens of keV, protons and neutrons fused into deuterium, helium-4 (about a quarter by mass), helium-3 and lithium-7, while expansion shut reactions off before heavier nuclei formed. The yields depend on the baryon density and expansion rate, so matching observed abundances fixes the baryon density parameter at about 0.04-0.05. | — |
| [[nucleosynthesis-bound-on-relativistic-species]] Nucleosynthesis bound on relativistic species | advanced | Extra massless particle species would add energy density, speed up the early expansion and raise the helium yield, so observed abundances limit the number of light neutrino families to the known three. The same argument bounds any early background of relativistic energy, including primordial gravitational waves, and requires dark matter to have been non-relativistic. | — |
| [[baryon-density-parameter]] Baryon density parameter | core | The fraction of the critical density in ordinary matter made of protons, neutrons and electrons, about 0.04-0.05 from nucleosynthesis and the CMB. It is several times the matter in visible stars but only about a sixth of the total matter density, showing that most ordinary matter is dark and most matter is not baryonic. | — |
| [[galaxy-rotation-curve]] Galaxy rotation curve | core | The orbital speed of stars and gas plotted against distance from a galaxy's centre. Newtonian circular orbits predict speeds falling as r^(-1/2) outside the visible mass, but observed curves stay roughly flat, implying enclosed mass growing in proportion to r in an unseen halo. | — |
| [[dark-matter]] Dark matter | core | Matter that emits no light and is detected only by its gravity, through cluster dynamics (Zwicky), flat galaxy rotation curves, gravitational lensing and structure formation; it forms extended halos around galaxies and makes up about 85 per cent of all matter. Nucleosynthesis shows it cannot be ordinary baryons, and the favoured cold form moves slowly compared with light, but its particle nature is unknown. | — |
| [[dark-matter-candidates]] Dark matter candidates | advanced | Proposals for what dark matter is: astrophysical objects such as primordial black holes or massive compact halo objects (limited by microlensing), light neutrinos (too hot to build structure), and new particles such as axions or weakly interacting massive particles sought in direct-detection and collider experiments. None has been confirmed. | — |
| [[recombination]] Recombination and photon decoupling | core | About 380,000 years after the big bang (redshift about 1100, temperature about 3000 K) the plasma cooled enough for electrons to bind into neutral hydrogen, photons stopped scattering, and the universe became transparent, releasing the CMB. It happened far below the 13.6 eV ionization energy because the huge number of photons per baryon kept the gas ionized until then. | — |
| [[last-scattering-surface]] Last scattering surface | core | The sphere around a given observer made of the points where the CMB photons now reaching that observer last scattered at decoupling. It is not a physical shell: light was released everywhere, and observers at other places or times see different spheres. | — |
| [[cmb-temperature-anisotropy]] CMB temperature anisotropies | core | After removing the dipole, the CMB temperature varies across the sky by about one part in 100,000, tracing density and velocity perturbations at decoupling. The positions of hot and cold spots are random, but their typical size as a function of angular scale is fixed by early-universe physics and cosmological parameters. | — |
| [[cmb-angular-power-spectrum]] CMB angular power spectrum | core | Expanding the sky temperature in spherical harmonics, with multipole l corresponding to angular scale of roughly 180 degrees divided by l, and averaging the squared coefficients at each l gives the power spectrum. Its series of acoustic peaks, the first near l of about 200, encodes spatial curvature and the baryon, dark matter and dark energy content. | — |
| [[baryon-acoustic-oscillation]] Baryon acoustic oscillations | advanced | Before decoupling, pressure waves travelled through the tightly coupled baryon-photon plasma, and the distance they covered by then is imprinted both on the CMB peaks and as a preferred separation of about 150 Mpc in today's galaxy distribution. This known physical length acts as a standard ruler for angular-diameter distances and the expansion history. | — |
| [[cmb-polarization]] CMB polarization | advanced | Scattering off electrons polarizes light perpendicular to the plane of scattering; isotropic incident light cancels, but radiation brighter from some directions than others (a quadrupole pattern around the scatterer) leaves net linear polarization. The CMB is therefore weakly polarized in a pattern tied to its anisotropies at last scattering. | — |
| [[lambda-cdm-model]] Lambda-CDM model | core | The standard cosmological model: a spatially flat Friedmann-Robertson-Walker universe with about 5 per cent baryons, 26 per cent cold dark matter and 69 per cent cosmological constant, plus a little radiation, starting hot and probably preceded by inflation. Once radiation is negligible its scale factor goes from t^(2/3) deceleration to exponential acceleration, and it fits the CMB, supernovae, BAO and nucleosynthesis with an age of about 13.8 billion years. | — |
| [[hubble-tension]] Hubble tension | frontier | Local distance-ladder measurements give a Hubble constant near 73-74 km/s/Mpc, while fitting Lambda-CDM to the CMB with BAO gives about 67, a disagreement of several standard deviations. It may come from unrecognised systematics in either method or signal missing physics, and standard sirens offer an independent arbiter. | — |
| [[growth-of-density-perturbations]] Growth of density perturbations | advanced | Slightly overdense regions pull in matter and grow denser while underdense regions empty, but expansion slows the runaway: in a matter-dominated Einstein-de Sitter universe the linear density contrast grows only as t^(2/3), or in proportion to the scale factor. Simulations reproduce observed galaxy clustering from the small CMB-era perturbations only when dark matter is included. | — |
| [[radiative-cooling-of-baryons]] Radiative cooling of baryons in dark matter halos | advanced | After decoupling, gas falling into dark matter wells is heated by collisions and radiates the energy away; losing energy makes the gas sink deeper and, because self-gravitating systems have negative specific heat, get hotter, so baryons concentrate at the centre. Collisionless dark matter cannot radiate and stays in extended halos. | — |
| [[hierarchical-structure-formation]] Hierarchical structure formation | advanced | In Lambda-CDM small clumps collapse first and then merge repeatedly into larger galaxies and clusters, a process that continues today (the Milky Way will merge with Andromeda). Because stars are nearly collisionless, groups accreted together stay coherent streams in position-velocity space, and Gaia velocities reveal the Galaxy's merger history. | — |
| [[population-iii-star]] Population III stars | advanced | The first generation of stars, formed from pristine hydrogen and helium; without heavy elements the gas was transparent and cooled poorly, so they were very massive, hot and short-lived, enriched their surroundings with metals and probably left many black holes. None has yet been observed directly. | — |
| [[reionization]] Reionization and the cosmic dark ages | advanced | After decoupling the universe was neutral and nearly lightless (the dark ages) until ultraviolet light from the first stars and galaxies ionized intergalactic hydrogen again, between redshifts of roughly 20 and 8. Light from the dark ages was largely absorbed, but low-frequency radio and gravitational waves pass through, so detectors such as LISA may see black-hole mergers from that time. | — |
| [[horizon-problem]] Horizon problem | core | In a decelerating hot big bang, the past light cones of points on opposite sides of the last scattering surface never overlap, so nothing could have brought them to a common temperature; only patches within about two degrees on today's sky shared any causal past. Yet the CMB has the same temperature in all directions to one part in 100,000, so the uniformity must be assumed rather than explained. | — |
| [[flatness-problem]] Flatness problem | core | In a decelerating universe the departure of the total density parameter from one grows with time (as t for radiation, t^(2/3) for matter), so a universe near critical density today must have started absurdly close to it. The problem is to explain this tuning rather than impose it as an initial condition. | — |
| [[cosmic-inflation]] Cosmic inflation | core | A proposed very early phase, around 10^-35 s, of nearly exponential expansion driven by a large temporary vacuum-like energy. It inflates a small causally connected patch beyond our observable universe, solving the horizon problem, drives any spatial curvature to negligible values so the density parameter approaches one, and turns quantum fluctuations into the seeds of structure. | — |
| [[inflaton-field]] Inflaton field | advanced | The hypothetical, nearly uniform scalar field whose potential energy dominates the early universe and drives inflation; in a Robertson-Walker background it obeys phi-double-dot + 3H phi-dot + dV/dphi = 0. Its energy density is the kinetic term plus the potential and its pressure the kinetic term minus the potential, so a field dominated by potential energy acts like a temporary cosmological constant. | — |
| [[slow-roll-inflation]] Slow-roll inflation | advanced | On a sufficiently flat stretch of potential, Hubble friction makes the field's acceleration and kinetic energy negligible, so 3H phi-dot is approximately -dV/dphi and the expansion rate is set by the nearly constant potential energy. The universe then expands almost exponentially until the field reaches the steep part of the potential. | — |
| [[reheating]] End of inflation and reheating | advanced | Inflation ends when the field's kinetic energy becomes comparable to its potential energy, typically as it falls into its minimum and oscillates. Couplings to other fields then convert that energy into particles and radiation, refilling the cold, empty post-inflation universe and starting the standard hot big bang. | — |
| [[primordial-density-perturbations]] Primordial density perturbations from inflation | advanced | Quantum fluctuations of the inflaton are stretched to cosmic scales during inflation and become classical density perturbations with a nearly scale-invariant spectrum. They later appear as CMB anisotropies and seed galaxies, and the measured spectrum constrains the shape of the inflaton potential. | — |
| [[primordial-gravitational-waves]] Primordial gravitational waves | frontier | Gravitational waves produced in the very early universe, for example amplified from vacuum fluctuations during inflation, which travel almost unaffected except for redshift. They could be detected directly by future detectors or indirectly through B-mode polarization of the CMB, and their energy density is bounded by nucleosynthesis. | — |
| [[e-mode-and-b-mode-polarization]] E-mode and B-mode polarization | frontier | A CMB polarization map splits into a curl-free, gradient-like part (E modes) and a curl-like part (B modes). Scalar density perturbations produce only E modes, while gravitational waves produce both, so primordial B modes would be a clean signature of gravitational waves at decoupling, though foreground dust and lensing also make B modes. | — |
