# Einstein field equations and their structure

Obtaining, reading and analysing the field equations of general relativity: stress-energy as the source, the criteria and heuristic routes (Poisson plus covariance plus conservation, the tidal trace argument, the rejected Ricci guess), the Einstein equation with and without a cosmological constant, trace reversal, vacuum Ricci-flatness, the Newtonian limit and the coupling constant, pressure and rho + 3p as sources, Lovelock uniqueness, nonlinearity, gauge freedom and the hole argument, motion implied by the field equations, exact and Einstein-Maxwell solutions as notions, junction conditions, gravitational energy and mass (non-localizability, far-field, Komar, ADM and quasi-local mass, positive energy theorem), and the initial-value formulation: Cauchy problem, constraints and their propagation, gauge conditions (Gaussian normal, harmonic, slicing and shift conditions), hyperbolicity and well-posedness, characteristic data, the 3+1 decomposition (foliations, lapse and shift, extrinsic curvature, Gauss-Codazzi-Ricci equations, Hamiltonian and momentum constraints, ADM evolution and Hamiltonian formalism, degrees of freedom, conformal initial data, maximal slicing), numerical relativity and the 2+2 formalism. Action derivations, linearized theory, specific solutions and cosmological uses of Lambda belong to other domains.

| Concept | Tier | Summary | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [[stress-energy-tensor-as-source-of-gravity]] Stress-energy tensor as the source of gravity | core | In general relativity the source of gravity is the whole stress-energy tensor, not rest mass or a single observer's energy density: energy, momentum density and stresses all gravitate. Only the full tensor can be the source without singling out a preferred frame. | — |
| [[requirements-for-field-equations]] Requirements for the gravitational field equations | core | A relativistic law for the metric should generalize Poisson's equation: a tensor equation, second order in derivatives of the metric, with a symmetric curvature-built tensor set proportional to the stress-energy tensor, no preferred coordinates, and compatible with local energy-momentum conservation for every metric. These criteria nearly single out Einstein's equation. | — |
| [[tidal-derivation-of-vacuum-field-equations]] Tidal route to the vacuum field equations | core | In Newtonian gravity the vacuum law (Laplace's equation) says the tidal tensor is trace-free; in relativity the tidal tensor comes from the Riemann tensor via geodesic deviation, and demanding its trace vanish for every observer's four-velocity gives R_ab = 0. This is the heuristic path from tides to the vacuum Einstein equations. | — |
| [[ricci-tensor-field-equation]] Ricci-tensor field equation (rejected candidate) | core | The naive guess that the Ricci tensor is proportional to the stress-energy tensor fails: taking its divergence and using the contracted Bianchi identity with energy-momentum conservation forces the Ricci scalar, and hence the trace of T, to be constant everywhere, which real matter distributions violate. Repairing it leads to the Einstein tensor. | — |
| [[einstein-field-equations]] Einstein field equations | core | The dynamical law of general relativity: the Einstein tensor of the spacetime metric equals 8 pi G/c^4 times the stress-energy tensor (G = 8 pi T in geometrized units). They are ten coupled, nonlinear, second-order partial differential equations for the metric, consistent with energy-momentum conservation because both sides are divergence-free. | — |
| [[trace-reversed-einstein-equations]] Trace-reversed Einstein equations | core | Tracing the field equation gives R = -8 pi T in four dimensions; substituting back gives the equivalent form R_ab = 8 pi (T_ab - (1/2) g_ab T), where the Ricci tensor is fixed directly by matter. This form makes the vacuum equations and the Newtonian limit especially transparent. | — |
| [[vacuum-einstein-equations]] Vacuum Einstein equations | core | Where there is no matter or non-gravitational field (and no cosmological constant) the field equations reduce to G_ab = 0, which in four dimensions is equivalent to R_ab = 0 because the trace forces R = 0. Ricci-flat does not mean flat: the Weyl part of the Riemann tensor, which carries tidal effects, can remain, as outside a star. | — |
| [[newtonian-limit-of-field-equations]] Newtonian limit of the field equations | core | For weak, nearly static fields and slowly moving matter with pressure much less than energy density, the time-time component of the (trace-reversed) Einstein equation reduces to Poisson's equation for the Newtonian potential, with g_00 approximately -(1 + 2 Phi). Matching this fixes the coupling constant. | — |
| [[einstein-gravitational-constant]] Einstein gravitational constant | core | The constant kappa = 8 pi G/c^4 multiplying the stress-energy tensor in the field equations (8 pi when G = c = 1). Its value is not a free choice: it is fixed by requiring the Newtonian limit to reproduce Poisson's equation with 4 pi G. | — |
| [[cosmological-constant]] Cosmological constant | core | A constant Lambda times the metric can be added to the field equations, G_ab + Lambda g_ab = 8 pi T_ab, without spoiling conservation because the metric is covariantly constant. Moved to the right-hand side it acts like a stress-energy with constant energy density Lambda/8 pi and pressure equal to minus that density; positive Lambda produces a repulsion growing with distance. | — |
| [[lovelock-theorem]] Lovelock's theorem | advanced | In four dimensions, the only symmetric, divergence-free tensor built from the metric and its first two derivatives (linear in second derivatives) is a combination of the Einstein tensor and the metric itself. So field equations that are generally covariant, second order and compatible with conservation must be Einstein's equations with a possible cosmological constant. | — |
| [[pressure-as-a-source-of-gravity]] Pressure as a source of gravity | core | Because every component of the stress-energy tensor sources curvature, pressure adds to gravitational attraction as well as to inertia. In a relativistic star, raising pressure to resist collapse also strengthens gravity, and in cosmology a hot radiation-filled universe decelerates more strongly than dust. | — |
| [[active-gravitational-mass-density]] Active gravitational mass density | core | For slowly moving, weakly self-gravitating matter with possibly large stresses, the Newtonian-like potential is sourced by rho plus the trace of the spatial stresses, which for a perfect fluid is rho + 3p (in c = 1 units). A fluid with p < -rho/3, such as a cosmological constant, therefore gravitates repulsively. | — |
| [[nonlinearity-of-general-relativity]] Nonlinearity of general relativity | core | The field equations are nonlinear in the metric: curvature contains products of connection coefficients, the source depends on the metric being solved for, and gravitational energy itself gravitates. Hence the sum of two solutions is generally not a solution, and gravitational waves can scatter off each other, unlike light in vacuum. | — |
| [[gauge-freedom-in-general-relativity]] Gauge freedom and the independent field equations | core | The four contracted Bianchi identities make only six of the ten Einstein equations independent, and correspondingly four of the ten metric functions can be changed at will by coordinate transformations. The field equations therefore determine the metric only up to diffeomorphism, and four gauge conditions must be supplied to fix a unique representative. | — |
| [[hole-argument]] Hole argument | advanced | Einstein's worry that if matter outside a region fixes the metric inside, a diffeomorphism acting only inside the region produces a different metric with the same matter, seemingly destroying determinism. It is resolved by accepting that diffeomorphism-related metrics describe the same physical spacetime, so spacetime points have no identity apart from the fields. | — |
| [[equations-of-motion-from-field-equations]] Equations of motion from the field equations | core | In general relativity the motion of matter is not a separate postulate: the field equations imply local energy-momentum conservation, and conservation forces dust and small test bodies to move along geodesics. The law of motion is thus contained in the field law. | — |
| [[general-relativity]] General relativity | core | Einstein's classical theory of gravitation: spacetime is a four-dimensional Lorentzian manifold whose curvature is determined by energy and momentum through the Einstein field equations, while free particles and light follow timelike and null geodesics. Axiomatically, it is special relativity with the flatness condition replaced by the field equations. | — |
| [[exact-solution]] Exact solutions of the Einstein equations | core | A metric (with any matter fields) that satisfies the full nonlinear field equations in closed form, rather than approximately or numerically. Because the general equations are enormous, exact solutions are found by imposing symmetry or a restricted ansatz, often with computer algebra, and physical relevance must still be checked. | — |
| [[einstein-maxwell-equations]] Einstein-Maxwell equations | core | The coupled system in which the electromagnetic stress-energy tensor sources curvature through the Einstein equations while the field obeys Maxwell's equations written with the covariant derivative of the same curved metric. Metric and field must be solved together. | — |
| [[non-localizability-of-gravitational-energy]] Non-localizability of gravitational energy | core | General relativity has no covariant, pointwise energy density for the gravitational field: a freely falling frame removes first derivatives of the metric at any point, and generic spacetimes lack the symmetries that define conserved energy. Gravitational energy is meaningful only globally (for isolated systems), quasi-locally, or averaged over regions, as for gravitational waves. | — |
| [[total-mass-of-isolated-system]] Total mass of an isolated system | core | In curved spacetime the four-momenta of separate particles cannot be invariantly added, but an isolated, asymptotically flat system still has a well-defined total mass: the M in its far-field potential -M/r, measured by the Kepler orbits of distant test bodies. It includes gravitational binding energy and is not an integral of density over the interior. | — |
| [[initial-value-formulation]] Initial-value formulation of general relativity | core | Viewing Einstein's equations as an evolution system: data given on a spacelike hypersurface (for vacuum, the metric and its first time derivative, subject to constraints) are evolved to determine the spacetime to the future, up to the freedom to choose coordinates. Existence, uniqueness and stability of this evolution become central questions. | — |
| [[cauchy-kowalevskaya-theorem]] Cauchy-Kowalevskaya theorem | advanced | For a system of partial differential equations solved for its highest time derivatives, with analytic coefficients and analytic initial data, there is a unique analytic solution near the initial surface. It gives local existence for the vacuum Einstein equations with analytic data once the gauge is fixed. | — |
| [[evolution-equations-of-general-relativity]] Evolution equations of general relativity | advanced | Splitting the vacuum equations with respect to a time coordinate, the six spatial components R_ij = 0 contain second time derivatives of the spatial metric and can be solved for them, so they drive the evolution off the initial slice once the four g_0a components are prescribed. | — |
| [[constraint-equations]] Constraint equations | core | The four components of Einstein's equations with one index along the time direction (G^0_a = 8 pi T^0_a) contain no second time derivatives of the metric, so they do not evolve anything but restrict which initial data are allowed. The other six equations are the evolution equations. | — |
| [[propagation-of-constraints]] Propagation of constraints | advanced | If the constraints hold on the initial slice and the evolution equations hold everywhere, the contracted Bianchi identities give a homogeneous system for the constraint quantities whose only solution with zero data is zero, so the constraints remain satisfied. They need only be imposed initially. | — |
| [[coordinate-condition]] Coordinate (gauge) condition | core | Four conditions imposed on the metric or connection that use up the coordinate freedom of general relativity, selecting one representative from the family of diffeomorphic metrics. Good choices make the equations and their interpretation simpler, while physical predictions stay coordinate independent. | — |
| [[gaussian-normal-coordinates]] Gaussian normal coordinates | core | Coordinates built by labelling points of a spacelike hypersurface, carrying the labels along geodesics that leave it orthogonally, and using proper time along them as the time coordinate, so that g_00 = -1 and g_0i = 0. In 3+1 language this is unit lapse with zero shift (geodesic slicing), which tends to run into caustics and singularities. | — |
| [[harmonic-coordinates]] Harmonic coordinates | advanced | Coordinates in which each coordinate function satisfies the covariant wave equation, equivalently the contracted Christoffel symbols g^cd Gamma^a_cd vanish. In this gauge the principal part of the Ricci tensor becomes a wave operator acting on every metric component. | — |
| [[reduced-einstein-equations]] Reduced Einstein equations | advanced | Dropping the gauge-dependent terms from the Ricci tensor gives a quasi-linear hyperbolic system of wave equations for all ten metric components. If the harmonic condition and constraints hold initially, the Bianchi identity gives a wave equation for the gauge quantity with zero data, so solutions of the reduced system solve the full vacuum equations. | — |
| [[well-posed-initial-value-problem]] Well-posedness of the Einstein equations | advanced | An initial-value problem is well posed if solutions exist, are unique, and depend continuously on the data. Choquet-Bruhat (1952) proved local existence and uniqueness, up to diffeomorphism, for vacuum general relativity with smooth (not merely analytic) data by using harmonic coordinates and the reduced equations. | — |
| [[hyperbolicity-of-einstein-equations]] Hyperbolicity of the Einstein equations | advanced | Suitably gauge-fixed, the vacuum Einstein equations form a hyperbolic system: disturbances travel at finite speed along characteristics, the light cones. A telling consequence is that a solution may be exactly static up to some moment and then become dynamic, something elliptic or parabolic equations forbid. | — |
| [[characteristic-hypersurface]] Characteristic hypersurface | advanced | A hypersurface on which Cauchy data fail to determine the normal second derivatives of the field, so distinct solutions can join continuously across it. For Einstein's equations the characteristic hypersurfaces are exactly the null hypersurfaces, and their bicharacteristics are the null geodesics generating them. | — |
| [[characteristic-initial-value-problem]] Characteristic initial-value problem | advanced | Posing data for the field equations on null hypersurfaces (for example two intersecting null surfaces, or one null surface plus data at its boundary) instead of on a spacelike slice, and marching from one null surface to the next. It suits radiation problems, null infinity and colliding plane waves. | — |
| [[foliation]] Foliation of spacetime | core | A decomposition of spacetime into non-intersecting hypersurfaces, the level sets of a smooth scalar with nowhere-vanishing gradient, so that each event lies on exactly one leaf. A foliation by spacelike leaves is the arena for the 3+1 view of space evolving in time. | — |
| [[extrinsic-curvature-of-a-hypersurface]] Extrinsic curvature of a hypersurface | advanced | The symmetric tensor on a hypersurface formed by projecting the spacetime gradient of the unit normal into the surface (with a sign convention): it measures how the normal tilts as one moves along the surface, i.e. how the slice bends inside spacetime. Its trace K is the mean curvature, which measures local expansion of normal observers. | — |
| [[induced-covariant-derivative]] Induced covariant derivative on a hypersurface | advanced | For tensors tangent to a hypersurface, the covariant derivative intrinsic to the surface is the spacetime covariant derivative with every index projected into the surface; it is the Levi-Civita derivative of the induced metric. The spacetime derivative of a tangent vector along the surface equals this intrinsic part plus K times the unit normal. | — |
| [[gauss-codazzi-equations]] Gauss-Codazzi equations | advanced | Relations reconstructing projections of the spacetime Riemann tensor from hypersurface data: the Gauss equation gives the fully tangential projection as the slice's intrinsic Riemann tensor plus terms quadratic in K, and the Codazzi equation gives the projection with one normal index as an antisymmetrized intrinsic derivative of K. | — |
| [[eulerian-observer]] Eulerian (normal) observer | advanced | An observer whose worldline follows the unit normal of a spacelike foliation, so every leaf is a surface of simultaneity for them. Their four-acceleration is orthogonal to the normal, vanishes exactly when the normal curves are geodesics, and equals the spatial gradient of the logarithm of the lapse. | — |
| [[ricci-equation]] Ricci equation (3+1) | advanced | The projection of the spacetime Riemann tensor with two indices along the normal equals the Lie derivative of the extrinsic curvature along the normal plus acceleration and K-squared terms. Unlike the Gauss and Codazzi equations it needs a whole foliation, and it is the curvature relation that turns into the evolution equation for K. | — |
| [[hamiltonian-constraint]] Hamiltonian constraint | advanced | The normal-normal projection of Einstein's equations, obtained from the twice-contracted Gauss equation: the scalar curvature of the slice plus K squared minus K_ij K^ij equals 16 pi times the energy density measured by normal observers (plus a Lambda term). It is a constraint on initial data, not an evolution law. | — |
| [[momentum-constraint]] Momentum constraint | advanced | The mixed normal-tangential projection of Einstein's equations, obtained by contracting the Codazzi equation: the intrinsic divergence of K_ij minus the gradient of its trace equals 8 pi times the momentum density measured by normal observers. Together with the Hamiltonian constraint it makes up the four constraints. | — |
| [[lapse-function]] Lapse function | advanced | The positive scalar relating coordinate time to the proper time of normal observers between neighbouring slices: an Eulerian observer ages N dt as the slice label advances by dt. It encodes how fast the slicing advances in different places and is freely choosable gauge. | — |
| [[shift-vector]] Shift vector | advanced | The part of the time-evolution vector tangent to the slices: it measures how far the lines of constant spatial coordinates drift sideways relative to the normal per unit coordinate time. With the lapse it makes up the four free gauge functions of the 3+1 split. | — |
| [[3-plus-1-decomposition]] 3+1 decomposition | advanced | Writing spacetime as a foliation by spacelike slices threaded by time lines, so the metric is described by the lapse, the shift and the spatial metric of each slice: ds^2 = -N^2 dt^2 + gamma_ij (dx^i + beta^i dt)(dx^j + beta^j dt). It turns the covariant field equations into constraints plus evolution equations for the spatial geometry. | — |
| [[adm-evolution-equations]] ADM evolution equations | advanced | A first-order-in-time system: the time derivative of the spatial metric is minus twice the lapse times K plus shift terms (K is the velocity of the 3-metric), and the time derivative of K involves the Hessian of the lapse, the slice's Ricci tensor, K-quadratic terms, shift Lie-derivative terms and matter. With the constraints they are equivalent to Einstein's equations. | — |
| [[adm-formalism]] ADM formalism | advanced | The 1959 Hamiltonian formulation of general relativity using the spatial metric and its conjugate momentum (built from the extrinsic curvature) as canonical variables, with lapse and shift acting as Lagrange multipliers that enforce the Hamiltonian and momentum constraints. It frames gravity as the dynamics of 3-geometry, Wheeler's geometrodynamics. | — |
| [[gravitational-degrees-of-freedom]] Gravitational degrees of freedom | advanced | Once the four constraints and four coordinate freedoms are removed from the twelve pieces of 3+1 data (spatial metric and K), the vacuum gravitational field carries two degrees of freedom per spatial point, i.e. four freely specifiable data. These correspond to the two polarizations of gravitational waves. | — |
| [[conformal-method-for-initial-data]] Conformal method for initial data | advanced | A practical way to solve the constraints: freely choose a conformal 3-metric, the mean curvature and a transverse-traceless part of K, then solve coupled elliptic equations for a conformal factor and a vector potential, and rescale to get data satisfying the Hamiltonian and momentum constraints. | — |
| [[3-plus-1-gauge-conditions]] Slicing and shift conditions | advanced | Because lapse and shift obey no evolution equations, they must be chosen by rule. Slicing conditions fix the lapse (geodesic, comoving, maximal) and shift conditions fix how the grid moves (zero, comoving with matter, or chosen to simplify the 3-metric); good choices avoid singularities and grid distortion. | — |
| [[maximal-slicing]] Maximal slicing | advanced | A slicing in which the mean curvature K vanishes on every slice, enforced by solving an elliptic equation for the lapse. It stops normal observers from focusing and so slows the evolution near singularities; it is also a convenient simplification for initial data. | — |
| [[numerical-relativity]] Numerical relativity | advanced | Solving the full Einstein equations on computers: build initial data that satisfy the constraints, then evolve them with a stable (hyperbolic) formulation and well-chosen gauge conditions while monitoring constraint violation. Since the 2005 breakthroughs it simulates black-hole mergers and supplies waveforms for gravitational-wave detection. | — |
| [[2-plus-2-formalism]] 2+2 formalism | frontier | A splitting of spacetime by two families of hypersurfaces into spacelike 2-surfaces and their orthogonal 2-spaces, with a lapse matrix and two shift vectors playing the roles of 3+1 lapse and shift. When both families are null it suits characteristic evolution, radiation and null infinity, and it exhibits the two free gravitational data as the conformal 2-structure. | — |
| [[komar-mass]] Komar mass | advanced | The total mass of a stationary, asymptotically flat spacetime defined as a surface integral at infinity of the derivative of the timelike Killing vector, built by analogy with Gauss's law for charge. It gives m for Schwarzschild and extends to spacetimes that are only asymptotically stationary using asymptotic Killing vectors. | — |
| [[adm-mass]] ADM mass | advanced | The total mass-energy of an asymptotically flat spacetime computed on a spacelike slice from the fall-off of the spatial metric at spatial infinity, as a flux integral of its first derivatives; it arises as the boundary term of the ADM Hamiltonian. It is conserved and agrees with the Komar mass for stationary spacetimes. | — |
| [[positive-energy-theorem]] Positive energy theorem | frontier | If matter obeys the dominant energy condition, every asymptotically flat spacetime has non-negative ADM mass, and the mass is zero only for Minkowski spacetime. Proved by Schoen and Yau and later by Witten using spinors, it underpins the stability of flat spacetime. | — |
| [[quasi-local-mass]] Quasi-local mass | frontier | Attempts to assign mass-energy to a finite region bounded by a closed 2-surface, since no local gravitational energy density exists and global masses need infinity. Several inequivalent definitions exist and none is universally accepted. | — |
