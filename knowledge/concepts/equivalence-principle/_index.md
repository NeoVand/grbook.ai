# Equivalence principle and the principles of general relativity

The physical argument that gravity is spacetime geometry and the principles that turn it into a theory: Mach's principle and Newton's bucket; inertial versus gravitational mass and the universality of free fall with its torsion-balance tests; the weak, Einstein and strong equivalence principles (with the books' conflicting names) and the Einstein lift; free fall, weight, freely falling and local inertial frames as physics; tides as the only local signature of gravity and the resulting absence of global inertial frames; gravitational redshift and time dilation with their clock experiments (Pound-Rebka, Gravity Probe A, Hafele-Keating, GPS) and Schild's argument; general relativity and general covariance; the comma-to-semicolon rule and minimal versus non-minimal coupling; the correspondence principle, weak-field limit, weak-field metric and Newtonian limit of geodesic motion; metric theories and why scalar theories fail. The local-flatness theorem as mathematics, geodesic deviation, exact Schwarzschild redshift and the Newtonian limit of the field equations live in other domains.

| [[heuristic-role-of-physical-principles]] Heuristic role of physical principles | foundation | General relativity was built by letting a handful of physical principles (Mach, equivalence, covariance, minimal coupling, correspondence) constrain the mathematics. They are imprecise, but they explain why the theory has its form and where to look if it must be changed. | — |
| [[newtonian-spacetime]] Newtonian spacetime | foundation | Newton's arena is a stack of Euclidean spaces, one per instant of absolute time, related by Galilean transformations. Observers agree on time intervals and on distances between simultaneous events, but there is no single invariant spacetime length; relativity replaces this layered structure with one metric. | — |
| [[newtons-bucket-experiment]] Newton's bucket experiment | foundation | A bucket of water set spinning shows a curved surface only when the water itself rotates, regardless of its motion relative to the bucket. Newton read this as evidence for rotation relative to absolute space; Mach asked whether the distant matter of the universe is the real reference. | — |
| [[machs-principle]] Mach's principle | foundation | The idea that inertia and the inertial frames are fixed by the distribution of all matter in the universe rather than by absolute space. It inspired Einstein, and GR realizes it only partially: matter shapes geometry, yet vacuum solutions such as Minkowski space still have inertia. | — |
| [[hughes-drever-experiment]] Hughes-Drever experiment | advanced | Nuclear magnetic resonance experiments around 1960 that looked for a direction dependence of inertial mass as the Earth turned relative to the galaxy and found none to extraordinary precision. The null result rules out the anisotropic inertia a naive Machian picture predicts and is now read as a test of local Lorentz invariance. | — |
| [[free-fall]] Free fall | foundation | A body is in free fall when gravity is the only influence on it and no non-gravitational force acts. A freely falling observer feels no weight and an accelerometer she carries reads zero. | — |
| [[universality-of-free-fall]] Universality of free fall | foundation | All test bodies started from the same place with the same velocity follow the same trajectory in a gravitational field, whatever their mass or composition. Newton treated this as a coincidence; general relativity makes it the reason gravity can be geometry. | — |
| [[equality-of-inertial-and-gravitational-mass]] Equality of inertial and gravitational mass | foundation | The mass that resists acceleration equals the mass that couples to a gravitational field, for every body. Cancelling the two in Newton's second law shows why free-fall acceleration is independent of the body, which is the universality of free fall in Newtonian language. | — |
| [[equality-of-active-and-passive-gravitational-mass]] Equality of active and passive gravitational mass | foundation | A body's strength as a source of gravity equals its response to gravity. In Newtonian theory this follows from the third law applied to two bodies attracting each other, and it guarantees momentum conservation for gravitating systems. | — |
| [[eotvos-experiment]] Eotvos experiment | foundation | A torsion balance carrying two different materials twists if their ratios of gravitational to inertial mass differ. Such experiments, and their satellite successors, bound the fractional difference in free-fall acceleration (the Eotvos ratio) to parts in 10^13 and better. | — |
| [[universal-coupling-of-gravity]] Universal coupling of gravity | foundation | Gravity acts on and is sourced by everything: all matter and, through mass-energy equivalence, all forms of energy. Nothing can be shielded from it, which is why every contribution to the stress-energy must gravitate. | — |
| [[equivalence-of-gravity-and-acceleration]] Equivalence of gravity and acceleration | foundation | In a small enough region, a frame accelerating uniformly relative to an inertial frame cannot be distinguished by any local experiment from a frame held at rest in a uniform gravitational field. A strictly uniform field can therefore be transformed away entirely and has no invariant content. | — |
| [[gravity-as-an-inertial-force]] Gravity as an inertial force | foundation | Of all forces, only inertial (fictitious) forces and gravity are proportional to the mass of the body acted on. Einstein read this as a common origin: gravity is an inertial effect of describing motion from frames that are not freely falling, which in geometric language puts gravity into the connection coefficients. | — |
| [[weak-equivalence-principle]] Weak equivalence principle | foundation | The motion of an uncharged test body under gravity alone depends only on its initial position and velocity, so a freely falling frame removes gravitational acceleration for all nearby particles at once. In geometric form it says free test particles follow timelike geodesics of one spacetime metric. | — |
| [[freely-falling-frame]] Freely falling frame | foundation | A reference frame that moves with a test particle falling under gravity alone. If it also does not rotate, gravity disappears inside it over a small region, and it stays locally inertial along the whole free-fall world line. | — |
| [[einstein-lift-thought-experiment]] Einstein lift thought experiment | foundation | An observer in a sealed room compares four cases: an accelerating rocket, a coasting rocket, resting on Earth, and falling freely. With one released body the pairs (accelerating, resting) and (coasting, falling) look identical; with two separated bodies, relative motion reveals the real field. | — |
| [[tidal-force]] Tidal force | foundation | Tidal effects are relative accelerations of nearby freely falling bodies, caused by the spatial variation of the gravitational field. No choice of frame removes them, so they are the local, frame-independent signature of real gravity, which general relativity identifies with spacetime curvature. | — |
| [[local-inertial-frame]] Local inertial frame | foundation | A freely falling, non-rotating frame in which special relativity holds within a region small enough that tidal variations are undetectable. Mathematically, coordinates at an event where the metric is Minkowskian and its first derivatives vanish, so the Christoffel symbols are zero there. | — |
| [[einstein-equivalence-principle]] Einstein equivalence principle | foundation | In any freely falling local inertial frame, every local non-gravitational experiment gives the same result as in special relativity. It combines the weak equivalence principle with local Lorentz invariance and local position invariance, and it implies that gravity is described by a spacetime metric. | — |
| [[local-lorentz-invariance]] Local Lorentz invariance | foundation | The outcome of a small non-gravitational experiment in a freely falling laboratory does not depend on the velocity of that laboratory. It is one of the three clauses of the Einstein equivalence principle and is tested by modern Michelson-Morley-type and clock-comparison experiments. | — |
| [[local-position-invariance]] Local position invariance | foundation | The outcome of a small non-gravitational experiment in a freely falling laboratory does not depend on where or when it is done, so dimensionless constants of nature are the same everywhere and always. Redshift experiments with clocks test its spatial part. | — |
| [[strong-equivalence-principle]] Strong equivalence principle | advanced | The extension of the Einstein equivalence principle to all experiments, including gravitational ones and self-gravitating bodies: local physics in free fall, gravity included, is independent of velocity, location and gravitational binding energy. General relativity satisfies it; many alternative theories do not, which lunar laser ranging tests. | — |
| [[weightlessness]] Weightlessness and weight as a contact force | foundation | What we feel as weight is not gravity but the floor pushing us off our free-fall path; a scale measures that support force. Astronauts in orbit feel weightless even though gravity there is nearly as strong as at the surface, because they are falling freely. | — |
| [[gravitational-redshift]] Gravitational redshift | foundation | Light climbing out of a gravitational potential arrives with lower frequency; to first order the fractional shift is gh/c^2, or the potential difference over c^2. It follows from energy conservation or from the equivalence principle alone, before any field equations. | — |
| [[gravitational-time-dilation]] Gravitational time dilation | foundation | A clock held lower in a gravitational potential ticks more slowly, when compared by signals, than an identical clock higher up; in a weak field the rate ratio is about 1 + (difference in potential)/c^2. It is the clock-rate face of gravitational redshift and needs no curvature: accelerated clocks in flat spacetime show it too. | — |
| [[pound-rebka-experiment]] Pound-Rebka experiment | foundation | In 1960 (improved with Snider in 1965) gamma rays were sent up a 22.5 m tower at Harvard, using the Mossbauer effect to resolve frequency shifts of a few parts in 10^15. A small Doppler velocity of the source compensated the gravitational shift and confirmed the predicted gh/c^2 to about 1 percent. | — |
| [[gravity-probe-a]] Gravity Probe A | foundation | A 1976 rocket flight carried a hydrogen-maser clock to about 10,000 km and compared it with a ground maser, confirming the gravitational frequency shift to a few parts in 10^4, about a hundred times better than the tower experiment. | — |
| [[hafele-keating-experiment]] Hafele-Keating experiment | foundation | Atomic clocks flown around the world in both directions were compared with ground clocks; the measured gains and losses matched the combination of velocity time dilation and gravitational time dilation at altitude. | — |
| [[gps-relativistic-corrections]] GPS relativistic corrections | foundation | GPS satellite clocks run slow by about 7 microseconds per day from their orbital speed and fast by about 45 from their altitude, a net gain near 38 microseconds per day. Left uncorrected, this would build up positioning errors of kilometres per day. | — |
| [[schilds-redshift-argument]] Schild's redshift argument | foundation | If gravitational redshift occurs in a static field, a frame at rest on Earth cannot have Minkowski geometry: static time translation would force equal intervals between successive light crests at the bottom and top of a tower, contradicting the measured frequency difference. Gravity therefore requires curved spacetime. | — |
| [[absence-of-global-inertial-frames]] Absence of global inertial frames | foundation | When gravity is present one generally cannot build a single frame with rigid rulers, synchronized clocks and Euclidean geometry everywhere: freely falling frames are inertial only locally, and non-uniform fields prevent patching them together. This is the reason special relativity must be generalized. | — |
| [[gravity-as-spacetime-curvature]] Gravity as spacetime curvature | foundation | Since gravity enters motion like an inertial effect yet real fields cannot be removed globally, gravity is modelled as the curvature of spacetime: matter tells spacetime how to curve, and spacetime tells free bodies how to move. The connection stays the metric connection, but the metric is no longer flat. | — |
| [[metric-as-gravitational-potential]] Metric as gravitational potential | foundation | Connection coefficients play the role of gravitational forces and are first derivatives of the metric, so the metric components take over the role of the Newtonian potential. Hence the field equations should be second-order differential equations for the metric, as Poisson is for the potential. | — |
| [[rubber-sheet-analogy]] Rubber-sheet analogy | foundation | The popular picture of a heavy ball denting a stretched sheet so that marbles roll on curved paths. It hints at geodesic motion but is circular (it uses gravity to pull the ball down) and shows only spatial curvature, missing the time part of the metric that governs slow-moving bodies. | — |
| [[principle-of-general-relativity]] Principle of general relativity | foundation | All observers, inertial or not, are equally able to discover the laws of physics. It extends the special principle of relativity, although the essential difference between the two theories is the inclusion of gravity, not the admission of accelerated observers. | — |
| [[general-covariance]] General covariance | foundation | Physical laws must be expressible as tensor equations that hold in every coordinate system, since curved spacetime has no privileged coordinates. On its own it has little content, because almost any theory can be written covariantly; its force comes when paired with the equivalence principle. | — |
| [[correspondence-principle]] Correspondence principle | foundation | A new theory must reproduce the confirmed predictions of the theory it replaces within that theory's domain. General relativity must reduce to special relativity without gravity and to Newtonian gravity for weak fields, slow motion and small pressures. | — |
| [[weak-field-limit]] Weak-field limit | core | A gravitational field is weak when coordinates exist in which the metric differs from Minkowski by small perturbations, g = eta + h with \|h\| << 1, equivalently when \|Phi\|/c^2 << 1. Terms of second order in the perturbation or potential are then dropped. | — |
| [[newtonian-limit]] Newtonian limit | core | The regime of weak fields, speeds small compared with light, and stresses small compared with energy density, in which general relativity must reduce to Newtonian gravity. For slow bodies the time component of the four-velocity dominates, and slowness itself forces the field to be weak. | — |
| [[weak-field-metric]] Weak-field metric | core | The first-order metric of a static weak field with Newtonian potential Phi is ds^2 = -(1 + 2Phi/c^2)c^2 dt^2 + (1 - 2Phi/c^2)(dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2). The time part alone reproduces Newtonian motion and clock-rate effects; the equal spatial part matters for light. | — |
| [[newtonian-limit-of-geodesic-equation]] Newtonian limit of the geodesic equation | core | For slow motion in the weak-field metric, only Gamma^i_00 = partial_i Phi survives, and the geodesic equation reduces to d^2x^i/dt^2 = -partial_i Phi, Newton's law of gravity. Read backwards, requiring Newtonian motion fixes g_00 = -(1 + 2Phi/c^2) without any field equation. | — |
| [[newtonian-gravity-as-curvature-of-time]] Newtonian gravity as curvature of time | core | For slowly moving bodies the proper time along a world line is dominated by the g_00 term, so Newtonian orbits are set by g_00 alone, the same component that fixes gravitational redshift. Spatial curvature only shows up in post-Newtonian effects such as the full bending of light. | — |
| [[spatial-metric-of-stationary-spacetime]] Spatial metric of a stationary spacetime | advanced | In a stationary metric with time-space cross terms, the distance measured by bouncing light between neighbouring points is dl^2 = (g_ij - g_0i g_0j / g_00) dx^i dx^j. It is how rulers, rather than coordinates, define lengths for observers at rest in the field. | — |
| [[metric-theory-of-gravity]] Metric theory of gravity | core | A theory in which spacetime carries a symmetric metric, freely falling test bodies follow its geodesics, and non-gravitational laws take special-relativistic form in local freely falling frames. The Einstein equivalence principle essentially forces this structure; GR and Brans-Dicke are both metric theories. | — |
| [[comma-goes-to-semicolon-rule]] Comma-goes-to-semicolon rule | core | To carry a law from special to general relativity, write it as a tensor equation, then replace eta by g and partial derivatives (commas) by covariant derivatives (semicolons). It works because the law holds in a local inertial frame, where the two derivatives agree, and a tensor equation true in one frame is true in all. | — |
| [[minimal-coupling]] Minimal coupling | core | When a special-relativistic law or Lagrangian is moved into curved spacetime, add no terms that involve curvature explicitly: replace eta by g, partial by covariant derivatives, and d^4x by sqrt(-g) d^4x, and stop. It is a simplicity rule, not a consequence of the equivalence principle. | — |
| [[non-minimal-coupling]] Non-minimal coupling | advanced | Terms such as a Ricci-scalar coupling xi R phi^2 that vanish in flat spacetime, so agreement with special relativity cannot rule them out. They also arise as an ambiguity: covariant derivatives do not commute, so a flat law with second derivatives has several curved versions differing by curvature terms. | — |
| [[scalar-theory-of-gravity]] Scalar theory of gravity | advanced | Attempts to describe gravity by a single scalar potential on flat spacetime, or equivalently a conformally flat metric. They can respect the weak equivalence principle but couple only to the trace of the stress-energy, so light (traceless) is not deflected, contradicting observation. | — |
