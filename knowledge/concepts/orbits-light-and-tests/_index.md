# Orbits, light propagation and tests of general relativity

Motion of test bodies and light in weak fields and in the Schwarzschild exterior, and the experiments that test these predictions: equatorial-plane reduction and the Schwarzschild conserved specific energy and angular momentum, the massive and photon effective potentials and orbit equations, circular, bound precessing, scattering and capture orbits, the ISCO, marginally bound orbit and photon sphere, critical impact parameter and escape cone for static emitters, perihelion precession (Mercury, binary-pulsar periastron advance), coordinate speed of light and optical analogies, light deflection and its eclipse and radio tests, Shapiro delay, gravitational lensing optics (lens equation, Einstein ring, microlensing, weak lensing), the classical tests, post-Newtonian and PPN frameworks, and gyroscope precession tests (geodetic precession, Gravity Probe B). Excludes horizon crossing, radial infall, shadows and all Kerr orbits (black-holes), gravitational redshift and clock experiments (equivalence-principle), frame-dragging theory (gravitational-waves), radiation-driven orbital decay (gravitational-wave-astronomy), and lensing as a cosmological probe (physical-cosmology).

| [[equatorial-plane-reduction]] Equatorial-plane reduction of Schwarzschild orbits | core | Because the Schwarzschild geometry is spherically symmetric, any geodesic can be rotated so that it starts in the plane theta = pi/2 with no polar velocity, and the theta geodesic equation then keeps it there forever. Orbits in general relativity therefore stay planar, just as in Newtonian central-force motion. | — |
| [[specific-energy]] Specific energy (conserved energy per unit rest mass) | core | For a geodesic in Schwarzschild spacetime the quantity E-tilde = (1 - 2M/r) dt/dtau = -u_t is constant because the metric does not depend on t; it is the particle's energy per unit rest mass as a static observer at infinity would measure it. E-tilde < 1 means the particle is bound, E-tilde = 1 means it was released from rest at infinity, and for light the same construction with an affine parameter gives the conserved photon energy E = -p_t. | — |
| [[specific-angular-momentum]] Specific angular momentum | core | Because the Schwarzschild metric does not depend on the azimuthal angle, L-tilde = r^2 sin^2(theta) dphi/dtau = u_phi is constant along every geodesic; in the equatorial plane it is r^2 dphi/dtau, the Newtonian angular momentum per unit mass with proper time replacing coordinate time. Its ratio to M controls the shape of the effective potential and hence which orbits exist. | — |
| [[schwarzschild-effective-potential]] Schwarzschild effective potential | core | Inserting the conserved specific energy and angular momentum into the four-velocity normalization turns equatorial Schwarzschild geodesic motion into one-dimensional radial motion, (1/2)(dr/dtau)^2 + V_eff(r) = (E-tilde^2 - 1)/2 with V_eff = -M/r + L^2/(2r^2) - M L^2/r^3. Drawing horizontal energy lines on V_eff reads off turning points, circular orbits, bound, scattering and capture orbits exactly as in Newtonian mechanics; Schutz writes the same content as (dr/dtau)^2 = E^2 - V^2(r) with V^2 = (1 - 2M/r)(1 + L^2/r^2). | — |
| [[relativistic-correction-to-effective-potential]] Relativistic 1/r^3 correction to the effective potential | core | Compared with the Newtonian effective potential, the Schwarzschild one has a single extra attractive term -M L^2/r^3 (G M L^2/(c^2 r^3) in ordinary units), suppressed by 1/c^2 but growing faster than the centrifugal barrier as r shrinks. This term gives the barrier a finite height, and is the source of perihelion precession, the innermost stable circular orbit and capture of particles with angular momentum. | — |
| [[apsidal-angle]] Apsidal angle and orbit closure | prerequisite | The apsidal (turning) angle is the azimuth swept while r goes from its minimum to its maximum and back; a bound orbit closes only if this angle is a rational multiple of 2 pi. Any departure from exactly 2 pi per radial cycle makes the orbit's axis rotate, which is the geometric content of precession. | — |
| [[epicyclic-frequency]] Radial (epicyclic) oscillation frequency | core | A slightly perturbed circular orbit oscillates radially about the potential minimum with a frequency set by the curvature of the effective potential. If this radial frequency equals the orbital frequency the orbit closes; in Schwarzschild it is smaller by the factor (1 - 6M^2/L^2)^{1/2} (roughly (1 - 6M/r)^{1/2}), so the perihelion advances each orbit and radial oscillations become impossible at the innermost stable circular orbit. | — |
| [[stable-and-unstable-circular-orbits]] Stable and unstable circular orbits in Schwarzschild | core | Circular geodesics sit where dV_eff/dr = 0: for L^2 > 12 M^2 there are two radii r = (L^2/2M)[1 +/- (1 - 12M^2/L^2)^{1/2}], an outer minimum where the orbit is stable and an inner maximum (between 3M and 6M) where any nudge sends the particle inward or outward. On a circular orbit L^2 = M r^2/(r - 3M) and E^2 = (1 - 2M/r)^2 r/(r - 3M), so circular orbits for massive particles require r > 3M. | — |
| [[innermost-stable-circular-orbit]] Innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO) | core | In Schwarzschild spacetime stable circular orbits exist only for r >= 6M: at L = sqrt(12) M the potential minimum and maximum merge into an inflection at r = 6M, and for smaller angular momentum there is no well at all. Inside the ISCO a particle cannot orbit stably, which sets the inner edge of accretion discs around black holes. | — |
| [[marginally-bound-circular-orbit]] Marginally bound circular orbit | core | For L-tilde = 4M the peak of the Schwarzschild effective potential sits at r = 4M with height exactly equal to the energy of a particle at rest at infinity, so the unstable circular orbit there is just unbound. Particles falling in from infinity with less angular momentum than this cannot be turned back and are captured. | — |
| [[keplers-third-law-in-schwarzschild]] Kepler's third law for circular Schwarzschild orbits | core | For a circular geodesic at areal radius r the angular velocity in Schwarzschild coordinate time satisfies (dphi/dt)^2 = M/r^3, so the period measured by a distant static observer is 2 pi (r^3/M)^{1/2}, formally identical to Kepler's law. The orbiting body's own clock records a shorter proper period, 2 pi r (r/M - 3)^{1/2}. | — |
| [[bound-precessing-orbit]] Bound precessing orbit | core | A particle whose effective energy lies inside the potential well oscillates between a periapsis and an apoapsis radius while its azimuth advances by more than 2 pi per radial cycle. The nearly elliptical orbit therefore rotates, tracing a rosette. | — |
| [[scattering-orbit]] Scattering orbit | core | An unbound particle (E-tilde > 1) whose energy line meets the inner potential barrier comes in from infinity, reaches a single minimum radius and returns to infinity. Close to the barrier peak it can whirl around the centre several times before escaping, which has no Newtonian counterpart. | — |
| [[capture-orbit]] Capture (plunging) orbit | core | If a particle's effective energy exceeds the peak of the Schwarzschild effective potential, or the potential has no peak at all, nothing turns it back and it spirals inward through r = 2M. Unlike Newtonian gravity, where any angular momentum guarantees a bounce, general relativity lets particles with angular momentum be captured. | — |
| [[schwarzschild-orbit-equation]] Schwarzschild orbit equation (relativistic Binet equation) | core | Dividing the radial energy equation by (dphi/dtau)^2 and substituting u = 1/r gives the shape of an equatorial timelike geodesic: u'' + u = M/L^2 + 3M u^2. The last term is absent in Newtonian gravity and is smaller than the Newtonian term by about 3v^2/c^2, which is why it produces only a slow precession in the solar system. | — |
| [[perihelion-precession]] Perihelion precession | core | In Schwarzschild spacetime a bound orbit's radial oscillation is slightly slower than its angular motion, so successive perihelia advance by Delta phi = 6 pi M/[a(1 - e^2)] (6 pi GM/(c^2 a (1 - e^2)) in SI) per orbit. The result follows from perturbing the orbit equation: the 3Mu^2 term produces a secular term that resums into a slightly lengthened angular period. | — |
| [[perihelion-precession-of-mercury]] Anomalous perihelion precession of Mercury | core | Mercury's perihelion advances about 575 arcseconds per century relative to the stars (far more in Earth-based coordinates); Newtonian perturbations by the other planets account for about 532, and the leftover 43 arcseconds per century, which defeated nineteenth-century fixes such as the planet Vulcan, is exactly the general-relativistic prediction. It was the first observational success of the theory. | — |
| [[binary-pulsar-periastron-advance]] Binary pulsar periastron advance | core | The orbit of the Hulse-Taylor binary pulsar rotates by about 4.2 degrees per year, some 35,000 times faster than Mercury's perihelion, because the two neutron stars are close and massive. Measuring this advance and assuming general relativity fixes the total mass of the system, which then feeds the other relativistic timing tests. | — |
| [[photon-effective-potential]] Photon effective potential | core | For light in Schwarzschild spacetime the null condition with the conserved E and L becomes 1/b^2 = (1/L^2)(dr/dlambda)^2 + W_eff(r) with W_eff = (1 - 2M/r)/r^2. W_eff has a single maximum 1/(27M^2) at r = 3M and no minimum, so light has no bound orbits, and the path depends only on the impact parameter b = L/E, not on the photon's energy. | — |
| [[impact-parameter]] Impact parameter | core | The impact parameter b of an unbound trajectory is the perpendicular distance from the centre to the straight line the particle or ray follows far away. For Schwarzschild light rays b = L/E, a ratio independent of how the affine parameter is normalized, and to first order it equals the closest-approach radius used in deflection and time-delay formulas. | — |
| [[photon-sphere]] Photon sphere | core | At r = 3M in Schwarzschild spacetime the photon effective potential peaks, so light with b = sqrt(27) M can circle the mass on a circular orbit. The orbit is unstable, any perturbation sends the light inward or out to infinity, and its radius does not depend on the photon's energy; an observer there could in principle see the back of their own head. | — |
| [[critical-impact-parameter]] Critical impact parameter | core | Light approaching a Schwarzschild mass is captured if its impact parameter is smaller than b_c = sqrt(27) M = 3 sqrt(3) M (about 5.2 M), where 1/b^2 equals the peak of the photon potential; rays with larger b are deflected and escape. Rays with exactly b_c asymptotically wind onto the photon sphere. | — |
| [[photon-capture]] Photon capture | core | An incoming light ray whose 1/b^2 exceeds the peak of the photon potential meets no turning point and falls through r = 2M. Outside r = 3M an outward-moving ray always escapes, whatever its impact parameter. | — |
| [[photon-escape-cone]] Photon escape cone | core | A static emitter at radius r measures the angle psi of an emitted ray from the outward radial direction in its own orthonormal frame, with sin psi = (b/r)(1 - 2M/r)^{1/2}. Setting b = sqrt(27) M gives the critical angle psi_c that separates escaping from captured rays: outside 3M the captured cone surrounds the inward direction, inside 3M only a shrinking cone around the outward direction escapes, and at r = 2M nothing does. | — |
| [[photon-orbit-equation]] Photon orbit equation | core | The path of an equatorial light ray in Schwarzschild spacetime obeys (du/dphi)^2 = 1/b^2 - u^2 + 2M u^3 with u = 1/r, or after differentiation u'' + u = 3M u^2. Without the right-hand side the solutions are straight lines, so the 3Mu^2 term is entirely responsible for light bending. | — |
| [[coordinate-speed-of-light]] Coordinate speed of light | core | Setting ds^2 = 0 in a static metric gives the rate dx/dt at which light covers coordinate distance per coordinate time; near a mass it is less than c (about 1 - 2GM/(rc^2) in the weak field). Every local observer still measures c, so the reduced coordinate speed is bookkeeping, yet it correctly predicts delays and bending; imposing ds^2 = 0 along an assumed path is a quick alternative to solving the null geodesic equation. | — |
| [[fermats-principle-in-static-spacetime]] Fermat's principle in a static spacetime | advanced | In a static spacetime the spatial track of a light ray makes the coordinate travel time stationary; equivalently it is a geodesic of the optical metric g_ij/(-g_00). This explains why a null geodesic of spacetime projects onto a bent path in space. | — |
| [[gravitational-refractive-index]] Gravitational refractive index | core | Because the coordinate speed of light depends on position, a static weak gravitational field acts like an optical medium with refractive index n about 1 - 2Phi/c^2 (with Phi < 0), larger closer to the mass, so rays bend toward it. With separate time and space potentials the index is 1 - (Phi + Psi)/c^2, which shows that half of the bending comes from spatial curvature. | — |
| [[gravitational-deflection-of-light]] Gravitational deflection of light | core | A light ray passing a spherical mass with impact parameter b is turned through 4GM/(c^2 b) to first order, 1.75 arcseconds for a ray grazing the Sun, independent of the light's frequency. The equivalence principle alone forces some bending, but it gives only half this value; the other half comes from the curvature of space. | — |
| [[newtonian-deflection-of-light]] Newtonian (corpuscular) deflection of light | core | Treating light as a Newtonian particle moving at c past a mass gives a deflection 2GM/(c^2 b), about 0.87 arcseconds at the solar limb, as Soldner computed in 1801. It is exactly half the general-relativistic value because it captures only the time part g_00 of the metric and misses the equal contribution of spatial curvature. | — |
| [[1919-eclipse-expedition]] 1919 eclipse expedition | core | During the total solar eclipse of May 1919, Eddington's teams photographed stars near the darkened Sun and compared their positions with night-time plates, finding an outward shift near the 1.75 arcsecond general-relativistic prediction. The result made Einstein famous, though its systematic errors were large and eclipse measurements remained scattered for decades. | — |
| [[radio-interferometric-light-deflection]] Radio interferometric light-deflection measurement | core | Very long baseline interferometry tracks the apparent angular separation of compact radio sources such as quasars as the Sun passes near their line of sight. It needs no eclipse, avoids optical systematics, and confirms the general-relativistic deflection to a small fraction of a percent, constraining the PPN parameter gamma. | — |
| [[shapiro-time-delay]] Shapiro time delay | core | A radar or light signal passing near a mass takes longer, in coordinate time, than it would in flat space: for a round trip Earth-planet-Earth grazing the Sun the extra time is about (4GM/c^3)[ln(4 D_E D_P / b^2) + 1], a couple of hundred microseconds. Proposed by Shapiro in 1964 as the fourth classical test, it has since been measured precisely with planetary radar and spacecraft such as Cassini. | — |
| [[gravitational-lensing]] Gravitational lensing | core | An intervening mass deflects light from a background source so that rays passing on different sides can reach the same observer, producing displaced, magnified, distorted or multiple images. Because the deflection 4GM/(c^2 b) falls off as 1/b, a point mass has no single focus: parallel rays at impact parameter b cross the axis at distance b^2/(4GM/c^2), so the lens has a focal line. | — |
| [[gravitational-lens-equation]] Point-mass lens equation | core | In the small-angle thin-lens approximation the observed image angle theta and the true source angle beta of a point-mass lens are related by theta = beta + theta_E^2/theta, where theta_E is the Einstein angle. For beta not zero it has two image solutions on opposite sides of the lens; for beta = 0 the images merge into a ring. | — |
| [[einstein-ring]] Einstein ring | core | When a point source, a spherically symmetric lens and the observer are exactly aligned, rays deflected by the same angle arrive from every azimuth, so the image is a ring. Its angular radius, the Einstein angle theta_E = [4GM D_ls/(c^2 D_l D_s)]^{1/2}, sets the natural angular scale of any lens. | — |
| [[microlensing]] Gravitational microlensing | core | When a compact foreground object passes close to the line of sight to a star, the lensed images are too close together to resolve, but the source brightens and fades in a characteristic symmetric light curve. Microlensing surveys search for dark compact objects in the Galactic halo and for exoplanets. | — |
| [[weak-lensing]] Weak gravitational lensing | core | When deflection angles are small, lensing may not split images but still slightly distorts and magnifies them; the lens can concentrate more light on a telescope than would otherwise arrive. Weak lensing of many background galaxies is statistical and is the regime relevant to cluster and cosmological lensing. | — |
| [[classical-tests-of-general-relativity]] Classical tests of general relativity | core | The historic weak-field checks of general relativity in the solar system are the anomalous perihelion advance of Mercury, the deflection of starlight by the Sun and gravitational redshift, with the radar echo (Shapiro) time delay added as a fourth in 1964. Each probes a different combination of the metric's time and space parts. | — |
| [[post-newtonian-approximation]] Post-Newtonian approximation | advanced | The post-Newtonian approximation expands general relativity about Newtonian gravity in the small parameters GM/(rc^2) ~ v^2/c^2 of a weak-field, slow-motion system. Its first corrections include perihelion advance, light deflection, gravitomagnetic effects and pressure contributions to active mass; higher orders are needed for accurate waveforms of inspiralling binaries. | — |
| [[parametrized-post-newtonian-formalism]] Parametrized post-Newtonian (PPN) formalism | advanced | The PPN formalism writes the weak-field, slow-motion metric of any metric theory of gravity as the Newtonian metric plus post-Newtonian terms multiplied by theory-dependent coefficients (ten in general). Experiments then measure the coefficients, so theories are compared through numbers rather than one at a time; for static spherical symmetry only beta and gamma appear, and general relativity has beta = gamma = 1. | — |
| [[ppn-parameter-gamma]] PPN parameter gamma | advanced | Gamma measures how much spatial curvature a unit rest mass produces: it multiplies the 2GM/(c^2 r) term in the spatial part of the isotropic weak-field metric and equals 1 in general relativity. Light deflection and Shapiro delay are proportional to (1 + gamma)/2, so radio interferometry and spacecraft timing measure it to within about 10^-5 of unity. | — |
| [[ppn-parameter-beta]] PPN parameter beta | advanced | Beta is the coefficient of the second-order (GM/c^2 r)^2 term in g_00 and measures how nonlinear the superposition of gravity is; it equals 1 in general relativity. Once gamma is known, the perihelion advance, proportional to (2 + 2 gamma - beta)/3, determines beta. | — |
| [[spin-four-vector]] Spin four-vector of a gyroscope | core | A gyroscope's angular momentum is represented by a four-vector S orthogonal to its four-velocity, S.u = 0, so in the gyroscope's rest frame it is purely spatial. With no torque S is Fermi-Walker transported along the world line, which for a freely falling gyroscope is parallel transport. | — |
| [[geodetic-precession]] Geodetic precession | advanced | A gyroscope carried in free fall around a non-rotating mass has its spin parallel transported through curved spacetime, and after each orbit its axis has turned relative to distant stars at the rate Omega = (3M/2r^3) r x v. For a gyroscope in low Earth orbit this amounts to about 6.6 arcseconds per year. | — |
| [[gravity-probe-b]] Gravity Probe B | advanced | Gravity Probe B (launched 2004, results 2011) flew superconducting gyroscopes in a polar Earth orbit and measured their drift relative to a guide star: a geodetic precession of about 6602 milliarcseconds per year and a frame-dragging precession of about 37 milliarcseconds per year, both consistent with general relativity. Frame dragging of satellite orbits has also been measured by laser ranging of geodetic satellites. | — |
