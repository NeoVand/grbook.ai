# Cosmology I: geometry and kinematics of an expanding universe

The geometry of a homogeneous, isotropic universe and what observers measure in it, independent of which matter drives the expansion. Covers the cosmological and Copernican principles, homogeneity and isotropy, comoving observers, Weyl's postulate and cosmic time; the FLRW metric, its curvature, the parameter k and the three spatial geometries with their topology; the scale factor, Hubble parameter, Hubble-Lemaitre law, peculiar velocity and the non-expansion of bound systems; light propagation, cosmological redshift and energy non-conservation; proper, comoving, luminosity and angular-diameter distances, the Hubble radius, lookback time, the deceleration parameter and cosmography; conformal time, particle and event horizons, and Penrose diagrams of FLRW models. How the field equations fix a(t), specific model universes and measured parameters belong to cosmological-dynamics and physical-cosmology.

| Concept | Tier | Summary | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [[cosmology]] Cosmology | core | Cosmology treats the whole Universe as a single physical system, asking about its large-scale geometry, contents and history. In general relativity this means finding a spacetime metric that solves the field equations for the smoothed-out matter and energy of the Universe. | — |
| [[homogeneity]] Spatial homogeneity | core | A spatial slice is homogeneous when every point on it is equivalent to every other: some isometry of the slice carries any point to any other, so on large scales the density and statistical properties of the Universe are the same everywhere at a given cosmic time. | — |
| [[isotropy]] Spatial isotropy | core | Isotropy about a point means no spatial direction there is preferred: an isometry fixing the point can rotate any direction into any other. A space isotropic about every point is automatically homogeneous, but a homogeneous space need not be isotropic (think of a uniform field pointing one way). | — |
| [[copernican-principle]] Copernican principle | core | The Copernican principle is the working assumption that we occupy no special place in the Universe, so observers elsewhere at the same epoch would see essentially the same cosmos. It is the conceptual ancestor of the cosmological principle and implies the expansion is not centred on us. | — |
| [[cosmological-principle]] Cosmological principle | core | The cosmological principle asserts that, once structure below roughly 100 Mpc is averaged out, the Universe is spatially homogeneous and isotropic at each cosmic time. Geometrically, spacetime is sliced into spacelike hypersurfaces each homogeneous and isotropic about all its points, which is extremely restrictive on the metric. | — |
| [[homogeneity-scale]] Homogeneity scale and coarse-graining | core | Cosmological models treat matter as a smooth fluid obtained by averaging galaxies over regions of about 100 Mpc or more, above which the averaged density is uniform. The models make no claim about structure on smaller scales. | — |
| [[perfect-cosmological-principle]] Perfect cosmological principle | advanced | The perfect cosmological principle strengthens the cosmological principle by requiring the large-scale Universe to look the same at every time as well as every place. It was the founding assumption of steady-state theory and geometrically leads to de Sitter spacetime. | — |
| [[comoving-observer]] Comoving observer | core | A comoving (fundamental) observer moves with the smoothed cosmic fluid, the substratum whose particles are averaged galaxies. Only such observers see the Universe as isotropic; their world lines are timelike geodesics and their proper time is cosmic time. | — |
| [[weyl-postulate]] Weyl's postulate | core | Weyl's postulate states that the world lines of the cosmic substratum form a smooth congruence of timelike geodesics that never cross, except possibly at an initial or final singularity. It guarantees a single well-defined matter four-velocity at every event. | — |
| [[local-cosmological-rest-frame]] Local cosmological rest frame | core | At each event the local cosmological rest frame is the local inertial frame in which the cosmic microwave background shows no dipole anisotropy. It moves with the Hubble flow and is a cleaner definition than the average motion of nearby galaxies, which have bulk flows. | — |
| [[orthogonality-of-comoving-world-lines]] Orthogonality of comoving world lines to homogeneous slices | core | In a homogeneous, isotropic cosmology the comoving world lines must meet the homogeneous slices at right angles, because any tilt would single out a spatial direction. In comoving coordinates this gives g_0i = 0, and with t as proper time g_00 = -1. | — |
| [[cosmic-time]] Cosmic time | core | Cosmic time is a global time coordinate whose constant-time slices are the homogeneous, isotropic hypersurfaces and which equals the proper time of comoving observers. It defines a cosmic simultaneity reflecting the matter distribution, not a violation of relativity, and can be read off any uniformly evolving quantity such as the CMB temperature. | — |
| [[comoving-coordinates]] Comoving coordinates | core | Comoving coordinates label each comoving observer by fixed spatial coordinates and use cosmic time as the time coordinate. All expansion then lives in the time dependence of the spatial metric, and physical separations equal the scale factor times fixed comoving separations. | — |
| [[world-map-and-world-picture]] World map and world picture | core | The world map is the Universe on one slice of constant cosmic time, everything 'as it is now', which no one can observe. The world picture is what an observer actually sees, lying on their past light cone, so more distant objects appear as they were at earlier cosmic times. | — |
| [[scale-factor]] Scale factor | core | The scale factor a(t) is the single function of cosmic time multiplying the spatial metric, so every proper distance between comoving points grows by the same ratio a(t2)/a(t1). It is usually normalized to 1 today; growth that differed between directions would violate isotropy. | — |
| [[hubble-parameter]] Hubble parameter | core | The Hubble parameter H = (da/dt)/a is the fractional expansion rate; its present value H0, the Hubble constant, is about 70 km/s/Mpc. It generally changes with time, and 1/H0 sets the characteristic Hubble time of the Universe. | — |
| [[hubble-lemaitre-law]] Hubble-Lemaitre law | core | On average galaxies recede with speed proportional to distance, v = H0 d. Uniform scaling of all distances is the only velocity field that keeps a homogeneous Universe homogeneous, so every comoving observer sees the same law; the recession due purely to growth of a(t) is called the Hubble flow. | — |
| [[peculiar-velocity]] Peculiar velocity | core | A peculiar velocity is a galaxy's motion relative to the local comoving frame, caused by local gravitational pulls and superposed on the Hubble flow. It is small (below about 1e-3 c) but dominates the redshifts of very nearby galaxies, and a freely moving particle's peculiar momentum decays as 1/a with expansion. | — |
| [[bound-systems-do-not-expand]] Bound systems do not expand | core | Atoms, planetary systems, stars and galaxies are held together by local forces and are not stretched by the cosmic scale factor; the expanding FLRW description applies only above the homogeneity scale. A common misconception is that space expands everywhere, dragging bound objects apart. | — |
| [[three-sphere]] Three-sphere | foundation | The three-sphere is the set of points at fixed distance a from the origin of four-dimensional Euclidean space, with metric a^2[dchi^2 + sin^2 chi dOmega^2]. It is compact with no boundary and finite volume 2 pi^2 a^3, and spheres drawn around any point first grow then shrink to zero area at chi = pi. | — |
| [[hyperbolic-3-space]] Hyperbolic 3-space | foundation | Hyperbolic 3-space is the space of constant negative curvature, with metric a^2[dchi^2 + sinh^2 chi dOmega^2] and infinite volume. It can be embedded as a hyperboloid in four-dimensional Minkowski space but not in Euclidean space. | — |
| [[robertson-walker-metric]] Robertson-Walker metric | core | The Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric ds^2 = -dt^2 + a(t)^2[dr^2/(1-kr^2) + r^2 dOmega^2] is the most general spacetime whose constant-time slices are homogeneous and isotropic. The scale factor a(t) carries all the dynamics and k = +1, 0, -1 fixes the spatial geometry; the matter content only enters later through the field equations. | — |
| [[curvature-index]] Curvature index k | core | The constant k in the FLRW metric gives the sign of spatial curvature: +1 for spherical (closed) slices, 0 for flat, -1 for hyperbolic (open). Rescaling r and a can set its magnitude to 1 but never change its sign, and the physical curvature of a slice is k/a^2. | — |
| [[flat-universe]] Spatially flat universe | core | In the k = 0 case each constant-time slice is ordinary Euclidean 3-space scaled by a(t), infinite in extent. Its spacetime is still curved whenever a(t) is not constant, since the Riemann tensor involves the expansion rate and acceleration. | — |
| [[closed-universe]] Closed universe | core | In the k = +1 case each spatial slice is a three-sphere of radius a(t): finite volume 2 pi^2 a^3, no edge and no centre, with circles whose circumference is less than 2 pi times their radius. Light can travel around the Universe, so a source may in principle appear in more than one direction. | — |
| [[open-universe]] Open hyperbolic universe | core | In the k = -1 case each spatial slice is hyperbolic 3-space of curvature -1/a^2: infinite volume, diverging parallel geodesics, triangle angles summing to less than pi, and circles with circumference exceeding 2 pi times their radius. | — |
| [[cosmic-topology]] Cosmic topology | advanced | Local curvature does not fix the global shape of space: the simplest choices are S^3 for k = +1 and R^3 for k = 0 or -1, but identifying points can make flat or hyperbolic slices finite with the same local geometry. | — |
| [[curvature-of-the-flrw-metric]] Curvature of the FLRW metric | core | The FLRW metric has connection coefficients such as Gamma^i_tj = (da/dt / a) delta^i_j, and in an orthonormal frame R_tt = -3 a"/a with Ricci scalar R = 6(a"/a + (da/dt)^2/a^2 + k/a^2). These components are the geometric input that turns the field equations into the Friedmann equations. | — |
| [[cosmological-proper-distance]] Proper distance in cosmology | core | The proper distance to a comoving object is the length measured along a slice of constant cosmic time, l(t) = a(t) chi. It grows with the scale factor but cannot be measured directly, because we only receive light from the past. | — |
| [[recession-velocity]] Recession velocity | core | The recession velocity is the rate of change of proper distance with cosmic time, v = H d. It exceeds the speed of light beyond the Hubble radius without contradicting relativity, because it is not a velocity measured by any local observer. | — |
| [[hubble-radius]] Hubble radius | core | The Hubble radius c/H is the proper distance at which the recession velocity equals the speed of light. It is an expansion scale, not in general a causal horizon, and should not be confused with the particle or event horizon. | — |
| [[radial-null-geodesics-in-flrw]] Radial light rays in FLRW spacetime | core | A radial light ray in FLRW obeys dt = a(t) dchi, so the comoving distance it covers is the integral of dt/a. Drawn in comoving coordinates and cosmic time, light cones narrow as the Universe expands, since light crosses less comoving distance per unit time. | — |
| [[comoving-distance]] Comoving distance | core | The comoving distance to a source is the fixed coordinate separation chi = integral of dt/a along the light ray from emission to reception (times a0 if a carries length). The source radial coordinate is r = sin chi, chi or sinh chi for k = +1, 0, -1, and multiplying by a(t) gives proper distance at time t. | — |
| [[conformal-time]] Conformal time | core | Conformal time tau is defined by dtau = dt/a(t), which turns the FLRW metric into a(tau)^2 times a static metric. Radial light rays then move at 45 degrees in (tau, chi), and the conformal time elapsed equals the comoving distance light has covered. | — |
| [[cosmological-redshift]] Cosmological redshift | core | Light travelling between comoving observers has its wavelength stretched in proportion to the scale factor, 1 + z = a(t_obs)/a(t_em). It is a blueshift in a contracting universe, reduces to an ordinary Doppler shift z = v for nearby sources, and is distinct from both kinematic Doppler and static gravitational redshift. | — |
| [[energy-nonconservation-of-redshifted-photons]] Energy conservation in an expanding universe | core | A time-dependent FLRW metric has no timelike Killing vector, so there is no conserved total energy along a photon path and the energy lost to cosmological redshift is not transferred anywhere. Local energy-momentum conservation still holds; the global law simply does not exist in such a spacetime. | — |
| [[redshift-drift]] Redshift drift | advanced | The redshift of a given comoving source changes slowly as the observer's time advances, according to dz/dt_obs = (1+z)H(t_obs) - H(t_em). Measuring it would track the expansion history directly rather than through distance measures. | — |
| [[lookback-time]] Lookback time | core | Because light travels at finite speed, a more distant source is seen as it was at an earlier cosmic time; the lookback time is the cosmic time elapsed between emission of light received with redshift z and its reception. For small z it is approximately [z - (1 + q0/2) z^2]/H0. | — |
| [[deceleration-parameter]] Deceleration parameter | core | The deceleration parameter q = -a a"/(da/dt)^2 is the dimensionless measure of whether cosmic expansion is slowing (q > 0) or speeding up (q < 0). Named when gravity was expected to decelerate expansion, it is negative for today's accelerating Universe, with q0 roughly -0.55. | — |
| [[cosmography]] Cosmography | core | Cosmography maps the expansion history, a(t) or H(z), through distance measures and redshifts without yet using Einstein's equations. It expands the scale factor in a Taylor series about today, so H0 and q0 appear as the first coefficients. | — |
| [[luminosity-distance]] Luminosity distance | core | The luminosity distance d_L = sqrt(L/4 pi F) is the distance at which a source of known luminosity would give the observed flux in static Euclidean space. In FLRW, d_L = a0 r (1+z), larger than the present transverse distance by a factor 1+z because each photon loses energy and photons arrive stretched out in time. | — |
| [[angular-diameter-distance]] Angular-diameter distance | core | The angular-diameter distance d_A = D/theta is the distance at which an object of known proper size D would subtend the observed angle in Euclidean space. In FLRW, d_A = a(t_em) r = d_L/(1+z)^2, and it can decrease with redshift beyond z of order 1, so very distant objects look larger. | — |
| [[second-order-hubble-law]] Second-order Hubble law | core | Expanding the scale factor to second order about today gives H0 d_L = z + (1 - q0) z^2/2 + ..., so departures from the linear Hubble law at moderate redshift measure the deceleration parameter. | — |
| [[luminosity-distance-redshift-relation]] Exact luminosity distance-redshift relation | advanced | For a given model the exact d_L(z) follows by writing the comoving distance as an integral over the Friedmann expansion rate, d_L = (1+z) a0 S_k(chi) with chi an integral of dz/H(z). Fitting it to distant supernovae constrains a combination of the matter and dark-energy density parameters. | — |
| [[particle-horizon]] Particle horizon | core | The particle horizon separates the comoving particles an observer could have seen since the Big Bang from those whose light has not yet arrived. Its present proper distance is a(t) times the integral of dt'/a from 0 to t (3ct for matter domination), it grows with time, and it exists only when the past boundary of spacetime is spacelike. | — |
| [[cosmological-event-horizon]] Cosmological event horizon | core | An observer's cosmological event horizon bounds the events from which signals can ever reach them: it is the past light cone of the observer's endpoint at future infinity. It exists when the comoving distance light can travel in the infinite future is finite, as in eternally accelerating universes like de Sitter, and sources crossing it redshift toward infinity. | — |
| [[penrose-diagram-of-flrw-spacetime]] Penrose diagram of FLRW spacetime | advanced | Using conformal time, each FLRW model maps onto part of the Einstein static cylinder and compactifies to a two-dimensional causal diagram. The Big Bang appears as a spacelike boundary: a triangle for k = 0 or -1 dust and a rectangle with spacelike bang and crunch for k = +1, which shows at a glance why particle horizons exist. | — |
