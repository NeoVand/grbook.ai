# Vectors, one-forms and tensor algebra

Multilinear algebra at a single point and differentiation in inertial (constant-basis) coordinates: index notation and the summation convention; four-vectors versus their components; bases, dual bases and transformation matrices; one-forms, their pairing with vectors and their stacked-surface picture; gradients; tensors as multilinear maps with type, components, tensor products, contraction and the transformation law; covariance of tensor equations; symmetry, bracket notation and the Levi-Civita symbol; the flat-spacetime metric as a map between vectors and one-forms with raising and lowering of indices; and gradients, divergences and the d'Alembertian of tensor fields in Minkowski coordinates. Position-dependent bases, manifolds, densities and exterior forms are left to later domains.

| Concept | Tier | Summary | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [[index-notation]] Index notation and index ranges | foundation | Spacetime components are labelled by indices: Greek letters run over 0 to 3 (time and space) and Latin letters over 1 to 3 (space only), and a prime or bar on an index marks components in a second frame. Superscripts are labels, not powers. | — |
| [[einstein-summation-convention]] Einstein summation convention | foundation | When an index letter appears once as a superscript and once as a subscript in the same term, a sum over all its values is understood and the sigma is dropped. Sums of this kind are exactly matrix multiplication written out in components. | — |
| [[free-and-dummy-indices]] Free and dummy indices | foundation | A free index appears once in every term and turns an equation into one equation per value; it must carry the same name in every term. A dummy index is summed over, so its letter is arbitrary and may be renamed to any unused letter of the same range. | — |
| [[kronecker-delta]] Kronecker delta | foundation | The Kronecker delta equals 1 when its two indices are equal and 0 otherwise; as a matrix it is the identity. With one index up and one down it is a genuine (1,1) tensor whose components are the same in every basis, and contracting with it simply renames an index. | — |
| [[four-vector]] Four-vector | foundation | A four-vector is an arrow in spacetime, such as the displacement between two events, whose four components change from one inertial frame to another by the same Lorentz transformation that acts on coordinate differences. Four-vectors add and scale component by component, forming a four-dimensional real vector space. | — |
| [[basis-vectors]] Basis vectors | foundation | A basis is a set of four reference vectors e_0 to e_3 such that every four-vector is a unique linear combination of them. In its own inertial frame each basis vector points along one coordinate axis, and its components are a column of the identity matrix; the lower index names which vector is meant, not a component. | — |
| [[vector-components]] Components of a vector | foundation | The components of a vector are the numbers A^mu in the expansion A = A^mu e_mu on a chosen basis. They depend on the whole basis, not only on the basis vector they multiply, so changing any member of the basis can change every component. | — |
| [[inner-product]] Inner product, norm and orthogonality | foundation | An inner product is a symmetric bilinear rule giving a number for each pair of vectors; in relativity it is indefinite, so the squared norm of a nonzero vector can be positive, negative or zero. Two vectors are orthogonal when their inner product vanishes, which means a null vector is orthogonal to itself. | — |
| [[orthonormal-basis]] Orthonormal basis | foundation | An orthonormal basis has mutually orthogonal vectors each of unit magnitude, so the metric components in it are those of the identity (Euclidean) or of eta (Lorentzian). Hats on indices mark components in such a basis, for example the unit polar vectors e_r-hat and e_theta-hat = e_theta / r. | — |
| [[geometric-object-versus-components]] Geometric object versus components | foundation | A vector or tensor is a single frame-independent object; its components are just the numbers it produces on a particular basis. Changing frame changes the components but never the object, because the new components and new basis vectors recombine into the same sum. | — |
| [[coordinate-independence]] Coordinate independence of physical laws | foundation | Coordinates are arbitrary labels with no physical meaning, so physical laws should be statements about geometric objects that hold in every coordinate system. Tensors are the tool for this: equations between them and fully contracted scalars do not depend on how coordinates are laid down. | — |
| [[coordinate-transformation-matrix]] Coordinate transformation matrix | foundation | The transformation matrix Lambda^{mu'}_nu = partial x^{mu'} / partial x^nu collects the first derivatives of new coordinates with respect to old ones at a point; its inverse is partial x^mu / partial x^{nu'}, and their product is the Kronecker delta. For inertial frames it is the constant Lorentz matrix. | — |
| [[active-versus-passive-transformation]] Active versus passive transformation | foundation | A passive transformation relabels the same points and objects with new coordinates, so components change while nothing physical moves. An active transformation keeps the coordinates and moves the points, dragging tensors along, and its formulas can look identical to a passive change while meaning something different. | — |
| [[vector-transformation-law]] Transformation law for vector components | foundation | Under a change of frame or coordinates, vector components transform with the forward matrix, A^{mu'} = (partial x^{mu'} / partial x^nu) A^nu. Any set of four quantities that obeys this rule is the component set of a vector. | — |
| [[basis-vector-transformation-law]] Transformation law for basis vectors | foundation | Basis vectors transform with the inverse of the matrix used for components, e_{mu'} = Lambda^nu_{mu'} e_nu. This inverse pattern is forced by requiring the vector A^mu e_mu itself to be unchanged by the frame change. | — |
| [[scalar-invariant]] Scalar invariant | foundation | A scalar invariant is a single number whose value is the same in every frame or coordinate system, making it a tensor of type (0,0); a scalar field assigns such a number to each event. Fully contracting tensors always produces scalars, so a scalar can be computed in whichever frame is easiest. | — |
| [[one-form]] One-form | foundation | A one-form is a linear map that takes a vector and returns a real number. One-forms at a point add and scale like vectors, so they form a vector space of the same dimension, the dual space; their components carry lower indices. | — |
| [[one-form-vector-contraction]] Contraction of a one-form with a vector | foundation | Feeding a vector to a one-form gives the number p(V) = p_mu V^mu, a sum with all plus signs that needs no metric. It is the same in every frame because the forward and inverse transformation matrices cancel, and it lets a vector be read equally as a linear function of one-forms, so the two spaces are dual to each other. | — |
| [[one-form-as-stack-of-surfaces]] One-form as a stack of surfaces | foundation | A one-form at a point can be pictured as a family of evenly spaced parallel surfaces; its value on a vector is the number of surfaces the arrow crosses. Closer spacing means a larger one-form, and the picture needs no metric. | — |
| [[dual-basis]] Dual basis | foundation | Given basis vectors e_nu, the dual basis consists of the one-forms omega^mu defined by omega^mu(e_nu) = delta^mu_nu. In this basis any one-form expands as p = p_mu omega^mu with p_mu = p(e_mu), which is what makes p(A) = p_mu A^mu hold. | — |
| [[one-form-transformation-law]] Transformation law for one-form components | foundation | One-form components transform with the inverse matrix, p_{mu'} = (partial x^nu / partial x^{mu'}) p_nu, the same way basis vectors transform. This opposite pattern to vector components is exactly what keeps the contraction p_mu A^mu invariant. | — |
| [[contravariant-and-covariant-components]] Contravariant and covariant components | foundation | Traditionally, quantities that transform like coordinate differentials are called contravariant and carry upper indices, while those that transform like gradients (and like basis vectors) are covariant and carry lower indices. Geometrically, in a skewed basis the upper components come from parallel decomposition and the lower ones from dot products with the basis vectors, and they coincide only in an orthonormal Euclidean basis. | — |
| [[gradient-one-form]] Gradient one-form | foundation | The gradient of a scalar field phi is the one-form d(phi) whose value on a vector V is the rate of change of phi along V; its components in any coordinates are the partial derivatives partial phi / partial x^mu. The chain rule makes these components transform like a one-form, so the gradient is the archetypal one-form. | — |
| [[comma-notation]] Comma notation for partial derivatives | foundation | A comma before an index denotes a partial derivative with respect to that coordinate, phi_{,alpha} = partial phi / partial x^alpha. The index sits low because the result transforms as a one-form component. | — |
| [[normal-one-form]] Normal one-form | foundation | A normal one-form to a surface is one that vanishes on every vector tangent to the surface; the gradient of f is normal to the surfaces f = constant. Unlike a normal vector it needs no metric, and it is fixed only up to an overall factor. | — |
| [[multilinearity]] Multilinearity | foundation | A function of several arguments is multilinear if it is linear in each argument when the others are held fixed: scaling one input scales the output, and splitting one input into a sum splits the output. This property is what lets every tensor be evaluated by expanding its inputs on a basis. | — |
| [[tensor]] Tensor | foundation | A tensor of type (M, N) at a point is a multilinear machine that takes M one-forms and N vectors and returns a real number. Its value does not depend on any frame; only its components do. | — |
| [[tensor-type]] Type (valence) of a tensor | foundation | The type or valence (M, N) counts a tensor's one-form slots (upper indices) and vector slots (lower indices); a mixed tensor has both. Vectors are (1,0), one-forms (0,1), the metric (0,2), and a number results only when the slots of a tensor are all filled. | — |
| [[tensor-product]] Tensor product | foundation | The tensor (outer) product of two tensors is the tensor whose slots are the first factor's slots followed by the second's, with value equal to the product of the factors' values: (p x q)(A, B) = p(A) q(B). It adds the types and is not commutative, because slot order matters. | — |
| [[tensor-components]] Components of a tensor | foundation | The components of a tensor are its values when basis one-forms are put into its upper slots and basis vectors into its lower slots. Multiplying the components by tensor products of the matching basis elements and summing rebuilds the tensor, so the 16 products omega^alpha x omega^beta form a basis for (0,2) tensors. | — |
| [[tensor-transformation-law]] Tensor transformation law | foundation | Tensor components transform with one forward matrix partial x'/partial x for each upper index and one inverse matrix partial x/partial x' for each lower index, with no extra terms. Coordinate-based treatments take this rule as the definition of a tensor. | — |
| [[covariance-of-tensor-equations]] Covariance of tensor equations | foundation | If two tensors of the same type are equal in one coordinate system at a point, they are equal in every coordinate system, since their difference has all components zero and stays zero under the homogeneous transformation law. So an equation with matching tensor types and free indices on both sides can be proved in the most convenient frame and trusted everywhere. | — |
| [[tensor-contraction]] Tensor contraction | foundation | Contraction sets one upper index equal to one lower index and sums, turning a type (M, N) tensor into a type (M-1, N-1) tensor; the result is again a tensor because the paired transformation matrices cancel. The trace of a (1,1) tensor, S^mu_mu, is the simplest example and is a scalar. | — |
| [[permissible-tensor-operations]] Permissible tensor operations | foundation | Some operations on tensor components always produce another tensor: adding tensors of the same type at the same point, multiplying by a scalar, taking outer products, contracting an upper with a lower index, and (in curved spacetime) covariant differentiation. Building expressions only from these makes type checking routine. | — |
| [[quotient-theorem]] Quotient theorem | foundation | If an array of numbers, contracted with an arbitrary tensor, always gives a tensor, then the array is itself the component set of a tensor. It is a quick way to establish tensor character without checking the transformation law directly. | — |
| [[symmetric-and-antisymmetric-tensors]] Symmetric and antisymmetric tensors | foundation | A two-slot tensor is symmetric if swapping its inputs leaves the output unchanged, T_ab = T_ba, and antisymmetric if the swap flips the sign; either property survives any change of coordinates. Every (0,2) tensor splits uniquely into a symmetric and an antisymmetric part, which in n dimensions have n(n+1)/2 and n(n-1)/2 independent components (10 and 6 in spacetime). | — |
| [[symmetrization-brackets]] Symmetrization and antisymmetrization brackets | foundation | Round brackets around indices mean the average over all orderings of those indices, T_(ab) = (T_ab + T_ba)/2, and square brackets mean the signed average, T_[ab] = (T_ab - T_ba)/2, each with weight 1/r! for r indices. Vertical bars inside brackets exclude indices from the operation. | — |
| [[levi-civita-symbol]] Levi-Civita symbol | foundation | The Levi-Civita symbol epsilon_{abcd} is totally antisymmetric: it equals +1 for an even permutation of 0123, -1 for an odd permutation, and 0 if any index repeats. It writes determinants as signed sums over permutations and gives the components of the volume form in an orthonormal basis. | — |
| [[generalized-kronecker-delta]] Generalized Kronecker delta | foundation | The generalized Kronecker delta with k upper and k lower indices is +1 if the upper indices are distinct and an even permutation of the lower ones, -1 for an odd permutation, and 0 otherwise. It equals the determinant of the k-by-k array of ordinary deltas, the antisymmetrized product of deltas. | — |
| [[epsilon-delta-identity]] Epsilon-delta contraction identity | foundation | The product of an upper and a lower Levi-Civita symbol equals the generalized Kronecker delta with the same indices, and contracting successive index pairs gives lower-order deltas with factorial factors (in four dimensions: 1, 2, 3! and 4!). With a Lorentzian metric, an extra factor (-1)^s appears when both symbols come from the metric volume tensor. | — |
| [[minkowski-metric-tensor]] Metric tensor of flat spacetime | foundation | The scalar product is itself a symmetric (0,2) tensor g: it takes two vectors and returns A.B = g_{mu nu} A^mu B^nu. Its components in any basis are the dot products of the basis vectors, g_{mu nu} = e_mu . e_nu, which in every inertial frame are eta_{mu nu} = diag(-1, 1, 1, 1). | — |
| [[musical-isomorphism]] Metric as a map from vectors to one-forms | foundation | Filling one slot of the metric with a vector V leaves a one-form g(V, _) whose value on any A is V.A; because the metric is nondegenerate, this correspondence between vectors and one-forms is one-to-one and invertible. It is the metric's fundamental role: without it, vectors and one-forms are separate objects with no scalar product. | — |
| [[raising-and-lowering-indices]] Raising and lowering indices | foundation | Contracting a tensor index with the metric g_{mu nu} lowers it, V_mu = g_{mu nu} V^nu, and contracting with the inverse metric g^{mu nu} raises it; the results are treated as components of the same geometric object and keep its letter. In an inertial frame, moving a time index flips the component's sign and moving a spatial index leaves it unchanged. | — |
| [[inner-product-of-one-forms]] Inner product and magnitude of one-forms | foundation | The squared magnitude of a one-form is defined as that of its associated vector and is computed directly with the inverse metric, eta^{mu nu} p_mu p_nu; the inner product of two one-forms follows the same way. A one-form is called timelike, spacelike or null according to whether this magnitude is negative, positive or zero. | — |
| [[vector-gradient]] Vector gradient | foundation | Raising the index of the gradient one-form gives the gradient vector, with inertial-frame components (-phi_{,t}, phi_{,x}, phi_{,y}, phi_{,z}); it is orthogonal to every vector tangent to a surface of constant phi. The elementary "grad" of vector calculus is this vector, which is why it secretly depends on the metric. | — |
| [[momentum-one-form]] Momentum one-form | foundation | The momentum of a particle is naturally a one-form p_mu, since a Lagrangian gives lower-index components p_mu = partial L / partial (dx^mu / d tau). Fed an observer's four-velocity it returns minus the energy that observer measures, E = -p(u). | — |
| [[wave-vector-one-form]] Wave-vector one-form | foundation | For a wave, the one-form k whose surfaces are the surfaces of constant phase has components k_mu = (-omega, k); contracting it with a displacement vector counts the phase difference between the two events. It is the most physical instance of the stacked-surfaces picture. | — |
| [[constant-basis-in-lorentz-frame]] Constant basis in an inertial frame | foundation | In an inertial (Lorentz) frame the basis vectors and basis one-forms are the same at every event, so differentiating a tensor field only differentiates its components. This fails in curvilinear coordinates and in curved spacetime, and dropping it is the entry point to the covariant derivative. | — |
| [[gradient-of-a-tensor-field]] Gradient of a tensor field | foundation | The gradient of a type (M, N) tensor field is a type (M, N+1) tensor nabla T whose extra vector slot, filled with U, gives the rate of change of T along U. In inertial coordinates its components are just partial derivatives of the components, such as T^alpha_{beta,gamma}. | — |
| [[four-divergence]] Four-divergence | foundation | The four-divergence of a vector field is the contraction of its gradient, V^a_{,a} = partial_t V^0 + partial_x V^x + partial_y V^y + partial_z V^z, a frame-independent scalar in special relativity. Local conservation laws take the form "four-divergence equals zero". | — |
| [[dalembertian-operator]] d'Alembertian operator | foundation | The d'Alembertian is the Lorentz-invariant second-order operator box = eta^{mu nu} partial_mu partial_nu, equal to -partial_t^2 + nabla^2 in signature (-+++) (with the opposite overall sign in (+---)). Setting box f = 0 gives the wave equation, and for static fields it reduces to the Laplacian. | — |
| [[index-free-notation]] Index-free notation | advanced | Index-free notation writes tensors as whole objects with empty slots, such as S( , , , ) or nabla_u v, rather than as arrays of components. Coordinate independence is then built in and the geometric meaning is visible, though long contractions are clumsier. | — |
| [[abstract-index-notation]] Abstract index notation | advanced | In abstract index notation, index letters record the type and slot structure of a tensor (for example T^{ab}_c) without referring to any basis, so index manipulations are automatically coordinate-free; a separate alphabet is reserved for actual components. | — |
