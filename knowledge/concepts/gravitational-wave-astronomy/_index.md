# Gravitational-wave detection and astronomy

Gravitational waves as an observational science, taking the wave theory (linearized gravity, polarizations, strain on test masses, energy flux, quadrupole formula, chirp mass, quasinormal-mode theory) as given from gravitational-waves. Covers detector response (light travel time and the three-term beam-detector relation, interferometers with Fabry-Perot cavities, resonant bars, detector tensor, antenna patterns, response beyond the long-wavelength limit), noise (spectral density, seismic, thermal, shot and quantum, Newtonian noise, glitches, calibration), space and pulsar-timing detectors, signal analysis (signal-to-noise ratio, matched filtering and its square-root gain, template banks, false alarms, trials factor, coincidence, time slides, coherent networks, null streams, localization, burst and cross-correlation searches), source classes and their scaling laws (compact binary coalescence, orbital decay, time to merger, redshifted masses, inclination, harmonics, tides, ringdown, recoil, formation channels and mass gaps, continuous waves and their searches, supernova bursts, stochastic backgrounds), binary-pulsar orbital decay, landmark detections and catalogs, electromagnetic counterparts and multimessenger astronomy, standard sirens and the Hubble constant, and tests of general relativity with waves.

| Concept | Tier | Summary | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [[gravitational-wave-astronomy]] Gravitational-wave astronomy | core | Gravitational-wave astronomy studies astrophysical and cosmological systems through the gravitational radiation they emit, measured as tiny tidal strains in ground and space interferometers and in pulsar timing. Since the 2015 detection it has been an observational science, not only a prediction of general relativity. | — |
| [[complementarity-of-gravitational-and-electromagnetic-astronomy]] Complementarity of gravitational and electromagnetic astronomy | core | Gravitational waves come from coherent bulk motion of mass-energy at low frequencies, pass through matter almost unabsorbed and cannot be focused, while light comes from microscopic charges, is easily absorbed and can be imaged. Each channel therefore reveals what the other hides; both travel at c, are transverse, and are gravitationally lensed. | — |
| [[gravitational-wave-sound-analogy]] Gravitational waves as sound | core | Detecting gravitational waves is more like listening than looking: detectors follow the phase of a low-frequency wave, have broad antenna patterns instead of a field of view, locate sources by arrival-time differences across an array, and pick up radiation generated coherently by bulk motion, which is why the field speaks of chirps and sirens. | — |
| [[gravitational-wave-spectrum]] Gravitational-wave spectrum and detector bands | core | Different sources radiate in different frequency bands, each matched to a detection method: ground interferometers cover about 10 Hz to a few kHz (stellar-mass compact binaries), space interferometers the millihertz band (massive black-hole binaries), pulsar timing arrays nanohertz (supermassive binaries), and CMB polarization the ultra-low frequencies of primordial waves. Ground detectors are blocked below about 1 Hz by the Earth's fluctuating Newtonian gravity. | — |
| [[gravitational-wave-light-travel-time]] Round-trip light travel time in a gravitational wave | core | Timing a light signal sent between free masses and back measures their separation; in TT coordinates the wave changes the light's coordinate speed, so to first order the round-trip time is the flat value plus half the wave amplitude integrated along the unperturbed path. For arms short compared with the wavelength this recovers the proper-distance strain. | — |
| [[beam-detector-return-time-response]] Return-time response of a beam detector | core | The rate of change of the round-trip time of light in one arm depends only on the wave amplitude at emission and at reception (plus a third term at reflection for oblique incidence); the same ratio is the Doppler frequency shift of the returned light. This three-term relation is the basic response formula of every timing detector. | — |
| [[beam-detector]] Beam detector | advanced | A beam detector senses gravitational waves through their effect on the travel times of electromagnetic signals exchanged between free masses: spacecraft Doppler tracking, pulsar timing and laser interferometers are all examples. A single beam is limited by clock stability, which is why interferometers compare two arms instead. | — |
| [[resonant-bar-detector]] Resonant bar detector | advanced | A resonant bar is a massive elastic body whose fundamental vibration is driven by the tidal force of a passing wave, modelled as two masses on a damped spring; the response is largest on resonance and grows with the quality factor. Weber's bars and their cryogenic successors never reached the needed sensitivity and bandwidth and have been retired. | — |
| [[laser-interferometer-gravitational-wave-detector]] Laser interferometer gravitational-wave detector | core | A Michelson interferometer with suspended, effectively free end mirrors: a passing wave lengthens one arm while shortening the other by a fraction of order h, and the difference in round-trip light time appears as a change in the light at the output port. It is broadband with an antenna pattern covering most of the sky; LIGO, Virgo and KAGRA are kilometre-scale examples. | — |
| [[fabry-perot-arm-cavity]] Fabry-Perot arm cavity | advanced | Partially transmitting mirrors near the beamsplitter make light bounce many times along each arm, multiplying the effective path (to hundreds of kilometres in LIGO) and the phase response, as long as the storage time stays below about half a wave period. A power-recycling mirror returns the otherwise wasted light to build up circulating power. | — |
| [[detector-tensor]] Detector tensor | advanced | For an L-shaped interferometer with unit arm vectors e_x and e_y, the detector tensor d = L(e_x e_x - e_y e_y) contracts with the wave's TT strain tensor to give the difference of arm-length changes, making the response a single coordinate-free contraction. | — |
| [[antenna-pattern-function]] Antenna pattern functions | advanced | The antenna pattern functions F_+ and F_x are the geometric weights in the detector output F_+ h_+ + F_x h_x; they depend on source direction and polarization angle relative to the detector arms. The polarization-averaged power pattern F_+^2 + F_x^2 is largest overhead and vanishes for waves arriving in the detector plane along an arm bisector. | — |
| [[detector-transfer-function]] Detector response beyond the long-wavelength limit | advanced | When a detector's arms are comparable to or longer than the gravitational wavelength, the wave changes while light is in transit, so the response depends on the wave at emission, reflection and return and on the incidence angle, not on one instantaneous strain. This regime governs LISA at high frequencies and pulsar timing arrays. | — |
| [[amplitude-spectral-density]] Amplitude spectral density of detector noise | advanced | The square root of the strain noise power spectral density, in units of 1/sqrt(Hz), gives the r.m.s. noise per unit bandwidth as a function of frequency. Plotted as a sensitivity curve, it shows which sources a detector can see and which noise dominates in each band. | — |
| [[seismic-noise]] Seismic noise and seismic isolation | advanced | Ground vibrations reaching the test masses through their supports create the steep low-frequency wall of ground-based sensitivity curves. Detectors fight it by hanging mirrors from chains of pendulums that transmit little motion above their resonance, plus active cancellation and, for KAGRA, underground siting. | — |
| [[thermal-noise]] Thermal noise in detectors | advanced | Every mechanical mode of a detector carries random thermal energy of order k_B T, which competes with the energy a wave deposits. It limits the mid band of interferometers through Brownian motion of mirrors, coatings and suspensions, and is fought with cryogenics and very high-Q materials that confine the noise to narrow resonances. | — |
| [[shot-noise]] Shot noise | advanced | Shot noise is the statistical fluctuation in detected light power caused by the random arrival of discrete photons; its fractional size falls as one over the square root of photon number, so it dominates interferometer noise at high frequencies and is reduced by raising laser power. | — |
| [[standard-quantum-limit]] Standard quantum limit and squeezed light | advanced | Raising laser power lowers shot noise but increases radiation-pressure noise on the mirrors, and balancing the two sets a standard quantum limit on position measurement. Squeezed light and quantum nondemolition schemes redistribute quantum uncertainty to beat it, and current LIGO and Virgo detectors inject squeezed light. | — |
| [[newtonian-noise]] Newtonian noise | advanced | Changing local mass distributions (seismic waves, air-pressure changes, moving vehicles) produce fluctuating tidal forces on the test masses that couple exactly as a gravitational wave does, so they cannot be shielded, only reduced by going underground or into space; this is why ground detectors cannot work below about 1 Hz. | — |
| [[detector-glitch]] Detector glitches | advanced | Glitches are short, loud, non-Gaussian noise transients from instrumental and environmental faults; they come and go, can mimic astrophysical bursts, and dominate the background of short-duration searches, which is why detections require multiple detectors. | — |
| [[detector-calibration]] Detector calibration | advanced | Calibration establishes, at every frequency, the conversion in amplitude and phase between a detector's raw output and true strain; errors bias amplitudes and hence distances, and inconsistent calibrations between sites spoil coherent analyses such as null streams. | — |
| [[laser-interferometer-space-antenna]] Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA) | advanced | LISA is a planned ESA-led mission of three drag-free spacecraft in a triangle with arms of about 2.5 million km trailing the Earth around the Sun, forming effectively three interferometers in the millihertz band. Its targets include massive black-hole mergers, extreme mass-ratio inspirals and Galactic white-dwarf binaries, located on the sky through the modulation produced by its yearly orbit. | — |
| [[drag-free-control]] Drag-free control | advanced | A drag-free spacecraft shields a free-floating test mass from solar radiation pressure and solar wind and uses tiny thrusters to follow it without contact, keeping the mass in nearly perfect free fall; LISA Pathfinder demonstrated this beyond LISA's requirements. | — |
| [[time-delay-interferometry]] Time-delay interferometry | advanced | When arms are long, unequal and changing, light cannot simply be recombined at a beamsplitter; time-delay interferometry instead records one-way laser phase measurements and combines them in software at suitably delayed times to cancel laser frequency noise and synthesize interferometer outputs. | — |
| [[pulsar-timing-array]] Pulsar timing array | advanced | A pulsar timing array monitors the pulse arrival times of many millisecond pulsars for years; a nanohertz gravitational wave shifts arrival times through a pulsar term and an Earth term, and the Earth term is correlated between pulsars in a characteristic way. Arrays such as NANOGrav, EPTA and PPTA target the background from supermassive black-hole binaries. | — |
| [[signal-to-noise-ratio]] Signal-to-noise ratio | advanced | The signal-to-noise ratio of a detection statistic is its expected value when a signal is present divided by its standard deviation in noise alone; thresholds on it set detection confidence, and for a network the matched-filter SNRs of the detectors add in quadrature. | — |
| [[matched-filtering]] Matched filtering | advanced | Matched filtering correlates detector data with a predicted waveform at every time offset; for a known signal in Gaussian noise this is the linear filter that maximizes signal-to-noise ratio, so it can dig out signals far below the raw noise level and the best-matching template estimates the source parameters. | — |
| [[coherent-integration-gain]] Square-root-of-cycles gain of matched filtering | advanced | Coherently integrating a known oscillating signal lowers the smallest detectable amplitude, relative to the noise, by roughly the square root of the number of signal cycles observed; for long continuous signals sensitivity therefore improves as the square root of observation time. | — |
| [[characteristic-strain]] Characteristic strain | advanced | Characteristic strain h_c folds the accumulation of signal over the observation into a single amplitude, roughly the instantaneous strain times the square root of the number of cycles observed, so that long-lived and short sources can be compared fairly with detector noise curves. | — |
| [[template-bank]] Template bank | advanced | A template bank is a discrete set of model waveforms covering the source parameter space (masses, spins) densely enough that every real signal closely matches at least one template; templates join post-Newtonian inspirals to numerical-relativity mergers. | — |
| [[intrinsic-and-extrinsic-parameters]] Intrinsic and extrinsic parameters | advanced | Intrinsic parameters such as component masses and spins shape the waveform and can be estimated by a single detector; extrinsic parameters such as sky position, distance, inclination, polarization angle and arrival time describe the source's relation to the observer and need a detector network. Chirp mass is the best-measured intrinsic parameter. | — |
| [[false-alarm-probability]] False alarm probability | advanced | The false alarm probability is the chance that noise alone crosses a detection threshold; for Gaussian noise and a threshold of a standard deviations it is the two-sided tail area of the normal distribution, which falls roughly like exp(-a^2/2)/a. | — |
| [[trials-factor]] Trials factor | advanced | When a statistic is tested many independent times, across samples, templates or sky positions, the chance that some noise value exceeds a threshold grows with the number of trials, so detection thresholds must be raised; a 5-sigma excursion is routine in a year of filtered data. | — |
| [[coincidence-analysis]] Coincidence analysis | advanced | A candidate is accepted only if triggers appear in two or more detectors within a window set by the light-travel time between sites plus a margin, which suppresses glitches that occur independently at each site; this is why LIGO was built as two separated detectors. | — |
| [[time-slides]] Time slides | advanced | The accidental-coincidence background is estimated by shifting one detector's data relative to another by more than any physical delay and repeating the search many times; every coincidence found this way is noise, giving the combined glitch and Gaussian background rate. | — |
| [[false-alarm-rate]] False alarm rate | advanced | The false alarm rate of an event is the expected rate, usually per year, at which noise including glitches would produce a candidate at least as significant; catalogs include events below a chosen threshold, trading purity against completeness. | — |
| [[coherent-network-analysis]] Coherent network analysis | advanced | Coherent analysis combines all detector data streams, time-shifted and weighted by antenna factors for a hypothesized sky direction, before thresholding, so it exploits the requirement that one two-polarization wave explains every detector's amplitude and phase, information that simple coincidence throws away. | — |
| [[polarization-reconstruction]] Polarization reconstruction with two detectors | advanced | If the source direction and a common arrival time are known, the outputs of two non-aligned detectors form a 2 by 2 linear system for h_+ and h_x, which can be inverted using the determinant of their antenna-factor matrix. | — |
| [[null-stream]] Null stream | advanced | With three or more detectors, a linear combination of time-aligned outputs weighted by antenna-factor determinants cancels any gravitational wave from an assumed direction, leaving only instrumental noise; a network of N detectors has N - 2 null streams, used to veto glitches, localize sources and check calibration. | — |
| [[sky-localization]] Sky localization by a detector network | core | Arrival-time differences between widely separated detectors place a source on a ring on the sky for two sites and shrink it to small patches with three or more, with relative amplitudes helping further; non-aligned detectors also measure both polarizations and hence the binary's inclination. | — |
| [[detection-range]] Detection range and volume | advanced | Because strain falls as one over distance, the farthest detectable source lies at a distance inversely proportional to the smallest detectable amplitude, and the surveyed volume, and so the event rate for uniformly spread sources, grows as the cube of that distance. | — |
| [[burst-search]] Burst search | advanced | Burst searches look for short transients with little or no waveform model, requiring excess power or a match to generic wavelets such as sine-Gaussians and relying on coincidence, coherence or external triggers for confidence; they target sources like core collapse whose waveforms cannot be predicted. | — |
| [[gravitational-wave-source-classification]] Burst, continuous and stochastic sources | core | Gravitational-wave sources are classified by time structure: short bursts (mergers, collapses, captures), long-lived periodic or continuous signals (spinning neutron stars, binaries far from merger), and stochastic backgrounds from unresolved populations or the early Universe; each class needs a different search strategy. | — |
| [[stochastic-gravitational-wave-background]] Stochastic gravitational-wave background | advanced | A stochastic background is a random superposition of waves from very many unresolved sources arriving from all directions, described statistically by its spectrum rather than by a waveform; astrophysical backgrounds come from populations of binaries, and a cosmological one would be a relic of the very early Universe. | — |
| [[cross-correlation-search]] Cross-correlation search | advanced | A stochastic background is sought by multiplying the outputs of two detectors whose instrumental noises are independent but which see the same wave field and summing over time: noise products average away while the common signal accumulates, with sensitivity improving only as the fourth root of observing time. | — |
| [[gravitational-wave-amplitude-estimate]] Order-of-magnitude gravitational-wave amplitude | core | A source of mass M with internal speeds v produces strain of order M v^2 / r at distance r (G = c = 1); for self-gravitating motion v^2 is about the internal potential, so h is roughly the product of the source's own potential and M/r, with M/r as an absolute ceiling. Realistic amplitudes at Earth are 1e-21 or smaller. | — |
| [[maximum-gravitational-luminosity]] Maximum luminosity c^5/G | advanced | No source can radiate much more than c^5/G, about 3.6e52 W: radiation of luminosity L crossing a region carries enough energy that its own gravity would trap it in a black hole if L exceeded order unity in geometrized units. Merging black holes reach about a percent of this bound, vastly outshining quasars. | — |
| [[compact-binary-coalescence]] Compact binary coalescence | core | Two neutron stars or black holes lose orbital energy to gravitational waves, spiral together with rising frequency and amplitude (the chirp), merge, and the remnant settles down; this inspiral-merger-ringdown sequence is the loudest source for ground detectors and its waveform encodes the component masses and spins. | — |
| [[orbital-decay-by-gravitational-radiation]] Orbital decay by gravitational radiation | core | A bound binary that radiates energy becomes more tightly bound, so its separation and period shrink while its orbital speed rises; for a circular orbit dP/dt = 3PL/(2E), a dimensionless rate that binary pulsars have measured in agreement with general relativity. | — |
| [[circularization-by-gravitational-radiation]] Circularization by gravitational radiation | advanced | Gravitational-wave emission removes eccentricity faster than it shrinks the orbit (de/dt scales as e/a^4 while da/dt scales as 1/a^3), so binaries reaching ground-detector bands are almost circular; the Hulse-Taylor system will have e below 1e-5 when it gets there. | — |
| [[scale-free-nature-of-gravity]] Scale-free nature of gravity | core | Vacuum general relativity in geometrized units has no built-in mass, length or time scale, so multiplying all masses of a binary by a factor stretches its waveform in time by that factor and scales its distance-normalized amplitude likewise; every dimensionless property of a circular inspiral depends only on chirp mass times orbital frequency. | — |
| [[time-to-merger]] Time to merger and chirp time | core | At leading order a circular binary emitting at a given frequency merges after a time proportional to chirp mass to the -5/3 power and frequency to the -8/3 power (about 3 s for a one-solar-mass chirp mass at 100 Hz); the chirp time omega/omega-dot is 8/3 of this, and the orbital phase left before merger scales as (chirp mass times omega) to the -5/3. | — |
| [[merger-frequency]] Merger frequency estimate | advanced | Evaluating the leading-order phase-to-merger relation when about half an orbit remains gives the highest inspiral frequency, roughly 4 kHz times one solar mass over the chirp mass; heavier binaries therefore merge at lower frequency, which sets the upper mass limit of ground detectors. | — |
| [[redshifted-mass]] Redshifted (detector-frame) mass | core | Because a detector measures only times and cosmological redshift stretches all of them by (1 + z), a binary at redshift z looks exactly like one with (1 + z) times larger masses; gravitational waves alone cannot separate mass from redshift, and catalogs quote source-frame masses after inferring z from the distance. | — |
| [[orbital-inclination]] Orbital inclination of a radiating binary | core | The inclination iota is the angle between a binary's orbital axis and the line of sight; the plus amplitude scales as (1 + cos^2 iota) and the cross amplitude as 2 cos iota, so face-on binaries emit circular polarization and edge-on ones linear polarization. Measuring the ratio of the two polarizations gives iota, which must be known to turn amplitude into distance. | — |
| [[higher-harmonics-in-binary-waveforms]] Higher harmonics in binary waveforms | advanced | Beyond the dominant quadrupole signal at twice the orbital frequency, unequal masses and post-Newtonian corrections produce harmonics with other frequencies and angular patterns; detecting them sharpens estimates of inclination, polarization and distance, as in GW190521 and GW190814. | — |
| [[standard-siren]] Standard siren | core | A compact binary is a standard siren: its frequency evolution fixes its chirp mass and hence its intrinsic luminosity, so the observed amplitude gives the luminosity distance directly once the inclination is known, with no cosmic distance ladder. A redshift must come separately, from a host galaxy or statistically. | — |
| [[distance-inclination-degeneracy]] Distance-inclination degeneracy | advanced | For nearly face-on binaries the amplitudes change only at second order in inclination, so a signal is fit almost equally well by a nearer inclined source or a farther face-on one, inflating distance errors; because face-on systems are loudest they dominate detected samples. | — |
| [[tidal-deformability]] Tidal deformability | advanced | Tidal deformability is the coefficient relating the quadrupole moment a star develops to the external tidal field acting on it; it depends on the nuclear equation of state and speeds up the final orbits of a neutron-star inspiral, leaving a measurable phase shift. | — |
| [[merger-remnant]] Merger remnant outcomes | advanced | The product of a merger depends on the pair: two neutron stars form a hypermassive star that probably collapses to a black hole unless the equation of state can support it; a neutron star and black hole may leave a short-lived disk; two black holes form a distorted black hole that rings down to Kerr, probably without light. | — |
| [[ringdown]] Ringdown | core | Ringdown is the exponentially damped oscillation with which a newly formed, distorted black hole sheds its deformations and settles to a Kerr black hole; its quasinormal frequencies and damping times depend only on the final mass and spin, so the signal fingerprints the remnant. | — |
| [[gravitational-recoil]] Gravitational recoil | advanced | Asymmetric emission of gravitational-wave momentum in the final orbits and merger kicks the remnant black hole, typically at hundreds of km/s and sometimes enough to eject it from its host cluster or galaxy; the kick depends only on mass ratio and spins, not on total mass. | — |
| [[extreme-mass-ratio-inspiral]] Extreme mass-ratio inspiral | frontier | An extreme mass-ratio inspiral is the slow spiral of a stellar-mass compact object into a supermassive black hole, lasting 1e5 to 1e6 orbits in the LISA band and mapping the strong-field spacetime of the large hole in exquisite detail, at the cost of very demanding waveform modelling. | — |
| [[multiband-gravitational-wave-astronomy]] Multiband gravitational-wave astronomy | frontier | Observing one binary in different detector bands at different stages, for example a stellar-mass black-hole binary seen by LISA months before it merges in the ground band, lets its merger time and sky position be predicted in advance. | — |
| [[confusion-noise]] Confusion noise | advanced | When very many overlapping signals share a frequency band they cannot be resolved individually and act as an extra noise: for LISA the Galactic white-dwarf binaries below about 1 mHz exceed instrumental noise, and for pulsar timing unresolved supermassive binaries form a background. | — |
| [[compact-binary-formation-channels]] Compact binary formation channels | advanced | Merging compact binaries may form through isolated evolution of massive binary stars (surviving common-envelope inspiral and supernova kicks), dynamical capture in dense clusters, evolution in triples, or hierarchical mergers of earlier merger products; spin orientations help distinguish them, aligned for isolated binaries and random for captures. | — |
| [[compact-object-mass-gaps]] Compact-object mass gaps | advanced | Stellar remnants seem sparse in two mass ranges: a lower gap between the heaviest neutron stars (below about 2.5-3 solar masses) and the lightest X-ray-binary black holes (about 5 solar masses), and an upper gap near 60-150 solar masses where pair instability disrupts stars entirely. Gravitational-wave events such as GW190814 and GW190521 appear to populate both. | — |
| [[continuous-gravitational-waves]] Continuous gravitational waves from spinning neutron stars | advanced | A rotating neutron star that is not symmetric about its spin axis emits nearly monochromatic, long-lived gravitational waves, at twice the spin frequency for a rigid deformation; possible asymmetries include crustal mountains, magnetic or accretion-driven deformations, and unstable oscillation modes. | — |
| [[neutron-star-ellipticity]] Neutron star ellipticity | advanced | Ellipticity measures a neutron star's departure from axisymmetry about its spin axis, roughly the fractional difference of its equatorial moments of inertia; continuous-wave strain is proportional to it, and upper limits near 1e-7 for some pulsars show neutron stars are remarkably round. | — |
| [[r-mode-instability]] r-mode instability | frontier | In a rapidly spinning neutron star, rotational (Rossby-type) oscillation modes can be driven unstable by their own gravitational-wave emission, a Chandrasekhar-Friedman-Schutz type instability that could make young or accreting neutron stars strong continuous-wave sources. | — |
| [[spin-down-limit]] Spin-down limit | advanced | The spin-down limit is the gravitational-wave amplitude a pulsar would have if all its observed rotational energy loss went into gravitational waves; an observed upper limit below it bounds the gravitational fraction of the spin-down by the square of the amplitude ratio (about 0.2 percent for the Crab when its amplitude limit is 4 percent). | — |
| [[continuous-wave-doppler-modulation]] Doppler modulation of continuous-wave signals | advanced | The Earth's spin and orbit Doppler-modulate a monochromatic gravitational wave in a direction-dependent way, smearing it over many frequency bins once observations exceed about an hour at kHz frequencies; searches undo it by resampling the data onto a clock at the Solar System barycentre for each trial sky position. | — |
| [[all-sky-continuous-wave-search]] All-sky continuous-wave search | frontier | Because a long observation makes the moving detector act like a telescope the size of Earth's orbit, a blind search must separately demodulate trillions of resolvable sky patches and many spin-down values; the optimal coherent search is computationally impossible, forcing semi-coherent, suboptimal strategies. | — |
| [[targeted-continuous-wave-search]] Targeted continuous-wave search | advanced | When a neutron star's position and spin are known from electromagnetic timing, the demodulation is fixed and only a few spin-down parameters need scanning, making the search far cheaper and somewhat more sensitive than a blind one; accreting X-ray pulsars such as Sco X-1 add uncertain orbital parameters. | — |
| [[core-collapse-supernova-gravitational-waves]] Gravitational waves from core-collapse supernovae | advanced | Non-spherical motion in a collapsing stellar core and newborn proto-neutron star emits gravitational waves that, with neutrinos, directly probe the core; predicted amplitudes are small and waveforms stochastic, so only Galactic or very nearby events are likely detectable with burst searches keyed to neutrino timing. | — |
| [[gravitational-wave-energy-density-parameter]] Gravitational-wave energy density parameter | advanced | Omega_gw is the energy density of a gravitational-wave background per logarithmic frequency interval divided by the critical density of the Universe; it is used because a random field has no single amplitude but does have a mean energy density, proportional to frequency squared times mean-square strain. | — |
| [[hulse-taylor-binary-pulsar]] Hulse-Taylor binary pulsar | core | PSR B1913+16, discovered in 1974, is a 59 ms radio pulsar orbiting another neutron star every 7.75 hours in an eccentric orbit; its orbital period decreases at the rate the quadrupole formula predicts to better than one percent, the first strong though indirect evidence for gravitational radiation. | — |
| [[gw150914]] GW150914 | core | GW150914, recorded by both LIGO detectors on 14 September 2015, was the first direct detection of gravitational waves: black holes of about 36 and 29 solar masses spiralled together about 400 Mpc away, forming a 62 solar-mass black hole and radiating about 3 solar masses of energy in a fraction of a second. | — |
| [[kilonova]] Kilonova | advanced | A kilonova is an optical and infrared transient lasting days, powered by radioactive decay of heavy neutron-rich nuclei made in material ejected during a neutron star merger; the one following GW170817 was found within about 11 hours. | — |
| [[short-gamma-ray-burst]] Short gamma-ray bursts from neutron star mergers | advanced | Short gamma-ray bursts are brief flashes produced by narrow ultra-relativistic jets launched along the orbital axis in neutron star mergers; an observer slightly off axis sees a weaker burst, and because the jet and orbital axes coincide, jet viewing-angle measurements constrain the binary's inclination. GW170817 and GRB 170817A confirmed the association. | — |
| [[gw170817]] GW170817 | core | GW170817 was the first binary neutron star merger detected (about 1.4 and 1.3 solar masses at about 40 Mpc), followed 1.7 s later by a short gamma-ray burst and then by a kilonova in NGC 4993; it launched multimessenger gravitational-wave astronomy, gave a standard-siren Hubble constant and showed gravitational waves travel at the speed of light to about one part in 1e15. | — |
| [[gw190521]] GW190521 | advanced | GW190521 was an unusually massive binary black hole merger (about 85 and 65 solar masses) whose remnant of about 142 solar masses is an intermediate-mass black hole and whose primary lies in the pair-instability mass gap, suggesting hierarchical mergers. | — |
| [[gw190814]] GW190814 | advanced | GW190814 was the merger of a roughly 23 solar-mass black hole with a 2.6 solar-mass object in the lower mass gap, the most asymmetric binary seen by 2021; its higher harmonics constrained the inclination and allowed a statistical standard-siren estimate of the Hubble constant. | — |
| [[gravitational-wave-transient-catalog]] Gravitational-Wave Transient Catalog | advanced | The LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA collaborations periodically publish catalogs (GWTC-1, -2, -3 and later) of all confident compact-binary detections in an observing period with their parameters, used for population studies, merger rates and combined tests of general relativity; events are named GW plus the UTC date. | — |
| [[r-process-nucleosynthesis]] r-process nucleosynthesis in neutron star mergers | advanced | Rapid neutron capture in the neutron-rich ejecta of compact mergers builds the heaviest elements such as gold, platinum and uranium; the spectra of the GW170817 kilonova established mergers as a major site of this process. | — |
| [[multimessenger-astronomy]] Multimessenger astronomy | core | Multimessenger astronomy observes one astrophysical event through several carriers, gravitational waves, light across the spectrum, neutrinos and cosmic rays, so that each constrains what the others cannot; for gravitational waves it began with GW170817. | — |
| [[standard-siren-hubble-constant]] Hubble constant from standard sirens | advanced | Dividing a host galaxy's Hubble-flow velocity by the standard-siren distance gives H0; without an identified host, the redshifts of all galaxies in the localization volume are combined statistically. GW170817 gave about 70 km/s/Mpc with errors dominated by inclination. | — |
| [[gravitational-wave-tests-of-general-relativity]] Gravitational-wave tests of general relativity | frontier | Detected signals test general relativity in the strong-field, dynamical regime: waveform consistency between inspiral and merger, bounds on the speed and dispersion of waves, polarization content, and ringdown spectra; combined catalog analyses so far find no deviation. | — |
| [[graviton-mass-bound]] Graviton mass bound from dispersion | frontier | A massive graviton would make wave speed depend on frequency, distorting a chirp over cosmological propagation distances; the absence of such distortion bounds the graviton mass (about 1e-22 eV from GW150914 and tighter from later events). | — |
| [[gravitational-wave-polarization-test]] Polarization test of general relativity | frontier | With three or more differently oriented detectors one can check whether a signal is explained by the two tensor polarizations of general relativity alone rather than scalar or vector modes; GW170814 favoured pure tensor over pure scalar or vector, though mixed alternatives remain hard to test. | — |
