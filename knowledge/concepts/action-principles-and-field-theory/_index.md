# Action principles, classical field theory and gauge fields

General relativity and its matter as Lagrangian field theories: functionals and functional derivatives, Lagrangian densities and the stationary-action principle for fields, Euler-Lagrange field equations and boundary terms, the metric-variation toolkit (inverse metric, determinant, connection, Palatini identity), the Einstein-Hilbert action and its variation, Gibbons-Hawking-York and Palatini formulations, matter actions with the gravitational coupling and the Hilbert stress-energy tensor, Noether's theorem, canonical energy-momentum and diffeomorphism invariance as the origin of conservation, scalar (Klein-Gordon, complex, phi-fourth) and Maxwell field theories, spontaneous symmetry breaking with Landau theory and its field-theory consequences, and gauge theory with fibre bundles, connections and curvature as the language shared by electromagnetism and gravity. Particle Lagrangian mechanics, the stress-energy tensors that result, and Kaluza-Klein or quantum aspects belong to other domains.

| Concept | Tier | Summary | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [[classical-field-theory]] Classical field theory | foundation | A classical field theory describes nature by fields, functions on spacetime giving a scalar, vector or tensor value at every event, obeying deterministic local equations without quantization. General relativity is such a theory: its fields are the matter fields and the metric itself. | — |
| [[functional]] Functional | foundation | A functional is a rule that takes an entire function as input and returns a single number; the action, which assigns a number to a whole history of a system, is the model example. | — |
| [[functional-derivative]] Functional derivative | core | The functional derivative of an action with respect to a field is the quantity whose integral against a small change of the field gives the first-order change of the action; for a Lagrangian depending on a field and its first derivatives it equals the Euler-Lagrange expression, with an extra double-divergence term when second derivatives appear. The variation symbol obeys the rules of a derivative (linearity, Leibniz rule) and commutes with partial differentiation. | — |
| [[lagrangian-density]] Lagrangian density and field action | core | A Lagrangian density is a local function of the fields and their first derivatives whose integral over space gives the Lagrangian and whose integral over spacetime gives the action of a field theory. In curved spacetime it must be a scalar density of weight one (a scalar times sqrt(-g)) so that the action is coordinate invariant. | — |
| [[hamiltons-principle-for-fields]] Principle of stationary action for fields | core | The field configuration realized in nature is one for which the action does not change to first order under any small variation of the fields that vanishes on the boundary of the spacetime region considered. Applied to the metric as the varied field, the same principle yields Einstein's equations. | — |
| [[boundary-term-in-the-action]] Boundary terms and total-divergence ambiguity | core | Integrating by parts in a variation produces a total divergence, which the divergence theorem turns into an integral over the boundary; it drops out when the variation vanishes there. For the same reason, two Lagrangian densities that differ by a total divergence give identical field equations. | — |
| [[euler-lagrange-equations-for-fields]] Euler-Lagrange equations for fields | core | A field that makes the action stationary satisfies dL/dphi = d_mu[dL/d(d_mu phi)], one equation per field component; the time derivative of particle mechanics becomes a spacetime divergence. In curved spacetime the partial derivatives are replaced by covariant ones when the Lagrangian is written with a scalar Lagrangian and the invariant volume element. | — |
| [[hamiltonian-density]] Hamiltonian density | advanced | The Hamiltonian density of a field is the Legendre transform of its Lagrangian density, pi dphi/dt minus the Lagrangian density, where pi = dL/d(dphi/dt) is the momentum density conjugate to the field; it leads to first-order Hamilton equations for fields. | — |
| [[real-scalar-field]] Real scalar field theory | core | The simplest field theory: one real number phi per event, with Lagrangian density -(1/2) g^{mu nu} d_mu phi d_nu phi - (1/2) m^2 phi^2 in signature (-+++) (sign conventions differ between books). Its quanta are spin-0 particles of mass m, and it is the standard test case for field equations, stress-energy and Noether currents. | — |
| [[klein-gordon-equation]] Klein-Gordon equation | core | The Klein-Gordon equation is the field equation of a free scalar of mass m, (box - m^2) phi = 0 with box = g^{mu nu} nabla_mu nabla_nu, obtained as the Euler-Lagrange equation of the scalar Lagrangian. Its static form with a source has the short-range Yukawa solution, which reduces to the long-range 1/r potential of Poisson's equation when m = 0. | — |
| [[interaction-lagrangian]] Interaction Lagrangian | advanced | When several fields are present, the total Lagrangian density is the sum of each field's free Lagrangian plus interaction terms containing products of different fields; these terms couple the fields, as J^mu A_mu couples a current to the electromagnetic potential and h_{mu nu} T^{mu nu} couples energy-momentum to a weak gravitational field. | — |
| [[charged-particle-action]] Action of a charged particle | advanced | The simplest Lorentz-invariant way for a field to influence a particle is to add q times the line integral of a one-form A_mu dx^mu along the world line to the free action -m integral dtau; the added term does not depend on how the path is parametrized, and varying the total action gives the Lorentz force law. | — |
| [[maxwell-lagrangian]] Maxwell Lagrangian | advanced | The electromagnetic field has Lagrangian density -(1/4) F_{mu nu} F^{mu nu} plus the coupling J^mu A_mu (in Heaviside-Lorentz-type units), with A as the varied field; its Euler-Lagrange equations are the sourced Maxwell equations, and varying it with respect to the metric gives the Maxwell stress-energy tensor. | — |
| [[metric-variation]] Variation with respect to the metric | core | Varying an action with respect to the metric means computing its change under a small change delta g_{mu nu}; because the metric sits inside the volume element sqrt(-g), every raised index and the connection, each of these dependences must be varied explicitly rather than by quoting the matter-field Euler-Lagrange formula. | — |
| [[variation-of-the-inverse-metric]] Variation of the inverse metric | core | Because g^{mu alpha} g_{alpha nu} is the fixed Kronecker delta, a change of the metric changes its inverse by delta g^{mu nu} = -g^{mu alpha} g^{nu beta} delta g_{alpha beta}: the indices are raised and a minus sign appears. | — |
| [[variation-of-the-metric-determinant]] Variation of the metric determinant | core | A change of the metric changes the volume density by delta sqrt(-g) = (1/2) sqrt(-g) g^{mu nu} delta g_{mu nu} = -(1/2) sqrt(-g) g_{mu nu} delta g^{mu nu}, a consequence of Jacobi's formula delta det M = det M tr(M^{-1} delta M). | — |
| [[variation-of-the-connection]] Variation of the connection | advanced | Connection coefficients are not tensor components, but the difference between two connections is, so a first-order change delta Gamma^a_bc is a (1,2) tensor; for the metric connection it is given by the Christoffel formula with partial derivatives of g replaced by covariant derivatives of delta g. | — |
| [[palatini-identity]] Palatini identity | advanced | For any change of the connection, the Riemann tensor changes by delta R^a_bcd = nabla_c(delta Gamma^a_bd) - nabla_d(delta Gamma^a_bc); contracting gives delta R_bd = nabla_a(delta Gamma^a_bd) - nabla_d(delta Gamma^a_ba), which makes g^bd delta R_bd a total divergence. | — |
| [[einstein-hilbert-action]] Einstein-Hilbert action | core | The Einstein-Hilbert action is the spacetime integral of the Ricci scalar weighted by the invariant volume element, S = (1/16 pi G) integral R sqrt(-g) d^4x (in c = 1 units); it is the simplest scalar action built from the metric that yields second-order field equations, and adding a constant to R gives the cosmological-constant term. | — |
| [[variation-of-the-einstein-hilbert-action]] Einstein equations from the action | core | Varying the Einstein-Hilbert action with respect to the metric produces three pieces: the Ricci tensor from varying g^{mu nu}, minus half R g_{mu nu} from varying sqrt(-g), and a total divergence from the Palatini identity; discarding the boundary term, stationarity gives the vacuum Einstein equation G_{mu nu} = 0, and adding a matter action gives G_{mu nu} = 8 pi G T_{mu nu}. | — |
| [[gamma-gamma-lagrangian]] Gamma-Gamma Lagrangian | advanced | Einstein's Gamma-Gamma Lagrangian is the part of sqrt(-g) R that is quadratic in Christoffel symbols; it differs from the Einstein-Hilbert density by a total divergence, so it depends only on the metric and its first derivatives and gives the same field equations, at the price of not being a scalar density. | — |
| [[gibbons-hawking-york-boundary-term]] Gibbons-Hawking-York boundary term | advanced | Fixing the metric on a boundary does not fix its normal derivatives, so the Einstein-Hilbert action alone leaves a boundary contribution in its variation; adding twice the integral of the trace of the boundary's extrinsic curvature over the induced volume element cancels it and makes the variational problem well posed. | — |
| [[palatini-formulation]] Palatini formulation | advanced | In the Palatini (metric-affine) approach the metric and a symmetric connection are varied independently in g^ab R_ab(Gamma) sqrt(-g); the metric variation gives the vacuum field equations, and the connection variation forces the connection to be metric compatible (in more than two dimensions), so the Levi-Civita connection is derived rather than assumed. | — |
| [[matter-lagrangian]] Matter action and gravitational coupling | core | The full action of general relativity is the Einstein-Hilbert action plus a matter action built from the non-gravitational fields and the metric; the relative weight (16 pi G in S = S_EH/16 pi G + S_M, with c = 1) is fixed by requiring the combined field equations to read G_{mu nu} = 8 pi G T_{mu nu}. | — |
| [[hilbert-stress-energy-tensor]] Hilbert stress-energy tensor | core | The Hilbert (metric) stress-energy tensor is defined by how the matter action responds to a change of metric, T^{mu nu} = (2/sqrt(-g)) delta S_M/delta g_{mu nu}, equivalently T_{mu nu} = -2 dL_M/dg^{mu nu} + g_{mu nu} L_M for a scalar Lagrangian; it is symmetric and covariant by construction and is exactly the source that appears in Einstein's equations. | — |
| [[canonical-energy-momentum-tensor]] Canonical energy-momentum tensor | advanced | The canonical energy-momentum tensor, [dL/d(d_alpha phi)] d^beta phi - g^{alpha beta} L, collects the four Noether currents of spacetime translations and is conserved in flat spacetime; unlike the Hilbert tensor it is generally not symmetric and not defined covariantly in curved spacetime, though for a scalar field the two agree. | — |
| [[perfect-fluid-action]] Perfect fluid action | advanced | A perfect fluid can be derived from the Lagrangian density equal to minus its total energy density, varied by displacing each flow line along a vector field while adjusting the rest-mass density to keep the particle current conserved; the resulting Hilbert tensor is the perfect-fluid stress-energy tensor. | — |
| [[symmetry-of-the-action]] Symmetry of an action | core | A symmetry of a field theory is a transformation of the fields, and possibly of the coordinates, under which the Lagrangian or action is unchanged (up to a boundary term), so the equations of motion are unchanged too; continuous families of such transformations are the input to Noether's theorem. | — |
| [[noethers-theorem]] Noether's theorem | core | Noether's theorem states that every continuous symmetry of an action yields a current with vanishing divergence on solutions, and hence a conserved quantity; for a scalar field and a symmetry generated by a Killing vector xi the current is [dL/d(d_alpha phi)] Lie_xi phi - xi^alpha L. | — |
| [[diffeomorphism-invariance]] Diffeomorphism invariance | core | General relativity is unchanged when every field, including the metric, is pushed along by a smooth map of spacetime to itself; configurations related this way are physically identical, so diffeomorphisms are the gauge symmetries of the theory, and an action built from a scalar density is exactly invariant under them. | — |
| [[conservation-law-from-diffeomorphism-invariance]] Stress-energy conservation from diffeomorphism invariance | advanced | If the matter action is unchanged when the metric is varied by a Lie derivative along any vector field, and the matter fields obey their own equations of motion, then the Hilbert stress-energy tensor has zero covariant divergence; local energy-momentum conservation is thus a consequence of diffeomorphism invariance rather than an extra postulate. | — |
| [[global-symmetry]] Global symmetry | advanced | A global symmetry acts by the same amount at every spacetime point, for example multiplying a complex field everywhere by one constant phase e^{i alpha} or flipping phi to -phi everywhere; it contrasts with a local (gauge) transformation whose parameter varies from point to point. | — |
| [[order-parameter]] Order parameter | advanced | An order parameter is a macroscopic quantity that vanishes in the symmetric, high-temperature phase and becomes non-zero once a symmetry is broken, such as the magnetization of a ferromagnet or the equilibrium value of a scalar field. | — |
| [[spontaneous-symmetry-breaking]] Spontaneous symmetry breaking | advanced | Spontaneous symmetry breaking occurs when the laws of a system (its Lagrangian or free energy) have a symmetry that its lowest-energy state lacks, so the system settles into one of several equivalent ground states (vacua) related by that symmetry; which one is picked is decided by small perturbations, not by the laws. | — |
| [[landau-theory-of-phase-transitions]] Landau theory of phase transitions | advanced | Landau theory describes a continuous phase transition by expanding the free energy near the transition in even powers of an order parameter, F = F_0 + a M^2 + b M^4 with b > 0, where a changes sign at the critical temperature T_c; minimizing F gives M = 0 above T_c and a non-zero M below it. | — |
| [[phi-fourth-theory]] Phi-fourth scalar field theory | advanced | Phi-fourth theory is a real scalar field with potential U = (mu^2/2) phi^2 + (lambda/4!) phi^4 (lambda > 0), symmetric under phi -> -phi; for positive mu^2 the vacuum is phi = 0, while for negative mu^2 there are two degenerate vacua at nonzero phi, the field-theory version of Landau's transition. | — |
| [[complex-scalar-field]] Complex scalar field | advanced | A complex scalar field psi has Lagrangian density -(d_mu psi)*(d^mu psi) - m^2 \|psi\|^2 and can be written as an amplitude times a phase e^{i theta} at each point; it is the simplest theory with a U(1) phase symmetry and the standard model for charged matter in gauge theory. | — |
| [[mexican-hat-potential]] Mexican-hat potential | advanced | The Mexican-hat potential for a complex scalar depends only on \|psi\|, with a local maximum at zero and a circle of degenerate minima at \|psi\| = \|psi_0\|; picking a ground state means picking a phase, which spontaneously breaks the U(1) symmetry. | — |
| [[higgs-mechanism]] Higgs mechanism | frontier | In the Standard Model a scalar (Higgs) field acquires a non-zero vacuum value as the universe cools, breaking electroweak symmetry, separating electromagnetism from the weak force and giving mass to the W and Z bosons and to quarks and charged leptons. | — |
| [[topological-defect]] Topological defect | frontier | A topological defect forms when a field settles into different vacua in different places and cannot join them smoothly; for a complex field whose phase winds by 2 pi around a point (a vortex, or a string in three dimensions), continuity forces the field magnitude to vanish at the core. | — |
| [[internal-symmetry]] Internal variables and internal symmetry | advanced | An internal variable is something a field carries at each point that is not a spacetime direction, such as the phase of a complex field or an isospin label; an internal symmetry transforms only these variables and moves nothing in spacetime. | — |
| [[local-gauge-invariance]] Local gauge invariance | advanced | Local gauge invariance demands that a theory be unchanged when its internal variables are transformed by a different amount at each point; because derivatives of the field then pick up extra terms, the demand can only be met by introducing a compensating gauge field. | — |
| [[gauge-field]] Gauge field | advanced | A gauge field is a one-form A_mu introduced so that derivatives of a charged field transform covariantly under local internal transformations; under a local phase change it shifts by the gradient of the transformation parameter, and for U(1) with coupling q equal to the electric charge it is the electromagnetic potential. | — |
| [[gauge-covariant-derivative]] Gauge covariant derivative | advanced | The gauge covariant derivative D_mu = d_mu + i q A_mu removes the part of a charged field's change that is due only to the local phase convention, so that D_mu psi transforms exactly like psi; it is total change minus parallel-transport change, just as nabla is for tangent vectors. | — |
| [[gauge-parallel-transport]] Parallel transport of a charged field | advanced | Moving a charged field value to a neighbouring point with no change except that dictated by the gauge connection rotates its phase by delta psi = -i q A_mu psi dx^mu; transport along a curve with D_mu psi = 0 is the bundle analogue of parallel transport (a horizontal lift). | — |
| [[gauge-field-strength]] Gauge field strength | advanced | The field strength of a gauge theory is the commutator of gauge covariant derivatives, [D_mu, D_nu]; it is gauge covariant and carries the physical content of the gauge field. For U(1) it equals i q F_{mu nu}, so the electromagnetic field tensor is the curvature of the phase bundle. | — |
| [[gauge-theory]] Gauge theory | advanced | A gauge theory is a field theory made invariant under local internal transformations by replacing ordinary derivatives with gauge covariant derivatives built from gauge fields; electromagnetism is the U(1) case, and the same structure underlies particle physics. | — |
| [[yang-mills-theory]] Yang-Mills theory | frontier | A Yang-Mills (non-abelian) gauge theory has an internal symmetry group whose elements do not commute, so the gauge field is matrix-valued and its field strength contains a commutator of potentials besides their derivatives, making the gauge field self-interacting. | — |
| [[fibre-bundle]] Fibre bundle | advanced | A fibre bundle is a space built by attaching a copy of a fibre V above every point of a base manifold M, with a projection back to M, so that it looks locally like the product M x V; a field with internal variables is a choice of point in the fibre above each spacetime point, and for a complex phase the fibre is the U(1) circle. | — |
| [[tangent-bundle]] Tangent bundle | advanced | The tangent bundle TM of a manifold is the bundle whose fibre over each point is the tangent space there, so a point of TM is a point together with a tangent vector and dim TM = 2 dim M; a vector field is a choice of vector in each fibre. | — |
| [[trivial-bundle]] Trivial and twisted bundles | advanced | Every bundle looks like a product over small regions of the base (local triviality), but it is trivial only if one product description M x V works globally; a twisted bundle such as the Moebius band is locally a product yet globally not, which can be detected by whether a section must meet the zero section. | — |
| [[section-of-a-bundle]] Section of a bundle | advanced | A section (cross section) of a bundle is a smooth choice of one point in the fibre above each base point, so fields are sections: a vector field is a section of the tangent bundle and the zero section picks the zero vector everywhere. | — |
| [[bundle-connection]] Connection on a fibre bundle | advanced | A connection on a bundle is the rule for comparing values in neighbouring fibres, deciding which change counts as no change when moving across the base; in U(1) gauge theory its components are i q A_mu, playing the role that Christoffel symbols play for tangent vectors. | — |
| [[bundle-curvature]] Curvature of a bundle connection | advanced | The curvature of a bundle connection is the two-form obtained from the commutator of its covariant derivatives; for the tangent bundle with the Levi-Civita connection it is the Riemann tensor, and for the U(1) phase bundle it is i q F. | — |
| [[gravity-as-a-gauge-theory]] Gravity-gauge theory analogy | advanced | Gravity and electromagnetism share one geometric structure: coordinate transformations correspond to local phase changes, Christoffel symbols to the gauge potential, and Riemann curvature to the field strength F, all being connections and curvatures on bundles. The analogy has limits: the Einstein-Hilbert action is linear in curvature while Yang-Mills actions are quadratic. | — |
