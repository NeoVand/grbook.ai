# Curvature

Measuring how a geometry fails to be flat, from the classical curvature of curves and surfaces in Euclidean space (curvature and Frenet-Serret frames, second fundamental form, principal and Gaussian curvature, theorema egregium, intrinsic versus extrinsic curvature, circumference and angle-excess tests) to the Riemann tensor of a general manifold: path dependence and holonomy of parallel transport, the Riemann tensor from loops and from commutators of covariant derivatives, its form in normal coordinates, symmetries, cyclic identity and component count, the flatness criterion, sign conventions, the Ricci tensor and scalar, the Bianchi and contracted Bianchi identities and the Einstein tensor as pure geometry, the Weyl tensor and conformal flatness, sectional curvature, curvature invariants and the equivalence problem, spaces of constant curvature and Einstein spaces, and geodesic deviation read as the relativistic tidal tensor. Curvature forms and Cartan's method, hypersurface Gauss-Codazzi relations in spacetime, Petrov types, and the curvature of named spacetimes belong to other domains.

| Concept | Tier | Summary | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [[parallel-postulate]] Euclid's parallel postulate | foundation | Euclid's fifth axiom says, in one common form, that straight lines which start out parallel stay parallel however far they are extended. It holds on a plane or an unrolled cylinder but fails on a sphere, and its failure became the first test for curvature. | — |
| [[intrinsic-geometry]] Intrinsic viewpoint on geometry | foundation | Geometry should be described using only measurements that a being confined to the space can make (lengths, angles, parallel transport), with no appeal to a larger space it might sit in. For spacetime this is the only option, since nothing can step outside it. | — |
| [[curvature]] Curvature as departure from flatness | foundation | A space (or spacetime) is curved when its geometry cannot be made flat everywhere by any choice of coordinates; the tell-tale sign is that geodesics which start parallel converge (positive curvature) or diverge (negative curvature) instead of staying parallel. Flat is not the same as Euclidean: Minkowski spacetime is flat though its metric is indefinite. | — |
| [[intrinsic-versus-extrinsic-curvature]] Intrinsic versus extrinsic curvature | foundation | Intrinsic curvature is detectable from within a space by its own distance and parallelism measurements; extrinsic curvature describes only how the space bends inside a larger one. A paper cylinder is extrinsically curved but intrinsically flat (it unrolls without stretching), while a sphere is intrinsically curved, and GR curvature is always the intrinsic kind. | — |
| [[circumference-to-radius-test]] Circumference-to-radius test for curvature | foundation | Draw a circle by walking a fixed distance r from a point and measure its circumference C: in flat space C = 2 pi r, on positively curved surfaces C is smaller (on a sphere of radius R it is 2 pi R sin(r/R)), and on negatively curved ones it is larger. The shortfall for small circles gives the Gaussian curvature, K = lim (3/pi)(2 pi r - C)/r^3 (Bertrand-Diquet-Puiseux). | — |
| [[angular-excess]] Angular excess of geodesic triangles | foundation | The angles of a triangle made of geodesics add up to more than pi on a positively curved surface and less than pi on a negatively curved one, and the difference equals the integrated Gaussian curvature over its area. On a sphere of radius R the excess is A/R^2 (Girard's theorem); on a hyperbolic plane of curvature -1/R^2 the deficit is A/R^2 (Lambert). | — |
| [[curvature-of-a-curve]] Curvature of a curve | prerequisite | For a curve parametrized by arc length s, curvature kappa = /dt/ds/ is how fast the unit tangent turns per unit length; its reciprocal is the radius of the osculating circle that best fits the curve at that point. A curve has no intrinsic curvature, only this extrinsic bending. | — |
| [[frenet-serret-equations]] Frenet-Serret equations | prerequisite | Along a space curve the unit tangent t, principal normal p and binormal b = t x p form an orthonormal triad whose arc-length derivatives are t' = kappa p, p' = -kappa t + tau b, b' = -tau p. Curvature kappa(s) and torsion tau(s) determine the curve up to a rigid motion. | — |
| [[second-fundamental-form]] Second fundamental form | advanced | For a surface in flat 3-space with basis vectors e_mu = dX/dx^mu and unit normal n, the derivative of a basis vector splits into a tangential connection part and a normal part K_mu nu n (Gauss's formula); K_mu nu is the second fundamental form, the quadratic term of the surface's height above its tangent plane. The first fundamental form is simply the induced metric g_mu nu = e_mu . e_nu. | — |
| [[normal-curvature]] Normal and geodesic curvature | advanced | A curve lying in a surface has a curvature vector dt/ds that splits into a part along the surface normal, the normal curvature kappa_n = K_mu nu t^mu t^nu (fixed by the tangent direction alone), and a part in the tangent plane, the geodesic curvature, which is intrinsic and vanishes exactly for the surface's geodesics. | — |
| [[principal-curvatures]] Principal curvatures | advanced | At a point of a surface the normal curvature has a maximum and minimum over directions; these principal curvatures k1, k2 are the eigenvalues of the shape operator g^{-1}K and occur along perpendicular principal directions (Euler). Their product is the Gaussian curvature and their sum is twice the mean curvature. | — |
| [[gaussian-curvature]] Gaussian curvature | foundation | Gaussian curvature K is a single number at each point of a surface that measures its intrinsic curvature: K = k1 k2, equal to the area swept by the unit normal on the unit sphere per unit surface area (Gauss map), and in two dimensions it is the only independent Riemann component, R_1212 = K g. A sphere of radius R has K = 1/R^2, a plane 0, and a hyperbolic plane a negative value. | — |
| [[theorema-egregium]] Theorema egregium | foundation | Gauss's remarkable theorem: the Gaussian curvature of a surface, although first defined through its bending in 3-space, can be computed from the metric components and their first and second derivatives alone. It is therefore unchanged by bending without stretching and measurable by inhabitants of the surface. | — |
| [[path-dependence-of-parallel-transport]] Path dependence of parallel transport | core | On a curved manifold, carrying a vector parallel to itself from one point to another gives a result that depends on the route, so there are no globally parallel vector fields and no absolute way to compare or add vectors at separated events. On a sphere, a vector taken round a triangle with two corners on the equator and one at the pole comes back turned by 90 degrees. | — |
| [[holonomy]] Holonomy of parallel transport around a loop | core | A vector parallel transported around a small closed loop returns changed by an amount linear in the vector and in the loop area, delta V^a = -R^a_bcd V^b dx^c dy^d, so the Riemann tensor is the holonomy per unit area. On a sphere of radius R the rotation angle for any loop is the enclosed area divided by R^2. | — |
| [[riemann-curvature-tensor]] Riemann curvature tensor | core | The Riemann tensor R^a_bcd is the (1,3) tensor built from the connection and its first derivatives (so from the metric and its first and second derivatives), R^a_bcd = d_c Gamma^a_bd - d_d Gamma^a_bc + Gamma^a_ec Gamma^e_bd - Gamma^a_ed Gamma^e_bc. It measures the change of a vector carried round a small loop, the failure of covariant derivatives to commute and the relative acceleration of neighbouring geodesics, and it vanishes everywhere exactly when the space is flat. | — |
| [[riemann-curvature-operator]] Riemann curvature operator | advanced | For vector fields u and v the curvature operator R(u,v) = [nabla_u, nabla_v] - nabla_[u,v] is a linear map on vectors that contains no derivatives of the vector it acts on; its components are the Riemann tensor, R(u,v)w = R^a_bcd w^b u^c v^d e_a. The Lie-bracket term corrects for the failure of the displacement loop to close. | — |
| [[ricci-identity]] Ricci identity | core | Second covariant derivatives do not commute in curved space: for a vector, nabla_c nabla_d V^a - nabla_d nabla_c V^a = R^a_bcd V^b (with a torsion-free connection), and a higher-rank tensor picks up one Riemann term per index, with a minus sign for lower indices. It is the infinitesimal version of going round a loop in two orders. | — |
| [[riemann-tensor-in-normal-coordinates]] Riemann tensor in normal coordinates | core | At a point where coordinates are locally inertial (metric Minkowskian and Christoffel symbols zero), the lowered Riemann tensor is a signed sum of four second derivatives of the metric, and the metric near the point is g_mn = eta_mn - (1/3) R_manb x^a x^b + O(x^3). Coordinate freedom can remove the metric's value and first derivatives but leaves exactly 20 combinations of second derivatives, which are the curvature. | — |
| [[symmetries-of-the-riemann-tensor]] Symmetries of the Riemann tensor | core | With all indices lowered and the metric (Levi-Civita) connection, R_abcd is antisymmetric in its first pair (ab), antisymmetric in its last pair (cd), and symmetric under exchanging the two pairs. These hold in every basis and make many components vanish or equal each other. | — |
| [[cyclic-identity]] Cyclic (first Bianchi) identity | core | For a torsion-free connection the Riemann tensor summed over cyclic permutations of its last three indices vanishes, R^a_[bcd] = 0, i.e. R_abcd + R_acdb + R_adbc = 0. Given the pair symmetries it adds one new condition only when all four indices differ, removing one component in four dimensions. | — |
| [[number-of-independent-riemann-components]] Number of independent Riemann components | core | The symmetries cut the n^4 components of the Riemann tensor to n^2(n^2-1)/12 independent ones: 1 in two dimensions, 6 in three and 20 in four. In four dimensions the pair symmetries leave a symmetric 6x6 array (21 entries) and the cyclic identity removes one more; the 20 match the second metric derivatives that no coordinate change can remove. | — |
| [[flatness-criterion]] Flatness criterion | core | A manifold is flat, meaning coordinates exist in which the metric components are constant (Euclidean or Minkowskian) and parallel transport is path independent, if and only if its Riemann tensor vanishes everywhere. Nonzero Christoffel symbols alone prove nothing, since curvilinear coordinates produce them in flat space. | — |
| [[integrability-condition-for-parallel-fields]] Integrability condition for parallel fields | advanced | A vector field that is covariantly constant everywhere, nabla_b V^a = 0, can exist only if the mixed second derivatives agree, which requires R^a_bcd V^b = 0; conversely, if the Riemann tensor vanishes on a simply connected region, such fields exist through every vector at a point. This is the step that turns small-loop holonomy into the flatness criterion. | — |
| [[curvature-sign-conventions]] Curvature sign conventions | core | Books disagree on the overall sign of the Riemann tensor, on which index pair is contracted to form the Ricci tensor, and on the metric signature, so the sign of R_ab, R and even the Einstein equation can differ between texts. A reliable check is that the unit 2-sphere should have positive Ricci scalar R = 2 in the chosen conventions. | — |
| [[ricci-tensor]] Ricci tensor | core | The Ricci tensor R_bd = R^a_bad is the contraction of the Riemann tensor on its upper index and third index; it is symmetric and, because of the Riemann symmetries, essentially the only nonzero contraction. Physically it is the part of curvature that changes the volume of a small ball of freely falling particles, holding 10 of the 20 components in four dimensions. | — |
| [[ricci-scalar]] Ricci scalar | core | The Ricci scalar R = g^ab R_ab is the full trace of the Ricci tensor, a single coordinate-invariant number at each point; it vanishes in flat space and equals 2/a^2 on a sphere of radius a (twice the Gaussian curvature in two dimensions). | — |
| [[bianchi-identity]] Bianchi identity | core | The differential Bianchi identity says that the covariant derivative of the Riemann tensor, cycled over the derivative index and the last two indices, sums to zero: nabla_e R_abcd + nabla_c R_abde + nabla_d R_abec = 0, for every metric. Geometrically it expresses that the boundary of a boundary is zero, the gravitational counterpart of dF = 0. | — |
| [[contracted-bianchi-identity]] Contracted Bianchi identity | core | Contracting the Bianchi identity twice gives nabla_a R^a_b = (1/2) nabla_b R, which says the Einstein tensor has identically vanishing divergence, nabla_a G^ab = 0, for any metric. It is why the Einstein equation automatically enforces local energy-momentum conservation and why its ten components obey four differential identities. | — |
| [[einstein-tensor]] Einstein tensor | core | The Einstein tensor G_ab = R_ab - (1/2) g_ab R is the symmetric curvature tensor whose covariant divergence vanishes identically; it is the Ricci tensor with its trace reversed (its trace is -R in four dimensions). It is the geometric side of the Einstein field equations. | — |
| [[sectional-curvature]] Sectional curvature | advanced | The sectional curvature of a two-plane spanned by X and Y is the Gaussian curvature of the surface swept out by geodesics tangent to that plane, K(X,Y) = R(X,Y,Y,X)/(/X/^2/Y/^2 - (X.Y)^2) up to sign convention. Knowing it for all planes determines the full Riemann tensor. | — |
| [[curvature-of-the-two-sphere]] Curvature of the 2-sphere | core | For a sphere of radius a, ds^2 = a^2(dtheta^2 + sin^2 theta dphi^2), the only independent Riemann component gives R^theta_phithetaphi = sin^2 theta, Gaussian curvature 1/a^2, Ricci tensor proportional to the metric, and Ricci scalar 2/a^2 everywhere. It is the standard first calculation of curvature and the calibration check for sign conventions. | — |
| [[space-of-constant-curvature]] Space of constant curvature | core | A space of constant curvature has R_abcd = K(g_ac g_bd - g_ad g_bc) with the same K at every point, so every point and every two-plane look alike; it has the maximum number n(n+1)/2 of Killing vectors (maximally symmetric) and its Ricci tensor is (n-1)K g_ab. Spheres (K > 0), flat space (K = 0) and hyperbolic space (K < 0) are the Riemannian examples; de Sitter and anti-de Sitter are the Lorentzian ones. | — |
| [[einstein-space]] Einstein space | advanced | An Einstein space is one whose Ricci tensor is a constant multiple of the metric, R_ab = lambda g_ab; in dimension above two the contracted Bianchi identity forces lambda to be constant. Every space of constant curvature is an Einstein space but not conversely, and in spacetime the condition is the vacuum field equation with a cosmological constant. | — |
| [[ricci-flat-spacetime]] Ricci-flat versus Riemann-flat | core | A Ricci-flat space has R_ab = 0 everywhere, which is weaker than flatness: the Weyl part of the curvature can survive, so tidal distortion without volume change remains, as outside a star or in a gravitational wave. When Ricci vanishes, the Riemann tensor equals the Weyl tensor. | — |
| [[weyl-tensor]] Weyl tensor | core | The Weyl tensor C_abcd is the trace-free part of the Riemann tensor: Riemann minus the terms built from the Ricci tensor, Ricci scalar and metric, so that every contraction vanishes while the Riemann symmetries remain. In four dimensions it carries the 10 curvature components not fixed locally by matter, describes shape-changing tides and gravitational waves, is unchanged by conformal rescaling, and vanishes identically in three or fewer dimensions. | — |
| [[weyl-criterion-for-conformal-flatness]] Weyl criterion for conformal flatness | advanced | In four or more dimensions a metric can be written as a positive function times a flat metric (conformally flat) if and only if its Weyl tensor vanishes. Minkowski and Robertson-Walker spacetimes are conformally flat; in three dimensions the Weyl tensor is always zero and the Cotton tensor decides instead, while every two-dimensional metric is locally conformally flat. | — |
| [[weyl-tensor-field-equation]] Weyl tensor field equation | advanced | Splitting the Riemann tensor into Weyl and Ricci parts turns the Bianchi identity into a divergence equation for the Weyl tensor whose source is built from derivatives of the Ricci tensor, and so, through Einstein's equation, from matter. It is the closest gravitational analogue of Maxwell's equations with a current: curvature at one place produced by matter elsewhere. | — |
| [[kretschmann-scalar]] Kretschmann scalar and curvature invariants | core | Curvature invariants are scalars built from the Riemann tensor, such as the Kretschmann scalar R_abcd R^abcd, and take the same value in every coordinate system. If one diverges, the curvature is genuinely infinite; for Schwarzschild the Kretschmann scalar is 48 M^2/r^6, finite at r = 2M and divergent at r = 0. | — |
| [[equivalence-problem]] Equivalence problem for metrics | advanced | The equivalence problem asks whether two metrics given in different coordinates describe the same geometry, i.e. are related by a coordinate transformation. It is settled by comparing coordinate-independent curvature data (invariant classification), a task usually done with computer algebra; the Schwarzschild solution was rediscovered many times for lack of such tools. | — |
| [[cartan-karlhede-algorithm]] Cartan-Karlhede algorithm | frontier | Cartan showed that two metrics are equivalent if the components of the Riemann tensor and its covariant derivatives agree in suitably fixed frames; Karlhede's staged procedure fixes the frame step by step and stops when no new invariant information or frame freedom appears, needing at most seven derivatives in general and five in vacuum. | — |
| [[deviation-vector]] Deviation (connecting) vector | core | In a one-parameter family of geodesics, the deviation vector xi joins a point on one geodesic to the point with the same affine parameter on its infinitesimal neighbour; it is the tangent to the curves across the family and so commutes with the geodesic tangent (it is Lie dragged). Its part orthogonal to the four-velocity is the physical separation seen by the observer. | — |
| [[newtonian-deviation-equation]] Newtonian equation of deviation | foundation | In Newtonian gravity two nearby free particles with separation eta obey d^2 eta^i/dt^2 = -(d_i d_j Phi) eta^j to first order: their relative acceleration is the tidal tensor (Hessian of the potential) acting on the separation. It is the template that the relativistic geodesic deviation equation reproduces. | — |
| [[geodesic-deviation-equation]] Geodesic deviation equation | core | For neighbouring geodesics with tangent u and deviation vector xi, the relative acceleration is D^2 xi^a/dtau^2 = -R^a_bcd u^b xi^c u^d: curvature alone makes initially parallel free-fall paths converge or diverge. In a parallel-propagated frame on one geodesic it becomes d^2 xi^i/dtau^2 = -R^i_0j0 xi^j, the exact relativistic form of the Newtonian tidal equation. | — |
| [[relativistic-tidal-tensor]] Relativistic tidal tensor | core | Contracting the Riemann tensor twice with an observer's four-velocity, K^a_b = R^a_cbd u^c u^d, gives a symmetric spatial tensor that maps separation to relative acceleration; its frame components R^i_0j0 (the electric part of curvature) replace the Newtonian Hessian of the potential. Positive eigenvalues act like spring constants pulling particles together, negative ones push them apart, and its trace is R_ab u^a u^b. | — |
| [[volume-preserving-tidal-deformation]] Volume-preserving tidal deformation | core | A small ball of test particles released from rest is initially reshaped by tides, but its volume begins to change only through the trace of the tidal tensor, which is the Ricci term R_ab u^a u^b. In vacuum that trace vanishes, so the ball becomes an ellipsoid of equal volume (stretched along the field direction by twice the squeeze in each perpendicular direction), while matter inside makes the volume shrink. | — |
