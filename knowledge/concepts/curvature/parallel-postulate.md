# Euclid's parallel postulate

*Why straight lines that start parallel keep their gap on flat ground but meet on a ball*

`parallel-postulate` · curvature · foundation · physics-reviewed (revision 10)

**Needs:** [[euclidean-space]] (entry)

> Two friends stand side by side, facing the same way at a right angle to the straight line between them. Then both walk without ever steering. On flat ground they keep the same gap forever, just as Euclid's parallel postulate says. On a smooth ball they always meet a quarter of the way around the ball, however close they started. The rule cannot be proved from geometry's other starting rules. So only measuring can tell whether the space we live in obeys it.

## Ways in

### 1. Two friends on a ball · entry · picture

*Do two friends who set off side by side and walk straight stay the same distance apart?*

Picture perfectly flat ground that goes on as far as you like. Two friends stand on it, 10 metres apart. Imagine a straight chalk line drawn between them. Each friend faces the same way, at a right angle to the chalk line. Then both walk ahead at the same pace.

As they walk, both follow one rule: never steer to the left or to the right. The path of someone who walks without ever steering is called a straight line.

On flat ground, a tape measure stretched between the friends reads 10 metres however far they go. Two straight lines that begin like this are said to start parallel: each leaves the same straight line at a right angle, toward the same side.

About 2,300 years ago, the Greek mathematician Euclid built geometry on a few starting rules. One of them comes down to this: straight lines that start parallel stay parallel, keeping the same gap forever. This rule is called the parallel postulate.

Now try the same test on a giant smooth ball. Seen from outside, as on a globe, call the top point the North Pole and the circle around the middle the equator. Picture the lines printed on a globe from the equator to the North Pole. The ball on one side of such a line is a mirror image of the ball on the other side. So a walker who follows the line has no reason to steer either way. The path bends over the ball's curve, but the walker never steers, so the path counts as a straight line.

The two friends stand on the equator, 10 metres apart, and both face the North Pole. The equator is the straight line between them, and each friend leaves it at a right angle, toward the same side. So their straight lines start parallel. As they walk straight, their paths draw together, and the friends meet at the North Pole.

The North Pole is a quarter of the way around the ball from the equator. Walk on past it without steering, and you reach the equator on the far side, then the South Pole, then your start: four equal stretches.

The starting gap makes no difference. Every straight line that leaves the equator at a right angle, toward the North Pole, passes through the North Pole. So friends who start 10 metres apart or 1,000 kilometres apart walk the same distance before they meet.

Starting parallel matters. Halfway from the equator to the North Pole, two friends who both face the North Pole meet after only an eighth of the way around. They did not start parallel: they stand side by side along the circle halfway to the North Pole, not along a straight line. Staying on that circle takes steering.

On Earth, treated as a smooth ball, a quarter of the way around is about 10,000 kilometres. The gap closes very slowly at first, which is why nobody notices the closing. Two friends who start 100 metres apart and walk straight for 10 kilometres end up only about a tenth of a millimetre closer. That is about the thickness of a sheet of paper.

**Try it:** Stretch a rubber band around the middle of a round orange, along the line where one flat knife cut through the centre would split it in half. Hold the orange with that band level. The half on one side of the band is a mirror image of the half on the other side. So an ant walking along the band would have no reason to steer either way, and the band lies along a straight line on the orange. Stretch two more bands around the orange, each along another flat cut through the centre, so each splits the orange into two equal halves. Make each new band cross the middle band at a right angle, a finger's width from the other. Next to the middle band, the two new bands run side by side. Follow them upward: they cross at the top of the orange, a quarter of the way around.

**Takeaway:** On flat ground, straight lines that start parallel keep the same gap forever; on a ball they meet a quarter of the way around the ball, whatever the starting gap.

### 2. A steady gap is not the test · entry · contrast

*Does a pair of paths that keeps a steady gap show that the parallel postulate holds?*

**Recap:** A straight line is the path of someone who walks without ever steering left or right. Two straight lines start parallel when they leave the same straight line side by side, each at a right angle to it, toward the same side. The parallel postulate says that straight lines that start parallel keep the same gap forever.

In "Two friends on a ball", the friends started parallel and walked straight, and only then did anyone measure the gap. Picture two runners in neighbouring lanes of a running track. All the way around, they stay about 1.2 metres apart, the width of one lane.

Yet neither runner follows a straight line all the way around. On each curved end of the track, both runners keep steering toward the grass in the middle. On those curved ends, their steady gap comes from steering, not from the ground. On the straight parts, the flat ground passes the test.

A steady gap on its own tells you nothing about the parallel postulate. The postulate is a rule about straight lines, and a steered path is not one.

So the test has three parts. First, the two people start parallel. Next, both walk straight, never steering. Only then do you measure the gap. If the gap stays the same however far they walk, the ground passes. If the gap changes, the ground fails.

**Takeaway:** A steady gap proves nothing by itself; the parallel postulate is tested only when both paths are straight lines.

### 3. The rule nobody could prove · entry · historical-puzzle

*Why could nobody prove, from simpler rules, that straight lines that start parallel stay parallel?*

**Recap:** A straight line is the path of someone who walks without ever steering left or right. Two straight lines start parallel when they leave the same straight line side by side, each at a right angle to it, toward the same side. Euclid's parallel postulate says that straight lines that start parallel keep the same gap forever. On flat ground they do. On a ball they meet a quarter of the way around the ball.

In "Two friends on a ball", the parallel postulate held on flat ground but failed on a ball. Euclid set out geometry as a chain of reasons. Every fact had to follow from a few starting rules, taken as true without proof. A starting rule like this is called a postulate.

Four of Euclid's postulates are short, such as the first, "any two points can be joined by a straight line". The fifth is long. Together with the other four, it leads in several steps to the rule that straight lines that start parallel stay parallel.

Many mathematicians felt that such a long rule should be proved from the other four, not taken on trust. For about two thousand years they tried. Each attempt failed, or secretly used another rule that turned out to be the fifth postulate in disguise.

Around 1830, the Russian mathematician Nikolai Lobachevsky and the Hungarian mathematician János Bolyai tried the opposite: each assumed that the fifth postulate is false. Each worked out a geometry that keeps the other four, but in which straight lines that start parallel spread apart. Neither found a contradiction, meaning a pair of results that cannot both be true.

A ball does not settle the question on its own, because a ball breaks another rule too. Mathematicians read that first postulate as saying that two points are joined by only one straight line. On a ball, the North Pole and the South Pole are joined by many straight lines, one along each printed line of a globe.

About forty years later, mathematicians drew the whole of the new geometry inside an ordinary flat disc. Each point of the new geometry is drawn as a point of the disc. Each of its straight lines is drawn as an arc of a circle, or as a ruler line through the disc's centre. Every fact of the new geometry then becomes a fact about ordinary flat drawings.

So if the new geometry contained a contradiction, ordinary flat geometry would contain one too. Mathematicians are confident that flat geometry has none, so they accept that the new geometry has none either.

Together, these results mean that logic alone, starting from Euclid's other rules, cannot decide whether straight lines that start parallel stay parallel. Only measuring can tell whether the space we live in obeys the rule.

**Takeaway:** The parallel postulate cannot be proved from Euclid's other rules, so whether the space we live in obeys it is a question for measurement.

### 4. How fast the gap closes · working · calculation

*How fast does the gap between lines that start parallel change, and on which surfaces does it stay fixed?*

In "Two friends on a ball", straight lines that start parallel on the equator met at the North Pole, a quarter of the way around, whatever their starting gap. The law behind that result is short. On a sphere of radius $a$ the straight lines, called geodesics, are great circles: circles centred on the sphere's centre. Let two of them leave the equator at right angles, a distance $D_0$ apart, toward the same side. After each has run a distance $s$, the circle of latitude through both crosses them at right angles and has radius $a\cos(s/a)$ about the sphere's axis. The gap measured along that circle is therefore

$$D(s) = D_0\cos(s/a), \qquad 0 \le s \le \pi a/2,$$

built step by step in the derivation "The gap on a sphere". It vanishes at $s = \pi a/2$ for every $D_0$: a quarter circumference, 10 008 km for a sphere of Earth's mean radius, 6371 km.

Near the start, $\cos x \approx 1 - x^2/2$, so $D \approx D_0(1 - s^2/2a^2)$. The lines begin with no rate of approach, $D'(0) = 0$, which is what starting parallel means, and the gap then closes quadratically. For $D_0 = 100$ m and $s = 10$ km on Earth the loss is $D_0s^2/2a^2 = 0.12$ mm.

Differentiating twice gives $D'' = -D/a^2$. On a plane the law is $D'' = 0$, and with $D'(0) = 0$ the gap stays $D_0$ for all $s$: the parallel postulate. A paper tube gives the same result, because rolling paper bends it without stretching, so every gap along the surface is unchanged. On the hyperbolic plane, whose small pieces are saddle-shaped, with constant negative curvature, the law is $D'' = +D/a^2$ and $D = D_0\cosh(s/a)$: the lines spread apart and never meet.

All three cases are one equation, stated here without proof,

$$\frac{d^2D}{ds^2} = -K\,D,$$

with $K = 1/a^2$ on a sphere, $0$ on a plane or tube, and $-1/a^2$ on the hyperbolic plane. The number $K$ is the Gaussian curvature. For constant $K$ the equation is exact when $D$ is measured along the curve that stays a fixed distance $s$ from the starting line; where $K$ varies, it holds for small gaps. For nearby lines on a surface, the parallel postulate holds in exactly those cases where $K = 0$ at every point of the strip of surface between the two lines. The strip matters, and not only the two lines themselves: on a flat cone, two lines that start parallel on opposite sides of the tip cross, although $K = 0$ along each of them. All of that cone's curvature sits at the tip, and the tip lies in the strip between the two lines.

**Takeaway:** On a sphere the gap shrinks as the cosine of distance over radius; on any surface, for small gaps, its second derivative is minus the Gaussian curvature times the gap.

*What this leaves out:* Measures the gap along the curve at a fixed distance from the starting line; for small gaps this agrees with the shortest distance to leading order.

### 5. Measuring whether space obeys it · working · operational

*How is the parallel postulate tested on Earth's surface and across the universe?*

"How fast the gap closes" predicts that on a sphere the gap between lines that start parallel shrinks as $D_0\cos(s/a)$. Earth's surface shows this. Lines of longitude are geodesics that leave the equator at right angles, so they start parallel. On the WGS 84 ellipsoid, the model of Earth's shape fitted to satellite measurements, one degree of longitude spans 111.32 km along the equator and 55.80 km along the circle at latitude $60^\circ$. The ratio is 0.501, against $\cos 60^\circ = 0.500$ for a perfect sphere; the small excess comes from Earth's flattening.

Distant space cannot be walked, so astronomers follow light. In a universe that is the same everywhere and in every direction, a light ray's path, traced through the space of observers moving with the expansion, is a geodesic of that space. The cosmic microwave background carries a ruler: the distance sound travelled in the early plasma before the light was released, about 144 megaparsecs in today's units, with 1 Mpc $= 3.086\times10^{22}$ m. That ruler spans $0.5965^\circ$ on the sky. If space had positive curvature, rays leaving us would draw together again and the ruler would look larger than in flat space; negative curvature would make it look smaller.

Combining the microwave background data with the same ruler seen in the clustering of galaxies gives the curvature parameter $\Omega_K = 0.001 \pm 0.002$, where zero means flat spatial slices and positive values mean negative curvature. On the largest scales measured, space at one cosmic time obeys the parallel postulate within the errors. Spacetime is still curved: flat slices of an expanding universe do not make spacetime flat.

**Takeaway:** Lines of longitude close as the sphere law predicts, while light across the universe shows space at one cosmic time obeying the postulate within the errors.

*What this leaves out:* Treats the universe as the same everywhere and in every direction, and quotes curvature for space at one cosmic time as seen by observers moving with the expansion.

### 6. Axioms, equivalents and models · formal · structure

*What exactly does the postulate assert, what is it equivalent to, and why is it independent of the other axioms?*

"The rule nobody could prove" ended with a drawing of Lobachevsky and Bolyai's geometry inside a flat disc, and "How fast the gap closes" ended with the gap law. Both can be made precise.

Axioms. A Hilbert plane satisfies Hilbert's axioms of incidence, betweenness and congruence. Here neutral geometry means a Hilbert plane that also satisfies the Archimedean axiom, with nothing assumed about parallels. Playfair's axiom states that for every line $\ell$ and point $P \notin \ell$, at most one line through $P$ misses $\ell$. At least one always exists. The exterior angle theorem, proved without any parallel axiom, says an exterior angle of a triangle exceeds each remote interior angle. So two lines making equal alternate angles with a transversal cannot meet; in particular, lines with a common perpendicular never meet.

Equivalents. In neutral geometry, each of the following is equivalent to Playfair's axiom and to Euclid's fifth postulate: some triangle has angle sum $\pi$; every triangle has angle sum $\pi$; a rectangle exists; two lines with a common perpendicular are equidistant. Sketch: a triangle with angle sum $\pi$ yields a rectangle, and so does equidistance: the common perpendicular, a second perpendicular to one line and the equal gap form a Lambert quadrilateral whose fourth angle must then be right. One rectangle yields arbitrarily large ones by the Archimedean axiom, so every right triangle, and hence every triangle, has angle sum $\pi$. Legendre's construction, again using the Archimedean axiom, shows that angle sum $\pi$ allows only one parallel. The converses follow from the fifth postulate by Euclid's own arguments.

Independence. The Poincaré disk has points $x^2 + y^2 < 1$, lines that are diameters or circular arcs meeting the boundary at right angles, and metric $ds^2 = 4(dx^2 + dy^2)/(1 - x^2 - y^2)^2$ of constant curvature $K = -1$. It satisfies every neutral axiom, yet infinitely many lines through a point miss a given line. It is built from Euclidean objects, so deriving Playfair's axiom from the neutral axioms would make Euclidean geometry inconsistent: the postulate is independent, relative to that consistency.

Riemannian reading. On a surface, let lines be geodesics. A normal Jacobi field $fN$ along a unit-speed geodesic obeys $f'' + Kf = 0$, and geodesics leaving a common geodesic orthogonally give $f'(0) = 0$. Nearby geodesics with a common perpendicular are therefore equidistant exactly where $K = 0$.

Limits. The sphere is not a neutral geometry: antipodal points lie on infinitely many great circles, and closed lines break betweenness. The exterior angle theorem fails there, and the octant triangle has three right angles. A cylinder has $K = 0$ and equidistant common perpendiculars, yet two of its points can be joined by many geodesics. On a flat cone, geodesics that start parallel on opposite sides of the tip cross, although $K = 0$ along both: the Jacobi test is local.

**Takeaway:** Given the other neutral axioms, the postulate is equivalent to angle sum pi or to equidistant common perpendiculars, and the hyperbolic plane proves it independent.

*What this leaves out:* Assumes the Archimedean axiom throughout; without it, some of these equivalences fail.

## Key equations

### Gap between lines that start parallel on a sphere · working

$$
D(s) = D_0\cos(s/a)
$$

On a sphere, the gap between two great circles that start parallel shrinks as the cosine of the distance run over the radius, vanishing a quarter circumference along.

| Symbol | Meaning | Say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $D(s)$ | gap measured along the circle that crosses both lines at right angles | the gap |
| $D_0$ | starting gap along the common line | the starting gap |
| $s$ | distance run along each line | the distance run |
| $a$ | radius of the sphere | the radius |

**Holds when:** Two great circles leaving a common great circle at right angles, toward the same side; $0 \le s \le \pi a/2$, with $s/a$ in radians.  
**Say it:** “The gap equals the starting gap times the cosine of the distance run divided by the radius.”

### Gap law for lines that start parallel · working

$$
\frac{d^2D}{ds^2} = -K\,D
$$

The gap between lines that start parallel bends toward zero, stays constant, or grows as the Gaussian curvature is positive, zero, or negative.

| Symbol | Meaning | Say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $D$ | gap between the two lines | the gap |
| $s$ | distance run along each line | the distance run |
| $K$ | Gaussian curvature: $1/a^2$ on a sphere, $0$ on a plane or tube, $-1/a^2$ on the hyperbolic plane | the Gaussian curvature |

**Holds when:** Geodesics leaving a common geodesic at right angles, so $D'(0) = 0$. Exact for constant $K$ with $D$ measured along the curve at fixed distance $s$ from the common geodesic; where $K$ varies, valid to first order in the gap. The strip of surface between the two lines must also be free of cone tips, single points where curvature is concentrated.  
**Say it:** “The second derivative of the gap equals minus the Gaussian curvature times the gap.”

## Derivations

### The gap on a sphere · working

**Goal:** Show that two great circles leaving the equator at right angles, a distance $D_0$ apart, are separated by $D_0\cos(s/a)$ after each has run a distance $s$.

1. Place the sphere's centre at the origin with the equator in the $xy$-plane. The point at latitude $\lambda$ and longitude $\phi$ is $a(\cos\lambda\cos\phi,\ \cos\lambda\sin\phi,\ \sin\lambda)$.
2. A great circle leaving the equator at right angles at longitude $\phi$ is the meridian of fixed $\phi$. Running a distance $s$ along it raises the latitude to $\lambda = s/a$ radians.
3. Points at latitude $\lambda$ lie on a circle of radius $a\cos\lambda$ about the $z$-axis, and this circle crosses every meridian at right angles.
4. Two meridians whose longitudes differ by $\Delta\phi$ cut from that circle an arc of length $a\cos\lambda\,\Delta\phi$.
5. At the equator the arc is $D_0 = a\,\Delta\phi$. Substituting $\lambda = s/a$ gives $D(s) = D_0\cos(s/a)$.

**Result:** $D(s) = D_0\cos(s/a)$ for $0 \le s \le \pi a/2$; it vanishes at $s = \pi a/2$ for every $D_0$.

## Worked examples

### Two lines of longitude one degree apart · working

**Problem:** Two lines of longitude one degree apart leave the equator of a sphere of radius 6371 km. Find their starting gap, the distance along them at which the gap has halved, and the distance at which they meet.

1. One degree is $\pi/180$ rad, so $D_0 = (6371\ \text{km})(\pi/180) = 111.19$ km.
2. The gap halves when $\cos(s/a) = 1/2$, so $s/a = \pi/3$ and $s = (6371\ \text{km})(\pi/3) = 6672$ km, at latitude $60^\circ$.
3. The lines meet when $\cos(s/a) = 0$, at $s = \pi a/2 = 10\,008$ km.

**Answer:** $D_0 = 111.2$ km; the gap halves after 6672 km; the lines meet after 10 008 km.

**Takeaway:** The gap halves only two thirds of the way to the meeting point, because the closing starts slowly and speeds up.

## Problems

### `surveyors-lose-a-millimetre` · working · difficulty 2 · estimate

Two surveyors lay out straight lines across a level plain. The lines leave a common straight line at right angles, 100 m apart. Treat Earth as a sphere of radius 6371 km and ignore hills. How far must the lines run before their gap has shrunk by 1 mm?

**Hints**

1. For small $s/a$, use $\cos x \approx 1 - x^2/2$.
2. Set the loss $D_0s^2/2a^2$ equal to 1 mm and solve for $s$.

**Answer:** About 28.5 km.

**Must contain:** The loss of gap is about the starting gap times s squared over two a squared; s equals a times the square root of twice the loss over the starting gap; About 28.5 kilometres

**Numeric:** distance run = 28.49 km (magnitude, ±3%)

**Solution**

1. The gap is $D = D_0\cos(s/a) \approx D_0(1 - s^2/2a^2)$, so the loss is $D_0s^2/2a^2$.
2. Setting the loss to $10^{-3}$ m with $D_0 = 100$ m gives $s^2/a^2 = 2\times10^{-5}$.
3. So $s = a\sqrt{2\times10^{-5}} = (6371\ \text{km})(4.472\times10^{-3}) = 28.5$ km.
4. The exact condition $\cos(s/a) = 1 - 10^{-5}$ gives the same 28.49 km.

### `common-perpendiculars-never-meet` · formal · difficulty 3 · proof

In neutral geometry, prove that two distinct lines $m$ and $n$ perpendicular to a common line $\ell$ never meet. Explain why great circles perpendicular to the equator escape this theorem, and give the separation of such lines in the hyperbolic plane of curvature $-1/a^2$.

**Hints**

1. Suppose they meet at $P$ and look at the triangle with two vertices on $\ell$.
2. Which neutral theorem compares an exterior angle of a triangle with its remote interior angles?
3. Which neutral axioms does the sphere violate?

**Answer:** A meeting point $P$ would give a triangle $ABP$ whose exterior angle at $A$ equals the remote interior angle at $B$, both right angles, contradicting the exterior angle theorem. The sphere is not a neutral geometry, so the theorem does not apply there. In the hyperbolic plane the lines never meet, and their separation grows as $D_0\cosh(s/a)$.

**Must contain:** Assume a meeting point and form a triangle with two right angles; The exterior angle theorem is neutral and forbids this; The sphere violates incidence and betweenness, so the theorem fails there; In the hyperbolic plane the lines diverge as D zero cosh of s over a

**Solution**

1. Let $m \perp \ell$ at $A$ and $n \perp \ell$ at $B \neq A$, and suppose $m$ and $n$ meet at $P$. Then $P \notin \ell$, since $m$ meets $\ell$ only at $A$ and $n$ only at $B$.
2. Extend $BA$ beyond $A$ to a point $C$. The exterior angle $\angle CAP$ is supplementary to the right angle $\angle BAP$, so it is a right angle, equal to the interior angle $\angle ABP$.
3. The exterior angle theorem, proved without any parallel axiom, says $\angle CAP$ exceeds $\angle ABP$. This contradiction shows that $m$ and $n$ do not meet.
4. The theorem's proof doubles a median and needs the new endpoint to lie inside the exterior angle. On the sphere lines are closed and antipodal points lie on many lines, so the step fails, and the octant triangle has right angles at both equator corners.
5. The hyperbolic plane satisfies the neutral axioms, so the lines never meet. The gap law with $K = -1/a^2$ and $D'(0) = 0$ gives $D = D_0\cosh(s/a)$, which grows without bound. What fails there is uniqueness: many lines through a point miss a given line.

## Observations

## Teaching arc

1. **Pose the walkers question** (entry). Ask whether two friends who set off side by side and walk straight keep their gap, first on flat ground and then on a giant ball. *Why:* The prediction brings out the everyday belief that such paths never close. *Predict:* On a giant ball, will two friends who start side by side and walk straight stay the same distance apart? *Visual:* [[two-walkers-set-off-side-by-side]] *Uses:* `ways_in/two-friends-on-a-ball`, `checks/pairs-near-and-far`
2. **Put straightness before the gap** (entry). Use running lanes to show that a steady gap proves nothing unless both paths are straight. *Why:* It blocks the railway-track picture of parallel lines. *Uses:* `ways_in/a-steady-gap-is-not-the-test`, `checks/runners-in-lanes`
3. **Tell the two-thousand-year puzzle** (entry). Explain why the rule could not be proved, and what that makes it. *Why:* It turns a school rule into a question about the world. *Uses:* `ways_in/the-rule-nobody-could-prove`, `checks/must-be-pure-logic`
4. **Put numbers on the gap and measure it** (working). Derive the cosine law, work the halfway check, then show longitude spacing and the cosmic ruler. *Why:* The law explains the slow start, and the measurements show the test in use. *Predict:* Halfway to the meeting point, is half the gap gone? *Visual:* [[two-walkers-set-off-side-by-side]] *Uses:* `ways_in/how-fast-the-gap-closes`, `checks/ships-halfway-to-meeting`, `ways_in/measuring-whether-space-obeys-it`
5. **Axioms and models** (formal). State Playfair's axiom and its equivalents, then show why the hyperbolic plane and not the sphere proves independence. *Why:* It separates what the postulate asserts from the everyday pictures of it. *Uses:* `ways_in/axioms-equivalents-and-models`, `checks/sphere-is-not-the-model`

## Misconceptions

### “If the two friends start closer together, they will meet sooner.” · entry · `closer-start-meets-sooner`

- **Why it is tempting:** People who are already close seem to have less gap left to close.
- **What is true:** Every straight line that leaves the equator at a right angle, toward the North Pole, passes through the North Pole. So every pair meets a quarter of the way around the ball, whatever its gap.
- **Exposed by:** `checks/pairs-near-and-far`

### “Paths that stay the same distance apart, like railway tracks, show that parallel lines stay parallel.” · entry · `same-gap-means-parallel`

- **Why it is tempting:** Railway tracks and running lanes are the everyday picture of parallel lines.
- **What is true:** A steady gap can be kept by steering, and a steered path is not a straight line. The postulate is tested only when both paths are straight.
- **Exposed by:** `checks/runners-in-lanes`

### “Parallel lines staying parallel is pure logic, so it must hold in every space.” · entry · `postulate-is-just-logic`

- **Why it is tempting:** The rule feels as certain as every other fact of school geometry.
- **What is true:** Mathematicians worked out a geometry that keeps Euclid's other rules, but in which straight lines that start parallel spread apart. That geometry stays free of contradiction as long as flat geometry does, so the rule cannot be proved from those rules; on a ball the rule fails outright as well.
- **Exposed by:** `checks/must-be-pure-logic`

### “On a sphere the gap closes at a steady rate, so halfway to the meeting point half of it is gone.” · working · `gap-closes-steadily`

- **Why it is tempting:** Two lines drawn converging on flat paper close at a steady rate.
- **What is true:** The gap follows a cosine, starting with zero rate of closing and speeding up. Halfway along, about 71 percent of it remains.
- **Exposed by:** `checks/ships-halfway-to-meeting`

### “Spherical geometry, where every two lines meet, is the model that proves the parallel postulate independent.” · formal · `sphere-shows-independence`

- **Why it is tempting:** The sphere is the most familiar geometry in which the postulate fails.
- **What is true:** The sphere violates other neutral axioms, so it cannot show independence. The hyperbolic plane satisfies all the others and fails only the postulate.
- **Exposed by:** `checks/sphere-is-not-the-model`

### “In non-Euclidean geometry, lines that start parallel eventually meet.” · formal · `non-euclidean-means-meeting`

- **Why it is tempting:** Spherical examples dominate popular accounts of non-Euclidean geometry.
- **What is true:** In hyperbolic geometry lines with a common perpendicular never meet; they spread apart. What fails is the uniqueness of parallels through a point.
- **Exposed by:** `checks/do-hyperbolic-parallels-meet`

## Checks

1. **Entry · predict** `checks/pairs-near-and-far`. On a smooth ball the size of Earth, two pairs of friends stand on the equator. One pair stands 1 metre apart, and the other stands 500 kilometres apart. In each pair, both friends face the North Pole, at a right angle to the equator, and walk straight. Which pair walks farther before its two friends meet?
   - **Hints:** Where does each friend's straight line reach, if it keeps going toward the North Pole?
   - **Answer:** Neither pair: both walk the same distance, about 10,000 kilometres. Each friend's path is a straight line that leaves the equator at a right angle, toward the North Pole. Every such line passes through the North Pole. So both pairs meet there, a quarter of the way around the ball, whatever their starting gap.
   - **Must contain:** Both pairs walk the same distance, about 10,000 kilometres; Every straight line leaving the equator at a right angle, toward the North Pole, passes through the North Pole; Both pairs meet a quarter of the way around the ball
   - **Targets:** `closer-start-meets-sooner`
   - **Visual:** [[two-walkers-set-off-side-by-side]]
2. **Entry · evaluate-claim** `checks/runners-in-lanes`. Two runners in neighbouring lanes of a running track stay 1.2 metres apart all the way around. A classmate says this proves that straight lines that start parallel stay parallel. Is the classmate right?
   - **Hints:** Do the runners ever steer?
   - **Answer:** No. On each curved end of the track, both runners keep steering toward the grass in the middle. So neither path is a straight line all the way around. The parallel postulate is a rule about straight lines. A gap kept by steering therefore says nothing about it.
   - **Must contain:** No; The runners steer on the curved ends; The postulate is about straight lines only
   - **Targets:** `same-gap-means-parallel`
3. **Entry · evaluate-claim** `checks/must-be-pure-logic`. A classmate says: "Straight lines that start parallel must stay parallel. That is pure logic, so it is true in every space." Is the classmate right?
   - **Hints:** What happened when mathematicians tried to prove the rule? / What happens to straight lines that start parallel on a ball?
   - **Answer:** No. For about two thousand years, mathematicians tried to prove the rule from Euclid's other starting rules, and every attempt failed. Lobachevsky and Bolyai then worked out a geometry that keeps the other rules, but in which such lines spread apart. That geometry can be drawn inside a flat disc, so any contradiction in it would also be a contradiction in ordinary flat geometry. So logic alone cannot settle the rule. The rule also fails on a ball, so it is not true in every space. Whether the space we live in obeys it has to be measured.
   - **Must contain:** No; The rule cannot be proved from the other starting rules; A geometry that keeps the other starting rules but makes such lines spread apart can be drawn in a flat disc, so it stays free of contradiction as long as flat geometry does; The rule fails on a ball, so it is not true in every space, and whether the space we live in obeys it must be measured
   - **Targets:** `postulate-is-just-logic`
4. **Working · numeric** `checks/ships-halfway-to-meeting`. Two ships start on the equator 200 km apart and sail along geodesics that leave it at right angles, toward the same side. Treat Earth as a sphere of radius 6371 km. What is their gap when each has sailed half the distance to their meeting point? Is it half the starting gap?
   - **Hints:** Where do they meet? Use $D = D_0\cos(s/a)$.
   - **Answer:** They meet after $\pi a/2$, so halfway is $s = \pi a/4 = 5004$ km. The gap is $200\cos(\pi/4) = 141.4$ km, not 100 km, because the gap closes slowly at first and faster later.
   - **Must contain:** Halfway is pi a over four, about 5004 kilometres; The gap is 200 cos 45 degrees, about 141 kilometres; Not half, because the closing speeds up
   - **Numeric:** gap = 141.4 km (magnitude, ±1%); distance sailed = 5004 km (magnitude, ±1%)
   - **Targets:** `gap-closes-steadily`
5. **Formal · explain** `checks/sphere-is-not-the-model`. Great circles on a sphere have no parallels at all. Why does this not prove that the parallel postulate is independent of the other axioms of neutral geometry? Which model does, and what exactly does it establish?
   - **Hints:** Through two antipodal points, how many great circles pass?
   - **Answer:** An independence proof needs a model of all the other axioms in which Playfair's axiom fails. The sphere is no such model: antipodal points lie on infinitely many great circles, and closed lines break betweenness. It even contradicts a neutral theorem, since neutral geometry always has at least one parallel through a point off a line. The Poincaré disk, or the Beltrami–Klein disk, satisfies every neutral axiom with infinitely many parallels through a point. Because it is built from Euclidean objects, a derivation of the postulate from the neutral axioms would make Euclidean geometry inconsistent. So the postulate is independent, relative to the consistency of Euclidean geometry.
   - **Must contain:** Independence needs a model of all other axioms; The sphere violates incidence and betweenness; The Poincaré or Beltrami–Klein disk is a neutral model with many parallels; Independence is relative to the consistency of Euclidean geometry
   - **Targets:** `sphere-shows-independence`
6. **Formal · evaluate-claim** `checks/do-hyperbolic-parallels-meet`. Evaluate the claim: "In non-Euclidean geometry, lines that start parallel eventually meet."
   - **Hints:** Which neutral theorem forbids a triangle with two right angles?
   - **Answer:** False for hyperbolic geometry, the geometry that keeps every other neutral axiom. Lines with a common perpendicular never meet in any neutral geometry, by the exterior angle theorem. In the hyperbolic plane of curvature $-1/a^2$ they spread apart as $D_0\cosh(s/a)$. What fails there is uniqueness: infinitely many lines through a point miss a given line. Lines that start parallel do meet on the sphere, but the sphere is not a neutral geometry at all.
   - **Must contain:** False for hyperbolic geometry; Common perpendiculars never meet in neutral geometry; In the hyperbolic plane they diverge; The sphere, where they meet, is not neutral
   - **Targets:** `non-euclidean-means-meeting`

## Tutor moves

**Open with**

- Picture two friends standing ten metres apart on the equator of a giant smooth ball, both facing the North Pole. Both walk without ever steering left or right. Will they stay the same distance apart? *(prediction)*

**If the learner is stuck**

- *The learner cannot picture why the friends' paths come together.* → Use a globe or an orange: trace two neighbouring lines from the equator to the North Pole and ask where they end. *Uses:* `ways_in/two-friends-on-a-ball`

**Common questions**

- *Does this have anything to do with gravity?* (entry) Yes. General relativity, today's theory of gravity, describes gravity as the curving of space and time. In general relativity, a freely falling stone follows the straightest possible path through space and time, like a walker who never steers. Drop two stones side by side from a tall tower. As they fall, they drift very slightly toward each other, because both head for the centre of Earth. Stones a metre apart, dropped a hundred metres, end up only about a sixtieth of a millimetre closer. General relativity treats that drift like the walkers' shrinking gap on a ball, as a sign of curving. The comparison is about how the gaps behave, not a claim that space and time are shaped like a ball. *Uses:* `ways_in/two-friends-on-a-ball`

**Switching levels**

- To working when: asks how fast the gap closes; wants a formula or a number for Earth. Derive the cosine law and work the halfway check. *Uses:* `ways_in/how-fast-the-gap-closes`, `checks/ships-halfway-to-meeting`
- To formal when: asks what an axiom system is; asks whether non-Euclidean geometry is consistent. State Playfair's axiom and its equivalents, then the disk model and independence. *Uses:* `ways_in/axioms-equivalents-and-models`, `checks/sphere-is-not-the-model`

## History

- **Euclid (-300).** Built plane geometry on five postulates; the fifth is equivalent, given the rest, to the parallel postulate. Euclid (-300), *Elements*
- **Nikolai Lobachevsky (1829).** Published a systematic geometry in which many lines through a point miss a given line. Nikolai I. Lobachevsky (1829), *O nachalakh geometrii (On the principles of geometry)*, Kazanskii Vestnik, in parts, 1829–1830
- **János Bolyai (1832).** Independently developed the same geometry. János Bolyai (1832), *Appendix, scientiam spatii absolute veram exhibens*, Appendix to Farkas Bolyai, Tentamen, Maros-Vásárhely
- **Eugenio Beltrami (1868).** Interpreted hyperbolic plane geometry on surfaces of constant negative curvature and in a disc, both built from Euclidean objects, which showed it consistent if Euclidean geometry is. Eugenio Beltrami (1868), *Saggio di interpretazione della geometria non-euclidea*, Giornale di Matematiche 6, 284–312
