# Causal structure, asymptotics and singularities

Global, whole-spacetime methods for arbitrary Lorentzian spacetimes. Causal curves, time orientation, causal and chronological futures and pasts, closed timelike curves and spacetime topology; time functions, domains of dependence, Cauchy surfaces and horizons, global hyperbolicity and existence of maximal curves. Conformal compactification and Penrose diagrams as a technique, with Minkowski space as the model, plus conformal infinity (i+, i-, i0, null infinity) and asymptotic flatness. Horizons as causal boundaries in general, trapped and marginally trapped surfaces. Geodesic congruences with expansion, shear and rotation, the timelike and null Raychaudhuri equations, convergence conditions, focusing and caustics. Singularities as geodesic incompleteness and inextendibility, curvature, spacelike, timelike and naked singularities and their classification (including BKL), the Penrose, Hawking and Hawking-Penrose singularity theorems, cosmic censorship, and exotic causal geometries (traversable wormholes, warp drives). Diagrams and horizons of named spacetimes, energy-condition definitions, the Cauchy problem and asymptotic mass definitions live in their own domains.

| Concept | Tier | Summary | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [[global-causal-structure]] Global causal structure of spacetime | advanced | In a curved spacetime the metric fixes a light cone at every event, and the pattern of these cones, together with a choice of future, decides which events can influence which across the whole manifold. Global methods study this pattern and properties of the manifold as a whole, rather than tensor equations at a point, and they are the tools behind horizons, Cauchy surfaces and singularity theorems. | — |
| [[time-orientability]] Time orientability | advanced | A time orientation is a choice of which half of each light cone counts as the future; a spacetime is time-orientable if that choice can be made continuously over the whole manifold. Then every timelike or null vector is consistently future- or past-pointing, which is needed before causal futures and pasts make sense globally. | — |
| [[causal-curve]] Causal curve | advanced | A causal curve is a curve whose tangent is everywhere timelike or null and future-pointing, the possible history of a signal or particle. It is inextendible when it has no endpoint in the spacetime in either direction, the notion used to define Cauchy surfaces and domains of dependence. | — |
| [[causal-future-and-past]] Causal and chronological future and past | advanced | The causal future J+(p) of an event is every event reachable from p along a future causal curve, and the causal past J-(p) is every event from which p can be reached; the chronological versions I+ and I- use timelike curves only and sit inside J+ and J-. These sets, taken for events, world lines or whole regions, are the building blocks of horizons and domains of dependence. | — |
| [[closed-timelike-curve]] Closed timelike curve | advanced | A closed timelike curve is a future-directed timelike world line that returns to an event it already passed through, so an observer always moving into their local future meets their own past. Such curves violate causality and are generally regarded as unphysical, yet exact solutions contain them, for instance near the ring singularity of Kerr and in anti-de Sitter space before unwrapping its time. | — |
| [[topology-of-spacetime]] Topology of spacetime | advanced | The topology of a spacetime is the set of its properties that survive continuous deformation: whether spatial sections are closed, how regions are glued together, whether there are handles. The field equations are local and do not fix it, so spacetimes with identical local geometry can differ globally. | — |
| [[time-function]] Time function | advanced | A time function is a scalar field on spacetime whose gradient is everywhere timelike, so it increases along every future causal curve and its level sets are spacelike slices. Its existence rules out closed timelike curves and gives a global notion of successive moments. | — |
| [[domain-of-dependence]] Domain of dependence | advanced | The future domain of dependence of a set S is the set of events every past-inextendible causal curve through which must cross S, and similarly for the past; together they form the region whose physics is completely fixed by data on S. In 1+1 Minkowski space the future domain of an interval is the triangle rising above it at 45 degrees. | — |
| [[cauchy-surface]] Cauchy surface | advanced | A Cauchy surface is a spacelike (more generally achronal) hypersurface met exactly once by every inextendible causal curve, so its domain of dependence is the whole spacetime and data on it determine everything. Not every spacelike slice is Cauchy: an achronal surface can miss curves that escape past its edge. | — |
| [[global-hyperbolicity]] Global hyperbolicity | advanced | A spacetime is globally hyperbolic if it possesses a Cauchy surface. Such spacetimes are the arena for deterministic evolution: wave equations have unique global solutions, the manifold splits as a line of time times a 3-manifold, and any two causally related events are joined by a longest causal curve. | — |
| [[cauchy-horizon]] Cauchy horizon | advanced | A Cauchy horizon is the null boundary of the domain of dependence of a surface, beyond which the future is no longer fixed by the data on that surface. It differs from an event horizon: it marks the end of predictability rather than the end of escape to infinity. | — |
| [[maximal-globally-hyperbolic-development]] Maximal globally hyperbolic development | frontier | For vacuum initial data on a slice, there is a unique (up to isometry) largest globally hyperbolic spacetime in which that slice is a Cauchy surface carrying the data, and every other such development embeds into it. It is the global counterpart of local existence and uniqueness for the Einstein equations. | — |
| [[avez-seifert-theorem]] Existence of maximal causal curves (Avez-Seifert theorem) | advanced | In a globally hyperbolic spacetime any event in the domain of dependence of a Cauchy surface is joined to that surface by a causal curve of greatest proper time, and that curve is a timelike geodesic meeting the surface orthogonally with no focal point in between. The technical input is that the space of causal curves between them is compact, so proper time attains its maximum. | — |
| [[asymptotic-flatness]] Asymptotic flatness | core | A spacetime is asymptotically flat if, far from an isolated source, its metric approaches the Minkowski metric (with specified fall-off of the corrections) so curvature vanishes at large distance. This boundary condition fixes integration constants in solutions, lets distant observers and total mass be defined, and is the setting for event horizons and outgoing radiation. | — |
| [[conformal-compactification]] Conformal compactification | advanced | Conformal compactification brings infinity to a finite place: choose coordinates (often arctan of null coordinates) with finite range, then multiply the physical metric by a conformal factor that vanishes at infinity, giving an unphysical metric that is regular on the added boundary. Because conformal rescaling preserves light cones, the compactified picture shows the true causal structure while distorting distances. | — |
| [[penrose-diagram]] Penrose diagram | advanced | A Penrose diagram is a two-dimensional picture of a conformally compactified spacetime, with angular directions suppressed, in which radial light rays run at 45 degrees and the entire spacetime, including its infinities and singularities, fits in a finite figure. Distances are distorted but which events can signal which can be read off by eye. | — |
| [[penrose-diagram-of-minkowski-spacetime]] Penrose diagram of Minkowski spacetime | advanced | Compactifying flat spacetime with arctan null coordinates maps it onto a bounded diamond (a triangle when only r >= 0 is drawn) inside the Einstein static cylinder. Its vertices are future and past timelike infinity and spatial infinity, its null edges are future and past null infinity, and it is the template for reading every other Penrose diagram. | — |
| [[conformal-infinity]] Conformal infinity | advanced | Conformal infinity is the boundary added to a spacetime by conformal compactification, split according to which kind of curve ends there: timelike curves at i+ and i-, light rays on the null infinities, spacelike curves at spatial infinity. Its causal character is informative: null in Minkowski space, spacelike in de Sitter (which is why de Sitter observers have horizons), timelike in anti-de Sitter (which behaves like a box light can reach). | — |
| [[future-and-past-null-infinity]] Future and past null infinity | advanced | Future null infinity is the boundary where outgoing light rays end as their affine parameter grows without bound, and past null infinity is where incoming rays begin. In an asymptotically flat spacetime these are null surfaces, the place where outgoing radiation is read off and the reference for defining an event horizon. | — |
| [[future-and-past-timelike-infinity]] Future and past timelike infinity | advanced | Future timelike infinity i+ is the idealised endpoint of timelike curves as proper time runs to plus infinity, and past timelike infinity i- is their idealised origin. In the Minkowski Penrose diagram each is a single vertex, the top and bottom corners. | — |
| [[spatial-infinity]] Spatial infinity | advanced | Spatial infinity i0 is the limit reached by going to infinite distance along spacelike curves at finite time; in compactified Minkowski space all constant-time slices end at this single point. It is causally disconnected from every observer. | — |
| [[observer-dependent-horizon]] Observer-dependent horizon | advanced | A horizon is the boundary of the causal past (or future) of an observer's world line: the edge of the region that observer can ever see (or influence). For accelerated observers and in cosmology this boundary depends on the chosen world line and a different observer has a different one, unlike the absolute event horizon of a black hole defined relative to all of future null infinity. | — |
| [[trapped-surface]] Trapped surface | advanced | A closed trapped surface is a compact spacelike 2-surface for which both families of future-directed light rays leaving it orthogonally, outgoing and ingoing, converge, so both wavefronts have less area than the surface. Every round sphere inside the Schwarzschild radius is trapped; the existence of such a surface is the input of Penrose's singularity theorem. | — |
| [[apparent-horizon]] Apparent horizon | advanced | An apparent horizon is the outer boundary of trapped surfaces on a given time slice, made of marginally trapped 2-surfaces whose outgoing light rays momentarily neither expand nor contract. Unlike the event horizon it can be located from data on one slice without knowing the future, depends on the slicing, and lies inside or on the event horizon. | — |
| [[geodesic-congruence]] Geodesic congruence | advanced | A geodesic congruence is a family of geodesics filling a region with exactly one through each event, so its tangents form a smooth velocity field; a cloud of freely falling dust is the physical picture. Studying how neighbouring members spread, shear and twist is the starting point for focusing arguments. | — |
| [[expansion-scalar]] Expansion (convergence) of a congruence | advanced | The expansion theta of a timelike congruence is the divergence of its unit tangent field and equals the fractional rate of change of the volume of a small comoving blob per unit proper time; GA uses its negative, the convergence c. Negative expansion means neighbouring world lines are drawing together. | — |
| [[expansion-shear-and-rotation]] Expansion, shear and rotation decomposition | advanced | The velocity gradient of a timelike geodesic congruence, projected orthogonal to the flow, splits into a trace (expansion: isotropic change of volume), a symmetric trace-free part (shear: change of shape at fixed volume) and an antisymmetric part (rotation: twisting of neighbouring world lines). Acting on a connecting vector it gives how that separation changes along the flow. | — |
| [[hypersurface-orthogonal-geodesic-congruence]] Hypersurface-orthogonal geodesic congruence | advanced | Launching timelike geodesics orthogonally from a spacelike hypersurface and labelling events by proper time along them gives a scalar whose gradient is the congruence's unit tangent. Such a gradient flow stays orthogonal to the level surfaces and has zero rotation, removing the only term in the Raychaudhuri equation that resists focusing. | — |
| [[raychaudhuri-equation]] Raychaudhuri equation | advanced | Along a timelike geodesic congruence the expansion obeys d theta/d tau = -theta^2/3 - sigma^2 + omega^2 - R_ab u^a u^b. Expansion itself, shear, and positive Ricci curvature along the flow all drive convergence, while only rotation opposes it; this is the precise sense in which gravity attracts. | — |
| [[timelike-convergence-condition]] Timelike convergence condition | advanced | The timelike convergence condition requires R_ab V^a V^b >= 0 for every timelike (or causal) vector, so the Ricci term in the Raychaudhuri equation always helps focusing. Through the Einstein equations with zero cosmological constant it is equivalent to the strong energy condition on matter. | — |
| [[focusing-theorem]] Focusing theorem | advanced | For a hypersurface-orthogonal timelike geodesic congruence obeying the timelike convergence condition, the Raychaudhuri equation gives d theta/d tau <= -theta^2/3. So a congruence that is converging somewhere, theta_0 < 0, must reach infinite convergence (a focal point) within proper time 3/\|theta_0\|, provided the geodesics extend that far. | — |
| [[caustic]] Caustic | advanced | A caustic is where neighbouring rays or geodesics of a congruence cross, so the congruence description (and any coordinates built from it) breaks down while spacetime itself stays perfectly regular. Focusing to a caustic is therefore not yet a singularity; even straight lines in flat spacetime cross and continue. | — |
| [[null-geodesic-congruence]] Null geodesic congruence | advanced | A null geodesic congruence is a family of light rays filling a region, one through each event, with affinely parametrized tangent l. Its expansion measures the fractional rate at which the cross-sectional area of a thin beam grows per unit affine parameter; the signs of the outgoing and ingoing expansions of a 2-surface decide whether it is trapped. | — |
| [[null-raychaudhuri-equation]] Null Raychaudhuri equation | advanced | Along an affinely parametrized null geodesic congruence the expansion obeys d theta/d lambda = -theta^2/2 - sigma^2 + omega^2 - R_ab l^a l^b. With the null convergence condition and zero twist, light beams that start converging must focus in finite affine parameter, the engine of Penrose's theorem. | — |
| [[null-convergence-condition]] Null convergence condition | advanced | The null convergence condition requires R_ab k^a k^b >= 0 for every null vector k, guaranteeing that light-ray congruences are focused rather than defocused by curvature. Via the Einstein equations it follows from the null energy condition, is implied by the weak energy condition, and is unaffected by a cosmological constant. | — |
| [[ricci-focusing-versus-weyl-shear]] Ricci focusing versus Weyl shear | advanced | A thin bundle of light passing through matter, where R_ab k^a k^b is positive, is focused isotropically like a converging lens, enlarging a round image. Weyl curvature instead acts like an astigmatic lens, squeezing one transverse direction and stretching the other, so circles become ellipses without net area focusing at first order. | — |
| [[generic-condition]] Generic condition | frontier | The generic condition requires every causal geodesic to meet, somewhere along it, curvature with a non-zero tidal component transverse to its direction. It fails only in special, highly symmetric spacetimes and ensures focusing actually occurs, which is why it appears as a hypothesis of the Hawking-Penrose theorem. | — |
| [[geodesic-incompleteness]] Geodesic incompleteness | advanced | A spacetime is geodesically complete if every geodesic can be extended to arbitrary values of its affine parameter in both directions. If some timelike or null geodesic ends after finite proper time or affine parameter and the spacetime cannot be extended to continue it, the spacetime is incomplete, which is the standard modern definition of a singular spacetime. | — |
| [[extension-of-a-spacetime]] Extension of a spacetime | advanced | An extension embeds a spacetime as a proper part of a larger smooth spacetime into which its incomplete geodesics continue, usually by letting coordinates take values the old chart could not reach. A maximal (inextendible) spacetime admits none, so every geodesic either runs forever or ends at a genuine singularity. | — |
| [[spacetime-singularity]] Spacetime singularity | advanced | A spacetime singularity is not a point of spacetime but an edge where the smooth Lorentzian manifold description fails: observers or light rays reach it in finite proper time or affine parameter and cannot continue, and typically curvature grows without bound. Known physics makes no prediction there; unlike infinity, a singularity lies a finite proper time away. | — |
| [[curvature-singularity]] Curvature singularity | core | A curvature singularity is where scalar invariants built from the Riemann tensor, such as the Kretschmann scalar, diverge along curves reaching it in finite affine parameter, so tidal forces become unbounded and no change of coordinates can make the geometry regular. It contrasts with a coordinate singularity, which is an artefact of a bad chart. | — |
| [[spacelike-singularity]] Spacelike singularity | advanced | A spacelike singularity is a singular boundary lying at a moment rather than a place: it is to the future (or past) of every nearby event, so once inside the region it bounds it cannot be avoided, and in a Penrose diagram it is a horizontal edge. The Schwarzschild r = 0 and the big bang are the standard examples. | — |
| [[timelike-singularity]] Timelike singularity | advanced | A timelike singularity has a timelike locus, a place beside observers in space rather than a moment in their future, drawn as a vertical line in a Penrose diagram. Future-directed world lines are not forced into it and can steer around it, as near r = 0 inside the inner horizon of charged and rotating black holes. | — |
| [[classification-of-spacetime-singularities]] Classification of spacetime singularities | frontier | Singularities are classified by how curvature misbehaves along incomplete curves: scalar curvature singularities where invariants diverge, weaker non-scalar (whimper-type) ones where curvature is unbounded only in a parallelly propagated frame, and quasi-regular ones with bounded curvature. Anisotropic cosmological singularities are further described by their shape, a region collapsing to a line (cigar) or a sheet (pancake). | — |
| [[bkl-singularity]] BKL singularity | frontier | The Belinski-Khalatnikov-Lifshitz analysis suggests that a generic cosmological singularity is locally a chaotic sequence of Kasner-like epochs, each point contracting and expanding along different axes, far more complex than the isotropic Friedmann big bang. It motivates studying anisotropic models and remains a research topic. | — |
| [[naked-singularity]] Naked singularity | advanced | A naked singularity is one not hidden behind an event horizon, so causal signals from it can reach distant observers and influence the exterior. Super-extremal Reissner-Nordstrom (charge exceeding mass) and Kerr (spin exceeding mass) contain them, which would destroy predictability if such spacetimes formed. | — |
| [[cosmic-censorship]] Cosmic censorship | frontier | Cosmic censorship is Penrose's conjecture that physically reasonable evolution does not produce naked singularities. The weak form says singularities arising from regular asymptotically flat data stay hidden behind event horizons; the strong form says generic data have a maximal globally hyperbolic development that cannot be extended past a Cauchy horizon, so determinism survives. | — |
| [[singularity-theorem]] Singularity theorems | advanced | Singularity theorems show that a spacetime satisfying an initial or boundary condition (a trapped surface or everywhere-converging slice), a curvature condition (an energy condition via Einstein's equations) and a causality condition must contain incomplete causal geodesics. They prove something singular happens without symmetry assumptions but say nothing about where it is or whether curvature blows up. | — |
| [[hawking-singularity-theorem]] Hawking cosmological singularity theorem | advanced | If spacetime is globally hyperbolic, obeys the strong energy condition, and the future normals to a Cauchy surface converge everywhere at rate at least c_0 > 0, then no future timelike curve from that surface lasts longer than 3/c_0 in proper time. Run backwards for an expanding universe, every past-directed world line has finite length: a beginning without assuming homogeneity. | — |
| [[penrose-singularity-theorem]] Penrose singularity theorem | advanced | Penrose's 1965 theorem: a spacetime with a non-compact Cauchy surface, obeying the null convergence (null energy) condition and containing a closed trapped surface, has at least one incomplete null geodesic. It shows gravitational collapse leads to something singular without spherical symmetry, and underlies trapped surfaces lying inside event horizons. | — |
| [[hawking-penrose-singularity-theorem]] Hawking-Penrose singularity theorem | frontier | The 1970 Hawking-Penrose theorem states that a spacetime with no closed timelike curves, satisfying the timelike and null convergence conditions and the generic condition, and containing a closed trapped surface (or an analogous converging condition), is causal-geodesically incomplete. It combines timelike and null focusing in one result. | — |
| [[traversable-wormhole]] Traversable wormhole | frontier | A traversable wormhole is a hypothetical tunnel geometry that travellers could actually pass through, possibly linking distant regions or universes. Unlike the Einstein-Rosen bridge, which pinches off too fast, known traversable constructions need matter of negative energy density, violating the standard energy conditions. | — |
| [[alcubierre-warp-drive]] Alcubierre warp drive | frontier | The Alcubierre metric describes a bubble of distorted spacetime moving along a path: inside it light cones are shifted by the bubble velocity, so the bubble can outrun light as judged from far away while nothing ever leaves its local light cone. Building it requires negative energy density. | — |
