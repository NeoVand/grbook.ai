# Mathematical methods

General-purpose mathematics a learner needs before or alongside the course, taught without reference to spacetime: single- and multivariable calculus (partial derivatives, chain rule, Taylor series, hyperbolic functions, Jacobians), abstract linear algebra (groups, vector spaces, bases, dual spaces, matrices, determinants, inverses, eigenvalues), Euclidean coordinates and parametrized curves and surfaces, three-dimensional vector calculus and its integral theorems, distributions (Dirac delta, Heaviside step), ordinary and partial differential equations with Green-function methods and the elliptic/hyperbolic distinction, Fourier analysis, perturbation methods, elementary Lie groups, and probability and statistics (noise models, Bayesian estimation, likelihood ratios). Point-set topology and maps for manifolds, curvature of curves and surfaces, index notation for spacetime tensors, and statistics applied to detector data are owned by their physics domains.

| Concept | Tier | Summary | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [[identity-versus-equation]] Identity versus equation | prerequisite | An equation holds only for particular values of its unknowns, whereas an identity (often written with a triple bar) holds for every value because its terms cancel once expanded. Recognising which one is meant tells you whether a relation constrains anything. | — |
| [[partial-derivative]] Partial derivative | prerequisite | The rate of change of a function of several variables with respect to one of them while all the others are held fixed. It is the basic building block of gradients, Jacobians and every field equation in the course. | physics-reviewed |
| [[chain-rule]] Chain rule | prerequisite | The derivative of a composite function is the product of the derivatives of its parts; for several variables, a small change in the output is the sum over inputs of each partial derivative times that input's change. It is how coordinate differentials and derivative operators transform. | — |
| [[taylor-series]] Taylor series | prerequisite | A smooth function near a point is approximated by a polynomial whose coefficients are its derivatives at that point divided by factorials; truncating after a few terms gives controlled local approximations with big-O error estimates. The multivariable version expands in all coordinate displacements at once. | — |
| [[hyperbolic-functions]] Hyperbolic functions | prerequisite | cosh x and sinh x are the even and odd parts of the exponential, obeying cosh^2 x - sinh^2 x = 1 in the way cosine and sine obey a Pythagorean identity. They parametrise hyperbolas and are the natural language for rapidity, Lorentz boosts and uniformly accelerated motion. | — |
| [[fundamental-theorem-of-calculus]] Fundamental theorem of calculus | prerequisite | Integrating a derivative over an interval gives the difference of the function's values at the two ends, upper end counted positively and lower end negatively. Every later integral theorem (Green, Gauss, Stokes) has this same shape: a derivative inside, signed boundary values outside. | — |
| [[abelian-group]] Abelian group | prerequisite | A group is a set with an associative operation, an identity element and an inverse for every element; it is abelian when the operation is also commutative. Vector addition is the prototypical abelian group. | — |
| [[vector-space]] Vector space | prerequisite | A set whose elements can be added (forming an abelian group) and multiplied by real numbers, with the scalings distributing over sums and behaving associatively. Arrows, column vectors, matrices, functions and tangent vectors at a point are all examples. | — |
| [[linear-independence]] Linear independence | prerequisite | A set of vectors is linearly independent when the only combination of them that gives the zero vector has all coefficients zero; otherwise some member can be written in terms of the others. | — |
| [[basis]] Basis and dimension | prerequisite | The dimension of a vector space is the largest number of linearly independent vectors it contains, and a basis is any independent set of that size; every vector is then a unique combination of basis vectors, whose coefficients are its components. | — |
| [[dual-vector-space]] Dual vector space | prerequisite | The set of all linear real-valued functions on a vector space is itself a vector space of the same dimension, called the dual; each basis of the original space induces a dual basis. Row vectors versus column vectors, and bras versus kets, are familiar instances. | — |
| [[positive-definite-inner-product]] Positive-definite inner product | prerequisite | An inner product is positive definite when every nonzero vector has strictly positive square, A.A > 0; only then does it define a norm and ordinary lengths. The Minkowski metric is an inner product that fails this condition. | — |
| [[matrix]] Matrix | prerequisite | A rectangular array of numbers whose entry A_ij sits in row i and column j; square n x n matrices form a vector space of dimension n^2, and column vectors are n x 1 matrices. | — |
| [[matrix-multiplication]] Matrix multiplication | prerequisite | The product AB has entries formed by pairing row i of A with column j of B and summing, (AB)_ij = sum_k A_ik B_kj; acting on a column vector is the special case. The product is associative but generally not commutative, and the identity matrix (Kronecker delta entries) leaves every matrix unchanged. | — |
| [[determinant]] Determinant | prerequisite | A single number attached to a square matrix, ad - bc for 2 x 2 and in general computed by expanding along any row as entries times their cofactors (signed minors). It measures the signed volume scaling of the linear map and vanishes exactly when the matrix is not invertible. | — |
| [[matrix-inverse]] Matrix inverse | prerequisite | The inverse of a square matrix B is the matrix that multiplies B from either side to give the identity; it exists exactly when det B is nonzero, and its entries are the transposed cofactors divided by the determinant. | — |
| [[orthogonal-matrix]] Orthogonal matrix | prerequisite | A real matrix whose transpose is its inverse; such matrices are exactly the linear maps that preserve Euclidean lengths and angles, rotations being the standard example. Because they preserve the identity metric, vector and covector components transform the same way under them. | — |
| [[eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors]] Eigenvalues and eigenvectors | prerequisite | An eigenvector of a linear map is a nonzero vector that the map only rescales, and the scale factor is its eigenvalue; they are found from det(A - lambda I) = 0. Symmetric real matrices have real eigenvalues and an orthogonal set of eigenvectors, which diagonalises them. | — |
| [[jacobi-formula]] Jacobi's formula | prerequisite | The derivative of a determinant is the determinant times the trace of the inverse matrix times the derivative of the matrix, d(det A) = det A tr(A^{-1} dA); equivalently the variation of ln det A is tr(A^{-1} delta A). For the metric this gives the derivative of g used for the contracted Christoffel symbol and for varying sqrt(-g). | — |
| [[lie-group]] Lie group | prerequisite | A group whose elements depend smoothly on continuous parameters, so that group multiplication is a smooth operation; rotations, Lorentz boosts and gauge transformations are examples. Its infinitesimal elements form a Lie algebra. | — |
| [[euclidean-space]] Euclidean space | prerequisite | The flat n-dimensional space of ordered n-tuples with distance given by Pythagoras, the square root of the sum of squared coordinate differences; lengths, angles and all of Euclid's theorems follow. It is the model space that manifolds resemble locally. | — |
| [[cartesian-coordinates]] Cartesian coordinates | prerequisite | Coordinates that locate a point by signed distances along mutually perpendicular straight axes, so that the squared distance between nearby points is just the sum of squared coordinate differences. | — |
| [[polar-coordinates]] Plane polar coordinates | prerequisite | Coordinates (r, theta) on the plane giving distance from the origin and angle from an axis, with x = r cos theta and y = r sin theta. The line element becomes dr^2 + r^2 dtheta^2, the simplest example where coordinate components vary from place to place. | — |
| [[spherical-polar-coordinates]] Spherical polar coordinates | prerequisite | Coordinates (r, theta, phi) on three-dimensional space built from a radius, a polar angle and an azimuth; the flat line element is dr^2 + r^2 dOmega^2, where dOmega^2 = dtheta^2 + sin^2 theta dphi^2 is the line element of the unit sphere (the solid-angle element). | — |
| [[conic-section]] Conic sections | prerequisite | The curves cut from a cone by a plane: circle, ellipse, parabola and hyperbola. With a focus at the origin they share the polar form 1/r proportional to 1 + e cos theta, with eccentricity e = 0, less than 1, equal to 1, or greater than 1. | — |
| [[parametrized-curve]] Parametrized curve | prerequisite | A curve described by coordinate functions x^mu(lambda) of a single parameter, so that points, tangent directions and rates of change along the path can be computed by differentiating with respect to lambda. | — |
| [[parametrized-surface]] Parametrized surface | prerequisite | A two- (or n-) dimensional region described by a map from parameters to points; the partial derivatives with respect to the parameters are tangent vectors, and small parameter steps span tiny parallelograms used to set up surface integrals. | — |
| [[parametric-versus-implicit-representation]] Parametric versus implicit representation | prerequisite | A curve, surface or subspace can be described either by giving its coordinates as functions of intrinsic parameters or by giving equations the coordinates must satisfy; for example the unit circle is (cos t, sin t) or x^2 + y^2 = 1. The number of parameters equals the dimension minus the number of independent constraints. | — |
| [[jacobian-determinant]] Jacobian determinant | prerequisite | The determinant of the matrix of partial derivatives of new coordinates with respect to old ones; it is the factor by which small coordinate volumes rescale, and where it is nonzero the change of coordinates is locally invertible (inverse function theorem). | — |
| [[gradient]] Gradient | prerequisite | For a scalar function in Euclidean space, the vector of its partial derivatives; it points in the direction of fastest increase, has magnitude equal to that rate, and is perpendicular to surfaces of constant value. | — |
| [[directional-derivative]] Directional derivative | prerequisite | The rate of change of a function along a given vector v, equal to v . grad f = v^i df/dx^i; heuristically the value at the tip of a small v minus the value at its base. It is the template that covariant derivatives and tangent-vector operators generalise. | — |
| [[lagrange-multiplier]] Lagrange multiplier | prerequisite | To extremise a function subject to a constraint, extremise the function minus an unknown multiplier times the constraint; at the solution the two gradients are parallel, and the multiplier measures how the optimum shifts as the constraint is relaxed. | — |
| [[divergence]] Divergence | prerequisite | For a vector field, the sum of the derivatives of each component along its own direction, div A = dA_x/dx + dA_y/dy + dA_z/dz; it measures net outflow per unit volume from a tiny box around a point. | — |
| [[curl]] Curl | prerequisite | For a vector field in three dimensions, the vector whose components measure the circulation per unit area around small loops in each coordinate plane; it vanishes for gradient fields. | — |
| [[laplacian]] Laplacian | prerequisite | The divergence of the gradient, which in Cartesian coordinates is the sum of unmixed second partial derivatives; in plane polar coordinates it reads (1/r) d/dr (r dphi/dr) + (1/r^2) d^2phi/dtheta^2, the same operator in different coordinates. | — |
| [[divergence-theorem]] Divergence theorem | prerequisite | The outward flux of a vector field through a closed surface equals the volume integral of its divergence over the enclosed region. It turns global conservation or flux laws, such as Gauss's law, into local differential equations and back. | — |
| [[green-theorem-in-the-plane]] Green's theorem in the plane | prerequisite | For a region of the plane bounded by an anticlockwise closed curve, the circulation of L dx + M dy around the curve equals the area integral of dM/dx - dL/dy over the region. | — |
| [[kelvin-stokes-theorem]] Kelvin-Stokes theorem | prerequisite | The flux of the curl of a vector field through an oriented surface equals the circulation of the field around the surface's boundary curve, with the direction of travel fixed by the right-hand rule. | — |
| [[dirac-delta-function]] Dirac delta function | prerequisite | A distribution concentrated at a single point with unit total integral, defined by its sifting action: integrating it against a smooth function returns that function's value at the point. Higher-dimensional deltas are products of one-dimensional ones and represent point sources. | — |
| [[heaviside-step-function]] Heaviside step function | prerequisite | The function that is 0 for negative argument and 1 for positive argument; its derivative in the distributional sense is the Dirac delta. | — |
| [[laplacian-of-1-over-r]] Laplacian of 1/r | prerequisite | The Laplacian of 1/\|x - x'\| vanishes everywhere except at x = x', where it equals -4 pi times a three-dimensional delta function; equivalently the inverse-square field of a point has divergence 4 pi delta. This single identity links point masses and charges to Poisson's equation. | — |
| [[ordinary-differential-equation]] Ordinary differential equation | prerequisite | An equation relating an unknown function of one variable to its derivatives; a linear equation of order n needs n pieces of initial data (such as position and velocity) to fix a unique solution, and any such equation can be rewritten as a first-order system. | — |
| [[laplace-equation]] Laplace's equation | prerequisite | The equation nabla^2 f = 0; its solutions in a source-free region are smooth, and one that vanishes at infinity outside a bounded region falls off as a constant over r plus faster-decaying multipole terms. | — |
| [[green-function]] Green's function | prerequisite | For a linear differential operator L, the Green's function G is its response to a point source, L G = delta; the solution for any source is then the superposition of G over the source. For Poisson's equation this gives the familiar integral of the source divided by distance, with a 1/(4 pi). | — |
| [[wave-equation]] Wave equation | prerequisite | The second-order partial differential equation stating that the second time derivative of a field equals the wave speed squared times its Laplacian (the d'Alembertian of the field vanishes); its solutions are non-dispersive disturbances moving at that speed, built from plane waves. | — |
| [[fourier-transform]] Fourier transform | prerequisite | Rewriting a function of time or space as a superposition of complex exponentials, one amplitude per frequency or wavenumber; derivatives become multiplications, which turns linear constant-coefficient equations into algebra and makes noise spectra and correlations easy to compute. Sampled data use the discrete version, computed quickly by the FFT. | — |
| [[retarded-green-function]] Retarded Green's function | prerequisite | The causal solution of the wave equation for a point source that flashes once: a spherical shell expanding at the wave speed with amplitude falling as 1/r, delta(t - r)/(4 pi r). Superposing it over a source gives retarded potentials evaluated at the time the signal left each source point. | — |
| [[hyperbolic-partial-differential-equation]] Hyperbolic partial differential equation | advanced | A partial differential equation whose highest-derivative terms have the structure of a wave operator, so that disturbances propagate at finite speed along characteristics and the Cauchy problem (data on an initial surface) is well posed. Elliptic equations like Laplace's behave very differently: their solutions are fixed by boundary data and respond instantly everywhere. | — |
| [[analytic-function]] Analytic function | advanced | A function equal to its convergent Taylor series near every point; its value and all derivatives at one point then fix it throughout a connected domain, which is far more rigid than mere smoothness. | — |
| [[perturbation-theory]] Perturbation theory | prerequisite | When an equation contains a small dimensionless parameter epsilon, write the solution as a zeroth-order solution plus epsilon times a correction plus higher orders, and solve order by order; nondimensionalising first makes clear which terms are genuinely small. | — |
| [[secular-term]] Secular term | prerequisite | A term in a perturbative solution that grows without bound with the independent variable, like phi sin phi, produced by forcing at the system's own natural frequency; it signals a slow cumulative change such as a shifted frequency, and resumming it restores a bounded solution. | — |
| [[linear-stability-analysis]] Linear stability analysis | prerequisite | Write a quantity as an equilibrium value plus a small deviation, keep only first-order terms in the equations of motion, and check whether the deviation oscillates (stable) or grows (unstable). | — |
| [[white-noise]] White noise | prerequisite | Random noise whose values at different times are statistically uncorrelated, so the covariance of samples is a variance times a Kronecker delta and its power spectrum is flat across frequencies. | — |
| [[bayesian-parameter-estimation]] Bayesian parameter estimation | prerequisite | Combine the likelihood of the observed data given model parameters with prior probabilities to obtain a posterior probability distribution over the parameters, which yields best estimates and credible intervals. | — |
| [[likelihood-ratio]] Likelihood ratio | prerequisite | The ratio of the probability of the data under one hypothesis to that under another, for example signal plus noise versus noise alone; thresholding it gives the optimal detection statistic, and the ratio of marginal likelihoods (the Bayes factor) compares models. | — |
| [[gaussian-noise]] Gaussian noise | prerequisite | Random noise whose samples (or frequency-bin amplitudes) follow a zero-mean normal distribution with a variance that does not change in time. It is the default model for detector noise because many small independent disturbances add up to a normal distribution, and it makes false-alarm rates computable from the Gaussian tail. | — |
