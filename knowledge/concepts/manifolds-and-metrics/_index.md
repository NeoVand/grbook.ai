# Manifolds, metrics and integration

Spacetime as a differentiable manifold equipped with a metric, taught intrinsically: the point-set topology and maps needed to define manifolds (open sets, continuity, homeomorphisms, Hausdorff, compactness, simple connectedness); charts, atlases, transition maps and smooth structure; smooth maps, diffeomorphisms, push-forward and pull-back; general coordinate transformations and why coordinates are labels rather than vectors; curves, tangent vectors as directional derivatives, tangent and cotangent spaces, coordinate bases, vector and tensor fields, integral curves and congruences; the metric tensor field, its components, line element, inverse, determinant, signature and operational meaning; Riemannian, Lorentzian and pseudo-Riemannian manifolds; flat metrics in disguise and standard 2D examples; local light cones read from a metric; the local flatness theorem and Riemann normal coordinates; hypersurfaces, normals, causal type, induced metrics, projection tensors, embeddings and embedding diagrams as a technique; conformally related and conformally flat metrics; tensor densities, sqrt(-g), invariant volume and hypersurface elements, and the covariant divergence theorem. Lie derivatives and isometries, connections, curvature, forms and frames, and specific spacetimes are taught elsewhere.

| Concept | Tier | Summary | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [[map-between-sets]] Map between sets | prerequisite | A map f: M -> N assigns to every element of its domain exactly one element of the target set; maps compose, and the image of a subset and the inverse image of a subset of N are the basic ways of transporting sets through them. | — |
| [[bijection]] Bijection | prerequisite | A map is injective if distinct points go to distinct images, surjective if every target point is hit, and bijective if both; exactly the bijections have an inverse map. | — |
| [[open-set]] Open set | prerequisite | In R^n (or any metric space) a set is open if every point in it is surrounded by a small ball lying wholly inside the set; open sets formalize "all nearby points" without needing exact distances afterwards. | — |
| [[topological-space]] Topological space | foundation | A topological space is a set together with a chosen family of "open" subsets, closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections and containing the empty set and the whole set; it captures nearness and continuity with no notion of distance. | — |
| [[continuous-map]] Continuous map | foundation | A map between topological spaces is continuous when the inverse image of every open set is open; equivalently, points close to x land close to f(x). This generalizes epsilon-delta continuity to spaces without distances. | — |
| [[homeomorphism]] Homeomorphism | foundation | A homeomorphism is a continuous bijection whose inverse is also continuous; homeomorphic spaces are topologically indistinguishable, such as a coffee mug and a doughnut, and manifolds are defined as spaces locally homeomorphic to R^n. | — |
| [[hausdorff-property]] Hausdorff property | foundation | A space is Hausdorff if any two distinct points have disjoint open neighbourhoods; the condition is imposed on manifolds to exclude pathologies such as a line with a doubled point or a branching line. | — |
| [[compactness]] Compactness | foundation | A set is compact if every open cover has a finite subcover (for metric spaces, every sequence has a convergent subsequence inside it); by the Heine-Borel theorem the compact subsets of R^n are exactly the closed and bounded ones. | — |
| [[simply-connected-space]] Simply connected space | foundation | A connected space is simply connected if every closed loop can be shrunk continuously to a point, so any two paths with the same endpoints can be deformed into one another; a plane is simply connected, a plane with a hole is not. | — |
| [[manifold]] Manifold | foundation | A manifold is a space in which every point has a neighbourhood that can be labelled continuously and one-to-one by n real numbers, so that small patches look like pieces of R^n while the global shape may be quite different; n is its dimension. | — |
| [[chart]] Chart | foundation | A chart (U, phi) is an open region U of a manifold with a homeomorphism phi onto an open subset of R^n; the components of phi are the coordinates of each point, and functions on the manifold are handled in practice as ordinary functions of those coordinates. | — |
| [[transition-map]] Transition map | foundation | Where two charts overlap, the transition map psi o phi^-1 converts one set of coordinates of a point into the other; requiring these maps to be smooth is what lets calculus be done consistently on a manifold. | — |
| [[atlas]] Atlas | foundation | An atlas is a collection of charts whose domains together cover the whole manifold; many spaces, such as the sphere, need more than one chart because any single coordinate system degenerates somewhere, like polar coordinates at the origin. | — |
| [[smooth-function]] Smooth function | prerequisite | A function on a region of R^n is of class C^k if all its partial derivatives up to order k exist and are continuous, and smooth (C-infinity) if this holds for every k. | — |
| [[differentiable-manifold]] Differentiable manifold | foundation | A differentiable manifold is a manifold whose charts are all smoothly compatible, forming a maximal atlas, so that smoothness of functions, curves and maps does not depend on which coordinates are used; general relativity models spacetime as a four-dimensional smooth manifold. | — |
| [[local-geometry-versus-global-topology]] Local geometry versus global topology | foundation | Being a manifold, or carrying a given metric, only constrains small neighbourhoods; the same local structure can be glued into different global shapes, as a flat plane, cylinder and torus share local geometry but differ in connectivity. | — |
| [[differentiable-map]] Differentiable map between manifolds | foundation | A map between manifolds is differentiable (smooth) when its expression in coordinates, chart of the target composed with the map composed with inverse chart of the source, is differentiable for every choice of charts. | — |
| [[diffeomorphism]] Diffeomorphism | foundation | A diffeomorphism is a smooth bijection between manifolds whose inverse is also smooth; diffeomorphic manifolds are the same as smooth manifolds, and in general relativity metrics related by a diffeomorphism describe the same physical spacetime. | — |
| [[general-coordinate-transformation]] General coordinate transformation | foundation | A general coordinate transformation is any smooth, locally invertible relabelling x -> x'(x); its matrix of partial derivatives dx'/dx varies from point to point and transforms tensor components at each point, and it is singular exactly where its Jacobian determinant vanishes. | — |
| [[coordinates-are-not-vectors]] Coordinates are not vectors | foundation | Coordinates are labels for points, and the array x^mu does not transform like vector components under nonlinear coordinate changes; only infinitesimal displacements dx^mu, which live in the tangent space, transform with the Jacobian. | — |
| [[curve-on-a-manifold]] Curve on a manifold | foundation | A curve is a map from an interval of a real parameter into the manifold, described in a chart by functions x^mu(lambda); the underlying set of points is the path, and reparametrizing changes the curve but not the path. | — |
| [[tangent-vector]] Tangent vector | foundation | A tangent vector at a point is the velocity of a curve through it, with components dx^mu/dlambda in a chart; curves that agree to first order at the point define the same tangent vector, and such vectors live at the point rather than in the manifold. | — |
| [[tangent-vector-as-directional-derivative]] Tangent vector as directional derivative | foundation | The modern definition identifies a tangent vector with the operator d/dlambda = (dx^mu/dlambda) partial_mu that returns the rate of change of any function along a curve; such linear, Leibniz-rule operators (derivations) correspond one-to-one with tangent vectors, and the coordinate basis vectors become the partial derivatives. | — |
| [[tangent-space]] Tangent space | foundation | The tangent space T_pM is the vector space of all tangent vectors at a point p, of the same dimension as the manifold; each point has its own, so vectors at different points cannot be compared or added without extra structure. | — |
| [[coordinate-basis]] Coordinate basis | foundation | A chart supplies at every point the basis e_mu = partial/partial x^mu of vectors tangent to the coordinate lines, each scaled so that moving along it by one unit advances its coordinate by one; its members commute, and their inner products are the metric components. | — |
| [[cotangent-space]] Cotangent space | foundation | The cotangent space at p is the dual of the tangent space: the linear real-valued functions on vectors at p, i.e. one-forms; the coordinate differentials dx^mu form the basis dual to partial/partial x^mu. | — |
| [[vector-field]] Vector field | foundation | A vector field assigns a tangent vector to every point of a region, each in its own tangent space; in a chart its components are functions of the coordinates, and it acts on functions as a first-order differential operator. | — |
| [[tensor-field]] Tensor field | foundation | A tensor field assigns to each point a tensor of fixed type built from that point's tangent and cotangent spaces; its components are functions of the coordinates, and it is smooth if they are smooth in every chart. | — |
| [[push-forward]] Push-forward | advanced | A smooth map phi from M to N carries tangent vectors forward: the push-forward of a vector at p acts on a function on N by first pulling the function back along phi, and in coordinates its components are multiplied by the Jacobian matrix of the map. | — |
| [[pull-back]] Pull-back | advanced | A smooth map phi from M to N carries one-forms and covariant tensors backwards: the pull-back of a covariant tensor on N evaluates it on the push-forwards of vectors from M; the induced metric of a submanifold is the pull-back of the ambient metric. | — |
| [[integral-curve]] Integral curve | foundation | An integral curve of a vector field is a curve whose tangent at each point equals the field there, a solution of dx^mu/dlambda = V^mu(x); through each point where the field is nonzero exactly one passes locally. | — |
| [[congruence-of-curves]] Congruence of curves | foundation | A congruence is a family of curves filling a region with exactly one through each point, so the curves never cross; the integral curves of a smooth nonvanishing vector field form one, and fluid or observer world lines are physical examples. | — |
| [[metric-as-additional-structure]] Metric as additional structure | foundation | A bare manifold has points, smoothness and vectors but no lengths, angles or shape; a metric is extra structure chosen on top, and different metrics on the same manifold (a round sphere and an ellipsoid) give different geometries. | — |
| [[metric-tensor]] Metric tensor | core | The metric tensor is a smooth, symmetric, non-degenerate (0,2) tensor field g that gives the inner product of vectors at each point, and so fixes lengths, proper times, angles, causal character and the correspondence between vectors and one-forms; in general relativity it is the gravitational field itself. | — |
| [[metric-components]] Metric components | foundation | In a coordinate basis the metric components g_{mu nu} = g(e_mu, e_nu) form a symmetric matrix of functions of position; a metric with no off-diagonal entries is called diagonal, and position dependence of the components alone does not signal curvature. | — |
| [[line-element]] Line element | core | The line element ds^2 = g_{mu nu} dx^mu dx^nu gives the squared interval between infinitesimally separated points; because its coefficients are the metric components, quoting it together with the coordinates specifies the metric completely. | — |
| [[proper-length-of-a-curve]] Proper length of a curve | core | The length of a curve is the integral along it of the square root of /g_{mu nu} (dx^mu/dlambda)(dx^nu/dlambda)/ dlambda, independent of how the curve is parametrized; for spacelike curves it is proper length and for timelike curves the same integral gives proper time. | — |
| [[operational-meaning-of-the-metric]] Operational meaning of the metric | core | The metric is physically measurable: a short spacelike displacement has a proper length read by a rod equal to the square root of /g(dx,dx)/, and a short timelike one has a proper time read by a clock carried between the events. | — |
| [[coordinate-freedom]] Coordinate freedom | foundation | In general relativity coordinates are arbitrary labels: a coordinate called t need not measure time nor r distance, and physical durations and lengths come only from combining coordinate differences with the metric. | — |
| [[inverse-metric]] Inverse metric | core | The inverse metric g^{mu nu} is the symmetric (2,0) tensor whose component matrix is the inverse of g_{mu nu}, so g^{mu lambda} g_{lambda nu} = delta^mu_nu; it takes two one-forms to a number and raises indices. | — |
| [[metric-signature]] Metric signature | foundation | At any point a change of basis brings the metric matrix to diagonal form with entries +1 and -1, and the numbers of each (the signature) cannot be altered by any change of basis; spacetime has one time-like and three space-like signs, summarized as signature +2 or -2 depending on convention. | — |
| [[riemannian-metric]] Riemannian metric | foundation | A Riemannian metric gives every nonzero vector a positive squared length, as on ordinary surfaces; a metric that allows negative or zero squared lengths for nonzero vectors is pseudo-Riemannian. | — |
| [[lorentzian-metric]] Lorentzian metric | core | A Lorentzian metric has one eigenvalue of one sign and the rest of the other, like Minkowski's, so every tangent space splits into timelike, null and spacelike vectors; this is the kind of metric spacetime carries. | — |
| [[pseudo-riemannian-manifold]] Pseudo-Riemannian manifold | core | A pseudo-Riemannian manifold is a differentiable manifold equipped with a smooth metric tensor field; with a positive-definite metric it is Riemannian, and with a Lorentzian metric it is a Lorentzian manifold. | — |
| [[spacetime-as-lorentzian-manifold]] Spacetime as a Lorentzian manifold | core | General relativity models spacetime as a four-dimensional smooth manifold of events carrying a Lorentzian metric; two such pairs related by a diffeomorphism are the same physical spacetime, so a solution is really an equivalence class of metrics. | — |
| [[metric-determinant]] Metric determinant | core | The metric determinant g = det(g_{mu nu}) is negative for a Lorentzian metric and picks up the square of the Jacobian under coordinate changes, making it a scalar density of weight two; where it vanishes the inverse metric does not exist in that chart. | — |
| [[flat-metric]] Flat metric | foundation | A metric is flat if some coordinate system makes its components constant with entries +1 or -1 on the diagonal throughout a region, not just at one point; the same flat metric can look complicated in other coordinates, as polar coordinates give ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2 dtheta^2. | — |
| [[two-sphere-metric]] Two-sphere metric | foundation | A sphere of radius a has intrinsic line element ds^2 = a^2 (dtheta^2 + sin^2 theta dphi^2); it is genuinely curved, and in any metric the coefficient of dOmega^2 fixes the area (4 pi times it) and circumference of the corresponding spheres. | — |
| [[local-light-cone]] Local light cone | core | At each event the metric picks out the null directions with g(k,k) = 0, forming a cone that separates timelike directions (inside, available to massive bodies) from spacelike ones; in curved spacetime or curvilinear coordinates these cones tilt and narrow from place to place, and drawing them is a quick way to read a metric. | — |
| [[local-flatness-theorem]] Local flatness theorem | core | About any point of a pseudo-Riemannian manifold one can choose coordinates in which the metric equals the Minkowski (or Euclidean) form at that point and all its first derivatives vanish there, but the second derivatives cannot all be removed; curvature lives in what remains. | — |
| [[riemann-normal-coordinates]] Riemann normal coordinates | advanced | Riemann normal coordinates about a point label each nearby event by the initial direction times affine distance of the geodesic reaching it from that point; they realize the local flatness theorem, with the metric Minkowskian and connection coefficients zero at the origin. | — |
| [[hypersurface]] Hypersurface | foundation | An m-dimensional subspace of an n-dimensional manifold is given by n functions of m parameters or by n - m independent equations; a hypersurface is the case m = n - 1, a single equation f(x) = constant, such as a surface of simultaneity in spacetime. | — |
| [[unit-normal-to-a-hypersurface]] Unit normal to a hypersurface | foundation | The gradient of the function defining a hypersurface is a one-form that annihilates every tangent vector of the surface; raising its index with the inverse metric gives the normal vector, and dividing by its magnitude gives the unit normal, which exists only when the surface is not null. | — |
| [[spacelike-timelike-and-null-hypersurfaces]] Spacelike, timelike and null hypersurfaces | core | A hypersurface is spacelike if all its tangent vectors are spacelike (its normal is timelike), timelike if it contains timelike directions (spacelike normal), and null if its normal is null and lies within it; the sign of g^{mu mu} likewise classifies the level surfaces of a coordinate x^mu. | — |
| [[induced-metric]] Induced metric | core | A submanifold inherits a metric from its surroundings by restricting the ambient metric to its tangent vectors; in practice one substitutes the embedding functions into the ambient line element, g_{mu nu} = G_{alpha beta} (dX^alpha/dx^mu)(dX^beta/dx^nu). | — |
| [[hypersurface-projection-tensor]] Hypersurface projection tensor | advanced | Given a unit normal n, the mixed tensor delta^a_b - n^a n_b (with the sign set by whether n is timelike or spacelike) removes the normal part of any vector or covector, leaving its component tangent to the hypersurface; lowering its index gives the induced metric. | — |
| [[embedding]] Embedding | foundation | An isometric embedding places a space inside a higher-dimensional flat space by a smooth one-to-one map whose induced metric reproduces the space's own distances; it is a visual aid only, since general relativity describes spacetime intrinsically with no surrounding space. | — |
| [[embedding-diagram]] Embedding diagram | core | An embedding diagram draws a two-dimensional slice of a curved space as a surface in flat three-dimensional space whose intrinsic distances match the slice's metric; only distances measured along the surface mean anything, and the extra direction is fictitious. | — |
| [[conformally-related-metrics]] Conformally related metrics | core | Two metrics on the same manifold are conformally related if one is a positive function Omega^2 times the other at every point; the rescaling changes lengths and curvature but preserves angles, ratios of lengths at a point and the light cones. | — |
| [[conformal-invariance]] Conformal invariance | advanced | A quantity is conformally invariant if it is unchanged when the metric is replaced by a conformally related one; angles, null cones, null geodesics as paths, and the Weyl tensor with one index raised are invariant, while lengths, Christoffel symbols and the Ricci tensor are not. | — |
| [[conformally-flat-metric]] Conformally flat metric | core | A metric is conformally flat if some coordinates write it as a positive function times a flat metric; every two-dimensional metric is locally conformally flat, which for Lorentzian 2-spaces means choosing the null curves as coordinate lines, ds^2 = F^2 (dt^2 - dx^2). | — |
| [[hyperbolic-plane]] Hyperbolic plane | advanced | The hyperbolic plane is the two-dimensional space of constant negative curvature; the half-plane model ds^2 = (dx^2 + dy^2)/y^2 and the Poincare disc map it into finite regions while preserving angles, with geodesics drawn as circular arcs meeting the boundary at right angles. | — |
| [[tensor-density]] Tensor density | advanced | A tensor density of weight W transforms like an ordinary tensor with the same indices except for an extra factor of the Jacobian determinant to the power W; products add weights, and multiplying by sqrt(-g) raises the weight by one. | — |
| [[square-root-of-metric-determinant]] Square root of minus the metric determinant | core | On a Lorentzian manifold g < 0, and sqrt(-g) is a positive scalar density of weight one; it is the factor that converts coordinate volumes into proper volumes and ordinary tensors into densities. | — |
| [[invariant-volume-element]] Invariant volume element | core | The coordinate cell d^4x is not invariant (it is a density of weight -1), but sqrt(-g) d^4x is: it gives the physically measured four-volume of a small cell in any coordinates, and integrating a scalar against it yields a coordinate-independent result. | — |
| [[hypersurface-element]] Hypersurface element | advanced | A small piece of a three-dimensional hypersurface is represented by a covector along its normal whose size is its three-volume, dS_mu; the flux of a current J through it is J^mu dS_mu, and integrating gives the total charge crossing the surface. | — |
| [[covariant-divergence-theorem]] Covariant divergence theorem | core | The integral of the covariant divergence of a vector field over a spacetime region, taken with sqrt(-g) d^4x, equals the flux of the field through the region's boundary; it works because sqrt(-g) V^mu_;mu = (sqrt(-g) V^mu)_,mu, and for a conserved current it says what enters a region must leave. | — |
