# Measurement, classical mechanics and Newtonian gravity

Pre-relativistic physics the course builds on: units and dimensional analysis, the c = 1, G = c = 1, hbar = c = 1 and Heaviside-Lorentz conventions and how to restore constants; Newton's laws, momentum and energy conservation, inertial frames, absolute space and time, and fictitious forces in accelerating and rotating frames; the calculus of variations and Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics of finite-dimensional systems; Newtonian gravity as a force law and as a field (inertial, passive and active mass as definitions, potential, Gauss and Poisson laws, shell theorem, tidal tensor, escape velocity); and central-force motion with the full Kepler problem (effective potential, Binet equation, conic orbits, Kepler's laws, Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector, Bertrand's theorem). Relativistic orbits, the equivalence of the masses as a principle, field Lagrangians and Newtonian foils inside relativistic topics belong to their own domains.

| Concept | Tier | Summary | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [[dimensional-analysis]] Units and dimensional analysis | prerequisite | Every physical quantity carries dimensions built from length, time and mass, and every term of a valid equation must share them; checking and converting units this way catches errors and fixes how constants must appear, though matching dimensions alone cannot prove an equation right. | — |
| [[relativistic-units]] Units with c = 1 | prerequisite | Measuring time and distance in the same unit (for example metres of light-travel time, or light-seconds) makes the speed of light the pure number 1, so velocities become fractions of c and energy, momentum and mass share one unit. | — |
| [[geometrized-units]] Geometrized units (G = c = 1) | prerequisite | Setting both c and Newton's G to 1 turns mass, time and energy into lengths (a mass M becomes GM/c^2, about 1.5 km for the Sun), which strips G and c from relativistic formulas; results are converted back to SI only at the end. | — |
| [[restoring-factors-of-c-and-g]] Restoring factors of c and G | prerequisite | To turn a geometrized formula into SI units, multiply each quantity by the power of c and G/c^2 that gives it its correct dimensions: a quantity with dimensions L^n T^m M^p becomes a length power after multiplying by c^m (G/c^2)^p, and the reverse restores the constants. | — |
| [[natural-units]] Natural units (hbar = c = 1) | prerequisite | Particle physicists set hbar and c to 1, so mass, energy and momentum carry dimensions of inverse length and any quantity reduces to a power of length; unlike geometrized units, G stays dimensional and defines the Planck scale. | — |
| [[heaviside-lorentz-units]] Heaviside-Lorentz units | prerequisite | An electromagnetic unit system that moves the 4 pi into Coulomb's law and removes epsilon_0 and mu_0, so Maxwell's equations carry no 4 pi or material constants (div E = rho); it is often combined with c = 1 in field theory. | — |
| [[astronomical-distance-units]] Astronomical distance units (AU, light-year, parsec) | prerequisite | The astronomical unit is the mean Earth-Sun distance (about 1.5 x 10^11 m); the parsec is the distance at which one AU subtends one arcsecond (about 3.26 light-years), with kiloparsecs and megaparsecs used for galactic and cosmological scales. | — |
| [[absolute-time]] Absolute time | prerequisite | Newtonian physics assumes a single universal time: once synchronized, every observer's clock agrees on when any event happens, so there is one shared present moment and simultaneity is the same for everyone. | — |
| [[newtons-first-law]] Newton's first law | prerequisite | A body acted on by no net force stays at rest or keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed; the law is really a statement that frames exist (inertial frames) in which this is true. | — |
| [[inertial-frame]] Inertial frame | prerequisite | A frame of reference in which a force-free body moves uniformly in a straight line, so Newton's laws hold in their simple form; any two inertial frames move uniformly relative to each other, and acceleration (unlike uniform velocity) is detectable from inside a system. | — |
| [[absolute-space]] Absolute space | prerequisite | Newton postulated an immovable background space against which all motion is ultimately measured; inertial observers are those at rest or moving uniformly relative to it, and inertial forces signal acceleration relative to it. | — |
| [[inertial-mass]] Inertial mass | prerequisite | Inertial mass measures how strongly a body resists changes in its motion; operationally, when two isolated bodies push on each other, the ratio of their masses is the inverse ratio of their accelerations, which lets one calibrate any mass against a standard. | — |
| [[linear-momentum]] Linear momentum | prerequisite | The linear momentum of a body is its mass times its velocity, p = m v, and the momentum of a system is the vector sum over its parts; it is the quantity whose rate of change Newton's second law equates to force. | — |
| [[force]] Force | prerequisite | Force is what changes a body's momentum; within Newtonian mechanics it is measured through F = dp/dt, so it gains independent physical content only through separate force laws (such as gravitation) and the action-reaction property. | — |
| [[newtons-second-law]] Newton's second law | prerequisite | The net force on a body equals the rate of change of its momentum, F = dp/dt, which reduces to F = m a when the mass is constant; given the forces and initial position and velocity, it predicts the whole motion. | — |
| [[newtons-third-law]] Newton's third law | prerequisite | When two bodies interact, the force on the first due to the second is equal in size and opposite in direction to the force on the second due to the first, F_ij = -F_ji. | — |
| [[conservation-of-linear-momentum]] Conservation of linear momentum | prerequisite | For an isolated system, on which no net external force acts, internal forces cancel in action-reaction pairs, so the total momentum stays constant through any interaction. | — |
| [[conservative-force]] Conservative force and potential energy | prerequisite | A force field is conservative when the work it does around every closed path is zero; it can then be written as minus the gradient of a potential energy, F = -grad U, whose zero point is arbitrary. | — |
| [[conservation-of-mechanical-energy]] Conservation of mechanical energy | prerequisite | The work done by the net force equals the change in kinetic energy (1/2) m v^2; when all forces are conservative this makes kinetic plus potential energy constant along the motion. | — |
| [[inertial-force]] Inertial (fictitious) force | prerequisite | When Newton's second law is written with accelerations measured in a non-inertial frame, extra force terms proportional to the body's mass appear (for a linearly accelerating frame, -m a); in curvilinear or accelerated coordinates these are exactly the connection terms of the equation of motion. | — |
| [[centrifugal-and-coriolis-forces]] Centrifugal and Coriolis forces | prerequisite | In a frame rotating with angular velocity omega, a body appears to feel an outward centrifugal force -m omega x (omega x r), a velocity-dependent Coriolis force -2m omega x v, and an Euler force -m (d omega/dt) x r if the rotation rate changes. | — |
| [[driven-damped-oscillator]] Driven damped harmonic oscillator and resonance | prerequisite | A damped oscillator driven sinusoidally settles into steady oscillation at the driving frequency, with an amplitude that peaks when the drive matches the natural frequency and a peak height inversely proportional to the damping. | — |
| [[validity-limits-of-newtonian-mechanics]] Range of validity of Newtonian physics | prerequisite | A physical theory is trusted only within the regimes where it matches experiment; Newtonian mechanics and gravity are excellent for speeds far below c and weak potentials (/Phi//c^2 much less than 1), and relativity refines rather than refutes them there. | — |
| [[generalized-coordinates]] Generalized coordinates | prerequisite | Any set of independent variables q^a that fixes a system's configuration, together with their time derivatives (generalized velocities) and the generalized forces -dV/dq^a, can describe its motion; the choice is free and adapted to the problem. | — |
| [[calculus-of-variations]] Calculus of variations | prerequisite | The calculus of variations finds the functions that make an integral of F(y, y', x) stationary: perturb the function by a small variation that vanishes at the fixed endpoints and demand that the first-order change of the integral be zero; the stationary point can be a minimum, maximum or saddle. | — |
| [[fundamental-lemma-calculus-of-variations]] Fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations | prerequisite | If a continuous function integrates to zero against every smooth test function that vanishes at the endpoints, the function itself must vanish everywhere; this is the step that turns a vanishing first variation into a differential equation. | — |
| [[euler-lagrange-equation]] Euler-Lagrange equation | prerequisite | A path makes the integral of L(q, dq/dt, t) stationary with fixed endpoints exactly when d/dt(dL/d q-dot) = dL/dq, with one such equation for each independent coordinate. | — |
| [[lagrangian]] Lagrangian | prerequisite | The Lagrangian is a function of generalized coordinates, velocities and time whose time integral is the action; for Newtonian systems it is kinetic minus potential energy, L = T - V, and multiplying it by a constant or adding a constant leaves the equations of motion unchanged. | — |
| [[hamiltons-principle]] Hamilton's principle | prerequisite | Among all paths between fixed initial and final configurations, a mechanical system follows one for which the action S = integral of L dt is stationary; the usual name "least action" is misleading because the stationary point may be a saddle or maximum. | — |
| [[canonical-momentum]] Canonical (conjugate) momentum | prerequisite | The momentum conjugate to a generalized coordinate is p_a = dL/d(q-dot^a); for a free particle it is ordinary momentum, and because it differentiates with respect to a velocity component it naturally carries a lower index. | — |
| [[cyclic-coordinate]] Cyclic (ignorable) coordinate | prerequisite | If a Lagrangian does not depend on some coordinate, the Euler-Lagrange equation for that coordinate says its conjugate momentum is conserved; for motion in a central potential the angle is cyclic, giving conserved angular momentum. | — |
| [[constant-of-the-motion]] Constant of the motion | prerequisite | A constant of the motion is a quantity that keeps the same value along every trajectory, such as energy or angular momentum; each one lowers the order of the equations that must be integrated, which is why symmetric problems are solvable. | — |
| [[hamiltonian]] Hamiltonian | prerequisite | The Hamiltonian is the Legendre transform of the Lagrangian, H = p_a q-dot^a - L, expressed in coordinates and momenta; when it has no explicit time dependence it is conserved and, for ordinary mechanical systems, equals the total energy. | — |
| [[hamiltons-equations]] Hamilton's equations and phase space | prerequisite | Hamilton's equations dq/dt = dH/dp and dp/dt = -dH/dq replace one second-order equation per coordinate with two first-order ones, so a state is a point in phase space (q, p) that flows along curves of constant energy. | — |
| [[hamilton-jacobi-equation]] Hamilton-Jacobi equation | advanced | Classical mechanics can be rewritten as one partial differential equation for Hamilton's principal function S, with H + dS/dt = 0 and momenta given by the gradient of S; its surfaces of constant S behave like wavefronts and are the classical limit of quantum phases. | — |
| [[newtons-law-of-universal-gravitation]] Newton's law of universal gravitation | prerequisite | Any two point masses attract along the line joining them with a force of magnitude G M m / r^2, where G is about 6.67 x 10^-11 N m^2 kg^-2; the same law governs falling apples and the orbiting Moon. | — |
| [[action-at-a-distance]] Action at a distance | prerequisite | In Newton's theory one mass affects another instantly across empty space with no mechanism or propagation delay, which requires a universal simultaneity that special relativity denies and so demands a new theory of gravity. | — |
| [[cavendish-experiment]] Cavendish experiment | prerequisite | Cavendish's 1798 torsion balance measured the tiny attraction between lead spheres, fixing G and so letting the Earth's mass and mean density be deduced from surface gravity. | — |
| [[newtonian-gravitational-field]] Newtonian gravitational field | prerequisite | The gravitational field g(x) is the gravitational force per unit test mass at each point: radially inward with magnitude GM/r^2 for a point mass, and an integral over the density for an extended source. | — |
| [[newtonian-gravitational-potential]] Newtonian gravitational potential | prerequisite | Because the gravitational field is conservative it can be written g = -grad Phi, with Phi = -GM/r for a point mass (potential energy -GMm/r); by superposition, any distribution gives Phi = -G times the integral of rho//x - x'/. | — |
| [[passive-gravitational-mass]] Passive gravitational mass | prerequisite | Passive gravitational mass measures how strongly a body responds to an external gravitational field, through F = -m^P grad phi; unlike inertial mass, it is tied to gravity by definition. | — |
| [[active-gravitational-mass]] Active gravitational mass | prerequisite | Active gravitational mass measures how strong a gravitational field a body produces, through phi = -G m^A / r for a point source; Newton's third law applied to two attracting bodies forces it to be proportional to passive mass. | — |
| [[gauss-law-for-gravity]] Gauss's law for gravity | prerequisite | The outward flux of the gravitational field through any closed surface equals -4 pi G times the mass enclosed; in differential form, div g = -4 pi G rho. | — |
| [[shell-theorem]] Newton's shell theorem | prerequisite | Outside a spherically symmetric mass distribution the field and potential are exactly those of a point with the same total mass at the centre, which justifies treating planets and stars as point masses. | — |
| [[poisson-equation-for-gravity]] Poisson equation for gravity | prerequisite | The field equation of Newtonian gravity is nabla^2 Phi = 4 pi G rho: two spatial derivatives of the potential are set by the local mass density, reducing to Laplace's equation in empty space; it is the template the Einstein equation must reproduce. | — |
| [[newtonian-tidal-tensor]] Newtonian tidal tensor | prerequisite | The matrix of second derivatives of the Newtonian potential, d^2 Phi/dx^i dx^j, gives the relative acceleration of two nearby freely falling particles, d^2 s^i/dt^2 = -(d_i d_j Phi) s^j; for a point mass it stretches along the radius and squeezes sideways, and outside matter its trace vanishes. | — |
| [[escape-velocity]] Escape velocity | prerequisite | The minimum launch speed that lets a projectile coast to infinity is found by setting kinetic plus gravitational potential energy to zero: v_esc = sqrt(2GM/r). | — |
| [[gravity-as-dominant-long-range-force]] Gravity as the dominant long-range interaction | prerequisite | Gravity is by far the weakest fundamental interaction, but it has infinite range and ordinary matter only attracts, so contributions add without cancelling, while electric charges neutralize; that is why gravity governs planets, galaxies and the universe. | — |
| [[central-force]] Central force | prerequisite | A central force always points along the line to a fixed centre and depends only on the distance r from it; Newtonian gravity of a fixed mass, F = -GMm r/r^3, is the prime example. | — |
| [[conservation-of-angular-momentum]] Conservation of angular momentum in central-force motion | prerequisite | A central force exerts no torque about the centre, so the angular momentum L = r x p is constant in size and direction; the motion stays in a fixed plane and r^2 dphi/dt = h is constant. | — |
| [[keplers-second-law]] Kepler's second law | prerequisite | The line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times; the areal velocity dA/dt = r^2 (dphi/dt)/2 = L/(2m) is constant for any central force, so this law is angular momentum conservation in disguise. | — |
| [[two-body-problem]] Two-body problem and reduced mass | prerequisite | Two bodies attracting each other can be separated into free motion of their centre of mass and the relative motion of one fictitious body of reduced mass m1 m2/(m1 + m2) in the inverse-square field of the total mass. | — |
| [[kepler-problem]] Kepler problem | prerequisite | The Kepler problem asks for every trajectory of a particle moving under an attractive inverse-square force from a fixed centre; writing L = (1/2) m (r-dot^2 + r^2 phi-dot^2) + GMm/r gives the radial and angular equations whose solutions are conic sections with the centre at a focus. | — |
| [[newtonian-effective-potential]] Newtonian effective potential | prerequisite | With angular momentum conserved, the radial motion obeys E = (1/2) m r-dot^2 + U_eff(r) with U_eff = L^2/(2 m r^2) - GMm/r, a one-dimensional problem read off graphically: turning points where E = U_eff, a centrifugal barrier at small r, and a stable circular orbit at the minimum. | — |
| [[binet-equation]] Binet equation | prerequisite | Using u = 1/r as the unknown and the angle as the independent variable turns the radial equation of central-force motion into d^2u/dphi^2 + u = GM/h^2 for gravity, a shifted harmonic oscillator whose solutions u = u0 + B cos phi are the orbits. | — |
| [[conic-section-orbit]] Conic-section solutions of the Kepler problem | prerequisite | Every trajectory in an attractive inverse-square field is a conic with the centre of force at a focus, l/r = 1 + e cos(phi - phi0), where the semi-latus rectum l = h^2/(GM) sets the size, e the shape and phi0 the direction of perihelion. | — |
| [[eccentricity]] Orbital eccentricity | prerequisite | Eccentricity is the dimensionless shape parameter of a conic orbit: 0 for a circle, between 0 and 1 for an ellipse, 1 for a parabola and above 1 for a hyperbola; for a Kepler orbit it is fixed by energy and angular momentum through e^2 = 1 + 2 E L^2/(G^2 M^2 m^3). | — |
| [[elliptical-orbit-geometry]] Geometry of an elliptical orbit | prerequisite | An elliptical orbit has semi-major axis a, semi-minor axis b = a sqrt(1 - e^2), area pi a b and a focus at distance e a from the centre; its closest and farthest points (apsides: perihelion and aphelion, periapsis and apoapsis in general) lie on the major axis, and L^2 = G M m^2 a (1 - e^2). | — |
| [[keplers-first-law]] Kepler's first law | prerequisite | Bound planetary orbits are ellipses with the Sun at one focus; found empirically by Kepler, it follows as the negative-energy solution of the inverse-square orbit equation. | — |
| [[keplers-third-law]] Kepler's third law | prerequisite | The square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis, T^2 = 4 pi^2 a^3/(GM); for two bodies M is the total mass, so a circular binary of separation a has angular frequency Omega^2 = G(m1 + m2)/a^3. | — |
| [[bound-and-unbound-orbits]] Bound and unbound orbits | prerequisite | The sign of the total energy classifies Kepler trajectories: E < 0 gives bound ellipses (circles as the special case), E = 0 the marginally unbound parabola, and E > 0 hyperbolic fly-bys that approach once and escape to infinity. | — |
| [[orbital-energy]] Energy of a Kepler orbit | prerequisite | The total energy of a bound Kepler orbit depends only on its semi-major axis, E = -GMm/(2a); because it is negative, a system that loses energy (for example to gravitational waves) moves to a tighter orbit and speeds up. | — |
| [[negative-specific-heat]] Negative specific heat of self-gravitating systems | foundation | A bound self-gravitating system that loses energy contracts and its particles move faster, so it heats up as it cools; this negative heat capacity makes such systems thermodynamically unstable and drives stellar cores to contract and heat after fuel runs out. | — |
| [[laplace-runge-lenz-vector]] Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector | prerequisite | For the inverse-square force the vector p x L - G M m^2 r/r is conserved; it points from the focus to perihelion with size proportional to the eccentricity, so its constancy means Newtonian orbits close and their perihelion does not move. | — |
| [[bertrands-theorem]] Bertrand's theorem | prerequisite | Among central forces, only the inverse-square law and the linear (harmonic) force make every bounded orbit close on itself, so any deviation from 1/r^2, relativistic or otherwise, makes orbits precess. | — |
