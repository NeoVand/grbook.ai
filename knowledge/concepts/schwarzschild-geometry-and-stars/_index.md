# Spherical symmetry: Schwarzschild geometry and relativistic stars

Static and time-dependent spherically symmetric spacetimes outside and inside matter: spherical symmetry and the areal radius, the general and static spherical metrics and their Einstein tensor, the Schwarzschild exterior with its derivation, mass parameter, Schwarzschild radius and Birkhoff's theorem, the operational meaning of Schwarzschild coordinates (proper radial distance, static observers and the observer at infinity, Flamm embedding, isotropic coordinates, surface redshift), relativistic stellar structure (hydrostatic equilibrium, mass function, TOV equation, junction conditions, regular centre, compactness, exact interior solutions, Buchdahl bound, mass-radius sequences, binding energy) and compact-star astrophysics (degeneracy pressure, white dwarfs and the Chandrasekhar limit, neutronization, neutron stars and their maximum mass, core-collapse supernovae, pulsars). Orbits and light in the exterior, the horizon and collapse to a black hole, and gravitational waves from neutron stars live elsewhere.

| Concept | Tier | Summary | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [[spherical-symmetry]] Spherical symmetry | core | A spacetime is spherically symmetric when its isometries include the rotation group SO(3) acting on round two-spheres, so every event lies on a sphere and nothing distinguishes one direction tangent to it from another. The symmetry constrains the spheres only; it does not by itself guarantee a central point. | — |
| [[areal-radius]] Areal radius | core | The areal radius r labels each symmetry sphere so that its proper area is 4 pi r^2 (circumference 2 pi r). It is defined by the spheres themselves and in curved space is not the proper distance from any centre. | — |
| [[general-spherically-symmetric-metric]] General spherically symmetric metric | core | By choosing the areal radius and coordinates whose radial and time lines are orthogonal to the symmetry spheres, any spherically symmetric metric can be written with only two unknown functions of t and r, e.g. ds^2 = -e^{nu} dt^2 + e^{lambda} dr^2 + r^2 dOmega^2. This form is the starting point for Birkhoff's theorem and for spherical collapse. | — |
| [[static-spherically-symmetric-metric]] Static spherically symmetric metric | core | A static, spherically symmetric spacetime has line element ds^2 = -e^{2Phi(r)} dt^2 + e^{2Lambda(r)} dr^2 + r^2 dOmega^2, with no time dependence and no dt dr cross term. Phi acts as a relativistic potential that controls clock rates, and e^Lambda converts dr into proper radial length. | — |
| [[einstein-tensor-of-spherical-metric]] Einstein tensor of a spherically symmetric metric | core | Feeding the two-function spherical metric through the connection and curvature gives a short list of non-zero components (G_tt, G_rr, G_theta-theta and, if time dependent, G_tr), expressed through the metric functions and their derivatives. These components turn the field equations for stars and for vacuum into ordinary differential equations. | — |
| [[proper-radial-distance]] Proper radial distance | core | The length a chain of rulers would measure along a radial line at fixed time, obtained by integrating sqrt(g_rr) dr; outside a mass it exceeds the difference in areal radius. It is the quantity that shows r is not a distance from the centre. | — |
| [[static-observer]] Static observer | core | A static observer stays at fixed spatial coordinates, moving along the timelike Killing vector; normalization gives u^t = (-g_tt)^{-1/2}, e.g. (1 - 2M/r)^{-1/2} in Schwarzschild. Such an observer is not in free fall, needs a rocket to hover, and uses an orthonormal frame built from the rescaled coordinate basis to turn coordinate results into measurements. | — |
| [[observer-at-infinity]] Observer at infinity | core | A static observer far from the source, where the metric is flat and proper time coincides with Schwarzschild coordinate time t. Clock rates, energies and redshifts elsewhere are conventionally quoted relative to this observer. | — |
| [[relativistic-hydrostatic-equilibrium]] Relativistic hydrostatic equilibrium | core | For a perfect fluid at rest in a static metric, conservation of stress-energy gives dp/dr = -(rho + p) dPhi/dr: the pressure gradient holds the fluid up against gravity, and the effective inertia is rho + p rather than rho alone. With Phi the Newtonian potential and p << rho it reduces to ordinary hydrostatics. | — |
| [[mass-function]] Mass function | core | The mass function m(r) is defined by g_rr = (1 - 2m(r)/r)^{-1}; the tt field equation gives dm/dr = 4 pi r^2 rho, and outside the matter m becomes the constant total mass M. Despite the Newtonian look, the integral is over coordinate rather than proper volume, so m is not simply the sum of local energies. | — |
| [[schwarzschild-metric]] Schwarzschild metric | core | The Schwarzschild metric ds^2 = -(1 - 2M/r) dt^2 + (1 - 2M/r)^{-1} dr^2 + r^2 dOmega^2 (G = c = 1) is the spherically symmetric vacuum solution of Einstein's equations. It describes the exterior of any spherical star and, taken for all r, a non-rotating black hole; it is static, asymptotically flat and fixed by the single parameter M. | — |
| [[schwarzschild-metric-derivation]] Derivation of the Schwarzschild metric | core | Insert the two-function spherical metric into the vacuum field equations: the tt equation integrates to e^{-2Lambda} = 1 - C/r, the combination of tt and rr equations forces Phi = -Lambda up to a constant absorbed in t, and the weak-field limit sets C = 2M. Staticity and asymptotic flatness come out of the calculation rather than being assumed when one starts from the time-dependent form. | — |
| [[schwarzschild-mass]] Schwarzschild mass | core | The constant M in the Schwarzschild metric is the total mass of the source as measured from far away, identified by matching g_tt to the weak-field form 1 + 2 Phi with Phi = -GM/r; it is what Kepler's third law gives for distant orbits. In geometrized units it is a length, GM/c^2 (about 1.48 km for the Sun), and for a star it equals m(R) at the surface. | — |
| [[schwarzschild-radius]] Schwarzschild radius | core | The Schwarzschild radius r_S = 2GM/c^2 (2M in geometrized units) is the areal radius where g_tt of the Schwarzschild metric vanishes and g_rr diverges; it is about 3 km per solar mass. Ordinary stars are far larger than r_S, so it lies in empty space only for a black hole, where it marks the horizon. | — |
| [[birkhoff-theorem]] Birkhoff's theorem | core | Every spherically symmetric vacuum solution of Einstein's equations (without cosmological constant) is locally a piece of Schwarzschild, so it is static even if the source pulsates, explodes or collapses radially. Hence a spherical star and a black hole of the same mass have identical exteriors, and spherical motion cannot radiate gravitational waves. | — |
| [[schwarzschild-coordinates]] Schwarzschild coordinates | core | Schwarzschild coordinates (t, r, theta, phi) combine the static Killing time t, which equals proper time of observers at infinity, with the areal radius r and the angles on the symmetry spheres. They are tied invariantly to the symmetries, but t and r are not what local observers measure, and the chart fails at r = 2M. | — |
| [[isotropic-coordinates]] Isotropic coordinates | core | Isotropic coordinates rewrite a spherical metric so its spatial part is a function times the flat metric; for Schwarzschild r = rho(1 + M/2rho)^2. They make the weak-field limit look like the Newtonian metric and are the form in which post-Newtonian parameters are usually defined, while rho is no longer an areal radius. | — |
| [[flamm-paraboloid]] Flamm's paraboloid | core | The equatorial slice t = const, theta = pi/2 of Schwarzschild, dl^2 = dr^2/(1 - 2M/r) + r^2 dphi^2, can be drawn as the surface z^2 = 8M(r - 2M) in flat 3D space, a sideways parabola revolved about the axis. Only distances within the surface are meaningful; it shows why radial rulers read more than the change in r. | — |
| [[surface-redshift]] Surface redshift | core | Light emitted at the surface of a static star of mass M and radius R reaches a distant observer redshifted by z = (1 - 2M/R)^{-1/2} - 1. It depends only on the compactness M/R, so it is a direct observational handle on how relativistic a star is. | — |
| [[tolman-oppenheimer-volkoff-equation]] Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff equation | core | The TOV equation dp/dr = -(rho + p)(m + 4 pi r^3 p)/[r(r - 2m)], together with dm/dr = 4 pi r^2 rho and an equation of state, governs a static spherical star in general relativity. Compared with Newtonian hydrostatics, pressure adds inertia, pressure gravitates, and the factor 1/(1 - 2m/r) strengthens gravity, so relativistic stars are harder to support. | — |
| [[junction-conditions]] Junction conditions | core | Two solutions can be glued across a hypersurface, such as a stellar surface, only if the induced metric is continuous and, without a surface layer, so is the extrinsic curvature (equivalently the metric and its first derivatives in suitable coordinates). For a star this means pressure vanishes at the surface R and the interior metric joins the Schwarzschild exterior with M = m(R) and e^{2Phi(R)} = 1 - 2M/R. | — |
| [[regularity-at-the-center]] Regularity at the centre | core | If a star contains a centre r = 0, spacetime must be locally flat there, so small circles have circumference 2 pi times their proper radius; this forces g_rr(0) = 1 and m(r) to vanish faster than r. It supplies one of the two initial conditions for integrating the stellar structure equations. | — |
| [[stellar-compactness]] Stellar compactness | core | Compactness, M/R in geometrized units (or 2M/R), measures how relativistic a star is: about 10^{-6} for the Sun, 10^{-4} for a white dwarf and roughly 0.15-0.2 for a neutron star. In any static fluid star 2m(r) < r everywhere, so no horizon can form inside an equilibrium configuration. | — |
| [[schwarzschild-interior-solution]] Schwarzschild interior solution | core | The exact static star of uniform energy density has closed-form pressure and metric functions and matches smoothly to the Schwarzschild exterior at p = 0. Its central pressure becomes infinite as M/R approaches 4/9, although the model is unphysical in implying an infinite sound speed. | — |
| [[buchdahl-limit]] Buchdahl limit | core | A static spherical perfect-fluid star whose density does not increase outward must have R > 9M/4 (2M/R < 8/9); more compact configurations would need infinite central pressure. A star squeezed past this bound cannot stay in equilibrium and must collapse. | — |
| [[causality-constraint-on-equation-of-state]] Causality constraint on the equation of state | advanced | A physically acceptable equation of state must keep the adiabatic sound speed sqrt(dp/drho) below the speed of light. This caps how stiff dense matter can be, rules out the uniform-density star as realistic, and limits the maximum masses and compactness of stellar models. | — |
| [[polytrope]] Polytrope | core | A polytrope is a stellar model with p = K rho^{1 + 1/n}; the Newtonian structure equations then reduce to the Lane-Emden equation, with n = 1 solved by rho proportional to sin(Ar)/(Ar). The ultrarelativistic degenerate gas is the n = 3 case that underlies the Chandrasekhar limit. | — |
| [[buchdahl-interior-solution]] Buchdahl interior solution | advanced | An exact relativistic star with equation of state rho = 12 sqrt(p_* p) - 5p that reduces to the n = 1 polytrope in the Newtonian limit. Its single shape parameter equals the compactness M/R, so it gives a family running from Newtonian to strongly relativistic stars, with causality restricting M/R < 1/6. | — |
| [[mass-radius-relation]] Mass-radius relation | core | For a given barotropic equation of state, regularity at the centre leaves the central density as the only free parameter, so static stars form a one-parameter sequence tracing a curve of mass against radius. Turning points on this curve mark maximum masses and changes of stability, though a turning point alone does not prove instability. | — |
| [[gravitational-binding-energy]] Gravitational binding energy | core | A bound system has less total mass-energy than its dispersed parts, the difference being gravitational binding energy. In a relativistic star M is an integral over coordinate volume while local energies add over the larger proper volume, and forming a compact star releases a sizeable fraction of the rest-mass energy, far more than fusion. | — |
| [[stellar-evolution]] Stellar evolution | prerequisite | Stars spend most of their lives burning hydrogen on the main sequence, then swell into red giants as their cores contract and ignite heavier fuels. When fuel runs out the core settles into a white dwarf or neutron star supported by degeneracy pressure, or collapses to a black hole if no pressure can halt it. | — |
| [[degeneracy-pressure]] Degeneracy pressure | core | Degeneracy pressure is the pressure of a cold, dense Fermi gas: the exclusion principle forces particles into high-momentum states, so compressing the gas costs energy even at zero temperature. Electron degeneracy supports white dwarfs and neutron degeneracy (with nuclear forces) supports neutron stars. | — |
| [[white-dwarf]] White dwarf | core | A white dwarf is the Earth-sized, roughly solar-mass remnant of a low- or intermediate-mass star, made of carbon, oxygen or helium nuclei and held up by electron degeneracy pressure. It has no energy source and slowly cools; its compactness is small, so Newtonian structure equations suffice. | — |
| [[chandrasekhar-limit]] Chandrasekhar limit | core | Electron degeneracy pressure can support at most about 1.4 solar masses (for two nucleons per electron). As electrons become ultrarelativistic, p scales as rho^{4/3} and the stellar radius drops out of the balance, leaving a mass set by fundamental constants alone; heavier cores must collapse further. | — |
| [[neutronization-instability]] Neutronization | advanced | At high enough density electrons are energetic enough to combine with protons into neutrons and neutrinos. Removing the pressure-supplying electrons makes pressure rise too slowly with density, so matter between white-dwarf and neutron-star densities cannot form stable stars and collapses. | — |
| [[neutron-star]] Neutron star | core | A neutron star is a collapsed stellar core of about 1-2 solar masses packed into a radius of roughly 10-12 km, at or above nuclear density, supported by neutron degeneracy and nuclear forces. With M/R around 0.15-0.2 it is strongly relativistic and must be modelled with the TOV equation. | — |
| [[core-collapse-supernova]] Core-collapse supernova | advanced | When the iron core of a massive star exceeds what degeneracy pressure can hold, it collapses in about a second to a proto-neutron star, releasing gravitational binding energy mostly as neutrinos; energy deposited in the envelope can blow it off. Successful explosions leave neutron stars, while failed or fallback explosions, and the most massive progenitors (hypernovae with long gamma-ray bursts), leave black holes. | — |
| [[neutron-star-maximum-mass]] Neutron-star maximum mass | core | Because matter above nuclear density is uncertain, candidate equations of state give different mass-radius curves, with maximum stable masses of roughly 2-2.5 solar masses and believed to be below about 3. Measured pulsar masses near 2 solar masses rule out soft equations of state, and remnants above the maximum must become black holes. | — |
| [[pulsar]] Pulsar | core | A pulsar is a rapidly rotating, strongly magnetized neutron star whose beamed emission sweeps past us once per rotation, producing extremely regular pulses. Spin rates reach hundreds of hertz, below the break-up frequency set by orbital speed at the surface, and pulsars in binaries provide precise neutron-star masses. | — |
