# DIV ch17 · Non-rotating black holes

> Opens Part D by learning to read the Schwarzschild vacuum geometry for all r: it asks what coordinates mean, separates the removable r = 2m singularity from the curvature singularity at r = 0, maps light-cone structure with spacetime diagrams, removes the horizon problem with Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates, and then describes event horizons, collapse, tides, the Newtonian dark-star analogue, observational evidence and theoretical doubts.

**Pages:** printed 319–342 · pdf 334–357 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 4/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

The calculations are single-variable integrals and metric substitutions that a reader of Chapters 7 and 15 can do, with the heavier (17.17) and tidal-frame work left as exercises. The conceptual load is high: separating chart artefacts from geometry, reading tipped light cones, accepting that a singular transformation can yield a valid extension, and grasping time asymmetry of extensions. The diagrams help a great deal but are dense (Fig. 17.7 is deliberately misleading, and Fig. 17.12 is hard to read). The last three sections are easy survey material, partly dated.

## Role in the book

Chapter 15 derived the Schwarzschild metric as the field outside a star and Chapter 16 tested it in the weak field, always with r well above 2m. Chapter 17 is the first chapter of Part D and takes the same metric away from any source, so the reader must confront coordinate artefacts, horizons and singularities. It supplies the tools used throughout the rest of the part: classifying coordinates by the inverse metric, light-cone diagrams, analytic extension between coordinate patches, and the idea of an absolute horizon. The misleading Schwarzschild-coordinate picture and the pair of black-hole and white-hole extensions set up the maximal (Kruskal) extension and conformal diagrams of Chapter 18, while the closing survey of objections points ahead to charged and rotating holes (Chapters 19-20), the singularity theorems (§20.13) and gravitational-wave detections (Chapter 21).

## Learning objectives

- Reader can explain why a coordinate label such as x'^0 carries no automatic meaning as time, and can decide whether each coordinate of a given metric is timelike, null or spacelike from the sign of the corresponding diagonal component of the inverse metric.
- Reader can justify why Schwarzschild's (t, r, theta, phi) are canonical: t is the static world time, r is the areal radius (spheres of area 4 pi r^2), and theta, phi are fixed by spherical symmetry up to rotation.
- Reader can distinguish a coordinate (removable) singularity from a curvature singularity, using the polar axis and the Kretschmann scalar 48 m^2/r^6 as test cases.
- Reader can describe what happens to the character of t and r inside r = 2m and why an observer there cannot remain at fixed r.
- Reader can derive the radial null geodesics of Schwarzschild in the form t = +/-(r + 2m ln|r - 2m|) + const and sketch the resulting light-cone diagram, including the tipping of cones in region II.
- Reader can compute the proper time and the Schwarzschild coordinate time for radial infall from rest at infinity, show the proper time is finite and Newtonian in form, and show that coordinate time diverges logarithmically with r - 2m decaying like exp(-t/2m).
- Reader can transform the Schwarzschild metric to advanced Eddington-Finkelstein form (17.22) and to the null-coordinate form (17.24), and explain why the new metric is regular on 0 < r < infinity.
- Reader can articulate the logic of an analytic extension: two metrics, their maximal regular domains, and an isometry on the overlap; and can explain why the advanced and retarded versions give different extensions (black hole versus white hole).
- Reader can define an event horizon, explain why r = 2m is a one-way null membrane, and contrast this absolute horizon with the observer-dependent horizon of uniform acceleration.
- Reader can narrate spherical collapse on an Eddington-Finkelstein diagram, including the growing interval between received signals and the rapid fading of the star.
- Reader can reproduce the Newtonian escape-velocity argument giving R = 2GM/c^2 and state what it does and does not capture.
- Reader can obtain the radial stretching (+2m/r^3) and transverse squeezing (-m/r^3) tidal accelerations from geodesic deviation and apply them to an infalling astronaut.
- Reader can summarize the main lines of evidence for black holes (X-ray binaries, gravitational waves, stellar orbits at the Galactic Centre, horizon-scale imaging) and the main theoretical caveats (symmetry, singularity theorems, interior matching, quantum effects), including why large black holes have low mean density.

## Teaching approach

A careful, coordinate-sceptical chapter that treats the Schwarzschild metric as an object to be interpreted rather than a formula to be used. The authors first build diagnostic tools (character of coordinates, invariant tests for singularities, light-cone diagrams), then show how a naive diagram in Schwarzschild coordinates misleads, fix it by designing coordinates in which ingoing light rays are straight, and only then introduce the physical vocabulary of horizons, black holes and white holes. The last third changes register to a qualitative survey: a Newtonian analogue, tidal forces, observational evidence, and a candid list of theoretical objections.

**Style:** diagram-driven, derivation-heavy, geometric-first, thought-experiment, observational-astrophysics, survey-overview

**Narrative arc**

1. Motivating worry: once any coordinate transformation is allowed, what, if anything, do the coordinates of a solution mean?
2. Tool 1: the level surfaces of a coordinate have a causal character read off from g^(a)(a); apply it to show Schwarzschild coordinates are canonical and adapted to the symmetries.
3. Tool 2: coordinate patches can fail; distinguish removable singularities (polar axis, r = 2m) from a real one (r = 0) by a curvature scalar.
4. Abstract the metric from its stellar source and notice that r = 2m splits the Schwarzschild chart into two disconnected pieces, with t and r exchanging roles inside.
5. Tool 3: spatial and spacetime diagrams of light flashes, built in flat space first, as the way to read causal structure.
6. Model: integrate radial null geodesics in Schwarzschild coordinates and draw the resulting light cones, which appear to show that nothing reaches r = 2m in finite time.
7. Check with a massive particle: proper time to reach r = 0 is finite (and Newtonian), whereas coordinate time diverges, so the Schwarzschild chart is the culprit.
8. Repair: define a new time that straightens ingoing null rays (advanced Eddington-Finkelstein), obtain a metric regular across r = 2m, and justify the analytic extension by patches and isometries.
9. Interpret: light cones tilt continuously, r = 2m is a one-way null surface, hence an absolute event horizon; the time-reversed (retarded) construction gives a white hole.
10. Application: spherical collapse of a star, signals to a distant observer, fading due to infinite redshift.
11. Bridge and intuition: Newtonian escape velocity gives the same critical radius; Newtonian and relativistic tidal stretching become unbounded at r = 0.
12. Reality check: observational evidence and the theoretical status of black holes, ending with Penrose's point that horizons need not involve high density.

**Signature moves**

- Deliberately scrambling the coordinate labels (theta as x'^0, t as x'^2) to shock the reader out of reading meaning into symbols.
- Using the sign of a single inverse-metric component as a one-line test for the character of each coordinate.
- Building the curved-space diagrams by first drawing the same constructions in Minkowski space (point flashes, stacked snapshots, cones).
- Presenting a diagram that is explicitly flagged as misleading (Fig. 17.7), then letting a proper-time calculation expose why.
- Placing proper time and coordinate time for the same infall on one plot (Fig. 17.8).
- Designing a coordinate transformation by demanding that a chosen family of light rays become straight 45 degree lines.
- Arguing the legitimacy of an extension from the two metrics and their overlap rather than from the singular transformation itself, with an analytic-continuation analogy.
- Pairing every black-hole construction with its time reverse (retarded coordinates, white hole, white-hole collapse exercise).
- Closing a highly mathematical chapter with a list of honest objections and their current status.

### §17.1 Characterization of coordinates — p.321 (pdf 336)

Raises the question of what coordinates mean when any transformation is allowed, illustrated by relabelling Schwarzschild's coordinates so that theta sits in the zeroth slot. Offers a coordinate-independent test: the gradient of a coordinate is the normal to its level surface, and its squared length equals the diagonal inverse-metric component g^(a)(a), whose sign fixes the character. Notes that coordinates may be any mixture of timelike, null and spacelike (common cases 1+3, 1 null + 3, 2 null + 2) as long as the normals are independent, and that symmetries single out adapted coordinates when Killing vectors commute. Applies this to (15.52): for r > 2m, t is timelike and the static world time, r is the areal radius, and theta, phi are the symmetry-defined angles, so Schwarzschild coordinates are canonical.

**Concepts:** arbitrariness of coordinate labels, causal character of a coordinate, normal covector to a hypersurface, Killing-adapted coordinates, Schwarzschild coordinates, areal radius, static spacetime and world time

**Key moves**
- Permute coordinate labels to show that position in the list does not confer meaning
- n_b = partial_b x^(a) = delta_b^(a), so n^2 = g^(a)(a) (no sum)
- Sign of g^(a)(a) classifies each coordinate; independence of the four normals is the only constraint
- Commuting Killing vectors can be simultaneously adapted to coordinates
- Read off (17.2): g^00 > 0 and g^11 < 0 for r > 2m; static metric identifies t as world time; sphere area 4 pi r^2 identifies r (contrast with isotropic rho)

### §17.2 Singularities — p.323 (pdf 338)

Explains that a chart generally covers only part of a manifold. The polar axis is a coordinate singularity cured by Cartesian coordinates. The metric also degenerates at r = 2m (the Schwarzschild radius) and r = 0; the Kretschmann scalar 48 m^2 r^-6 is finite at the former and infinite at the latter, suggesting r = 2m is removable and r = 0 is a genuine curvature singularity. Since g_00 vanishes at r = 2m, it is also a surface of infinite redshift. Contrasts the usual reading (exterior field of a body of radius a > 2m, with an interior solution inside) with the programme of studying the vacuum metric for all r, where r = 2m is null and separates region I (r > 2m) from region II (0 < r < 2m), in which t becomes spacelike and r timelike.

**Concepts:** coordinate singularity, curvature singularity, Kretschmann scalar, Schwarzschild radius, surface of infinite redshift, Schwarzschild vacuum solution, regions I and II of Schwarzschild, interchange of t and r inside the horizon

**Key moves**
- Polar-axis degeneracy as a familiar removable singularity
- Scalar invariant finite at r = 2m but divergent at r = 0; invariants are chart-independent
- Infinite redshift from g_00 -> 0 via (16.4)
- Drop the stellar source and split the chart into disconnected regions I and II
- Sign flips in (17.2) swap the roles of t and r in region II

### §17.3 Spatial and space-time diagrams — p.324 (pdf 339)

Introduces the diagnostic of local light-cone structure: the future light cone at a point is the set of null directions, light rays are tangent to it, and observers' world lines must stay inside it. Two kinds of picture are defined in Minkowski space: spatial diagrams showing where flashes emitted from many points at one time have spread by a later time (spheres, or circles with one dimension suppressed), and spacetime diagrams that stack successive snapshots of a single flash vertically in time, producing a cone (or a V with two dimensions suppressed); the past cone is added as a light front closing in on the event. Curvature will show up as cones that are narrowed, widened or tilted.

**Concepts:** local light cone, null geodesic, spatial light-flash diagram, spacetime diagram with suppressed dimensions

**Key moves**
- Define light cone as the set of null directions at a point; timelike world lines lie inside
- Spatial diagram: flashes from many points, where fronts are at a later time
- Spacetime diagram: stack snapshots t1, t2, t3 of one flash to form a cone
- Suppress one then two spatial dimensions; ellipses show perspective
- Add the past light cone, read as a light front converging onto P
- Announce that curvature appears as squashed, opened or tilted cones

### §17.4 Space-time diagram in Schwarzschild coordinates — p.325 (pdf 340)

Finds radial null geodesics from the Lagrangian 2K = 0 with dtheta = dphi = 0. The t equation gives a conserved (1 - 2m/r) t-dot = k, so r-dot = +/- k and r is itself an affine parameter. Writing dt/dr = r/(r - 2m) and integrating yields outgoing rays t = r + 2m ln|r - 2m| + const and ingoing rays with the opposite sign; t -> -t swaps them. The resulting diagram (Fig. 17.7), in which each point is a 2-sphere of area 4 pi r^2, shows 45 degree cones at large r, cones that close up near r = 2m, and cones tipped over in region II so that observers there must move to r = 0. The diagram appears to say that neither particles nor light reach r = 2m in finite time, but the authors warn that this is misleading because I and II are separate charts.

**Concepts:** radial null geodesics of Schwarzschild, variational method for geodesics, affine parameter, conserved energy constant from time-translation symmetry, light-cone tipping inside the horizon, asymptotic flatness

**Key moves**
- Lagrangian 2K = 0 and Euler-Lagrange equation for t give (1 - 2m/r) t-dot = k
- Null condition then forces r-dot = +/- k, so r is affine
- dt/dr = t-dot / r-dot = r/(r - 2m); integrate to logarithmic curves
- Classify outgoing/ingoing congruences by the sign of dr/dt in region I
- Draw cones: 45 degrees at infinity, degenerate at r = 2m, tipped in II
- Flag the apparent infinite time to reach r = 2m as a chart artefact still to be exposed

### §17.5 A radially infalling particle — p.327 (pdf 342)

For a timelike radial geodesic (2K = 1) with energy constant k = 1 (released from rest at infinity), (dtau/dr)^2 = r/2m. Taking the root appropriate to infall and integrating gives a proper time that is finite all the way to r = 0 and coincides with the Newtonian free-fall formula. Using t instead, dt/dr contains a factor (1 - 2m/r)^-1, the integral has a logarithm that diverges at r = 2m, and near the horizon r - 2m shrinks like exp(-(t - t0)/2m). Fig. 17.8 contrasts the two descriptions. Because t is the proper time of distant static observers it is physically meaningful far away, but it is the wrong clock for infall and the chart itself fails at r = 2m.

**Concepts:** radial free fall in Schwarzschild, proper time versus coordinate time, conserved energy constant from time-translation symmetry, exponential approach to the horizon in coordinate time

**Key moves**
- Geodesic equations (17.12)-(17.13) with k = 1 for rest at infinity
- (dtau/dr)^2 = r/2m; choose the root with r decreasing
- Integrate: tau - tau_0 proportional to r_0^{3/2} - r^{3/2}, identical to Newton
- Coordinate time integral (17.17) with divergent logarithm
- Near-horizon limit r - 2m = (r_0 - 2m) e^{-(t - t_0)/2m}
- Conclude the pathology belongs to the time coordinate, not the particle

### §17.6 Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates — p.328 (pdf 343)

Designs a new time t-bar = t + 2m ln(r - 2m) so that ingoing radial light rays become straight lines t-bar = -r + const. Substituting gives the Eddington-Finkelstein line element (17.22), which is regular and invertible at r = 2m and on the whole range 0 < r < infinity, so the manifold can be enlarged (an analytic extension, Fig. 17.9). The authors answer the objection that the transformation is singular at r = 2m: the real objects are two metrics, each regular on its largest domain, related by an isometry on the overlap r > 2m; inside, a different transformation with ln(2m - r) maps onto region II. The extension is not time symmetric; t* = t - 2m ln(r - 2m) straightens outgoing rays and gives a different extension. The null coordinate v = t-bar + r (advanced time) simplifies the metric to (17.24), with ingoing rays v = const. In Fig. 17.10 one edge of every cone stays at -45 degrees, the other rotates from +45 degrees at infinity to vertical at r = 2m and inward below it, and a radially infalling particle now crosses r = 2m smoothly.

**Concepts:** Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates, advanced time, analytic extension, isometry between coordinate patches, light-cone tipping inside the horizon, time asymmetry of the Eddington-Finkelstein extension, radial free fall in Schwarzschild

**Key moves**
- Choose the new time by demanding ingoing null rays be straight at -45 degrees
- dt-bar = dt + 2m dr/(r - 2m) substituted into (15.52) gives (17.22)
- Check regularity and invertibility of (17.22) for 0 < r < infinity
- Extension logic: maximal regular domains plus isometry on the overlap; separate map for r < 2m
- Note the loss of t -> -t symmetry and the alternative t* extension
- Null coordinate v = t-bar + r gives the compact form (17.24)
- Read the light cones continuously through r = 2m; outgoing photons at r = 2m stay at fixed r

### §17.7 Event horizons — p.331 (pdf 346)

Restores angular information with an equatorial spatial diagram (Fig. 17.11): far away, flash fronts are centred circles as in flat space; closer in they are displaced inward; at r = 2m only the radially outward photon holds its position; inside, every front moves toward the singularity. Hence r = 2m is a one-way membrane that future-directed causal curves can cross only inward, and it bounds the events any outside observer could ever see: an event horizon. Unlike the observer-dependent horizons of hyperbolic motion (§3.8), it is absolute. Using the retarded null coordinate w = t* - r gives (17.26), again regular for all r > 0 but describing the time reverse (Fig. 17.12), a white hole whose horizon admits only past-directed curves inward.

**Concepts:** event horizon, one-way membrane, observer-dependent horizon, retarded time, white hole, time asymmetry of the Eddington-Finkelstein extension

**Key moves**
- Spatial diagram of displaced wave fronts in advanced Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates
- Only future-directed causal curves from I to II can cross r = 2m
- Define event horizon as the boundary of events observable from outside
- Contrast absolute horizon with the Rindler-type horizons of §3.8
- Retarded coordinate w gives the time-reversed extension (white hole)

### §17.8 Black holes — p.332 (pdf 347)

Motivates collapse from stellar evolution: above the white dwarf and neutron star limits no pressure can halt contraction, and spherical collapse ends with all matter at the central singularity. Gives scale (Schwarzschild radius about 1 cm for Earth, 3 km for the Sun) and uses Birkhoff-type reasoning (the exterior of a spherical star stays Schwarzschild) to draw collapse on an Eddington-Finkelstein diagram (Fig. 17.13). A comoving observer passes r = 2m, but regularly spaced signals reach a distant observer at ever longer intervals, the signal sent at r = 2m never leaves, and later ones fall to the singularity. The distant observer in principle only sees the star just before it crossed, but infinite redshift makes it fade quickly, leaving a black hole. A three-dimensional version (Fig. 17.14) shows the collapsing star, the horizon cylinder and the singularity.

**Concepts:** gravitational collapse, black hole, maximum mass of white dwarfs and neutron stars, surface of infinite redshift, event horizon, Schwarzschild radius

**Key moves**
- No equilibrium above stellar mass limits, so collapse continues
- Schwarzschild exterior persists while collapse stays spherical
- Overlay star surface world line and outgoing signals on the Eddington-Finkelstein diagram
- Signal spacing diverges and the star appears frozen but rapidly fades
- Rotate the 2D diagram to get the 3D collapse picture

### §17.9 A Newtonian argument — p.334 (pdf 349)

Shows that a restricted notion of a black hole (light unable to reach distant regions) already follows from Newtonian gravity if light is treated as particles. Energy conservation gives the escape speed v_0^2 = 2GM/R; setting v_0 = c defines a critical radius R = 2GM/c^2, which is exactly 2m in relativistic units. Laplace noted in 1798 that a body with the Sun's density and 250 times its radius would trap light.

**Concepts:** Newtonian escape velocity, Newtonian dark star, Schwarzschild radius

**Key moves**
- E = (1/2) m v^2 - GMm/r; escape to infinity with zero speed means E = 0
- Escape speed v_0^2 = 2GM/R at the surface
- Set v_0 = c to get R = 2GM/c^2, numerically the Schwarzschild radius
- Historical anchor in Laplace

### §17.10 Tidal forces in a black hole — p.335 (pdf 350)

Recalls that a freely falling Newtonian dust sphere becomes a prolate ellipsoid of equal volume, stretched along the fall and squeezed across it. In general relativity the geodesic deviation equation, projected onto the orthonormal frame (17.32)-(17.35) built from Schwarzschild coordinates, gives radial relative acceleration +2m/r^3 times separation and transverse -m/r^3 times separation, so stretching and compression grow without bound as r -> 0. An astronaut falling feet first is pulled apart lengthwise and crushed sideways, ultimately at the atomic level.

**Concepts:** tidal acceleration, geodesic deviation, orthonormal static frame, spaghettification

**Key moves**
- Newtonian dust-sphere picture: elongation along motion, compression across, volume conserved
- Geodesic deviation (17.31) for spatial components of the connecting vector
- Orthonormal frame from normalized coordinate basis vectors
- Result: radial +2m/r^3, angular -m/r^3; signs mean tension and pressure; trace vanishes
- Divergence as r -> 0 and the astronaut narrative

### §17.11 Observational evidence for black holes — p.337 (pdf 352)

Surveys indirect and direct evidence. X-ray binaries: the orbit of a visible star gives the mass of an unseen compact partner; if it exceeds neutron-star limits it is a black-hole candidate, and accreted gas heats and emits X-rays (Fig. 17.18). Cygnus X-1 (rapid X-ray variability found by Uhuru, 1971) has a supergiant companion and a compact mass above nine solar masses, versus limits of roughly 1.4 and 4 solar masses for white dwarfs and neutron stars. LIGO's September 2015 signal matched the merger of black holes of about 36 and 29 solar masses. Active galactic nuclei are modelled with supermassive black holes; stellar proper motions at the Milky Way centre imply about 2.6 million solar masses in a small region. The Event Horizon Telescope imaged the M87 source (data 2017, image 2019) in agreement with lensing predictions.

**Concepts:** X-ray binary, accretion disc, maximum mass of white dwarfs and neutron stars, binary black hole merger, supermassive black hole, horizon-scale imaging

**Key moves**
- Mass function argument: unseen companion too heavy to be a neutron star
- Cygnus X-1 as first strong candidate
- Gravitational-wave detection as more direct evidence
- Galactic nuclei and the Milky Way's central dark mass
- EHT image compared with strong-lensing predictions

### §17.12 Theoretical status of black holes — p.338 (pdf 353)

Lists objections and responses. Too symmetric: charged and rotating solutions keep horizons and singularities (the text points to Chapters 18 and 19, but in this edition these are Chapters 19 and 20). Asymmetries excluded: perturbation studies suggest asymmetries are radiated away and the hole settles to Schwarzschild (Fig. 17.19). Wrong field equations: the Penrose-Hawking singularity theorems need only a metric theory, causality and energy conditions, and show collapse produces inextendible geodesics, though not where or what the singularity is. Unsolved: no realistic interior matched to Kerr, so stability might fail; quantum gravity might halt collapse with a bounce. Finally, Penrose's scaling: mean density goes as 1/m^2, so supermassive holes have low density and gentle horizon tides, meaning horizons do not require extreme physics.

**Concepts:** no-hair relaxation of perturbed black holes, singularity theorems, energy conditions, geodesic incompleteness, interior matching problem, quantum gravity bounce, black hole mean density scaling

**Key moves**
- Symmetry objection answered by charged/rotating solutions
- Asymmetry radiated away in perturbation analyses
- Singularity theorems from weak assumptions; conclusions about incomplete geodesics only
- Open problem of a realistic interior for Kerr
- Quantum effects may modify the endpoint
- R proportional to m and V proportional to m^3 so density proportional to m^-2

## Concepts

### arbitrariness of coordinate labels

*principle · developed* · also: general covariance of coordinate choice, meaninglessness of coordinates

Because a metric solving the field equations remains a solution under any smooth change of coordinates, the labels x^a have no built-in physical or geometrical meaning; significance must be established from invariant properties such as causal character, symmetries or curvature scalars.

**How introduced:** Posed as a puzzle: apply a wild transformation to Schwarzschild and ask someone to interpret it. A trivial reshuffle (theta placed in the time slot) shows you cannot assume the zeroth coordinate is a time.

**Prerequisites:** coordinate transformation, metric tensor

$$
x'^0=\theta,\; x'^1=r,\; x'^2=t,\; x'^3=\phi
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 17.1 presents Schwarzschild with the labels scrambled and asks for an interpretation.

**Where:** DIV §17.1 p.321 (pdf 336)

### causal character of a coordinate

*definition · core* · also: timelike/null/spacelike coordinate, character of a coordinate hypersurface

For a coordinate x^(a), the gradient n_b = partial_b x^(a) is normal to the level surfaces x^(a) = const and has squared norm g^(a)(a). With signature (+,-,-,-) the coordinate is called timelike, null or spacelike when g^(a)(a) is positive, zero or negative; in standard terminology a positive value means the level surface itself is spacelike (it has a timelike normal).

**How introduced:** Offered as the one invariant thing that can be said about an arbitrary coordinate; the computation takes three lines and is then applied to Schwarzschild's inverse metric (17.2).

**Prerequisites:** normal covector to a hypersurface, inverse metric, metric signature

$$
n^2=g^{(a)(a)}\ \text{(not summed)}
$$

**Notes:** The book attaches the words timelike/spacelike to the hypersurface where standard usage would call the hypersurface spacelike/timelike; see gaps_and_pitfalls. The mixtures met most often are one time coordinate with three spatial ones, one null coordinate with three spatial ones (as in Eddington-Finkelstein v, r, theta, phi), and a pair of null coordinates with two spatial ones (as in Kruskal-type charts). The only requirement is that the four gradient normals are linearly independent.

**Where:** DIV §17.1 p.321 (pdf 336); DIV §17.1 p.322 (pdf 337)

### normal covector to a hypersurface

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: gradient normal, unit normal

For a hypersurface given as f(x) = const, the covector n_b = partial_b f is orthogonal to every vector tangent to the surface; raising the index with g^{ab} gives the normal vector, whose norm determines the causal type of the surface.

**How introduced:** Used as the computational route to classifying coordinates: for f = x^(a) the gradient is a Kronecker delta.

**Prerequisites:** covector, inverse metric

$$
n_b=\partial x^{(a)}/\partial x^b=\delta_b^{(a)}
$$

$$
n^c=g^{c(a)}
$$

**Where:** DIV §17.1 p.321 (pdf 336)

### Killing-adapted coordinates

*technique · revisited* · also: coordinates adapted to a symmetry

If a spacetime has a Killing vector field one can choose coordinates in which it equals partial/partial x^a for one coordinate, so the metric components do not depend on that coordinate; several Killing fields can be adapted simultaneously exactly when they commute (vanishing Lie brackets).

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapter 15 as the reason symmetric solutions possess preferred coordinates, then used to certify t, theta, phi in Schwarzschild.

**Prerequisites:** Killing vector field, Lie bracket

**Notes:** Non-commuting symmetries (e.g. the rotation group) still constrain coordinates but less directly. Exercise 17.4 applies the idea to Dingle's metric.

**Where:** DIV §17.1 p.322 (pdf 337)

### Schwarzschild coordinates

*convention · core* · also: canonical coordinates (t, r, theta, phi), curvature coordinates

The coordinates (t, r, theta, phi) in which the Schwarzschild metric takes the form (15.52): t is the static Killing time, r is the areal radius, and theta, phi are standard angles on the symmetry spheres. They are regular only for r > 2m (region I) or separately for 0 < r < 2m (region II), away from the polar axis.

**How introduced:** Justified in §17.1 as invariantly defined by the symmetries, then shown in §§17.4-17.5 to be unsuitable for describing infall through r = 2m.

**Prerequisites:** static spacetime and world time, areal radius, Killing-adapted coordinates

$$
ds^2=(1-2m/r)dt^2-(1-2m/r)^{-1}dr^2-r^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)
$$

**Where:** DIV §17.1 p.322 (pdf 337); DIV §17.2 p.323 (pdf 338); DIV §17.4 p.326 (pdf 341); DIV §17.5 p.328 (pdf 343)

### areal radius

*definition · developed* · also: circumferential radius, Schwarzschild r, radial parameter

A radial coordinate defined so that the symmetry 2-sphere labelled r has proper area 4 pi r^2 (equivalently circumference 2 pi r); it is not the proper radial distance from the centre.

**How introduced:** Read off from the induced line element on t = const, r = const, and contrasted with the isotropic radius rho of (15.63), which lacks this property.

**Prerequisites:** spherical symmetry, induced metric

$$
ds^2=-r^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)
$$

$$
A=4\pi r^2
$$

**Where:** DIV §17.1 p.322 (pdf 337); DIV §17.4 p.326 (pdf 341)

### static spacetime and world time

*definition · revisited* · also: static solution, Killing time

A spacetime is static when it has a hypersurface-orthogonal timelike Killing vector; in adapted coordinates the metric is independent of t and has no dt dx^alpha cross terms, and t is an invariantly defined time (world time) up to scale and origin.

**How introduced:** Recalled from §15.3 to identify Schwarzschild t, valid only where the Killing vector is timelike (r > 2m).

**Prerequisites:** Killing vector field, Killing-adapted coordinates

**Notes:** Inside r = 2m the same Killing vector is spacelike, so the region II metric is not static.

**Where:** DIV §17.1 p.322 (pdf 337)

### coordinate singularity

*definition · core* · also: removable singularity

A place where a metric's components in a given chart become degenerate or infinite only because the coordinates fail there; a different chart covers the points smoothly and all curvature invariants stay finite.

**How introduced:** Introduced with the familiar polar axis theta = 0, pi, cured by Cartesian coordinates, then applied to r = 2m.

**Prerequisites:** coordinate chart, metric tensor

$$
x=r\sin\theta\cos\phi,\; y=r\sin\theta\sin\phi,\; z=r\cos\theta
$$

**Notes:** Finite invariants are evidence, not proof, of removability; the proof is the explicit regular chart (17.22).

**Where:** DIV §17.2 p.323 (pdf 338); DIV §17.6 p.329 (pdf 344)

### curvature singularity

*definition · core* · also: intrinsic singularity, physical singularity, essential singularity, real singularity

A singularity that no change of coordinates can remove, signalled for example by a curvature scalar diverging along curves that reach it in finite affine parameter; at r = 0 in Schwarzschild tidal forces become unbounded.

**How introduced:** Identified at r = 0 by the divergence of the Kretschmann scalar, listed with its many synonyms, and later given physical meaning through infinite tides (§17.10) and the singularity theorems (§17.12).

**Prerequisites:** Kretschmann scalar, coordinate singularity

$$
R_{abcd}R^{abcd}=48m^2r^{-6}\to\infty\ (r\to 0)
$$

**Where:** DIV §17.2 p.323 (pdf 338); DIV §17.10 p.336 (pdf 351); DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354)

### Kretschmann scalar

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: Riemann scalar invariant, R_{abcd}R^{abcd}

The full contraction R_{abcd} R^{abcd} of the Riemann tensor with itself; being a scalar it has the same value in every chart, and for Schwarzschild it equals 48 m^2/r^6.

**How introduced:** Quoted without derivation as the invariant test that separates r = 2m (finite) from r = 0 (divergent).

**Prerequisites:** Riemann tensor, scalar invariant

$$
R_{abcd}R^{abcd}=48m^2/r^6
$$

**Notes:** Ricci scalar and Ricci tensor vanish in vacuum, so a quadratic Riemann invariant is the simplest non-trivial test.

**Where:** DIV §17.2 p.323 (pdf 338)

### Schwarzschild radius

*physical-quantity · core* · also: gravitational radius, r = 2m

The areal radius r_s = 2GM/c^2 (2m in geometric units) at which the Schwarzschild metric component g_00 vanishes; it locates the event horizon of a non-rotating black hole.

**How introduced:** Named in §17.2 as a place where the metric degenerates, given numerical scale in §17.8 (Earth about 1 cm, Sun 3 km), and recovered by a Newtonian escape-speed argument in §17.9.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild vacuum solution, geometrized units

$$
r_s=2m=2GM/c^2

**Where:** DIV §17.2 p.323 (pdf 338); DIV §17.8 p.333 (pdf 348); DIV §17.9 p.335 (pdf 350)

### surface of infinite redshift

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: infinite redshift surface

A surface on which the timelike Killing vector becomes null (g_00 = 0 in static coordinates), so light emitted by a static source there would reach infinity with infinite redshift; in Schwarzschild it coincides with r = 2m.

**How introduced:** Noted in one sentence from the redshift formula (16.4), and cashed in §17.8 to explain why a collapsing star fades from view.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational redshift, Killing vector field

$$
g_{00}=1-2m/r=0\ \text{at}\ r=2m
$$

**Notes:** For Kerr the infinite-redshift surface and horizon separate (ergosphere); here they coincide.

**Where:** DIV §17.2 p.323 (pdf 338); DIV §17.8 p.333 (pdf 348)

### Schwarzschild vacuum solution

*solution-or-model · revisited* · also: exterior Schwarzschild solution, Schwarzschild metric

The unique spherically symmetric vacuum solution of Einstein's equations, with line element (15.52); physically it describes the field outside any spherical mass distribution, and taken on its own for all r it describes an eternal non-rotating black hole.

**How introduced:** Revisited from Chapter 15; the standard reading as an exterior field (Fig. 17.1) is contrasted with the programme of studying the metric for every r without a source.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein vacuum field equations, spherical symmetry

**Where:** DIV §17.1 p.321 (pdf 336); DIV §17.2 p.323 (pdf 338)

### Schwarzschild interior solution

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: interior Schwarzschild solution

Schwarzschild's static, spherically symmetric perfect-fluid solution of uniform density, which can be matched to the vacuum exterior at the surface r = a.

**How introduced:** Mentioned as the standard example of the non-vacuum metric that would replace the exterior inside a real star.

**Prerequisites:** perfect fluid, Schwarzschild vacuum solution

**Where:** DIV §17.2 p.323 (pdf 338)

### regions I and II of Schwarzschild

*definition · core* · also: exterior and interior Schwarzschild regions, region I (2m < r < infinity), region II (0 < r < 2m)

The two disconnected coordinate domains of the Schwarzschild chart: region I with 2m < r < infinity, where t is timelike, and region II with 0 < r < 2m, where r is timelike; in Schwarzschild coordinates they cannot be joined, but Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates show they are pieces of one connected spacetime.

**How introduced:** Introduced after dropping the stellar source: the null surface r = 2m separates the chart into two parts, which the authors insist must initially be treated as separate spacetimes.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild coordinates, coordinate singularity

**Where:** DIV §17.2 p.323 (pdf 338); DIV §17.4 p.327 (pdf 342); DIV §17.6 p.329 (pdf 344)

### interchange of t and r inside the horizon

*phenomenon · developed* · also: t spacelike and r timelike for r < 2m

For 0 < r < 2m the signs of g_00 and g_11 in Schwarzschild coordinates reverse, so t labels a spatial direction and r becomes a time coordinate; decreasing r is then as unavoidable as the passage of time.

**How introduced:** Read off from the sign flips in the inverse metric (17.2) and illustrated by light cones that tip over in Fig. 17.7.

**Prerequisites:** causal character of a coordinate, regions I and II of Schwarzschild

g^{00}<0,\; g^{11}>0\ \ (r<2m)

### local light cone

*definition · core* · also: null cone

At a point P, the set of null directions (tangent vectors with g_ab k^a k^b = 0); its future half contains all possible directions of light rays leaving P, and every future-directed timelike world line through P points into its interior.

**How introduced:** Presented as the main interpretive technique of the chapter, with light rays tangent to the cone and observers confined inside it.

**Prerequisites:** null vector, metric tensor

$$
ds^2=g_{ab}dx^adx^b=0
$$

**Where:** DIV §17.3 p.324 (pdf 339); DIV §17.3 p.325 (pdf 340)

### null geodesic

*mathematical-object · mention* · also: light ray

A geodesic whose tangent is everywhere null; in geometrical optics it is the path of a light ray.

**How introduced:** Taken as known and used to define light cones and then the radial null congruences.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic, local light cone

**Where:** DIV §17.3 p.324 (pdf 339); DIV §17.4 p.325 (pdf 340)

### spatial light-flash diagram

*technique · developed* · also: spatial diagram, wave-front diagram

A picture of space at one moment showing, for flashes emitted from several points at an earlier time, where each wave front has reached; in flat space the fronts are spheres (or circles) centred on their emission points.

**How introduced:** Built first for Minkowski space (Figs. 17.2-17.3) and later redrawn for Schwarzschild in Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates (Fig. 17.11), where fronts are displaced toward the centre.

**Prerequisites:** local light cone

### spacetime diagram with suppressed dimensions

*technique · core* · also: space-time diagram

A plot with time vertical and one or two spatial dimensions shown, made by stacking spatial snapshots; a flash from P becomes a cone (one dimension suppressed) or a pair of 45 degree lines (two suppressed), and in spherical symmetry each point of a (t, r) diagram stands for a 2-sphere of area 4 pi r^2.

**How introduced:** Motivated by stacking snapshots of an expanding flash (Figs. 17.4-17.6), then used for Schwarzschild, Eddington-Finkelstein and collapse diagrams.

**Prerequisites:** local light cone, areal radius

### radial null geodesics of Schwarzschild

*solution-or-model · core* · also: ingoing and outgoing null congruences, radial light rays

The light rays with theta, phi constant; in Schwarzschild coordinates they satisfy dt/dr = +/- r/(r - 2m), giving outgoing curves t = r + 2m ln|r - 2m| + const and ingoing curves t = -(r + 2m ln|r - 2m|) + const.

**How introduced:** Derived with the variational method, checked by the t -> -t symmetry, and drawn as Fig. 17.7; later straightened by Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates.

**Prerequisites:** variational method for geodesics, conserved energy constant from time-translation symmetry, local light cone

$$
t=r+2m\ln|r-2m|+\text{const}
$$

$$
t=-(r+2m\ln|r-2m|+\text{const})
$$

**Notes:** The combination r + 2m ln|r/2m - 1| is the tortoise coordinate in other texts; D'Inverno does not name it.

**Where:** DIV §17.4 p.325 (pdf 340); DIV §17.4 p.326 (pdf 341); DIV §17.6 p.330 (pdf 345)

### variational method for geodesics

*technique · revisited* · also: Lagrangian method, 2K method

Geodesics extremize the integral of K = (1/2) g_ab x-dot^a x-dot^b with respect to an affine parameter; the Euler-Lagrange equations give the geodesic equations, with the first integral 2K = 0 for null and 2K = 1 for timelike curves parametrized by proper time (in +--- signature).

**How introduced:** Invoked by reference to (7.47) to shortcut the geodesic equations for radial null and timelike motion.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic, Euler-Lagrange equations

$$
\frac{\partial K}{\partial x^a}-\frac{d}{du}\frac{\partial K}{\partial\dot x^a}=0
$$

**Where:** DIV §17.4 p.325 (pdf 340); DIV §17.5 p.327 (pdf 342)

### affine parameter

*definition · introduced*

A parameter along a geodesic in which the geodesic equation takes its standard form (tangent parallel-transported with no rescaling term); affine parameters are unique up to u -> a u + b.

**How introduced:** Arises when r-dot = +/- k shows r is a linear function of the affine parameter on radial null geodesics.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic

$$
\dot r=\pm k\Rightarrow r=\pm k u+\text{const}
$$

**Where:** DIV §17.4 p.325 (pdf 340)

### conserved energy constant from time-translation symmetry

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: Killing energy, energy per unit mass k

Because the Schwarzschild metric does not depend on t, the momentum conjugate to t, k = (1 - 2m/r) dt/du, is constant along any geodesic; for timelike geodesics k is the energy per unit rest mass measured at infinity, with k = 1 for release from rest at infinity.

**How introduced:** Appears as the first integral of the t Euler-Lagrange equation for null rays (17.5) and for massive infall (17.12).

**Prerequisites:** Killing vector field, variational method for geodesics

$$
(1-2m/r)\dot t=k
$$

**Where:** DIV §17.4 p.325 (pdf 340); DIV §17.5 p.327 (pdf 342)

### light-cone tipping inside the horizon

*phenomenon · core* · also: tilting of light cones, cones tip over

In coordinates adapted to the geometry, the future light cones progressively tilt toward smaller r as r decreases; at r = 2m the outward edge is vertical so an outgoing radial photon stays at fixed r, and inside every future direction points to smaller r.

**How introduced:** Seen first in the Schwarzschild-coordinate diagram (Fig. 17.7), where cones collapse at r = 2m and reappear sideways in II, then shown continuously in advanced Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates (Fig. 17.10).

**Prerequisites:** local light cone, Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates

**Where:** DIV §17.4 p.326 (pdf 341); DIV §17.6 p.330 (pdf 345); DIV §17.7 p.331 (pdf 346)

### asymptotic flatness

*definition · mention*

The property that the metric approaches the Minkowski metric at large distances from the source, so light cones become 45 degree cones in suitable units.

**How introduced:** Used to explain why Schwarzschild null curves make 45 degree angles as r -> infinity.

**Prerequisites:** Minkowski spacetime

**Where:** DIV §17.4 p.326 (pdf 341)

### radial free fall in Schwarzschild

*solution-or-model · core* · also: radially infalling particle

A timelike radial geodesic; for release from rest at infinity (k = 1) the proper time satisfies (dtau/dr)^2 = r/2m, so the particle travels from r_0 to r = 0 in finite proper time given by the Newtonian formula, passing r = 2m without incident.

**How introduced:** Worked out in §17.5 as a check on the suspicious Schwarzschild-coordinate diagram; later drawn as a smooth dashed curve in Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates.

**Prerequisites:** conserved energy constant from time-translation symmetry, proper time, variational method for geodesics

$$
\tau-\tau_0=\frac{2}{3(2m)^{1/2}}(r_0^{3/2}-r^{3/2})
$$

**Where:** DIV §17.5 p.327 (pdf 342); DIV §17.5 p.328 (pdf 343); DIV §17.6 p.330 (pdf 345)

### proper time versus coordinate time

*principle · core* · also: tau versus t

Proper time is what a clock carried along a world line records and is invariant; a coordinate time is merely a label and may run to infinity along a world line that ends in finite proper time. Schwarzschild t equals the proper time of static observers at infinity, not of infallers.

**How introduced:** Dramatized by Fig. 17.8, where the same infall is plotted against tau (reaching r = 0) and against t (asymptotic to r = 2m).

**Prerequisites:** proper time, Schwarzschild coordinates

**Where:** DIV §17.5 p.327 (pdf 342); DIV §17.5 p.328 (pdf 343)

### exponential approach to the horizon in coordinate time

*phenomenon · developed* · also: frozen star effect

As described by Schwarzschild time, an infalling body near r = 2m obeys r - 2m approximately proportional to exp(-t/2m), so it approaches but never reaches the horizon as t -> infinity; in physical units the e-folding time is 2GM/c^3.

**How introduced:** Derived by keeping the dominant 1/(r - 2m) term in dt/dr (Exercise 17.12), then explained as a coordinate artefact.

**Prerequisites:** proper time versus coordinate time, radial free fall in Schwarzschild

$$
r-2m=(r_0-2m)e^{-(t-t_0)/2m}
$$

**Notes:** The 2m e-folding refers to Schwarzschild t along the infaller's world line. What a distant observer receives is labelled by retarded time, in which the approach (and the redshift of received light) e-folds over 4m.

**Where:** DIV §17.5 p.328 (pdf 343); DIV §17.8 p.333 (pdf 348)

### Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates

*technique · core* · also: advanced Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates, ingoing Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates, (t-bar, r, theta, phi)

Coordinates obtained from Schwarzschild by t-bar = t + 2m ln(r - 2m) (or equivalently the null coordinate v = t-bar + r), chosen so ingoing radial light rays are straight; the resulting metric is regular and invertible for all 0 < r < infinity and covers regions I and II smoothly.

**How introduced:** Motivated by the failure of t for infall; derived by demanding straight ingoing null rays, used to extend the solution and to draw correct light-cone and collapse diagrams.

**Prerequisites:** radial null geodesics of Schwarzschild, coordinate singularity, Schwarzschild coordinates

$$
\bar t=t+2m\ln(r-2m)
$$

$$
ds^2=(1-\tfrac{2m}{r})d\bar t^2-\tfrac{4m}{r}d\bar t\,dr-(1+\tfrac{2m}{r})dr^2-r^2d\Omega^2
$$

**Notes:** Retarded (outgoing) versions use t* = t - 2m ln(r - 2m) and w = t* - r.

**Where:** DIV §17.6 p.328 (pdf 343); DIV §17.6 p.329 (pdf 344); DIV §17.6 p.330 (pdf 345); DIV §17.8 p.333 (pdf 348)

### advanced time

*definition · developed* · also: advanced time parameter, null coordinate v, ingoing null coordinate

The null coordinate v = t-bar + r (equivalently t + r + 2m ln(r - 2m)) whose level surfaces are ingoing radial null hypersurfaces; in (v, r, theta, phi) the Schwarzschild metric reads (1 - 2m/r) dv^2 - 2 dv dr - r^2 dOmega^2.

**How introduced:** Introduced to simplify (17.22); ingoing rays are then simply v = const.

**Prerequisites:** Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates, causal character of a coordinate

$$
v=\bar t+r
$$

$$
ds^2=(1-2m/r)dv^2-2dv\,dr-r^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)
$$

**Where:** DIV §17.6 p.330 (pdf 345)

### retarded time

*definition · introduced* · also: retarded time parameter, null coordinate w, outgoing null coordinate, retarded Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates

The null coordinate w = t* - r with t* = t - 2m ln(r - 2m), constant on outgoing radial null hypersurfaces; in (w, r, theta, phi) the metric is (1 - 2m/r) dw^2 + 2 dw dr - r^2 dOmega^2, regular for r > 0 and describing the time-reversed (white hole) extension.

**How introduced:** Introduced after the event-horizon discussion as the time reverse of the advanced construction.

**Prerequisites:** advanced time, time asymmetry of the Eddington-Finkelstein extension

w=t^*-r

$$
ds^2=(1-2m/r)dw^2+2dw\,dr-r^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)

### analytic extension

*technique · core* · also: extension of a spacetime

Enlarging a spacetime by finding a metric on a bigger manifold that is isometric to the original on the original region and regular beyond it; here the Eddington-Finkelstein metric on 0 < r < infinity extends Schwarzschild region I.

**How introduced:** Illustrated by Fig. 17.9 (hatched region of good coordinates growing) and likened to analytic continuation in complex analysis; the legitimacy is argued via maximal regular domains and an isometry on the overlap.

**Prerequisites:** isometry between coordinate patches, coordinate singularity, Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates

**Notes:** The extension is not unique: advanced and retarded coordinates give different extensions, resolved in the maximal Kruskal extension (Chapter 18).

### isometry between coordinate patches

### time asymmetry of the Eddington-Finkelstein extension

Although Schwarzschild is invariant under t -> -t, each Eddington-Finkelstein extension is not: straightening ingoing rays produces a region II into which things can fall (black hole), while straightening outgoing rays produces a different region II out of which things can emerge (white hole).

### white hole

### black hole

*solution-or-model · core* · also: Schwarzschild black hole, non-rotating black hole

A region of spacetime from which no causal curve can escape to distant regions, bounded by an event horizon; the non-rotating uncharged case is described by the advanced Eddington-Finkelstein extension of Schwarzschild and contains a curvature singularity at r = 0.

**How introduced:** Built up in stages: extension through r = 2m, one-way horizon, then physical formation by collapse, with the name introduced for the dark remnant left as a star fades.

### gravitational collapse

*phenomenon · developed* · also: spherical collapse, stellar collapse

The unhalted contraction of a star whose self-gravity exceeds the support of pressure and stresses; in spherical symmetry the exterior stays Schwarzschild, the surface crosses r = 2m in finite proper time, and all matter reaches the central singularity.

**How introduced:** Motivated from stellar evolution and drawn on Eddington-Finkelstein diagrams in 2D (Fig. 17.13) and 3D (Fig. 17.14).

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild vacuum solution, Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates, maximum mass of white dwarfs and neutron stars

### maximum mass of white dwarfs and neutron stars

*physical-quantity · mention* · also: Chandrasekhar limit, neutron star maximum mass

The largest mass that electron degeneracy (white dwarfs, about 1.4 solar masses) or neutron degeneracy plus nuclear forces (neutron stars, a few solar masses) can support; a compact remnant heavier than this cannot be in equilibrium.

**How introduced:** Used to motivate collapse in §17.8 and to classify Cygnus X-1's compact companion in §17.11.

### Newtonian dark star

*historical-idea · introduced* · also: Laplace-Michell dark star, Newtonian black hole

A Newtonian body so compact that the escape speed at its surface exceeds c, so corpuscular light cannot reach infinity; the critical radius 2GM/c^2 coincides numerically with the Schwarzschild radius.

**How introduced:** Presented as a restricted, pre-relativistic version of the black-hole idea, credited to Laplace.

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian escape velocity

$$
R=2GM/c^2
$$

**Notes:** Physically different from a relativistic horizon: Newtonian light could climb some distance and fall back, and a slower-than-escape projectile could be observed from nearby; the agreement of the radius is partly a coincidence.

**Where:** DIV §17.9 p.334 (pdf 349); DIV §17.9 p.335 (pdf 350)

### geodesic deviation

*law · revisited* · also: equation of geodesic deviation, Jacobi equation

The relative acceleration of neighbouring freely falling particles is governed by curvature: in a frame parallel-transported along one geodesic, D^2 eta^alpha / D tau^2 = - R^alpha_{0 beta 0} eta^beta for the spatial connecting vector.

**How introduced:** Recalled from (10.38)-(10.39) and evaluated in Schwarzschild as the relativistic measure of tides.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann tensor, orthonormal static frame

$$
\frac{D^2\eta^\alpha}{D\tau^2}+R^a{}_{bcd}e^\alpha_a e_0^b e_\beta^c e_0^d\eta^\beta=0
$$

**Where:** DIV §17.10 p.335 (pdf 350); DIV §17.10 p.336 (pdf 351)

### orthonormal static frame

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: static tetrad, Schwarzschild orthonormal frame

The frame obtained by normalizing the Schwarzschild coordinate basis vectors: e_0 along partial_t scaled by (1 - 2m/r)^(-1/2), e_1 along partial_r scaled by (1 - 2m/r)^(1/2), e_2 = r^-1 partial_theta, e_3 = (r sin theta)^-1 partial_phi; it is orthonormal only for r > 2m.

**How introduced:** Stated with the 'holds in these coordinates' symbol and used to project the Riemann tensor for the tidal equations (orthonormality is Exercise 17.18).

**Prerequisites:** tetrad, Schwarzschild coordinates

**Where:** DIV §17.10 p.335 (pdf 350)

### tidal acceleration

*phenomenon · core* · also: tidal force, tidal stretching and compression

The difference in gravitational acceleration across an extended body; for a body falling radially toward a mass m at radius r, the radial separation grows at rate 2m/r^3 (stretching) while transverse separations shrink at rate m/r^3 (compression), with volume preserved to first order.

**How introduced:** First in Newtonian form with a dust sphere becoming an ellipsoid (Fig. 17.16), then from geodesic deviation in Schwarzschild (17.37)-(17.39).

**Prerequisites:** geodesic deviation, Newtonian gravitational potential

$$
\ddot\eta^r=+\frac{2m}{r^3}\eta^r
$$

$$
\ddot\eta^{\theta}=-\frac{m}{r^3}\eta^{\theta}
$$

**Where:** DIV §17.10 p.335 (pdf 350); DIV §17.10 p.336 (pdf 351); DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354)

### spaghettification

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: tidal disruption of an infalling observer

The stretching of an infalling body along the radial direction and squeezing across it by tidal forces that grow without bound near r = 0, ultimately disrupting any material object.

**How introduced:** Narrated as an astronaut falling feet first (Fig. 17.17); the book does not use the popular name.

**Prerequisites:** tidal acceleration

**Where:** DIV §17.10 p.336 (pdf 351)

### X-ray binary

*experiment-or-observation · introduced* · also: black hole binary, accreting binary

A binary system with an ordinary star and a compact companion that pulls gas from it; the gas forms a hot accretion disc emitting X-rays, and the companion's mass inferred from the visible star's orbit can rule out a neutron star.

**How introduced:** Presented as the first class of black-hole evidence, with Cygnus X-1 and the schematic Fig. 17.18.

**Prerequisites:** maximum mass of white dwarfs and neutron stars, accretion disc

**Where:** DIV §17.11 p.337 (pdf 352)

### accretion disc

*phenomenon · mention* · also: accretion disk

A rotating disc of gas spiralling onto a compact object, heated by viscous dissipation so that its inner parts radiate strongly (X-rays for stellar-mass black holes).

**How introduced:** Mentioned as the source of X-ray emission in binaries and as the target imaged by the Event Horizon Telescope.

**Prerequisites:** X-ray binary

**Notes:** The book attributes the X-rays to synchrotron radiation; thermal and Comptonized emission dominate in most models.

**Where:** DIV §17.11 p.337 (pdf 352); DIV §17.11 p.338 (pdf 353)

### binary black hole merger

*experiment-or-observation · mention* · also: gravitational waves from merging black holes, GW150914

The inspiral and coalescence of two black holes, which emits gravitational waves whose waveform can be compared with general-relativistic predictions to infer the component masses.

**How introduced:** Cited as more direct evidence via LIGO's 2015 detection, with details deferred to Chapter 21.

**Prerequisites:** black hole

**Where:** DIV §17.11 p.337 (pdf 352); DIV §17.11 p.338 (pdf 353)

### supermassive black hole

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: SMBH, galactic-nucleus black hole

A black hole of millions to billions of solar masses at the centre of a galaxy, invoked to power active galactic nuclei and inferred in the Milky Way from stellar orbits.

**How introduced:** Introduced through active galaxies and the Milky Way's central mass; used again in §17.12 for the low-density, weak-tide argument.

**Prerequisites:** black hole

**Where:** DIV §17.11 p.338 (pdf 353); DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354)

### horizon-scale imaging

*experiment-or-observation · mention* · also: Event Horizon Telescope image, black hole shadow

Very-long-baseline interferometry with Earth-sized baselines resolving emission near a supermassive black hole, so the strongly lensed image (a bright ring around a dark shadow) can be compared with general relativity.

**How introduced:** Described briefly as further direct evidence, with the 2017 observation and 2019 image of the M87 source.

**Prerequisites:** supermassive black hole, accretion disc

**Notes:** The book calls the EHT an optical array; it is a millimetre-wavelength radio interferometer.

**Where:** DIV §17.11 p.338 (pdf 353)

### no-hair relaxation of perturbed black holes

*phenomenon · mention* · also: asymmetries radiated away, black hole ringdown

Perturbation studies indicate that deviations from symmetry in collapse are shed as gravitational radiation, so a non-rotating uncharged collapse settles asymptotically into a Schwarzschild black hole.

**How introduced:** Used to answer the objection that exact spherical symmetry forces the singular outcome (Fig. 17.19).

**Prerequisites:** gravitational collapse, Schwarzschild vacuum solution

**Where:** DIV §17.12 p.338 (pdf 353); DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354)

### singularity theorems

*theorem · mention* · also: Penrose-Hawking singularity theorems

Results showing that under general assumptions (a metric theory of gravity, reasonable causality, energy conditions and suitable initial trapping or expansion conditions) spacetime contains incomplete causal geodesics; they establish that something singular occurs without locating or characterizing the singularity.

**How introduced:** Invoked to answer the objection that singular collapse is an artefact of GR's specific field equations or of symmetry; details deferred to §20.13.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic incompleteness, energy conditions

**Where:** DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354)

### energy conditions

*principle · mention*

Inequalities on the energy-momentum tensor (for example positivity of energy density measured by any observer) that express physically reasonable matter and are inputs to the singularity theorems.

**How introduced:** Listed among the mild assumptions of the singularity theorems, with a caution that strong fields might violate them.

**Prerequisites:** energy-momentum tensor

**Where:** DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354)

### geodesic incompleteness

*definition · mention* · also: inextendible geodesics

A geodesic is incomplete if it cannot be extended to arbitrarily large affine parameter; incomplete timelike or null geodesics are the standard definition of a singular spacetime.

**How introduced:** Presented as what the singularity theorems actually prove, with the usual interpretation that such geodesics end at a singularity.

**Prerequisites:** affine parameter, curvature singularity

**Where:** DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354)

### interior matching problem

*historical-idea · mention* · also: realistic interior Kerr solution

The unsolved task of finding a realistic matter solution inside a rotating body that joins smoothly to the exterior Kerr metric; without it, the stability of collapse cannot be analysed in detail.

**How introduced:** Raised as an unresolved difficulty in the theoretical status of black holes.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild interior solution

**Where:** DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354)

### quantum gravity bounce

*historical-idea · mention*

The proposal, in some quantum gravity approaches, that quantum effects halt collapse before a singularity forms and produce a rebound.

**How introduced:** Mentioned as a possible modification of the classical endpoint at high density.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational collapse, curvature singularity

**Where:** DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354)

### black hole mean density scaling

*principle · introduced* · also: density proportional to 1/M^2, Penrose density argument

Since the horizon radius is proportional to the mass and the enclosed coordinate volume to the radius cubed, the mean density (mass over 4 pi r_s^3 / 3) scales as 1/M^2, so sufficiently massive black holes form at low density; the horizon tidal acceleration likewise falls as 1/M^2.

**How introduced:** Credited to Penrose to rebut the idea that black holes require exotic high-density physics.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild radius, tidal acceleration

$$
\bar\rho\propto M/R^3\propto M^{-2}
$$

**Notes:** The 'volume' is the Euclidean 4 pi r_s^3/3, a heuristic rather than a proper spatial volume.

**Where:** DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354)

## Key equations

### (15.52) Schwarzschild line element (recalled) · central · DIV §17.1 p.322 (pdf 337)

$$
ds^2=\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)dt^2-\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{-1}dr^2-r^2\left(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)
$$

The metric under study throughout the chapter, written in canonical Schwarzschild coordinates; degenerate at r = 2m, r = 0 and on the polar axis.

**Symbols:** m = GM/c^2 geometric mass; t static time; r areal radius

### (17.1) coordinate hypersurface · supporting · DIV §17.1 p.321 (pdf 336)

$$
x^{(a)}=\text{constant}
$$

Level surface of one fixed coordinate, whose causal character is to be tested.

**Symbols:** parentheses: index held fixed, no summation

### normal covector and vector · derivation-step · DIV §17.1 p.321 (pdf 336)

$$
n_b=\frac{\partial x^{(a)}}{\partial x^b}=\delta_b^{(a)},\qquad n^c=g^{cb}n_b=g^{c(a)}
$$

The gradient of a coordinate is normal to its level surface; raising the index picks out a column of the inverse metric.

### norm of the normal · central · DIV §17.1 p.321 (pdf 336)

$$
n^2=n^cn_c=g^{(a)(a)}\quad(\text{not summed})
$$

The squared length of the normal equals the diagonal inverse-metric component, so its sign decides whether the coordinate is timelike (>0), null (=0) or spacelike (<0) in the book's usage.

### (17.2) inverse Schwarzschild metric · central · DIV §17.1 p.322 (pdf 337)

$$
g^{00}=\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{-1},\quad g^{11}=-\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right),\quad g^{22}=-\frac{1}{r^2},\quad g^{33}=-\frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}
$$

Diagonal inverse components: for r > 2m, t is timelike and r, theta, phi spacelike; for r < 2m the first two signs flip so t and r swap character; g^11 = 0 at r = 2m marks a null surface.

### metric on symmetry spheres · supporting · DIV §17.1 p.322 (pdf 337)

$$
ds^2=-r^2\left(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)
$$

Induced line element on t, r constant, showing the sphere has area 4 pi r^2 and so r is the areal radius.

### Cartesian coordinates near the axis · supporting · DIV §17.2 p.323 (pdf 338)

$$
x=r\sin\theta\cos\phi,\quad y=r\sin\theta\sin\phi,\quad z=r\cos\theta
$$

Change of chart that removes the polar-axis coordinate singularity.

### Kretschmann scalar of Schwarzschild · central · DIV §17.2 p.323 (pdf 338)

$$
R_{abcd}R^{abcd}=48\,m^2r^{-6}
$$

Chart-independent curvature measure: finite at r = 2m (removable singularity) and divergent at r = 0 (curvature singularity).

### null condition · supporting · DIV §17.3 p.324 (pdf 339)

$$
ds^2=g_{ab}\,dx^a dx^b=0
$$

Light rays are null curves tangent to the local light cone.

### (17.3) radial null condition · derivation-step · DIV §17.4 p.325 (pdf 340)

$$
ds^2=\dot\theta=\dot\phi=0
$$

Restricts to light rays moving purely radially.

**Symbols:** dot: derivative with respect to affine parameter u

### (17.4) null Lagrangian · derivation-step · DIV §17.4 p.325 (pdf 340)

$$
2K=\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)\dot t^2-\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{-1}\dot r^2=0
$$

First integral of the geodesic equations for radial light rays.

**Symbols:** K: geodesic Lagrangian

### t Euler-Lagrange equation · derivation-step · DIV §17.4 p.325 (pdf 340)

$$
\frac{d}{du}\left[\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)\dot t\right]=0
$$

Since the metric does not depend on t, its conjugate momentum is conserved.

### (17.5) energy constant (null) · supporting · DIV §17.4 p.325 (pdf 340)

$$
\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)\dot t=k
$$

Conserved quantity from time-translation symmetry along a null geodesic.

**Symbols:** k: constant

### (17.6) · derivation-step · DIV §17.4 p.325 (pdf 340)

$$
\dot r^2=k^2
$$

Substituting the energy constant into the null condition removes the metric factors.

### (17.7) · supporting · DIV §17.4 p.325 (pdf 340)

$$
\dot r=\pm k
$$

Radial coordinate changes linearly with affine parameter, so r is itself affine on radial null geodesics; signs give outgoing and ingoing rays.

### (17.8) · derivation-step · DIV §17.4 p.325 (pdf 340)

$$
\frac{dt}{dr}=\frac{dt/du}{dr/du}=\frac{\dot t}{\dot r}
$$

Chain rule to get the ray shape t(r) without solving parametrically.

### (17.9) slope of outgoing radial null rays · central · DIV §17.4 p.325 (pdf 340)

$$
\frac{dt}{dr}=\frac{r}{r-2m}
$$

Coordinate slope of outgoing rays; tends to 1 at large r, diverges at r = 2m and is negative for 0 < r < 2m.

### (17.10) outgoing radial null geodesics · central · DIV §17.4 p.325 (pdf 340)

$$
t=r+2m\ln|r-2m|+\text{constant}
$$

Outgoing light rays in Schwarzschild coordinates; the logarithm makes them asymptote to r = 2m as t -> -infinity.

### outgoing criterion · derivation-step · DIV §17.4 p.326 (pdf 341)

$$
r>2m\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{dr}{dt}>0
$$

In region I, r grows with t along (17.10), justifying the name outgoing.

### (17.11) ingoing radial null geodesics · central · DIV §17.4 p.326 (pdf 341)

$$
t=-\left(r+2m\ln|r-2m|+\text{constant}\right)
$$

Ingoing light rays; they approach r = 2m only as t -> +infinity in this chart. t -> -t exchanges (17.10) and (17.11).

### (17.12) energy constant (timelike) · supporting · DIV §17.5 p.327 (pdf 342)

$$
\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)\dot t=k
$$

Conserved energy per unit mass for radial free fall; k = 1 means released from rest at infinity.

**Symbols:** dot: derivative with respect to proper time tau

### (17.13) timelike normalization · derivation-step · DIV §17.5 p.327 (pdf 342)

$$
\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)\dot t^2-\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{-1}\dot r^2=1
$$

Proper-time parametrization of a radial timelike geodesic (2K = 1).

### (17.14) · derivation-step · DIV §17.5 p.327 (pdf 342)

$$
\left(\frac{d\tau}{dr}\right)^2=\frac{r}{2m}
$$

With k = 1 the metric factors cancel, leaving a simple Newtonian-looking relation; the negative root is taken because r decreases during infall.

### (17.15) proper time of radial infall · central · DIV §17.5 p.327 (pdf 342)

$$
\tau-\tau_0=\frac{2}{3(2m)^{1/2}}\left(r_0^{3/2}-r^{3/2}\right)
$$

Finite proper time to reach any r including 2m and 0; identical in form to Newtonian free fall from rest at infinity.

**Symbols:** r_0: radius at proper time tau_0

### (17.16) coordinate-time slope for infall · supporting · DIV §17.5 p.327 (pdf 342)

$$
\frac{dt}{dr}=\frac{\dot t}{\dot r}=-\left(\frac{r}{2m}\right)^{1/2}\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{-1}
$$

The factor (1 - 2m/r)^-1 makes t diverge as r -> 2m.

### (17.17) coordinate time of radial infall · supporting · DIV §17.5 p.327 (pdf 342)

$$
t-t_0=-\frac{2}{3(2m)^{1/2}}\left(r^{3/2}-r_0^{3/2}+6mr^{1/2}-6mr_0^{1/2}\right)+2m\ln\frac{\left[r^{1/2}+(2m)^{1/2}\right]\left[r_0^{1/2}-(2m)^{1/2}\right]}{\left[r_0^{1/2}+(2m)^{1/2}\right]\left[r^{1/2}-(2m)^{1/2}\right]}
$$

Explicit Schwarzschild-time integral; agrees with (17.15) when r and r_0 are much larger than 2m, but the logarithm diverges as r -> 2m.

### (17.18) near-horizon approach in coordinate time · central · DIV §17.5 p.328 (pdf 343)

$$
r-2m=(r_0-2m)\,e^{-(t-t_0)/2m}
$$

Close to the horizon the gap to r = 2m decays exponentially in t, never reaching zero for finite t: the frozen-star appearance.

### (17.19) advanced Eddington-Finkelstein time · central · DIV §17.6 p.328 (pdf 343)

$$
t\to\bar t=t+2m\ln(r-2m)
$$

Transformation for r > 2m designed so ingoing radial null rays become straight lines.

### (17.20) straight ingoing rays · supporting · DIV §17.6 p.328 (pdf 343)

$$
\bar t=-r+\text{constant}
$$

Ingoing radial light rays at -45 degrees in the new coordinates.

### (17.21) · derivation-step · DIV §17.6 p.328 (pdf 343)

$$
d\bar t=dt+\frac{2m}{r-2m}\,dr
$$

Differential of (17.19), substituted for dt in (15.52).

### (17.22) Eddington-Finkelstein line element · central · DIV §17.6 p.328 (pdf 343)

$$
ds^2=\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)d\bar t^2-\frac{4m}{r}\,d\bar t\,dr-\left(1+\frac{2m}{r}\right)dr^2-r^2\left(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)
$$

Schwarzschild geometry in coordinates regular and invertible for all 0 < r < infinity (determinant -r^4 sin^2 theta), allowing extension through r = 2m.

**Symbols:** t-bar: advanced Eddington-Finkelstein time

### map for the inner region · supporting · DIV §17.6 p.329 (pdf 344)

$$
\bar t=t+2m\ln(2m-r)\quad(r<2m)
$$

The transformation that shows the r < 2m part of (17.22) is isometric to Schwarzschild region II.

### retarded Eddington-Finkelstein time · supporting · DIV §17.6 p.330 (pdf 345)

$$
t\to t^{\star}=t-2m\ln(r-2m)
$$

Alternative time that straightens outgoing radial null rays, yielding the time-reversed extension.

### (17.23) advanced time · supporting · DIV §17.6 p.330 (pdf 345)

$$
v=\bar t+r
$$

Null coordinate constant along ingoing radial light rays.

### (17.24) advanced null form of Schwarzschild · central · DIV §17.6 p.330 (pdf 345)

$$
ds^2=\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)dv^2-2\,dv\,dr-r^2\left(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)
$$

Compact ingoing Eddington-Finkelstein metric; radial null rays are v = const (ingoing) and dv/dr = 2/(1 - 2m/r) (outgoing in region I, turning inward inside r = 2m).

**Symbols:** v: advanced time

### (17.25) retarded time · supporting · DIV §17.7 p.331 (pdf 346)

$$
w=t^{\star}-r
$$

Null coordinate constant along outgoing radial light rays.

### (17.26) retarded null form (white hole extension) · central · DIV §17.7 p.331 (pdf 346)

$$
ds^2=\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)dw^2+2\,dw\,dr-r^2\left(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)
$$

Regular for 0 < r < infinity; the time reverse of (17.24), describing a white hole whose horizon only past-directed curves cross inward.

**Symbols:** w: retarded time

### (17.27) Newtonian energy · derivation-step · DIV §17.9 p.334 (pdf 349)

$$
E=\tfrac12 mv^2-\frac{GMm}{r}
$$

Conserved energy of a projectile moving radially outside a spherical mass.

**Symbols:** M: source mass; m: test mass

### (17.28) escape velocity · supporting · DIV §17.9 p.334 (pdf 349)

$$
v_0^2=\frac{2GM}{R}
$$

Speed at the surface needed to reach infinity with zero speed (E = 0).

**Symbols:** R: radius of the body

### (17.29) light-trapping condition · derivation-step · DIV §17.9 p.334 (pdf 349)

$$
c^2=\frac{2GM}{R}
$$

Newtonian corpuscular light just escapes when the escape speed equals c.

### (17.30) Newtonian critical radius · central · DIV §17.9 p.335 (pdf 350)

$$
R=\frac{2GM}{c^2}
$$

The critical radius equals the Schwarzschild radius 2m in relativistic units.

### (17.31) geodesic deviation in a frame · central · DIV §17.10 p.335 (pdf 350)

$$
\frac{D^2\eta^\alpha}{D\tau^2}+R^a{}_{bcd}\,e^\alpha{}_a\,e_0{}^b\,e_\beta{}^c\,e_0{}^d\,\eta^\beta=0
$$

Relative acceleration of neighbouring free particles determined by frame components of the Riemann tensor.

**Symbols:** eta^alpha: spatial connecting vector; e_i^a: orthonormal frame (e^alpha_a the dual)

### (17.32) static frame, time leg · supporting · DIV §17.10 p.335 (pdf 350)

$$
e_0{}^a\stackrel{\star}{=}\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{-1/2}(1,0,0,0)
$$

Unit vector along the static Killing direction; defined only for r > 2m.

**Symbols:** star-equals: valid in Schwarzschild coordinates

### (17.33) static frame, radial leg · supporting · DIV §17.10 p.335 (pdf 350)

$$
e_1{}^a\stackrel{\star}{=}\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{1/2}(0,1,0,0)
$$

Unit radial vector.

### (17.34) static frame, theta leg · supporting · DIV §17.10 p.335 (pdf 350)

$$
e_2{}^a\stackrel{\star}{=}r^{-1}(0,0,1,0)
$$

Unit polar-angle vector.

### (17.35) static frame, phi leg · supporting · DIV §17.10 p.335 (pdf 350)

$$
e_3{}^a\stackrel{\star}{=}(r\sin\theta)^{-1}(0,0,0,1)
$$

Unit azimuthal vector.

### (17.36) · derivation-step · DIV §17.10 p.336 (pdf 351)

$$
\eta^\alpha=(\eta^1,\eta^2,\eta^3)=(\eta^r,\eta^\theta,\eta^\phi)
$$

Labels frame components of the separation vector by the corresponding directions.

### (17.37) radial tidal stretching · central · DIV §17.10 p.336 (pdf 351)

$$
\frac{D^2\eta^r}{D\tau^2}=+\frac{2m}{r^3}\eta^r
$$

Positive coefficient: radially separated particles accelerate apart (tension), unbounded as r -> 0.

### (17.38) transverse tidal compression (theta) · central · DIV §17.10 p.336 (pdf 351)

$$
\frac{D^2\eta^\theta}{D\tau^2}=-\frac{m}{r^3}\eta^\theta
$$

Negative coefficient: transversely separated particles are pushed together (pressure).

### (17.39) transverse tidal compression (phi) · central · DIV §17.10 p.336 (pdf 351)

$$
\frac{D^2\eta^\phi}{D\tau^2}=-\frac{m}{r^3}\eta^\phi
$$

Same compression in the second transverse direction; the three coefficients sum to zero, as required in vacuum.

### mean density scaling · supporting · DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354)

$$
R\propto m,\quad V\propto R^3\propto m^3\;\Rightarrow\;\bar\rho\propto m^{-2}
$$

Heuristic showing larger black holes have lower mean density inside the horizon.

## Worked examples

### Example · intro · DIV §17.1 p.322 (pdf 337)

**Problem:** Determine the character and geometric meaning of each Schwarzschild coordinate.

**Method:** Invert the diagonal metric (15.52) to get (17.2); read off signs for r > 2m; use t-independence and absence of cross terms to identify t as static world time; compute the induced metric on t, r constant to show sphere area 4 pi r^2; note the angles are fixed by spherical symmetry.

**Key insight:** Invariant properties (signs of g^aa, Killing symmetries, areas) rather than labels give coordinates their meaning, and they certify Schwarzschild's coordinates as canonical outside r = 2m.

**Result:** t timelike and static time, r spacelike areal radius, theta and phi spacelike angles, for r > 2m; t and r swap character for r < 2m.

**Concepts:** causal character of a coordinate, Schwarzschild coordinates, areal radius, static spacetime and world time

### Example · intro · DIV §17.2 p.323 (pdf 338)

**Problem:** Decide which of the places where the Schwarzschild metric degenerates are genuine singularities.

**Method:** Treat the polar axis by switching to Cartesian coordinates; evaluate the chart-independent Kretschmann scalar 48 m^2/r^6 at r = 2m and r = 0.

**Key insight:** A scalar built from curvature cannot be changed by relabelling, so finiteness at r = 2m signals a chart failure while divergence at r = 0 signals a real singularity.

**Result:** Axis and r = 2m removable (the latter confirmed later by (17.22)); r = 0 is a curvature singularity.

**Concepts:** coordinate singularity, curvature singularity, Kretschmann scalar

### Example · standard · DIV §17.4 p.325 (pdf 340)

**Problem:** Find the radial light rays of Schwarzschild spacetime as curves t(r).

**Method:** Set 2K = 0 with angles fixed; the t Euler-Lagrange equation gives (1 - 2m/r) t-dot = k; substituting yields r-dot = +/- k; form dt/dr = t-dot/r-dot = +/- r/(r - 2m) and integrate using r/(r - 2m) = 1 + 2m/(r - 2m).

**Key insight:** Conserved Killing energy plus the null condition turns the problem into a single quadrature, and shows r is affine along these rays.

**Result:** t = +/-(r + 2m ln|r - 2m|) + const (17.10)-(17.11).

**Concepts:** radial null geodesics of Schwarzschild, variational method for geodesics, affine parameter, conserved energy constant from time-translation symmetry

### Example · standard · DIV §17.5 p.327 (pdf 342)

**Problem:** How long, by its own clock, does a particle dropped from rest at infinity take to fall from r_0 to r?

**Method:** Use (17.12)-(17.13) with k = 1, eliminate t-dot to get (dtau/dr)^2 = r/2m, take the negative root for infall and integrate r^{1/2} dr.

**Key insight:** Nothing special happens at r = 2m in proper time; the formula is the Newtonian one with r read as areal radius and tau as proper time.

**Result:** tau - tau_0 = (2/(3 sqrt(2m)))(r_0^{3/2} - r^{3/2}) (17.15), finite down to r = 0.

**Concepts:** radial free fall in Schwarzschild, proper time versus coordinate time

### Example · challenging · DIV §17.5 p.327 (pdf 342)

**Problem:** Describe the same infall in Schwarzschild coordinate time, and find its behaviour close to r = 2m.

**Method:** Form dt/dr = t-dot/r-dot with the extra factor (1 - 2m/r)^-1, integrate with the substitution r = x^2 to get (17.17); near r = 2m keep the dominant 1/(r - 2m) term (Exercise 17.12 with epsilon = 1 - r/2m) and integrate to an exponential.

**Key insight:** The logarithmic divergence at r = 2m means t is an unsuitable clock for infall; far from the hole t and tau agree.

**Result:** (17.17) and the near-horizon law r - 2m = (r_0 - 2m) exp(-(t - t_0)/2m) (17.18).

**Concepts:** exponential approach to the horizon in coordinate time, proper time versus coordinate time, Schwarzschild coordinates

### Example · standard · DIV §17.6 p.328 (pdf 343)

**Problem:** Construct coordinates in which ingoing radial light rays are straight and write the metric in them.

**Method:** From (17.11), define t-bar = t + 2m ln(r - 2m) so ingoing rays become t-bar + r = const; substitute dt = dt-bar - 2m dr/(r - 2m) into (15.52) and collect terms; then set v = t-bar + r to eliminate dr^2.

**Key insight:** Choosing a coordinate by making a family of null rays straight automatically absorbs the (1 - 2m/r)^-1 divergence, leaving a metric with non-degenerate determinant at r = 2m.

**Result:** (17.22) and the null form ds^2 = (1 - 2m/r) dv^2 - 2 dv dr - r^2 dOmega^2 (17.24).

**Concepts:** Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates, advanced time, analytic extension

### Example · standard · DIV §17.6 p.330 (pdf 345)

**Problem:** Read off the radial light-cone structure from the advanced null form (17.24).

**Method:** Set ds^2 = 0 with fixed angles: either dv = 0 (ingoing) or dv/dr = 2/(1 - 2m/r); convert to slopes in the (t-bar, r) diagram.

**Key insight:** The second family changes sign through r = 2m, so the 'outgoing' edge of every cone is vertical at the horizon and points inward inside it.

**Result:** Fig. 17.10: one cone edge fixed at -45 degrees, the other rotating from +45 degrees at infinity to vertical at r = 2m and inward for r < 2m.

**Concepts:** light-cone tipping inside the horizon, event horizon, advanced time

### Example · standard · DIV §17.7 p.331 (pdf 346)

**Problem:** Obtain the time-reversed (retarded) Eddington-Finkelstein form and its causal structure.

**Method:** Use t* = t - 2m ln(r - 2m) and w = t* - r, substitute into (15.52) and find radial null curves (Exercise 17.16).

**Key insight:** Straightening the other family of rays gives a different extension of region I, in which r = 2m lets future-directed curves out rather than in.

**Result:** ds^2 = (1 - 2m/r) dw^2 + 2 dw dr - r^2 dOmega^2 (17.26); white hole (Fig. 17.12).

**Concepts:** retarded time, white hole, time asymmetry of the Eddington-Finkelstein extension

### Example · intro · DIV §17.9 p.334 (pdf 349)

**Problem:** In Newtonian gravity with corpuscular light, how compact must a body be for light not to escape to infinity?

**Method:** Write E = (1/2) m v^2 - GMm/r, impose E = 0 for escape with zero final speed, evaluate at r = R, set v_0 = c.

**Key insight:** The critical radius is independent of the test mass and numerically equal to the Schwarzschild radius.

**Result:** R = 2GM/c^2 (17.30), i.e. 2m in relativistic units.

**Concepts:** Newtonian escape velocity, Newtonian dark star, Schwarzschild radius

### Example · challenging · DIV §17.10 p.335 (pdf 350)

**Problem:** Find the tidal relative accelerations experienced by neighbouring freely falling particles in Schwarzschild spacetime.

**Method:** Take geodesic deviation (17.31) projected onto the orthonormal frame (17.32)-(17.35); insert the Schwarzschild Riemann components (from Exercise 15.13) to get R^r_{0r0} = -2m/r^3 and R^theta_{0theta0} = R^phi_{0phi0} = m/r^3 (Exercise 17.18).

**Key insight:** Radial separation is pushed apart and transverse separations squeezed, with magnitudes growing like r^-3 and diverging at r = 0; the coefficients sum to zero as vacuum requires.

**Result:** (17.37)-(17.39): +2m/r^3 radially, -m/r^3 in each transverse direction.

**Concepts:** geodesic deviation, tidal acceleration, orthonormal static frame

### Example · intro · DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354)

**Problem:** How does the average density inside a black hole's horizon depend on its mass?

**Method:** Horizon radius proportional to mass; volume proportional to radius cubed; divide.

**Key insight:** Density falls as 1/M^2, so a large enough black hole forms at ordinary densities and has gentle tides at its horizon.

**Result:** mean density proportional to M^-2 (Penrose's observation).

**Concepts:** black hole mean density scaling, Schwarzschild radius


## Analogies and intuitions

### Polar coordinates failing on the axis of a sphere (and at the origin of the plane) → coordinate singularity · strong · DIV §17.2 p.323 (pdf 338)

The familiar breakdown of spherical polars on theta = 0, pi, fixed by Cartesian coordinates, is offered first so the removability of r = 2m seems natural.

**Where it breaks down:** On the axis only the angular coordinate fails; at r = 2m the time coordinate diverges and the fix needs a null-adapted time, and the new chart reveals a causal one-way surface with no counterpart on the axis.

**App idea:** Toggle polar/Cartesian grids on a sphere, then toggle Schwarzschild/EF grids on a (t, r) plane with the same UI.

### Analytic continuation of a complex function beyond its original domain → analytic extension · useful · DIV §17.6 p.329 (pdf 344)

The enlargement of the Schwarzschild spacetime from r > 2m to r > 0 is compared to continuing a function past the region where a power series converges.

**Where it breaks down:** Spacetime extensions need not be unique (advanced and retarded EF give different region II), and smoothness rather than analyticity is the usual requirement; learners without complex analysis gain little.

**App idea:** Show a power series disc of convergence being re-centred to extend a function, then a coordinate patch being extended across r = 2m.

### Stacking a sequence of still frames into a solid block (flip-book) → spacetime diagram with suppressed dimensions · strong · DIV §17.3 p.324 (pdf 339)

Snapshots of an expanding flash at t1, t2, t3 are lifted to their times so the circles form a cone.

**Where it breaks down:** Stacking suggests a preferred time slicing; in curved spacetime the cones must be computed from the metric, not by stacking Euclidean snapshots.

**App idea:** Animated flip-book whose pages slide up into a 3D light cone.

### A one-way membrane (like a valve that lets flow pass only in one direction) → event horizon · strong · DIV §17.7 p.331 (pdf 346)

The horizon's causal property is summarized as a surface crossable only inward by future-directed timelike and null curves.

**Where it breaks down:** Nothing material sits at the horizon and a freely falling observer notices nothing local on crossing it; the one-way property is global and causal, not a mechanical barrier.

**App idea:** Particles and photons fired at r = 2m from both sides in the EF explorer, with the forbidden direction shown blocked by the light-cone tilt rather than by a wall.

### Horizons of a uniformly accelerated (hyperbolic) observer in special relativity → event horizon · useful · DIV §17.7 p.331 (pdf 346)

Recalled to show that horizons already exist in flat spacetime, then used as a contrast: those depend on the observer, the Schwarzschild one hides region II from all outside observers.

**Where it breaks down:** Near a large black hole the horizon locally resembles a Rindler horizon, so the contrast is global rather than local.

**App idea:** Minkowski diagram with a hyperbolic world line and its horizon; switching to an inertial observer makes the horizon vanish, whereas in Schwarzschild no observer choice removes r = 2m.

### Light fronts 'dragged inward' toward the centre → light-cone tipping inside the horizon · useful · DIV §17.7 p.331 (pdf 346)

The spatial diagram describes expanding flashes displaced inward, as if carried by an inflow, until even the outward photon at r = 2m stands still.

**Where it breaks down:** Language of dragging suggests a flowing medium and a force on light; it is really a statement about null directions in a chart, though it matches the river (Gullstrand-Painleve) picture well.

**App idea:** Optional 'river' overlay showing an inflow speed equal to c at r = 2m beside the light-cone picture, labelled as a model.

### Newtonian dark star: escape velocity equal to the speed of light → Schwarzschild radius · useful · DIV §17.9 p.334 (pdf 349)

A particle model of light in Newtonian gravity reproduces R = 2GM/c^2, giving an accessible entry point and a historical precedent (Laplace).

**Where it breaks down:** Newtonian light could leave the surface and fall back, be seen by nearby observers, and has no causal horizon; the numerical agreement relies on a coincidence of factors, and the relativistic r is an areal coordinate.

**App idea:** Side-by-side launches of corpuscles (Newton) and photons (EF chart) from inside the critical radius.

### Falling astronaut stretched head to foot and squeezed at the sides → tidal acceleration · strong · DIV §17.10 p.336 (pdf 351)

Turns the signs of (17.37)-(17.39) into a bodily picture, and ties back to the Newtonian dust sphere becoming an ellipsoid.

**Where it breaks down:** Implies tides are always deadly at the horizon; for supermassive holes they are negligible there (as §17.12 notes), and the book's 'infinite force on the feet' language is Newtonian.

**App idea:** Mass slider showing where along the fall the astronaut's stretch becomes lethal relative to the horizon.


## Misconceptions addressed

### A coordinate called t, or listed first, must be a time. · DIV §17.1 p.321 (pdf 336)

**Correction:** Coordinate labels are arbitrary; whether a coordinate is timelike is decided by the sign of g^(a)(a), and Schwarzschild t is actually spacelike inside r = 2m.

**Why tempting:** Habits from Newtonian and special-relativistic physics where t is always time.

### Because metric components blow up at r = 2m, spacetime is singular there. · DIV §17.2 p.323 (pdf 338)

**Correction:** Curvature invariants are finite at r = 2m and Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates give a perfectly regular metric; only r = 0 is a curvature singularity.

**Why tempting:** Infinite components look like infinite physics, and the word 'singularity' is used for both.

### Infalling matter never reaches the horizon, because it takes infinite time. · DIV §17.5 p.328 (pdf 343)

**Correction:** Only Schwarzschild coordinate time diverges; the infalling proper time to r = 2m and to r = 0 is finite, and the EF diagram shows a smooth crossing.

**Why tempting:** Fig. 17.7 and the distant-observer description both suggest a freeze at r = 2m.

### The Schwarzschild-coordinate diagram (Fig. 17.7) correctly shows how regions I and II are joined. · DIV §17.4 p.327 (pdf 342)

**Correction:** In Schwarzschild coordinates I and II are disconnected charts; the picture misleads and must be replaced by one in a chart that covers r = 2m.

**Why tempting:** Both regions are drawn on one sheet of paper with a shared line r = 2m.

### An extension through r = 2m is illegitimate because the transformation (17.19) is singular there. · DIV §17.6 p.329 (pdf 344)

**Correction:** The transformation is only a device; the real content is two metrics regular on their maximal domains that agree (are isometric) on the overlap.

**Why tempting:** One expects results obtained with a singular map to inherit its singularity.

### Inside the horizon an observer with a strong enough rocket can hover at fixed r or move outward. · DIV §17.4 p.326 (pdf 341)

**Correction:** In region II r is timelike, so every future-directed world line has decreasing r, just as one cannot avoid moving forward in time.

**Why tempting:** Outside, rockets can always resist gravity; the horizon looks like just a stronger field.

### A light ray aimed straight outward at r = 2m moves outward at speed c. · DIV §17.6 p.330 (pdf 345)

**Correction:** At r = 2m the outgoing edge of the light cone is vertical in EF coordinates: the radially outgoing photon stays at r = 2m (it is a generator of the horizon).

**Why tempting:** Local measurements always give c, so it seems light must make progress.

### A black-hole horizon is just like the horizon of an accelerated observer, depending on who is looking. · DIV §17.7 p.331 (pdf 346)

**Correction:** The Schwarzschild horizon is absolute: no external observer, however moving, can receive signals from region II.

**Why tempting:** Both are null surfaces bounding what can be seen, and the equivalence principle links gravity with acceleration.

### Because Schwarzschild is time symmetric, its extension through r = 2m must be too. · DIV §17.6 p.329 (pdf 344)

**Correction:** Each Eddington-Finkelstein extension breaks t -> -t; the advanced one gives a black hole, the retarded one a different extension, the white hole.

**Why tempting:** The original metric is invariant under time reversal.

### A distant observer watches a collapsing star hang forever, clearly visible, at its Schwarzschild radius. · DIV §17.8 p.333 (pdf 348)

**Correction:** In principle the last image is of the star just outside r = 2m, but the infinite redshift makes the received light dim and redden extremely quickly, so the star effectively vanishes.

**Why tempting:** The frozen-star language focuses on arrival times and forgets intensity and redshift.

### Black holes require extraordinarily high densities to form. · DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354)

**Correction:** Mean density within the horizon scales as 1/M^2; very massive black holes can form at densities comparable to or below everyday matter.

**Why tempting:** Stellar-mass examples (neutron-star densities) dominate popular accounts.

### The prediction of singularities is only an artefact of perfect spherical symmetry. · DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354)

**Correction:** Perturbations appear to be radiated away, and the singularity theorems establish incomplete geodesics under weak, symmetry-free assumptions.

**Why tempting:** Perfectly radial infall to a single point looks contrived.

### Crossing the horizon is violent because tides there are always enormous. · DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354)

**Correction:** Horizon tides scale as 1/M^2; for supermassive black holes they are tiny, while for stellar-mass holes they are lethal well outside the horizon. Unbounded tides occur at r = 0.

**Why tempting:** The astronaut story and the name 'black hole' suggest destruction at the edge.

### The Newtonian dark star and the relativistic black hole are the same object. · DIV §17.9 p.334 (pdf 349)

**Correction:** They share the radius 2GM/c^2, but the Newtonian object has no causal horizon: corpuscles can rise and fall back and be seen nearby, whereas nothing inside r = 2m can move outward at all.

**Why tempting:** The identical formula looks like a derivation of the Schwarzschild radius from Newton.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Schwarzschild r measures the distance from the centre. · DIV §17.1 p.322 (pdf 337)

**Correction:** r is defined by the area of symmetry spheres; proper radial distance differs, and inside the horizon r is not a spatial coordinate at all.

**Why tempting:** In flat space the areal radius and radial distance coincide, and r looks like the usual polar coordinate.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The singularity at r = 0 is a point at the centre of space that one could avoid by steering sideways. · DIV §17.7 p.331 (pdf 346)

**Correction:** Inside the horizon r = 0 lies in every future direction because r is timelike; it is better thought of as a moment in the future than a place.

**Why tempting:** Diagrams draw r = 0 as a vertical line or a central dot (Fig. 17.11 marks it with a cross).

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Flashes from every point · DIV §17.3 p.324 (pdf 339)

**Setup:** At one instant a light flash is emitted from each point of interest; at a later instant we record where each wave front has reached.

**Lesson:** The shape and position of the fronts relative to their sources reveal the local light-cone structure; in flat space they are centred spheres, near a black hole they are displaced inward, and inside r = 2m every front moves inward.

**App idea:** Flash-field tool shared by Figs. 17.2, 17.3 and 17.11 redesigns.

### Particle dropped from rest at infinity · DIV §17.5 p.327 (pdf 342)

**Setup:** A test particle with zero speed at infinity (k = 1) falls radially into the Schwarzschild geometry; compare its own clock with the time assigned by distant static observers.

**Lesson:** Proper time to the horizon and singularity is finite, while coordinate time diverges; the horizon is a feature of the chart's clock, not an obstacle to the particle.

**App idea:** Two-clock infall plot (Fig. 17.8 redesign).

### Signalling from the surface of a collapsing star · DIV §17.8 p.333 (pdf 348)

**Setup:** An observer riding the surface of a collapsing star sends light pulses at equal intervals of their own proper time toward a distant observer.

**Lesson:** Received pulses spread out without limit, the pulse sent at r = 2m never leaves, later ones fall into the singularity; combined with infinite redshift the star fades out, leaving a black hole.

**App idea:** Collapse and signals simulator (Fig. 17.13 redesign).

### Laplace's light-trapping star · DIV §17.9 p.334 (pdf 349)

**Setup:** Treat light as particles moving at c and ask how small a body of given mass (or how large a body of given density) must be for the escape speed to exceed c.

**Lesson:** Newtonian gravity already implies a critical radius 2GM/c^2, a historical precursor of the black-hole idea.

**App idea:** Escape-speed launcher (Fig. 17.15 redesign).

### Falling sphere of dust · DIV §17.10 p.335 (pdf 350)

**Setup:** A spherical cloud of non-interacting particles falls freely toward a massive body.

**Lesson:** Differential gravity turns the sphere into a prolate ellipsoid of equal volume; relativistically the same stretching and squeezing follow from geodesic deviation and diverge at r = 0.

**App idea:** Falling dust cloud (Fig. 17.16 redesign).

### Astronaut falling feet first · DIV §17.10 p.336 (pdf 351)

**Setup:** An astronaut falls radially into a black hole, feet leading.

**Lesson:** Head-to-foot stretching and side-to-side crushing grow without bound near the singularity, eventually disrupting even atoms; for supermassive holes this happens deep inside, not at the horizon.

**App idea:** Tidal-strain gauge with mass slider (Fig. 17.17 redesign).

### Disguising the Schwarzschild solution · DIV §17.1 p.321 (pdf 336)

**Setup:** Apply an arbitrarily complicated coordinate transformation to Schwarzschild, or simply permute the coordinate labels, and hand the result to someone to interpret.

**Lesson:** The field equations are still satisfied but the coordinates carry no evident meaning; one must use invariant tests to recover the physics.

**App idea:** Tutor puzzle: present a scrambled metric and ask the learner to identify the time coordinate using g^aa.


## Applications and observations

- **Schwarzschild radii of familiar bodies** (numerical-estimate, DIV §17.8 p.333 (pdf 348)): Gives the scale of the horizon for the Earth and the Sun to make r = 2m concrete. Key numbers: Earth about 1 cm (precisely about 8.9 mm, own check); Sun about 3 km (2.95 km). General: r_s = 2GM/c^2, about 2.95 km per solar mass.
- **Laplace's light-trapping star** (numerical-estimate, DIV §17.9 p.335 (pdf 350)): The book cites Laplace's claim that a star of solar density and 250 times the Sun's radius would trap light. Key numbers: Book: 250 solar radii at solar density. Own check: at the Sun's mean density (1.4 g/cm^3) the critical radius is about 490 solar radii; Laplace's 250 figure corresponds to a body of the Earth's density (about 5.5 g/cm^3) with a diameter 250 times the Sun's.
- **Cygnus X-1** (astrophysical-system, DIV §17.11 p.337 (pdf 352)): X-ray binary with a supergiant companion; X-ray variability discovered by Uhuru in 1971; compact object's mass exceeds neutron-star limits. Key numbers: Book: compact mass above 9 solar masses; white dwarf limit about 1.4, neutron star limit taken as about 4 solar masses. Modern estimates (not in book): about 21 solar masses, neutron-star maximum about 2.2-2.5.
- **X-ray binaries as black-hole candidates** (observation, DIV §17.11 p.337 (pdf 352)): Orbital motion of the visible star yields the mass of the unseen companion; accretion-disc X-rays show a compact object.
- **GW150914 binary black hole merger** (detection-event, DIV §17.11 p.338 (pdf 353)): LIGO signal in September 2015 consistent with GR predictions for merging black holes; many further events since, treated in Chapter 21. Key numbers: Component masses about 36 and 29 solar masses.
- **Active galactic nuclei** (astrophysical-system, DIV §17.11 p.338 (pdf 353)): Unusual line and radio emission in active galaxies explained by accretion onto central supermassive black holes. Key numbers: Central masses of millions to billions of solar masses.
- **Galactic Centre dark mass (Sgr A*)** (observation, DIV §17.11 p.338 (pdf 353)): Proper motions of stars near the Milky Way centre imply a compact invisible mass for which a black hole is the only plausible explanation. Key numbers: Book: about 2.6 million solar masses; radius bound printed as '2^10 km', evidently a misprint (it would be smaller than the Schwarzschild radius, about 7.7 million km, contradicting the text); a bound of order 10^10 km (about 100 AU, the S2 pericentre scale) is presumably intended. Modern mass: about 4.3 million solar masses.
- **Event Horizon Telescope image of M87*** (detection-event, DIV §17.11 p.338 (pdf 353)): Horizon-scale image of the accretion flow around the M87 black hole, compared with strong-lensing predictions. Key numbers: Observed 2017, image released 2019. (Not in book: observing wavelength 1.3 mm, mass about 6.5 billion solar masses; Sgr A* image released 2022.)
- **Mean density of black holes** (numerical-estimate, DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354)): Density scales as 1/M^2; the book claims the Galactic Centre black hole has mean density about 10% of water. Key numbers: Own estimate: mean density about 1.8e16 g/cm^3 x (M_sun/M)^2, so about 3e3 g/cm^3 for 2.6 million solar masses (about 1e3 g/cm^3 for 4.3 million); 0.1 g/cm^3 requires about 4e8 solar masses. The book's figure fits much heavier holes such as M87* rather than Sgr A*.
- **Tidal acceleration at a supermassive horizon** (numerical-estimate, DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354)): Book asserts horizon tides for the Galactic Centre black hole are comparable to tides at the Earth's surface, so horizon formation involves no extreme physics. Key numbers: Own estimate: tidal gradient at the horizon c^6/(4G^2M^2), about 1e10 s^-2 for 1 solar mass, about 1.5e-3 s^-2 for 2.6 million solar masses (a few mm/s^2 across a 2 m body), negligible for a person though far larger than the Earth's own tidal gradient (about 3e-6 s^-2). For 10 solar masses the horizon value is about 1e8 s^-2, lethal.
- **Fading of a collapsing star** (numerical-estimate, DIV §17.8 p.333 (pdf 348)): Infinite redshift at r = 2m makes the collapsing star's light fade rapidly for a distant observer. Key numbers: Own estimate: (17.18) has e-folding 2m in Schwarzschild t, but light reaching a distant observer is labelled by retarded time u = t - r*, and near the horizon u grows twice as fast as t, so the observed redshift and dimming go as exp(u/4m). The timescale is 4GM/c^3, about 2e-5 s per solar mass, so a stellar collapse disappears from view within about a millisecond.

## Historical notes

- **Pierre-Simon Laplace (1798):** Argued from Newtonian gravity and a corpuscular theory of light that a sufficiently large, dense star would prevent its light from escaping. — An early precursor of the black-hole concept; the book uses it to show the critical radius 2GM/c^2 has a Newtonian counterpart. (DIV §17.9 p.334 (pdf 349))
- **Karl Schwarzschild:** Found, besides the vacuum solution, the static spherically symmetric perfect-fluid interior solution. — Recalled as the kind of matter solution that replaces the vacuum metric inside a star in the standard interpretation. (DIV §17.2 p.323 (pdf 338))
- **Uhuru satellite team (1971):** Detection of rapid X-ray variability from Cygnus X-1, whose compact companion was later shown to exceed plausible neutron-star masses. — First strong observational evidence that black holes exist. (DIV §17.11 p.337 (pdf 352))
- **LIGO (2015):** Detection in September 2015 of gravitational waves matching the merger of black holes of about 36 and 29 solar masses. — More direct evidence for black holes and for general relativity in the strong-field regime. (DIV §17.11 p.337 (pdf 352))
- **Event Horizon Telescope collaboration (2017-2019):** Observed the central object of Messier 87 in 2017 and released an image in 2019 consistent with general-relativistic lensing predictions. — Horizon-scale evidence for supermassive black holes. (DIV §17.11 p.338 (pdf 353))
- **Roger Penrose, Stephen Hawking:** Proved singularity theorems showing that gravitational collapse under weak assumptions leads to incomplete geodesics. — Suggests singularities are generic, not artefacts of symmetry or of the exact Einstein equations. (DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354))
- **Roger Penrose:** Pointed out that horizon formation needs no high density because black-hole mean density decreases as the inverse square of mass. — Undercuts the argument that black holes lie in a regime where classical GR cannot be trusted. (DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354))
- **Arthur Eddington, David Finkelstein:** The coordinates regular across r = 2m bear their names (the book gives no dates or history). — Naming only; the method of straightening null rays is the pedagogical point. (DIV §17.6 p.328 (pdf 343))

## Notation and conventions

- **Metric signature:** (+, -, -, -): ds^2 = (1 - 2m/r) dt^2 - ... ; timelike intervals positive, spatial parts negative. — Hence a coordinate with g^(a)(a) > 0 is called timelike and 2K = +1 on timelike geodesics parametrized by proper time. (DIV §17.1 p.322 (pdf 337))
- **Relativistic (geometric) units and m:** G = c = 1 with m = GM/c^2 the geometric mass, so the Schwarzschild radius is 2m; physical form R = 2GM/c^2 given in §17.9. (DIV §17.9 p.335 (pdf 350))
- **Parenthesized index x^(a):** An index in parentheses is a fixed label, not a summation index; g^(a)(a) is a single component. (DIV §17.1 p.321 (pdf 336))
- **Overdot:** Derivative with respect to an affine parameter u for null geodesics (§17.4) and with respect to proper time tau for timelike geodesics (§17.5). (DIV §17.4 p.325 (pdf 340))
- **Star-equals symbol:** An equals sign with a star over it marks a relation that holds only in the stated coordinate system (here Schwarzschild coordinates) rather than tensorially. (DIV §17.10 p.335 (pdf 350))
- **Latin versus Greek indices in geodesic deviation:** Latin a, b, c, d are coordinate indices 0-3; Greek alpha, beta run over spatial frame values 1-3; the frame vectors are e_i^a with i = 0..3, e_0 timelike. (DIV §17.10 p.335 (pdf 350))
- **Eddington-Finkelstein time and null coordinates:** t-bar = t + 2m ln(r - 2m) (advanced), t* = t - 2m ln(r - 2m) (retarded), v = t-bar + r (advanced time), w = t* - r (retarded time). — Some texts define v = t + r* directly with the tortoise coordinate r*; D'Inverno goes via t-bar. (DIV §17.6 p.330 (pdf 345))
- **Region labels:** Region I: 2m < r < infinity; region II: 0 < r < 2m. — Chapter 18's Kruskal extension adds further regions. (DIV §17.2 p.323 (pdf 338))
- **Diagram conventions:** Time axis vertical, radial coordinate horizontal; a wavy vertical line at r = 0 denotes the singularity; hatching marks matter or the domain where coordinates are good; in (t, r) diagrams each point represents a 2-sphere of area 4 pi r^2; perspective ellipses show circular fronts. (DIV §17.4 p.326 (pdf 341))
- **Naming of causal character of coordinates and hypersurfaces:** The book calls both a coordinate and its level hypersurface timelike when g^(a)(a) > 0. — Standard usage calls t = const a spacelike hypersurface (timelike normal); the book's labels effectively describe the normal. (DIV §17.1 p.322 (pdf 337))
- **Exercise keying:** Exercises labelled 17.N (§17.K) indicating the section they practise. (DIV p.340 (pdf 355))

## Exercises

About 18 exercises (pdf pages 355, 356).

**Skills practiced**
- recognizing a known metric under relabelled or transformed coordinates
- determining the causal character of coordinates from the inverse metric
- adapting coordinates to commuting Killing vectors
- transforming line elements (Cartesian, Eddington-Finkelstein, advanced and retarded null forms)
- integrating geodesic first integrals and identifying affine parameters
- sketching null congruences and light cones from explicit t(r) curves
- asymptotic expansion near r = 2m
- computing inverse metrics
- drawing spacetime diagrams including white-hole collapse
- verifying orthonormal frames and projecting the Riemann tensor for tidal equations
- comparing relativistic infall with Newtonian free fall

- **17.1** (intro): Given a line element in which Schwarzschild's four coordinates have been permuted (the angle phi playing the radial role, t appearing inside a sine), identify what spacetime it is and what each coordinate means. — *Directly exercises the chapter's opening warning that labels are meaningless.* Skills: coordinate interpretation, recognizing Schwarzschild
- **17.3** (standard): Classify the coordinates as timelike, null or spacelike in two unfamiliar metrics: a Weyl-type static axisymmetric metric in (t, rho, z, phi) and a metric in (u, r, x, y) with a du dr cross term. — *Shows null coordinates in practice (g^uu = 0) and that off-diagonal metrics require the full inverse.* Skills: inverse metric, causal character of coordinates
- **17.4** (intro): Start from Dingle's general diagonal metric with arbitrary functions of all coordinates and impose that the three spatial coordinate vector fields are commuting Killing vectors; determine the simplified form. — *Concrete check of the adapted-coordinates statement in §17.1.* Skills: Killing-adapted coordinates
- **17.8** (intro): Build the Schwarzschild-coordinate null congruence graphically by sketching a chain of logarithmic curves, shifting the axis to r = 2m, and adding constants; then find the ray slope at r = 0. — *A scaffolded route to drawing Fig. 17.7 by hand, well suited to an interactive plotting exercise.* Skills: curve sketching, null congruences
- **17.10** (intro): Integrate the proper-time equation for radial infall and show the result matches Newtonian radial fall from rest at infinity toward a point mass. — *Establishes the surprising exact coincidence with Newton claimed in the text.* Skills: integration, Newtonian comparison
- **17.12** (standard): Expand the coordinate-time infall equation near r = 2m using a small parameter measuring the fractional distance from the horizon and derive the exponential approach law. — *Quantifies the frozen-star effect and its e-folding time 2m.* Skills: asymptotic expansion, logarithmic integration
- **17.14** (standard): Compute the contravariant components of the Eddington-Finkelstein metric (17.22). — *Confirms explicitly that (17.22) is invertible at r = 2m: the inverse has g^{t-bar t-bar} = 1 + 2m/r, g^{t-bar r} = -2m/r and g^rr = -(1 - 2m/r). So t-bar stays timelike for every r > 0, while r is spacelike outside, null at r = 2m and timelike inside, all read off from a chart that is regular across the horizon.* Skills: matrix inversion, regularity checks
- **17.17** (standard): Draw the white-hole counterpart of the collapse diagram and describe what an outside observer would see. — *Forces the reader to think through time reversal of horizons and emission, a common conceptual stumbling block.* Skills: spacetime diagrams, time reversal
- **17.18** (challenging): Verify the static frame is orthonormal, derive the three tidal equations from geodesic deviation using previously computed Riemann components, and argue qualitatively that radial separation grows without bound near r = 0. — *Supplies the omitted derivation behind the chapter's tidal-force claims.* Skills: tetrad projection, Riemann components, physical reasoning

## Teaching gems

### Scramble the coordinate labels of a known metric, then recover their meaning using only the sign of each g^(a)(a). · DIV §17.1 p.321 (pdf 336)

**Why it works:** It breaks the reflex of reading physics into symbols and hands the learner a quick, invariant diagnostic they can reuse on any metric.

**App idea:** Coordinate detective: a randomized metric card whose coordinates the learner tags as timelike/null/spacelike; the app evaluates g^aa at a chosen point and reveals which tags hold and where they flip (e.g. at r = 2m).

### Use a curvature scalar as the arbiter between coordinate and physical singularities. · DIV §17.2 p.323 (pdf 338)

**Why it works:** Invariance under relabelling is the whole point, and the contrast finite-at-2m versus infinite-at-0 is stark and memorable.

**App idea:** Plot g_00, g_11 and the Kretschmann scalar along r on one axis set, with a chart toggle that makes the metric spikes vanish while the scalar curve stays unchanged.

### Teach spacetime diagrams by stacking time snapshots of expanding light fronts in flat space before any curvature appears. · DIV §17.3 p.324 (pdf 339)

**Why it works:** Learners build the cone themselves, so later tilted and squashed cones read as changes to something they already understand.

**App idea:** Flip-book-to-cone animation that then morphs into Schwarzschild EF cones as a mass slider is increased.

### Show the misleading Schwarzschild-coordinate diagram on purpose, then refute it with a proper-time calculation plotted beside coordinate time. · DIV §17.5 p.328 (pdf 343)

**Why it works:** A productive contradiction (diagram says never, clock says soon) motivates new coordinates far better than announcing them.

**App idea:** Linked panels: Fig. 17.7-style diagram with a stalling particle, Fig. 17.8-style tau/t plot, and a 'fix the chart' button that swaps in EF coordinates.

### Design the new coordinate by demanding that ingoing light rays be straight 45 degree lines. · DIV §17.6 p.328 (pdf 343)

**Why it works:** It turns a mysterious formula (t + 2m ln(r - 2m)) into the solution of an obvious geometric wish, and the same idea generates retarded, Kruskal and Penrose coordinates later.

**App idea:** Ray-straightener: the learner drags a warp control that reparametrizes t; ingoing ray curves update live and the correct logarithmic warp is found when they become straight.

### Justify extension through r = 2m by maximal regular domains of two metrics plus an isometry on their overlap, not by the singular transformation. · DIV §17.6 p.329 (pdf 344)

**Why it works:** It addresses the objection learners actually raise and models how mathematicians reason about atlases.

**App idea:** Patch overlap viewer (Fig. 17.9 redesign) with a quiz asking where each chart is valid.

### Read the horizon off a spatial map of wave fronts: centred far away, shifted inward nearer, pinned at r = 2m for the outward photon, all inward inside. · DIV §17.7 p.331 (pdf 346)

**Why it works:** It shows the one-way nature of the horizon in a picture with no time axis, accessible to beginners.

**App idea:** Equatorial flash map with an 'escaping fraction' shading that drops to zero on r = 2m.

### Pair each construction with its time reverse (advanced/retarded, black/white hole, collapse/white-hole exercise). · DIV §17.7 p.332 (pdf 347)

**Why it works:** Seeing both extensions guards against thinking the horizon's direction is built into the metric and prepares the Kruskal picture.

**App idea:** Global time-reversal switch across all EF-based demos.

### Penrose's scaling: black-hole density falls as 1/M^2, so big black holes are dilute and have mild horizon tides. · DIV §17.12 p.339 (pdf 354)

**Why it works:** A two-line estimate overturns a deep intuition and reframes black holes as geometry rather than extreme matter.

**App idea:** Log-log slider of black-hole mass with live mean density compared to familiar materials and horizon tidal acceleration compared to human tolerance.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **The text says the level hypersurface of a coordinate is timelike when g^(a)(a) > 0. In standard terminology a positive norm of the normal makes the hypersurface spacelike (e.g. t = const); the book effectively names the hypersurface by its normal, or conflates coordinate and hypersurface.** — Readers who later meet 'spacelike Cauchy surface t = const' may think one source is wrong or reverse the classification. *Suggestion:* Say 'the coordinate x^(a) is timelike (its gradient is timelike), so its level surfaces are spacelike', and show both words on a diagram.
- **The claim that r = 2m is a null hypersurface is read off from (17.2) in Schwarzschild coordinates, where the metric itself is singular at r = 2m.** — The argument looks circular or suspicious since the chart is invalid exactly there. *Suggestion:* Confirm the null character with the regular Eddington-Finkelstein inverse metric, where g^rr = -(1 - 2m/r) vanishes at r = 2m (Exercise 17.14), before drawing conclusions.
- **Printed slips in §§17.6-17.7: region I is written as 'r < 2m < infinity' (should be 2m < r < infinity), 'extend the metric (17.19)' should refer to the metric (17.22), and the equatorial plane is given as theta = 0 instead of theta = pi/2 (Exercise 17.15 uses pi/2).** — Careful readers can be derailed by inequalities and equation references that do not make sense. *Suggestion:* Silently correct in app text; flag the corrections in tutor notes if a learner is reading the book alongside.
- **The tidal equations use the static frame (17.32)-(17.35), whose e_0 is not the tangent to a freely falling world line and which does not exist inside r = 2m, yet the conclusion is applied to infall all the way to r = 0.** — Learners may doubt the derivation or believe static-observer quantities describe infallers. *Suggestion:* Explain that these Riemann frame components are unchanged by radial boosts, so the same coefficients hold in the infaller's own frame at every r, including inside the horizon (easily shown with the Painleve-Gullstrand or Lemaitre frame).
- **Stale chapter and equation references from the first edition: §17.12 sends charged and rotating black holes to 'Chapters 18 and 19' (they are Chapters 19 and 20 in this edition), and Exercise 17.5 cites the Schwarzschild line element as (14.47) instead of (15.52).** — Readers following references land on the wrong material. *Suggestion:* Use the corrected targets (DIV ch19, ch20; (15.52)) in any linked content.
- **The Galactic Centre radius bound is printed as '2^10 km' (about 1000 km), which is smaller than the Schwarzschild radius and contradicts the next sentence; the quoted mean density of '10% of water' for that black hole is off by roughly four orders of magnitude (own estimate about 1e3-3e3 g/cm^3). The following claim that horizon tides for this hole match those at the Earth's surface is also overstated: the tidal gradient c^6/(4G^2M^2) comes to about 1.5e-3 s^-2 for 2.6 million solar masses, roughly 500 times the Earth's surface value of about 3e-6 s^-2, though still harmless to a person.** — Numbers used in estimates or quizzes would be wrong; the correct density argument is still sound in form. *Suggestion:* Present the scaling rho proportional to M^-2 with a calculator: Sgr A* about 1e3 g/cm^3, while holes above about 1e8 solar masses fall below water density.
- **Dated or loose astrophysics: the Event Horizon Telescope is called an optical array (it is a millimetre-wave radio VLBI network); X-ray emission is attributed to synchrotron radiation (mostly thermal disc and Comptonized emission); neutron-star maximum mass given as 4 solar masses and Cygnus X-1 as above 9 (modern about 2.2-2.5 and about 21); Galactic Centre mass 2.6 million (modern about 4.3 million); no mention of the 2020 Nobel Prize for the Galactic Centre work or of the 2022 Sgr A* image.** — An AI tutor quoting these would give inaccurate facts. *Suggestion:* Keep the logical structure of each evidence line but source current numbers from recent reviews; mark the textbook values as historical.
- **Laplace's statement is attributed as 'same density as the Sun, 250 times its radius'; Laplace actually spoke of a body with the Earth's density and 250 times the Sun's diameter, and at solar density the required radius is about 490 solar radii. Michell's 1783/1784 dark-star argument, which predates Laplace, is not mentioned.** — Minor historical inaccuracy that a numerate learner can detect when checking the number. *Suggestion:* Let learners compute the critical radius for a chosen density and credit both Michell and Laplace.
- **The Newtonian argument is presented as yielding 'the idea of a black hole' without stressing that Newtonian corpuscles can rise and fall back and that there is no causal horizon; the equality of radii is partly coincidental.** — Learners may think GR black holes are just Newtonian dark stars, or that the Schwarzschild radius derivation is Newtonian. *Suggestion:* Explicitly list differences, and derive r = 2m relativistically from g_00 or from the light-cone tilt.
- **The fact that a spherical star's exterior stays Schwarzschild during collapse is asserted without naming Birkhoff's theorem, and the star's interior metric in Figs. 17.13-17.14 is not specified (no Oppenheimer-Snyder model).** — It is unclear why the vacuum solution can be glued to a dynamic interior or what the star-surface world line obeys. *Suggestion:* Name Birkhoff's theorem and model the surface as a radial geodesic from rest (the dust-ball case), which matches the free-fall formula of §17.5.
- **The derivation of the Kretschmann scalar is not shown and no other invariant check is given; the text also does not state that finite invariants alone do not prove a singularity is removable.** — Learners may overgeneralize 'finite scalar implies removable'. *Suggestion:* Note that removability is ultimately proven by constructing the regular chart, and that there are singular spacetimes with all scalar invariants finite.
- **The event horizon is defined informally (boundary of events observable by an external inertial observer) without future null infinity or the teleological, global nature of horizons; the distinction between event horizon and apparent/trapped surfaces is not raised.** — Learners may think the horizon can be found locally, e.g. by a measurement on crossing. *Suggestion:* Stress that crossing is locally unremarkable and that locating the horizon requires the whole future; revisit with conformal diagrams in Chapter 18.
- **Fig. 17.12 (retarded EF) is described in one sentence and the direction of time for the white hole is easy to misread; the white-hole region II is not the same spacetime region as the black-hole region II, a point essential for Chapter 18.** — Confusion about whether black and white holes are the same object seen differently. *Suggestion:* Show the two EF charts as covering different parts of the Kruskal manifold, with a preview Penrose diagram.
- **The fading of the collapsing star is described qualitatively only; no timescale is given.** — Learners may imagine a slow freeze lasting ages for distant observers. *Suggestion:* Give the observed e-folding time 4GM/c^3: about 2e-5 s per solar mass, or 0.2 ms for 10 solar masses. It differs by a factor 2 from the 2m of (17.18), because the observer receives light in retarded time rather than Schwarzschild t.

## Tutor notes

- Open with a puzzle: show the Schwarzschild metric with labels permuted (as in Exercise 17.1) and ask which coordinate is time and how they would decide. Guide them to the sign of g^aa.
- Before any Schwarzschild work, check the learner can draw a Minkowski light cone and knows timelike world lines stay inside it; if not, use the snapshot-stacking construction of §17.3.
- Recommended order: meaning of coordinates -> coordinate vs curvature singularities -> light-cone diagrams in flat space -> radial null rays in Schwarzschild coordinates (and their misleading picture) -> proper vs coordinate time for infall -> Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates and extension -> horizon and white hole -> collapse -> Newtonian analogue and tides -> evidence and open questions.
- Check for understanding after §17.2: 'If g_11 is infinite at r = 2m, why is spacetime not singular there?' A good answer mentions invariants and the existence of a regular chart.
- After §17.5, ask: 'Who says the particle never reaches the horizon, and whose clock should we trust for what happens to the particle?' Aim for the distinction between a coordinate label and the particle's proper time.
- When introducing EF coordinates, have the learner state the goal first (make ingoing light rays straight), then derive t-bar; do not present the logarithm as a trick.
- Common question: 'Does anything special happen when you cross the horizon?' Answer: locally no (for large holes not even strong tides); globally yes, because afterwards every future direction decreases r.
- Common question: 'Isn't a black hole just a Newtonian dark star?' Acknowledge the shared radius, then contrast: Newtonian light can go up and fall back and be seen from nearby; relativistic outgoing light inside r = 2m moves inward.
- Common question: 'Why does the singularity lie in the future rather than at a place?' Use the interchange of t and r in region II and the tipped cones in Fig. 17.10.
- Common question: 'What is a white hole and do they exist?' Explain it as the time reverse obtained from retarded coordinates; note that astrophysical collapse produces black holes, and preview Chapter 18 where both appear in one maximal diagram.
- When quoting numbers from §17.11-17.12, use modern values (Sgr A* about 4.3 million solar masses, Cygnus X-1 about 21 solar masses, neutron-star maximum about 2.2-2.5 solar masses, EHT is a radio array) and correct the density claim (Sgr A* mean density about 1e3 g/cm^3).
- For advanced learners, ask why the static-frame tidal result also applies to an infalling observer (radial boost invariance of R_{0r0r} and R_{0 theta 0 theta}) and why it must hold inside the horizon where the static frame does not exist.
- For beginners, lean on the flash-map and collapse-signal visuals and skip (17.17); the key equations to retain are the Schwarzschild metric, r_s = 2GM/c^2, the proper-time infall result and the tidal coefficients +2m/r^3 and -m/r^3.
- Watch for a learner treating r as distance from the centre; remind them r is defined by sphere area and becomes timelike inside the horizon.
- Quick quantitative checks: Schwarzschild radius of the Sun (about 3 km) and Earth (about 9 mm); horizon tidal acceleration across 2 m for 10 solar masses (enormous) versus 4 million solar masses (millimetres per second squared).
