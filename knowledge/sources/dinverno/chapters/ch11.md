# DIV ch11 · General relativity from a variational principle

> Re-derives Einstein's field equations from an action: proves the Palatini identity for the variation of curvature, shows that coordinate invariance of any action forces a divergence-free Euler-Lagrange tensor (the contracted Bianchi identity), varies sqrt(-g) R both by brute force and by a slicker delta-calculus, explains why the equations stay second order, treats metric and connection as independent (Palatini), and couples matter to define T^ab variationally.

**Pages:** printed 187–202 · pdf 202–217 · **Difficulty:** math 4/5, conceptual 4/5, novice-friendliness 2/5

Short but dense: nearly every line is an index manipulation with densities, variations and integration by parts, and many intermediate results are delegated to exercises. Conceptually the key ideas (off-shell identities from covariance, boundary terms, divergence ambiguity, connection as a dynamical field) are deep but stated tersely with almost no physical commentary. Readers need fluent command of Chapter 7 (densities, determinants, divergence theorem, Euler-Lagrange equations). Several misprints (a missing boundary symbol, a missing '= 0', exercise index errors) add friction.

## Role in the book

Chapter 10 reached G_ab = kappa T_ab from physical heuristics (lift experiments, geodesic deviation, the Newtonian limit). Chapter 11 deliberately gives a second, purely mathematical route that cashes in the machinery of Part B: geodesic coordinates and the Riemann tensor from Chapter 6, and tensor densities, the metric determinant, the divergence theorem and the Euler-Lagrange equations from Chapter 7. It confirms the vacuum and full equations, shows that the Bianchi identity is a structural consequence of general covariance, and supplies the variational definition of the energy-momentum tensor that Chapter 12 uses for the Maxwell and scalar fields. Its 'trivial' sqrt(-g) Lagrangian reappears as the cosmological term in Chapter 13, and the idea of a quadratic first-derivative Lagrangian returns for linearized gravity in Chapter 21.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can prove the Palatini identity delta R^a_bcd = nabla_c(delta Gamma^a_bd) - nabla_d(delta Gamma^a_bc) using geodesic coordinates and the tensor character of both sides, and contract it to delta R_bd.
- Reader can explain why the difference of two connections is a type (1,2) tensor even though a connection itself is not.
- Reader can set up a field-theory action I = integral of a weight +1 scalar density over a region, state the principle of stationary action with variations vanishing on the boundary, and define the Euler-Lagrange tensor density L^ab = delta L / delta g_ab.
- Reader can derive the identity nabla_b L^ab = 0 from the invariance of the action under an infinitesimal coordinate change generated by a vector field that vanishes on the boundary, and explain why it holds off-shell.
- Reader can compute the Euler-Lagrange derivative of the volume Lagrangian sqrt(-g), obtaining one half of the gothic inverse metric, and explain why it cannot be a physical theory on its own.
- Reader can write the Einstein Lagrangian sqrt(-g) R, write the Euler-Lagrange derivative for a Lagrangian containing second derivatives, and state that it yields -sqrt(-g) G^ab.
- Reader can use the variation operator as a derivation (linearity and product rule) to show delta g^ab = -g^ac g^bd delta g_cd and delta sqrt(-g) = (1/2) sqrt(-g) g^ab delta g_ab.
- Reader can carry out the indirect variation of sqrt(-g) R: use the contracted Palatini identity to turn the delta R_ab term into a boundary term and reduce what remains to the Einstein tensor.
- Reader can explain why an action containing second derivatives of the metric still gives second-order field equations, by splitting sqrt(-g) R into a first-order Gamma-Gamma Lagrangian plus a total divergence.
- Reader can state why Lagrangians that differ by a total divergence yield identical field equations, and that the Gamma-Gamma Lagrangian is not a scalar density.
- Reader can perform the Palatini variation, treating the metric and a symmetric connection as independent, and show that the connection equation forces metric compatibility and hence the Christoffel connection.
- Reader can add a matter Lagrangian with coupling 2 kappa, define the energy-momentum tensor as T^ab = (2/sqrt(-g)) delta L_M / delta g_ab, and recover G^ab = kappa T^ab.

## Teaching approach

A compact, formula-dense chapter written in the mathematical register of Part B. The authors build a general variational framework for a metric theory, extract what can be learned before choosing any Lagrangian, and only then specialise, first to a toy Lagrangian and then to sqrt(-g) R. The pedagogy is comparative: the same field equations are obtained three ways (brute-force Euler-Lagrange, indirect delta-calculus, Palatini), and each route is chosen to expose a different structural fact (off-shell Bianchi identities, the boundary term, the divergence ambiguity, metric compatibility as an output). Physical motivation is almost absent by design; the physics was done in Chapter 10. Many intermediate steps are handed to the reader as '(exercise)' or '(check)', so the chapter reads as a guided proof outline.

**Style:** derivation-heavy, axiomatic-mathematical, components-first, spiral-revisit

**Narrative arc**

1. Forge the key tool first: vary the connection, note the variation is a tensor, and prove the Palatini identity in geodesic coordinates (§11.1).
2. Set up the general action principle for a metric theory and define the Euler-Lagrange tensor density L^ab (§11.2).
3. Before choosing any Lagrangian, generate a fake variation by an infinitesimal coordinate change; invariance of I forces nabla_b L^ab = 0 identically (§11.2).
4. Test the machinery on the simplest density sqrt(-g): the equations are absurd and the identity is trivially true (§11.3).
5. Move to the next simplest candidate sqrt(-g) R, expand it fully in metric derivatives to show the brute-force route is hopeless, and quote the answer -sqrt(-g) G^ab; the identity becomes the contracted Bianchi identity (§11.4).
6. Replace brute force with the delta-calculus: product rule, delta of the inverse metric, Palatini identity to push delta R_ab to the boundary, and reduce to G^ab (§11.5).
7. Confront the puzzle that second derivatives in L should give fourth-order equations; resolve it by splitting off a total divergence and deriving R_ab = 0 from a first-order Gamma-Gamma Lagrangian (§11.6).
8. Offer the elegant alternative: vary metric and symmetric connection independently; one variation gives the vacuum equations, the other forces the connection to be metric (§11.7).
9. Add matter, define T^ab variationally with a factor of 2 chosen to match the canonical tensor, and recover the full equations of Chapter 10 (§11.8).

**Signature moves**

- Identities before equations: the constraint nabla_b L^ab = 0 is derived for an unspecified Lagrangian purely from the fact that the action is a scalar, so the Bianchi identity appears as a consequence of general covariance rather than an accident of curvature algebra.
- Prove-in-a-special-frame-then-promote: compute at a point in geodesic coordinates where connection terms vanish, then argue that both sides are tensors so the equation holds in all coordinates and at all points.
- Show the ugly thing on purpose: the full expansion (11.24) of sqrt(-g) R in metric derivatives is displayed and then abandoned, which motivates the indirect method more convincingly than any assertion.
- Warm-up with a deliberately trivial Lagrangian (sqrt(-g)) so that every step of the framework can be checked before the real calculation.
- Treat the variation symbol as a derivative-like operator and invite comparison between delta g^ab and the partial-derivative formula (7.9).
- Freely swap the choice of dynamical variables (g_ab, g^ab, gothic g^ab, gothic g_ab) to make a calculation tractable, emphasising that the resulting field equations are equivalent.
- Pose an apparent paradox (second derivatives in L but second-order equations) and resolve it by a total-divergence decomposition.
- Delegate heavy but routine algebra to tagged '(exercise)' prompts that map directly onto end-of-chapter problems.

## Section by section

### §11.1 The Palatini equation — p.187 (pdf 202)

Opens without motivation by recalling geodesic coordinates, in which the Riemann tensor at a point reduces to two derivative terms. A connection is perturbed to a nearby connection; their difference is a tensor. The induced change in curvature at the point is written with partial derivatives, which may be swapped for covariant derivatives there. Since both sides are tensors the relation holds in any chart and, the point being arbitrary, everywhere. Contracting gives the variation of the Ricci tensor, the form used repeatedly later.

**Concepts:** geodesic coordinates, variation of a connection, Palatini identity, contracted Palatini identity, tensor equation invariance

**Key moves**
- Choose geodesic coordinates at P so Gamma vanishes and R^a_bcd = partial_c Gamma^a_bd - partial_d Gamma^a_bc there (11.1).
- Observe that delta Gamma is a difference of two connections and so transforms as a (1,2) tensor.
- Commute delta with partial derivatives and replace partial by covariant derivatives at P.
- Promote the result to all coordinate systems because both sides are tensors, and to all points because P was arbitrary (11.3).
- Contract on a and c to get delta R_bd (11.4).

### §11.2 Differential constraints on the field equations — p.188 (pdf 203)

Sets out the action principle for a metric theory: a Lagrangian density of weight +1 built from the metric and its derivatives, integrated over a region; stationarity under boundary-vanishing variations gives field equations L^ab = 0, where L^ab is a symmetric tensor density. Then comes the chapter's most important structural argument: an infinitesimal change of coordinates produces a change in the metric components equal to minus the Lie derivative but cannot change the action. Integrating by parts, discarding a surface term via the divergence theorem, and using the arbitrariness of the region and of the generating vector field yields the identity nabla_b L^ab = 0, valid whether or not the field equations are satisfied.

**Concepts:** Lagrangian density, action integral, principle of stationary action, Euler-Lagrange derivative, diffeomorphism invariance of the action, metric variation induced by a coordinate change, divergence theorem for vector densities, fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations, generalized Bianchi identity

**Key moves**
- Require L to be a weight +1 scalar density so the action is coordinate invariant (11.5)-(11.6).
- Write the first-order change of the action as the integral of L^ab delta g_ab and conclude L^ab is a symmetric tensor density because delta I is a scalar (11.8)-(11.9).
- Generate a variation by x -> x + epsilon X with X vanishing on the boundary; delta g_ab = -epsilon(nabla_b X_a + nabla_a X_b) (11.12)-(11.13).
- Use the invariance delta I = 0 identically (11.11) and symmetry of L^ab to reduce to an integral of L^ab nabla_b X_a.
- Integrate by parts; the total divergence of the weight +1 density L^ab X_a is an ordinary divergence and becomes a vanishing boundary integral (11.14)-(11.15).
- Argue from arbitrary region then arbitrary X that nabla_b L^ab = 0 identically (11.16)-(11.18).

### §11.3 A simple example — p.189 (pdf 204)

Introduces the gothic-letter convention (a tensor multiplied by sqrt(-g), hence a weight +1 density) and tries the simplest scalar density built from the metric alone, sqrt(-g). Using the derivative of a determinant, its Euler-Lagrange derivative is half the gothic inverse metric. Setting that to zero is nonsensical as a set of field equations, while the differential identity reduces to a statement already guaranteed by metric compatibility.

**Concepts:** gothic tensor density notation, volume Lagrangian sqrt(-g), derivative of the metric determinant, generalized Bianchi identity

**Key moves**
- Define gothic quantities as tensors times sqrt(-g).
- Use partial g / partial g_ab = g g^ab (11.20) to get partial sqrt(-g) / partial g_ab = (1/2) gothic g^ab (11.21).
- Note gothic g^ab = 0 cannot be field equations, and nabla_b gothic g^ab = 0 is automatically true (11.22).

### §11.4 The Einstein Lagrangian — p.190 (pdf 205)

Asserts that the next simplest scalar after the volume element is the Ricci scalar, and takes sqrt(-g) R as the gravitational Lagrangian. Introduces comma notation for partial derivatives and writes the Lagrangian out in full in terms of metric components and their first and second derivatives, making the complexity visible. Gives the Euler-Lagrange derivative for Lagrangians with second derivatives, quotes one of the pieces as an exercise, declines the rest as far too laborious, and states the outcome: the Euler-Lagrange tensor is minus sqrt(-g) times the Einstein tensor, so the vacuum equations follow and the general identity of §11.2 becomes the contracted Bianchi identity.

**Concepts:** Einstein-Hilbert Lagrangian, comma notation, Euler-Lagrange derivative with second derivatives, vacuum field equations, contracted Bianchi identities, choice of dynamical variables

**Key moves**
- Choose L_G = sqrt(-g) R as the next simplest scalar density (11.23).
- Expand R in Christoffel symbols and then in metric derivatives to display the full dependence (11.24).
- Generalize the Euler-Lagrange derivative to include a second-derivative term with a plus sign (11.25).
- Quote the coefficient of second derivatives (11.26), which contains no metric derivatives.
- State L_G^ab = -sqrt(-g) G^ab (11.27), giving G^ab = 0 (11.28) and nabla_b G^ab = 0 (11.29).

### §11.5 Indirect derivation of the field equations — p.192 (pdf 207)

Develops a calculus for the variation symbol: it is linear and obeys the product rule. Applying it to the constant Kronecker delta written as g^ab g_bc shows that the variation of the inverse metric is minus the inverse metric twice contracted with the variation of the metric. The action is then written as gothic g^ab R_ab and varied by the product rule. The term containing delta R_ab is rewritten with the contracted Palatini identity; because gothic g^ab is covariantly constant, the integrand is a covariant divergence of a weight +1 vector density and hence a boundary term that drops out. The surviving term, using the variation of sqrt(-g), assembles into minus sqrt(-g) G^ab times delta g_ab, reproducing the field equations and identities in a few lines.

**Concepts:** variation as a derivation, variation of the inverse metric, variation of sqrt(-g), contracted Palatini identity, boundary term in the Einstein-Hilbert variation, Einstein-Hilbert Lagrangian

**Key moves**
- Vary g^ab g_bc = delta^a_c, which is constant, to get (11.31) and hence delta g^ad = -g^ab g^cd delta g_bc (11.32).
- Write I as the integral of gothic g^ab R_ab and apply the product rule (11.33).
- Replace delta R_ab by the contracted Palatini identity and pull gothic g^ab inside the covariant derivatives.
- Convert the resulting covariant divergence of a vector density into a partial divergence and discard it as a boundary term.
- Expand delta gothic g^ab using delta sqrt(-g) (11.35) and delta g^ab, and collect into -sqrt(-g) G^cd delta g_cd (11.34).

### §11.6 An equivalent Lagrangian — p.193 (pdf 208)

Addresses why second derivatives in sqrt(-g) R do not produce fourth-order equations: the coefficient of the second derivatives involves only the metric itself and the coefficient of first derivatives involves only first derivatives. To make this explicit, the Einstein Lagrangian is written as a first-order Lagrangian quadratic in the connection coefficients plus a total divergence Q^a_,a. Integrating the derivative-of-Gamma terms by parts and using the expression for derivatives of gothic g^ab in terms of Gamma produces the split. The divergence contributes only a surface term, so both Lagrangians give the same equations, though the first-order one is not a scalar density. Choosing gothic g^ab as the variables, the Euler-Lagrange equations of the Gamma-Gamma Lagrangian reduce, via the identity for the derivative of ln sqrt(-g), to R_ab = 0.

**Concepts:** second-order nature of the Einstein equations, total divergence ambiguity of a Lagrangian, Gamma-Gamma Lagrangian, derivative of gothic inverse metric in terms of the connection, contracted connection as gradient of ln sqrt(-g), choice of dynamical variables

**Key moves**
- Point out the puzzle using (11.25) and (11.26).
- Expand gothic g^ab R_ab into two derivative-of-Gamma terms minus the quadratic L-bar (11.37)-(11.38).
- Integrate by parts to move derivatives onto gothic g^ab, generating the divergence of Q^a (11.39)-(11.40).
- Use (11.41), from nabla gothic g^ab = 0, to show the non-divergence pieces sum to L-bar, giving L_G = L-bar + Q^a_,a (11.36).
- Treat L-bar as a function of gothic g^ab and its first derivatives; compute its partial derivatives (11.42)-(11.43) and the Euler-Lagrange expression (11.44)-(11.45).
- Use Gamma^d_ad = (ln sqrt(-g))_,a (11.46) to symmetrize the derivative terms and recognise R_ab.

### §11.7 The Palatini approach — p.195 (pdf 210)

Presents the metric-affine route: the Einstein Lagrangian is regarded as a function of gothic g^ab and of an arbitrary symmetric connection and its first derivatives, with the Ricci tensor built from the connection alone. Varying the metric gives R_ab = 0 at once. Varying the connection, using the contracted Palatini identity and integrating by parts, gives an integrand linear in delta Gamma; because the connection variation is symmetric in its lower indices only the symmetrized coefficient must vanish (11.48). Manipulating this (in an exercise) shows the covariant derivatives of gothic g^ab, sqrt(-g), g^ab and g_ab all vanish, and by Exercise 6.21 a symmetric metric-compatible connection must be the Christoffel connection.

**Concepts:** Palatini (metric-affine) variation, symmetric connection, metric compatibility from the field equations, metric connection, contracted Palatini identity

**Key moves**
- Declare gothic g^ab and a symmetric Gamma to be independent variables in gothic g^ab R_ab(Gamma) (11.47).
- Vary gothic g^ab only: delta I = integral of R_ab delta gothic g^ab, so R_ab = 0.
- Vary Gamma only: use (11.4), integrate by parts, drop the boundary term.
- Rename indices to factor out delta Gamma^c_ab and symmetrize in a and b because delta Gamma is symmetric (11.48).
- Deduce nabla_c g_ab = 0 and invoke Exercise 6.21 to identify the Christoffel connection.

### §11.8 The full field equations — p.197 (pdf 212)

Adds a matter Lagrangian with a coupling constant, using the combination L_G + 2 kappa L_M. Varying with respect to the metric, the gravitational part gives minus sqrt(-g) G^ab and the matter part is used to define the energy-momentum tensor through T^ab = (2/sqrt(-g)) delta L_M / delta g_ab. The factor of 2 is chosen so that this agrees with the canonical energy-momentum tensor of special relativity (worked out for a scalar field in Exercise 11.13). Dividing by sqrt(-g) gives G^ab = kappa T^ab, matching Chapter 10. The authors note that some texts drop the 2 in both places, and preview Chapter 12.

**Concepts:** matter Lagrangian, coupling constant, variational (Hilbert) energy-momentum tensor, full field equations, canonical energy-momentum tensor

**Key moves**
- Write the total action as the integral of L_G + 2 kappa L_M (11.49).
- Use delta L_G / delta g_ab = -sqrt(-g) G^ab (11.50).
- Define T^ab by the metric variation of L_M with a factor 2 (11.51)-(11.52).
- Sum, divide by sqrt(-g), obtain G^ab = kappa T^ab (11.53).

## Concepts

### geodesic coordinates

*technique · revisited* · also: normal coordinates at a point, locally inertial coordinates (for the metric connection)

A coordinate system adapted to a chosen point P in which all connection coefficients vanish at P, so that covariant derivatives equal partial derivatives at that point (though not in a neighbourhood).

**How introduced:** Recalled at the chapter's first line as the standard tool for proving tensor identities; the asterisk-equals notation flags equations valid only in that chart.

**Prerequisites:** affine connection, covariant derivative

$$
\Gamma^a_{bc} \stackrel{*}{=} 0
$$

$$
R^a{}_{bcd} \stackrel{*}{=} \partial_c\Gamma^a_{bd} - \partial_d\Gamma^a_{bc}
$$

**Notes:** Also suggested as the method for Exercise 11.14 (variation of the connection).

**Where:** DIV §11.1 p.187 (pdf 202)

### variation of a connection

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: delta Gamma, difference tensor of two connections

The first-order difference delta Gamma^a_bc between a connection and a nearby connection. Although connection coefficients do not transform as a tensor, the inhomogeneous terms cancel in the difference, so delta Gamma^a_bc is a type (1,2) tensor.

**How introduced:** Introduced by replacing Gamma with Gamma + delta Gamma; its tensor character is the hinge that lets the Palatini identity be promoted from geodesic coordinates to all coordinates.

**Prerequisites:** affine connection, tensor transformation law

$$
\bar\Gamma^a_{bc} = \Gamma^a_{bc} + \delta\Gamma^a_{bc}
$$

**Notes:** In the metric theory delta Gamma is fixed by delta g_ab (Exercise 11.14); in the Palatini approach it is an independent variation.

**Where:** DIV §11.1 p.187 (pdf 202)

### Palatini identity

*identity · core* · also: Palatini equation, variation of the Riemann tensor

For any variation of a connection, the change in the Riemann tensor is delta R^a_bcd = nabla_c(delta Gamma^a_bd) - nabla_d(delta Gamma^a_bc), where nabla is the covariant derivative of the unvaried connection.

**How introduced:** Derived at a point in geodesic coordinates where the curvature is a difference of two connection derivatives; partial derivatives commute with variation and equal covariant ones there, and tensor character extends the result everywhere.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic coordinates, variation of a connection, Riemann tensor, tensor equation invariance

$$
\delta R^a{}_{bcd} = \nabla_c(\delta\Gamma^a_{bd}) - \nabla_d(\delta\Gamma^a_{bc})
$$

**Notes:** The book derives it in one paragraph; it is then used in §11.5 and §11.7.

**Where:** DIV §11.1 p.187 (pdf 202)

### contracted Palatini identity

*identity · core* · also: variation of the Ricci tensor

Contraction of the Palatini identity on its first and third indices: delta R_bd = nabla_a(delta Gamma^a_bd) - nabla_d(delta Gamma^a_ba). Contracted with a covariantly constant density it becomes a total divergence.

**How introduced:** Obtained immediately from (11.3) and flagged as the useful form; it is the step that converts the delta R_ab term of the action variation into a boundary integral.

**Prerequisites:** Palatini identity, Ricci tensor

$$
\delta R_{bd} = \nabla_a(\delta\Gamma^a_{bd}) - \nabla_d(\delta\Gamma^a_{ba})
$$

**Where:** DIV §11.1 p.187 (pdf 202); DIV §11.5 p.193 (pdf 208); DIV §11.7 p.196 (pdf 211)

### tensor equation invariance

*principle · revisited* · also: a tensor identity in one chart holds in all charts

If an equation between tensors holds in one coordinate system at a point, it holds in every coordinate system at that point, because both sides transform identically.

**How introduced:** Invoked as the 'fundamental result' that lets a geodesic-coordinate computation become a general identity.

**Prerequisites:** tensor transformation law

**Where:** DIV §11.1 p.187 (pdf 202)

### Lagrangian density

*definition · core* · also: L, scalar density Lagrangian

A scalar density of weight +1 built from the dynamical fields (here the metric and its first and possibly higher derivatives) whose integral over a region with the coordinate volume element is coordinate invariant.

**How introduced:** Stated as the starting specification of the variational principle; the weight +1 requirement is justified by the need for an invariant action integral.

**Prerequisites:** tensor density, metric tensor

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}(g_{ab},\partial_c g_{ab},\partial_d\partial_c g_{ab},\dots)
$$

**Where:** DIV §11.2 p.188 (pdf 203)

### action integral

*definition · core* · also: action I

The integral I of a Lagrangian density over a region Omega of the manifold with the coordinate volume element dOmega; its stationarity under suitable variations defines the field equations.

**How introduced:** Formed right after the Lagrangian density is required to be weight +1.

**Prerequisites:** Lagrangian density

$$
I = \int_\Omega \mathcal{L}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega
$$

**Where:** DIV §11.2 p.188 (pdf 203)

### principle of stationary action

*principle · core* · also: variational principle, Hamilton's principle for fields

The physical field configuration is one for which the action does not change to first order under arbitrary variations of the dynamical variables that vanish on the boundary of the region.

**How introduced:** Stated formally in the variational notation of Chapter 7 as the demand delta I = 0 for all boundary-vanishing delta g_ab.

**Prerequisites:** action integral, Euler-Lagrange derivative

$$
g_{ab}\to g_{ab}+\delta g_{ab}\ \Rightarrow\ \delta I = 0
$$

**Notes:** The chapter does not discuss how much of the variation (value versus normal derivatives) must vanish on the boundary when L contains second derivatives.

**Where:** DIV §11.2 p.188 (pdf 203)

### Euler-Lagrange derivative

*operation · core* · also: variational derivative, functional derivative, L^ab

The quantity L^ab = delta L / delta g_ab defined so that the first-order change of the action is the integral of L^ab delta g_ab; for Lagrangians depending on derivatives it equals the partial derivative minus the divergence of the first-derivative coefficient plus the double divergence of the second-derivative coefficient. For a metric theory it is a symmetric tensor density of weight +1.

**How introduced:** Defined abstractly in §11.2; its tensor-density character is inferred from delta I being a scalar; generalized to second derivatives in (11.25).

**Prerequisites:** Lagrangian density, tensor density, Euler-Lagrange equations

$$
\delta I = \int_\Omega \mathcal{L}^{ab}\delta g_{ab}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega
$$

$$
\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}}{\delta g_{ab}} = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial g_{ab}} - \Big(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial g_{ab,c}}\Big)_{,c} + \Big(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial g_{ab,cd}}\Big)_{,cd}
$$

**Where:** DIV §11.2 p.188 (pdf 203); DIV §11.4 p.191 (pdf 206)

### diffeomorphism invariance of the action

*principle · core* · also: general covariance of the action, invariance under change of coordinates

Because the Lagrangian is a scalar density, an infinitesimal relabelling of coordinates inside a region changes the metric components but leaves the action exactly unchanged, so the induced first-order change of I vanishes identically.

**How introduced:** Used as a device: a coordinate change is treated as a 'variation' of g_ab, turning invariance into delta I = 0 identically.

**Prerequisites:** Lagrangian density, action integral, Lie derivative

$$
\delta I \equiv 0
$$

**Notes:** This is the content of Noether's second theorem, although the book does not name it.

**Where:** DIV §11.2 p.188 (pdf 203); DIV §11.2 p.189 (pdf 204)

### metric variation induced by a coordinate change

*operation · developed* · also: Lie derivative of the metric, gauge variation of g_ab

Under x^a -> x^a + epsilon X^a, the new metric components evaluated at the same coordinate values differ from the old by minus the Lie derivative: delta g_ab = -epsilon(nabla_a X_b + nabla_b X_a).

**How introduced:** Quoted with reference to the isometry calculation of §7.7 and left as Exercise 11.1.

**Prerequisites:** Lie derivative, infinitesimal coordinate transformation, covariant derivative

$$
\delta g_{ab} = -\mathrm{L}_{\varepsilon X}g_{ab} = -\varepsilon(\nabla_b X_a + \nabla_a X_b)
$$

**Notes:** The same combination set to zero is Killing's equation from §7.7.

**Where:** DIV §11.2 p.189 (pdf 204)

### divergence theorem for vector densities

*theorem · developed* · also: Gauss/Stokes theorem for densities, integration by parts in the action

For a weight +1 vector density the covariant divergence equals the ordinary divergence, and its integral over a region equals the flux through the boundary; if the density vanishes on the boundary the integral is zero.

**How introduced:** Used three times (§11.2, §11.5, §11.7) as 'the usual argument' for discarding total divergences.

**Prerequisites:** tensor density, covariant derivative

$$
\int_\Omega \partial_b[\mathcal{L}^{ab}X_a]\,\mathrm{d}\Omega = \int_{\partial\Omega}\mathcal{L}^{ab}X_a\,\mathrm{d}S_b
$$

**Where:** DIV §11.2 p.189 (pdf 204); DIV §11.5 p.193 (pdf 208); DIV §11.7 p.196 (pdf 211)

### fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations

*theorem · introduced* · also: arbitrary region implies zero integrand

If a continuous function integrates to zero over every region, it vanishes everywhere; and if W^a X_a = 0 for arbitrary X_a then W^a = 0.

**How introduced:** Used as the final two steps of the identity derivation, with proofs deferred to Exercise 11.8 (continuity and choice of test vectors).

**Prerequisites:** integration

**Where:** DIV §11.2 p.189 (pdf 204); DIV p.198 (pdf 213)

### generalized Bianchi identity

*identity · core* · also: differential identities, Noether identity, nabla_b L^ab = 0

For any metric Lagrangian that is a scalar density, the Euler-Lagrange tensor density is covariantly divergence-free identically, i.e. whether or not the field equations hold.

**How introduced:** Derived before any Lagrangian is chosen, from coordinate invariance of the action; later specialised to sqrt(-g) (trivial) and sqrt(-g) R (contracted Bianchi identity).

**Prerequisites:** diffeomorphism invariance of the action, metric variation induced by a coordinate change, divergence theorem for vector densities, fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations

\nabla_b\mathcal{L}^{ab} \equiv 0

**Notes:** Exercise 11.7 asks what the analogous identity is for other choices of dynamical variable.

**Where:** DIV §11.2 p.189 (pdf 204); DIV §11.3 p.190 (pdf 205); DIV §11.4 p.192 (pdf 207)

### contracted Bianchi identities

### Einstein-Hilbert Lagrangian

*definition · core* · also: Einstein Lagrangian, L_G, sqrt(-g) R

The gravitational Lagrangian density L_G = sqrt(-g) R, equivalently gothic g^ab R_ab. It depends on the metric and its first and second derivatives, and its Euler-Lagrange derivative with respect to g_ab is -sqrt(-g) G^ab.

**How introduced:** Presented as the next simplest scalar density after sqrt(-g); first expanded brute force (11.24), then varied indirectly (§11.5), rewritten as first order (§11.6) and treated in Palatini form (§11.7).

**Prerequisites:** Ricci scalar, gothic tensor density notation, Lagrangian density

\mathcal{L}_G = \sqrt{-g}\,R = \mathfrak{g}^{ab}R_{ab}

$$
\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}_G}{\delta g_{ab}} = -\sqrt{-g}\,G^{ab}

**Notes:** The book calls it the Einstein Lagrangian and does not name Hilbert in this chapter (Hilbert's 1915 variational derivation is mentioned in ch13 §13.3).

**Where:** DIV §11.4 p.190 (pdf 205); DIV §11.4 p.191 (pdf 206); DIV §11.5 p.192 (pdf 207); DIV §11.6 p.194 (pdf 209); DIV §11.7 p.195 (pdf 210)

### comma notation

*convention · mention* · also: comma for partial derivative

A comma followed by an index denotes partial differentiation, e.g. g_ab,c = partial_c g_ab, which keeps derivatives of Lagrangians with respect to derivatives of fields readable.

### Euler-Lagrange derivative with second derivatives

*operation · introduced* · also: higher-order Euler-Lagrange expression

For a Lagrangian depending on a field, its first and its second derivatives, the variational derivative has an extra term: the double divergence of the partial derivative of L with respect to the second derivatives, entering with a plus sign.

**How introduced:** Stated as a generalization of the first-order form (7.36) once sqrt(-g) R is recognised to contain second derivatives.

### vacuum field equations

**How introduced:** Recovered three times as the Euler-Lagrange equations: G^ab = 0 from the metric variation (§11.4-§11.5), R_ab = 0 from the Gamma-Gamma Lagrangian with gothic g^ab variables (§11.6), and R_ab = 0 in the Palatini form (§11.7).

### variation as a derivation

*technique · developed* · also: delta obeys linearity and the Leibniz rule

The first-order variation operator delta acts on sums and products like a derivative: it is linear with constant coefficients and satisfies delta(L1 L2) = L1 delta L2 + L2 delta L1; it also commutes with partial differentiation.

**How introduced:** Announced at the start of §11.5 (proof in Exercise 11.5) and demonstrated on the constant Kronecker delta.

**Prerequisites:** Euler-Lagrange derivative

\delta(L_1L_2) = L_1\,\delta L_2 + L_2\,\delta L_1

### variation of the inverse metric

*identity · developed* · also: delta g^ab

A change delta g_ab of the metric induces delta g^ab = -g^ac g^bd delta g_cd in the inverse metric: the indices are raised with a minus sign.

**How introduced:** Obtained by varying g^ab g_bc = delta^a_c, which cannot change, and multiplying through by the inverse metric; the reader is asked to compare with the partial-derivative formula (7.9).

**Prerequisites:** variation as a derivation, inverse metric

\delta g^{ab}g_{bc} + g^{ab}\delta g_{bc} = 0

\delta g^{ad} = -g^{ab}g^{cd}\delta g_{bc}

### boundary term in the Einstein-Hilbert variation

*technique · developed* · also: surface term, discarded divergence

The part of the action variation proportional to delta R_ab equals the integral of an ordinary divergence of a vector density built from gothic g^ab and delta Gamma, which converts to a surface integral and is discarded when variations vanish on the boundary.

**How introduced:** Derived in §11.5 by combining the contracted Palatini identity with the covariant constancy of gothic g^ab and (7.3).

**Prerequisites:** contracted Palatini identity, divergence theorem for vector densities

$$
\int_\Omega\mathfrak{g}^{ab}\delta R_{ab}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega = \int_\Omega\partial_c\big(\mathfrak{g}^{ab}\delta\Gamma^c_{ab} - \mathfrak{g}^{ac}\delta\Gamma^b_{ab}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\Omega
$$

**Notes:** Because delta Gamma contains first derivatives of delta g_ab, discarding this term strictly requires those derivatives to vanish on the boundary too; the modern fix is the Gibbons-Hawking-York term, which the book does not discuss.

**Where:** DIV §11.5 p.193 (pdf 208)

### second-order nature of the Einstein equations

*phenomenon · developed* · also: why no fourth-order terms

Although sqrt(-g) R contains second derivatives of the metric, the Euler-Lagrange equations are only second order, because the coefficient of the second derivatives depends only on g_ab and the first-derivative coefficient only on first derivatives, so the potentially fourth-order terms cancel.

**How introduced:** Raised as a surprise in §11.6 with reference to (11.25)-(11.26) and made manifest by the total-divergence split.

**Prerequisites:** Euler-Lagrange derivative with second derivatives, Einstein-Hilbert Lagrangian

**Where:** DIV §11.6 p.193 (pdf 208); DIV §11.6 p.194 (pdf 209)

### total divergence ambiguity of a Lagrangian

*theorem · developed* · also: equivalent Lagrangians, adding a divergence

Two Lagrangians that differ by a total divergence Q^a_,a give the same Euler-Lagrange equations, since the divergence integrates to a boundary term that does not affect the variation.

**How introduced:** Asserted in §11.6 and proved for the one-variable mechanics case in Exercise 11.9; it justifies replacing sqrt(-g) R by the first-order Gamma-Gamma Lagrangian.

**Prerequisites:** divergence theorem for vector densities, principle of stationary action

$$
\mathcal{L}_G = \bar{\mathcal{L}}_G + Q^a{}_{,a}
$$

**Where:** DIV §11.6 p.194 (pdf 209); DIV p.199 (pdf 214)

### Gamma-Gamma Lagrangian

*definition · developed* · also: first-order Einstein Lagrangian, L-bar_G, Einstein's non-covariant Lagrangian

The Lagrangian formed by contracting gothic g^ab with the difference of the two possible index-linked products of Christoffel symbols (see formulas), quadratic in connection coefficients and so depending only on the metric and its first derivatives. It differs from sqrt(-g) R by a total divergence and is not a scalar density.

**How introduced:** Extracted from the expansion of gothic g^ab R_ab by integrating the derivative-of-Gamma terms by parts; then used with gothic g^ab as dynamical variables to derive R_ab = 0.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein-Hilbert Lagrangian, total divergence ambiguity of a Lagrangian, derivative of gothic inverse metric in terms of the connection

$$
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_G = \mathfrak{g}^{ab}(\Gamma^d_{ac}\Gamma^c_{bd} - \Gamma^c_{ab}\Gamma^d_{cd})
$$

$$
Q^a = \mathfrak{g}^{bc}\Gamma^a_{bc} - \mathfrak{g}^{ab}\Gamma^c_{bc}
$$

**Notes:** Not a scalar density, so the generalized identity of §11.2 does not directly apply to it.

**Where:** DIV §11.6 p.194 (pdf 209); DIV §11.6 p.195 (pdf 210)

### choice of dynamical variables

*technique · introduced* · also: g_ab vs g^ab vs gothic variables

The same Lagrangian may be regarded as a function of g_ab, g^ab, gothic g^ab or gothic g_ab (and their derivatives); the Euler-Lagrange derivatives differ by invertible algebraic factors, so the field equations are equivalent, but one choice can make the algebra much simpler.

**How introduced:** Noted in §11.4 and §11.6, exploited with gothic g^ab in §11.6-§11.7, and systematised in Exercise 11.7 (giving R_ab, -R^ab and sqrt(-g) G_ab).

**Prerequisites:** Euler-Lagrange derivative, gothic tensor density notation

$$
\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}_G}{\delta\mathfrak{g}^{ab}} = R_{ab},\quad \frac{\delta\mathcal{L}_G}{\delta\mathfrak{g}_{ab}} = -R^{ab},\quad \frac{\delta\mathcal{L}_G}{\delta g^{ab}} = \sqrt{-g}\,G_{ab}
$$

**Where:** DIV §11.4 p.191 (pdf 206); DIV §11.6 p.194 (pdf 209); DIV p.198 (pdf 213)

### derivative of gothic inverse metric in terms of the connection

*identity · introduced* · also: partial derivative of gothic g^ab

Because the covariant derivative of the weight +1 density gothic g^ab vanishes, its partial derivative can be written purely with connection coefficients: gothic g^ab_,c = Gamma^d_dc gothic g^ab - Gamma^a_dc gothic g^db - Gamma^b_dc gothic g^ad.

**Prerequisites:** tensor density, metric connection

\mathfrak{g}^{ab}{}_{,c} = \Gamma^d_{dc}\mathfrak{g}^{ab} - \Gamma^a_{dc}\mathfrak{g}^{db} - \Gamma^b_{dc}\mathfrak{g}^{ad}

### Palatini (metric-affine) variation

*technique · core* · also: Palatini approach, first-order formalism

A variational method in which the metric (here gothic g^ab) and a symmetric connection are independent dynamical variables in L = gothic g^ab R_ab(Gamma); the metric variation gives the vacuum equations and the connection variation determines the connection.

**How introduced:** Introduced as the elegant alternative after the metric-only routes; relies again on the contracted Palatini identity and integration by parts.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein-Hilbert Lagrangian, contracted Palatini identity, symmetric connection, divergence theorem for vector densities

\mathcal{L}_G = \mathcal{L}_G(\mathfrak{g}^{ab},\Gamma^a_{bc},\Gamma^a_{bc,d})

**Notes:** With a non-symmetric connection the method leaves a projective freedom and torsion issues; the book avoids this by assuming symmetry.

### symmetric connection

*definition · revisited* · also: torsion-free connection

An affine connection whose coefficients are symmetric in their two lower indices, Gamma^a_bc = Gamma^a_cb.

**How introduced:** Assumed from the start in the Palatini approach; the symmetry of delta Gamma means only the symmetric part of its coefficient is forced to vanish.

**Prerequisites:** affine connection

\Gamma^a_{bc} = \Gamma^a_{cb}

**Where:** DIV §11.7 p.195 (pdf 210); DIV §11.7 p.196 (pdf 211)

### metric compatibility from the field equations

*phenomenon · core* · also: connection equation of the Palatini method

In the Palatini variation, stationarity with respect to an arbitrary symmetric connection yields equations whose only solution (in any dimension above two) is nabla_c g_ab = 0, so the metric connection is derived rather than assumed.

**How introduced:** Obtained from (11.48) through a chain of deductions for gothic g^ab, sqrt(-g), g^ab and g_ab (Exercise 11.11), closed by Exercise 6.21.

**Prerequisites:** Palatini (metric-affine) variation, metric connection

### metric connection

*definition · revisited* · also: Christoffel connection, Levi-Civita connection

The unique symmetric connection with nabla_c g_ab = 0, whose coefficients are half the inverse metric contracted with the usual three-term combination of first metric derivatives (the Christoffel symbols of the second kind).

### variational (Hilbert) energy-momentum tensor

## Key equations

### (11.1) Riemann tensor in geodesic coordinates · supporting · DIV §11.1 p.187 (pdf 202)

$$
R^a{}_{bcd} \stackrel{*}{=} \partial_c\Gamma^a_{bd} - \partial_d\Gamma^a_{bc}
$$

At a point where the connection vanishes, curvature is just the antisymmetrized derivative of the connection; the quadratic terms of (6.40) drop out.

**Symbols:** asterisk: valid only in geodesic coordinates at P

### (11.2) Varied connection · supporting · DIV §11.1 p.187 (pdf 202)

$$
\Gamma^a_{bc} \to \bar\Gamma^a_{bc} = \Gamma^a_{bc} + \delta\Gamma^a_{bc}
$$

A nearby connection; the difference delta Gamma is a (1,2) tensor.

### Variation of curvature at P · derivation-step · DIV §11.1 p.187 (pdf 202)

$$
\delta R^a{}_{bcd} \stackrel{*}{=} \partial_c(\delta\Gamma^a_{bd}) - \partial_d(\delta\Gamma^a_{bc}) \stackrel{*}{=} \nabla_c(\delta\Gamma^a_{bd}) - \nabla_d(\delta\Gamma^a_{bc})
$$

Variation commutes with partial derivatives, which equal covariant derivatives at P in geodesic coordinates.

### (11.3) Palatini identity · central · DIV §11.1 p.187 (pdf 202)

$$
\delta R^a{}_{bcd} = \nabla_c(\delta\Gamma^a_{bd}) - \nabla_d(\delta\Gamma^a_{bc})
$$

The first-order change in the Riemann tensor is a covariant 'curl' of the change in the connection; valid in all coordinates since both sides are tensors.

**Symbols:** nabla: covariant derivative of the unvaried connection

### (11.4) Contracted Palatini identity · central · DIV §11.1 p.187 (pdf 202)

$$
\delta R_{bd} = \nabla_a(\delta\Gamma^a_{bd}) - \nabla_d(\delta\Gamma^a_{ba})
$$

Variation of the Ricci tensor, obtained by contracting a with c; used to turn the delta R_ab contribution to the action into a divergence.

**Symbols:** R_bd = R^a_bad

### (11.5) Lagrangian dependence · supporting · DIV §11.2 p.188 (pdf 203)

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}(g_{ab},\partial_c g_{ab},\partial_d\partial_c g_{ab},\dots)
$$

The Lagrangian density is a function of the metric and its derivatives.

### (11.6) Action · central · DIV §11.2 p.188 (pdf 203)

$$
I = \int_\Omega \mathcal{L}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega
$$

Integral of the weight +1 scalar density over a region; coordinate invariant.

**Symbols:** Omega: region; dOmega: coordinate volume element

### (11.7) Stationary action · supporting · DIV §11.2 p.188 (pdf 203)

$$
g_{ab}\to g_{ab}+\delta g_{ab}\ \Rightarrow\ I\to I+\delta I\ \text{with}\ \delta I = 0
$$

For metric variations vanishing on the boundary, the action does not change to first order.

### (11.8) First variation of the action · central · DIV §11.2 p.188 (pdf 203)

$$
\delta I = \int_\Omega \mathcal{L}^{ab}\,\delta g_{ab}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega
$$

The change of the action is linear in delta g_ab with coefficient L^ab (after discarding boundary terms).

### (11.9) Euler-Lagrange tensor density · central · DIV §11.2 p.188 (pdf 203)

$$
\mathcal{L}^{ab} := \frac{\delta\mathcal{L}}{\delta g_{ab}}
$$

Definition of the variational derivative; it is a symmetric tensor density of weight +1.

### (11.10) Field equations · supporting · DIV §11.2 p.188 (pdf 203)

$$
\mathcal{L}^{ab} = 0
$$

The field equations of the theory are the vanishing of the Euler-Lagrange derivative.

### (11.11) Invariance under coordinate change · supporting · DIV §11.2 p.188 (pdf 203)

$$
\delta I \equiv 0
$$

If the 'variation' is produced by a coordinate change, the action is unchanged identically, not just on solutions.

### (11.12) Infinitesimal coordinate change · supporting · DIV §11.2 p.189 (pdf 204)

$$
x^a \to x'^a = x^a + \varepsilon X^a(x)
$$

Generator X^a is a smooth vector field vanishing on the boundary of Omega.

**Symbols:** epsilon: small parameter

### (11.13) Metric change under coordinate change · central · DIV §11.2 p.189 (pdf 204)

$$
\delta g_{ab} = g'_{ab}(x) - g_{ab}(x) = -\mathrm{L}_{\varepsilon X}g_{ab} = -\varepsilon(\nabla_b X_a + \nabla_a X_b)
$$

Comparing new and old metric components at the same coordinate values gives minus the Lie derivative, i.e. the symmetrized covariant derivative of X.

**Symbols:** L_{epsilon X}: Lie derivative along epsilon X

### Identity in integral form · derivation-step · DIV §11.2 p.189 (pdf 204)

Substituting (11.13) into (11.8) and using symmetry of L^ab.

### (11.14) Integration by parts · derivation-step · DIV §11.2 p.189 (pdf 204)

$$

$$

Leibniz rule for covariant derivatives splits the integral into a bulk term and a total divergence.

### (11.15) Divergence to surface term · derivation-step · DIV §11.2 p.189 (pdf 204)

$$

The covariant divergence of a weight +1 vector density is an ordinary divergence; the divergence theorem makes it a boundary flux, which vanishes since X does.

**Symbols:** dS_b: boundary surface element

### (11.16) · derivation-step · DIV §11.2 p.189 (pdf 204)

## Worked examples

### Inline derivation §11.1: Palatini identity · standard · DIV §11.1 p.187 (pdf 202)

**Problem:** Find the first-order change in the Riemann tensor when the connection is perturbed.

**Method:** Work at an arbitrary point in geodesic coordinates, where the curvature is the antisymmetrized derivative of Gamma; vary, swap partial for covariant derivatives at the point, then use the tensor character of delta Gamma and delta R to extend the equation to all charts and points; contract.

**Key insight:** A calculation that looks coordinate dependent becomes a general identity once every object in it is shown to be a tensor.

**Result:** \delta R^a{}_{bcd} = \nabla_c(\delta\Gamma^a_{bd}) - \nabla_d(\delta\Gamma^a_{bc}),\ \delta R_{bd} = \nabla_a(\delta\Gamma^a_{bd}) - \nabla_d(\delta\Gamma^a_{ba})

**Concepts:** Palatini identity, geodesic coordinates, variation of a connection

### Inline derivation §11.2: identities from coordinate invariance · challenging · DIV §11.2 p.189 (pdf 204)

**Problem:** Show that for any metric Lagrangian that is a scalar density, the Euler-Lagrange tensor density obeys four differential identities.

**Method:** Induce delta g_ab by an infinitesimal coordinate change generated by X vanishing on the boundary; the action is unchanged; substitute the Lie-derivative formula, integrate by parts, convert the divergence of a weight +1 density to a surface integral that vanishes, and apply the arbitrariness of Omega and of X.

**Key insight:** The identities follow from covariance alone and hold off-shell; for GR they are the contracted Bianchi identities, so ten field equations carry four built-in constraints.

**Result:** \nabla_b\mathcal{L}^{ab} \equiv 0

**Concepts:** generalized Bianchi identity, diffeomorphism invariance of the action, divergence theorem for vector densities

### Inline example §11.3: the Lagrangian sqrt(-g) · intro · DIV §11.3 p.190 (pdf 205)

**Problem:** Compute the field equations and identities for the Lagrangian consisting only of sqrt(-g).

**Method:** Differentiate the determinant using (7.8), apply the chain rule, and substitute into the general identity.

**Key insight:** The machinery works but this Lagrangian alone is physically empty; it is a harmless consistency check of the identity.

**Result:** \mathcal{L}^{ab} = \tfrac12\mathfrak{g}^{ab},\ \nabla_b\mathfrak{g}^{ab} \equiv 0

**Concepts:** volume Lagrangian sqrt(-g), derivative of the metric determinant

### Inline calculation §11.4: brute-force Einstein Lagrangian · challenging · DIV §11.4 p.191 (pdf 206)

**Problem:** Attempt the Euler-Lagrange derivative of sqrt(-g) R directly as a function of g_ab and its first and second derivatives.

**Method:** Expand R in Christoffel symbols and those in metric derivatives (11.24), write the second-order Euler-Lagrange expression (11.25), and compute the second-derivative coefficient (11.26); the remaining pieces are skipped and the final answer quoted.

**Key insight:** Displaying the expansion makes the case for a smarter method; the one computed coefficient already hints that the equations will be only second order.

**Result:** \delta\mathcal{L}_G/\delta g_{ab} = -\sqrt{-g}\,G^{ab}

**Concepts:** Einstein-Hilbert Lagrangian, Euler-Lagrange derivative with second derivatives

### Inline derivation §11.5: variation of the inverse metric · intro · DIV §11.5 p.192 (pdf 207)

**Problem:** Express delta g^ab in terms of delta g_ab.

**Method:** Vary the identity g^ab g_bc = delta^a_c to first order using the product rule and multiply by g^cd.

**Key insight:** Constants have zero variation; the inverse relation introduces a minus sign, just like the derivative of a matrix inverse.

**Result:** \delta g^{ad} = -g^{ab}g^{cd}\delta g_{bc}

**Concepts:** variation of the inverse metric, variation as a derivation

### Inline derivation §11.5: field equations by the indirect method · standard · DIV §11.5 p.193 (pdf 208)

**Problem:** Derive the vacuum field equations from sqrt(-g) R without brute force.

**Method:** Write the action as gothic g^ab R_ab; product rule; replace delta R_ab by (11.4) and use nabla gothic g^ab = 0 to form a total divergence that becomes a boundary term; expand delta(sqrt(-g) g^ab) with (11.35) and (11.32); collect into the Einstein tensor.

**Key insight:** The Palatini identity isolates all derivatives of the variation into a surface term, leaving an algebraic coefficient of delta g_ab.

**Result:** \delta I = \int_\Omega[-\sqrt{-g}\,G^{ab}]\delta g_{ab}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega

**Concepts:** Einstein-Hilbert Lagrangian, contracted Palatini identity, boundary term in the Einstein-Hilbert variation, variation of sqrt(-g)

### Inline derivation §11.6: first-order Lagrangian · challenging · DIV §11.6 p.194 (pdf 209)

**Problem:** Show sqrt(-g) R equals a first-order Lagrangian plus a divergence, and derive the field equations from the first-order Lagrangian.

**Method:** Split gothic g^ab R_ab into derivative-of-Gamma terms and quadratic terms; integrate by parts to generate Q^a_,a; use (11.41) to turn the remaining pieces into L-bar_G; then treat L-bar_G as a function of gothic g^ab and its first derivatives, compute (11.42)-(11.43), form the Euler-Lagrange expression and simplify with (11.46).

**Key insight:** The apparent second derivatives in the Einstein Lagrangian sit entirely inside a total divergence, which is why the equations are second order.

**Result:** \mathcal{L}_G = \bar{\mathcal{L}}_G + Q^a{}_{,a},\ \bar{\mathcal{L}}_G^{ab} = R_{ab}

**Concepts:** Gamma-Gamma Lagrangian, total divergence ambiguity of a Lagrangian, second-order nature of the Einstein equations

### Inline derivation §11.7: Palatini variation · challenging · DIV §11.7 p.196 (pdf 211)

**Problem:** Vary gothic g^ab R_ab(Gamma) independently with respect to the metric and a symmetric connection.

**Method:** Metric variation gives R_ab = 0 immediately. For the connection, apply (11.4), integrate by parts, relabel to factor out delta Gamma^c_ab, symmetrize in a,b, and deduce successively that gothic g^ab, sqrt(-g), g^ab and g_ab are covariantly constant; Exercise 6.21 then fixes Gamma.

**Key insight:** The Levi-Civita connection is an equation of motion of the first-order action rather than an assumption.

**Result:** \tfrac12\delta^b_c\nabla_d\mathfrak{g}^{ad} + \tfrac12\delta^a_c\nabla_d\mathfrak{g}^{bd} - \nabla_c\mathfrak{g}^{ab} = 0\ \Rightarrow\ \nabla_c g_{ab} = 0

**Concepts:** Palatini (metric-affine) variation, metric compatibility from the field equations

### Inline derivation §11.8: coupling matter · standard · DIV §11.8 p.197 (pdf 212)

**Problem:** Obtain the full field equations from a combined gravitational and matter action.

**Method:** Vary the integral of L_G + 2 kappa L_M with respect to g_ab, define T^ab from the matter variation, and divide by sqrt(-g).

**Key insight:** The energy-momentum tensor can be defined as the response of the matter action to a change of metric; the factor 2 makes it agree with the canonical tensor.

**Result:** G^{ab} = \kappa T^{ab}

**Concepts:** variational (Hilbert) energy-momentum tensor, matter Lagrangian, full field equations

### The variation symbol behaves like a derivative (linear, Leibniz rule), so delta g^ab mirrors the formula for differentiating an inverse matrix. → variation as a derivation · useful · DIV §11.5 p.192 (pdf 207)

The authors apply the product rule to g^ab g_bc = delta^a_c and explicitly ask the reader to compare the result with the partial-derivative relation (7.9).

**Where it breaks down:** Variation acts on the space of field configurations at fixed coordinates, not along a direction in spacetime; it commutes with partial derivatives but is not itself a derivative with respect to a coordinate.

**App idea:** Matrix sandbox: learner perturbs a 4x4 symmetric metric with a small random symmetric matrix, and the app shows the exact change in the inverse and in sqrt(-g) beside the first-order predictions -g^-1 dg g^-1 and (1/2) sqrt(-g) tr(g^-1 dg), with the error shrinking quadratically as the perturbation slider is reduced.

### A coordinate change as a 'variation' that cannot change anything physical → generalized Bianchi identity · strong · DIV §11.2 p.188 (pdf 203)

Relabelling points inside a region alters the numbers g_ab but not the action, so the Euler-Lagrange tensor must be orthogonal to every such change; this yields the identity without knowing L.

**Where it breaks down:** The argument needs the Lagrangian to be a true scalar density; it does not apply directly to the non-covariant Gamma-Gamma Lagrangian of §11.6.

**App idea:** Show a 2D region with a coordinate grid and a metric-coloured field; the learner drags a bump vector field X (zero at the boundary) that warps the grid; component plots of g_ab change visibly while an 'action' readout stays fixed.

### Proving an identity in a convenient frame, then promoting it: compute where the connection vanishes, then let tensor transformation carry the result everywhere → Palatini identity · strong · DIV §11.1 p.187 (pdf 202)

Geodesic coordinates kill the quadratic Gamma terms; since delta Gamma and delta R are tensors, the simplified equation is automatically general.

**Where it breaks down:** Works only for equations whose every term is a tensor; derivatives of the connection itself are not tensors and cannot be promoted.

**App idea:** A two-panel proof card: left shows a general chart with all Gamma terms visible; toggling 'geodesic coordinates at P' greys out the Gamma terms and highlights which surviving objects are tensors, with a check mark lighting up for each before the 'valid everywhere' badge appears.

### Adding a total time derivative to a mechanical Lagrangian does not change the equations of motion → total divergence ambiguity of a Lagrangian · useful · DIV p.199 (pdf 214)

Exercise 11.9 reduces the field-theory statement L_G = L-bar_G + Q^a_,a to the familiar one-dimensional version with dQ/dx.

**Where it breaks down:** In field theory the boundary term can matter for the well-posedness of the variational problem and for energy definitions, even though bulk equations are unchanged.

**App idea:** Pendulum plotter: the learner types an extra function Q(theta, t); the app shows the Lagrangian curve change while the computed trajectory from the Euler-Lagrange equation stays identical.

### Letting the action choose the connection, like letting a variational principle choose a path → Palatini (metric-affine) variation · useful · DIV §11.7 p.195 (pdf 210)

The Palatini approach treats the connection as another unknown field; the stationarity condition then selects the metric connection, just as stationarity selects the geodesic among trial curves.

**Where it breaks down:** Works cleanly only with a symmetric connection in more than two dimensions and with matter that does not couple to the connection independently.

**App idea:** On a curved 2-surface the learner blends the Levi-Civita connection with an added symmetric tensor C via a slider; the app parallel-transports a vector around a loop, shows its length drifting when C is not zero, and displays the residual of the connection equation which vanishes only at C = 0.


## Misconceptions addressed

### A Lagrangian containing second derivatives of the metric must give fourth-order field equations. · DIV §11.6 p.193 (pdf 208)

**Correction:** For sqrt(-g) R the second-derivative coefficient contains only undifferentiated metric, and the second derivatives can be packed into a total divergence, so the equations are second order.

**Why tempting:** The generic Euler-Lagrange formula (11.25) contains a double divergence of a term that could itself contain second derivatives.

### The divergence identity nabla_b L^ab = 0 holds only for solutions of the field equations. · DIV §11.2 p.188 (pdf 203)

**Correction:** It holds identically for every metric, because it follows from invariance of the action under coordinate changes rather than from stationarity.

**Why tempting:** Most results obtained from a variational principle are on-shell statements.

### A valid gravitational Lagrangian must itself be a scalar density. · DIV §11.6 p.194 (pdf 209)

**Correction:** The first-order Gamma-Gamma Lagrangian is not a scalar density but gives exactly the same field equations, since it differs from sqrt(-g) R by a divergence. Covariance lives in the equations, not necessarily in every equivalent Lagrangian.

**Why tempting:** §11.2 explicitly required a weight +1 scalar density to form an invariant action.

### The connection must be assumed to be the Christoffel connection before a gravitational action can be written. · DIV §11.7 p.196 (pdf 211)

**Correction:** In the Palatini approach the connection is an independent symmetric field and the connection field equation forces metric compatibility.

**Why tempting:** Earlier chapters work exclusively with the metric connection once it is introduced.

### The variation of the inverse metric is obtained by simply raising the indices of delta g_ab. · DIV §11.5 p.192 (pdf 207)

**Correction:** Varying the inverse metric gives delta g^ab = -g^ac g^bd delta g_cd. So delta(g^ab) is minus the object you get by raising both indices of delta g_ab with the unvaried metric. Leaving out that minus sign is the usual slip.

**Why tempting:** Index gymnastics with the metric normally raises indices without sign changes.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### A connection variation is not a tensor because connections are not tensors. · DIV §11.1 p.187 (pdf 202)

**Correction:** The non-tensorial part of the transformation law is the same for both connections and cancels in the difference, so delta Gamma is a (1,2) tensor.

**Why tempting:** Learners remember firmly that Gamma is not a tensor.

### The energy-momentum tensor can only be built by guessing physically reasonable expressions. · DIV §11.8 p.197 (pdf 212)

**Correction:** Given a matter Lagrangian, T^ab is defined systematically by its metric variation, normalized with a factor 2 to match the canonical tensor.

**Why tempting:** Chapter 12 first builds T_ab for dust and perfect fluids by physical reasoning. Only in §12.7-§12.8 do the authors contrast that ad hoc method with the variational route set up here.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The trivial Lagrangian sqrt(-g) is useless for physics. · DIV §11.3 p.190 (pdf 205)

**Correction:** On its own it gives impossible equations, but added to sqrt(-g) R with coefficient 2 Lambda it produces the cosmological term (used in DIV ch13 §13.3).

**Why tempting:** §11.3 dismisses it as unable to serve as a physical theory.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Different choices of dynamical variable (g_ab, g^ab, gothic g^ab) give different theories. · DIV §11.6 p.194 (pdf 209)

**Correction:** They change the Euler-Lagrange expression by invertible algebraic factors (e.g. -sqrt(-g) G^ab versus R_ab versus sqrt(-g) G_ab), so the solution sets coincide.

**Why tempting:** The resulting equations look different, e.g. G^ab = 0 versus R_ab = 0.


## Thought experiments

### Shaking the coordinates inside a region · DIV §11.2 p.188 (pdf 203)

**Setup:** Imagine relabelling spacetime points inside a region by an infinitesimal vector field that is zero on the boundary, and treat the resulting change in metric components as a variation of the field.

**Lesson:** Since nothing physical changed, the action cannot change for any metric; this forces the Euler-Lagrange tensor to be divergence-free identically, explaining the Bianchi identity and the four redundancies in Einstein's equations.

**App idea:** Animated grid warp inside a fixed boundary with a live readout of changed g_ab components and an unchanged action integral.

### Pretend the connection is unknown · DIV §11.7 p.195 (pdf 210)

**Setup:** Write the gravitational action with the metric and an unrelated symmetric connection, and ask the variational principle to decide both.

**Lesson:** The connection equation leaves only the Levi-Civita connection, so metric compatibility can be seen as dynamics rather than definition.

**App idea:** Connection slider on a sphere showing parallel transport with and without metric compatibility.


## Applications and observations

- **Counting independent field equations (own estimate)** (numerical-estimate, DIV §11.2 p.189 (pdf 204)): In four dimensions the symmetric tensor L^ab has 10 components, and nabla_b L^ab = 0 imposes 4 differential relations among them. That leaves 6 independent equations, while the 4 coordinate freedoms account for the other 4 metric functions. Chapter 11 does not do this count; DIV ch13 §13.2 does it for G_ab = 0. Key numbers: 10 components of g_ab; 4 identities; 4 coordinate functions; 6 genuinely dynamical equations (own count)
- **Size of the Palatini variational problem (own estimate)** (numerical-estimate, DIV §11.7 p.196 (pdf 211)): Treating a symmetric connection as independent in four dimensions adds 40 unknown functions to the 10 metric components; the connection equation (11.48) supplies 40 algebraic-differential conditions that fix all 40. Key numbers: 40 components of symmetric Gamma^a_bc; 10 of g_ab; 50 equations total (own count)

## Historical notes

- **Attilio Palatini, Albert Einstein (1919):** Palatini's name is attached both to the formula for the variation of the Riemann tensor and to the method of varying metric and connection independently. Own historical note, not in the book: Palatini's 1919 paper gave the curvature-variation identity. The independent metric-affine variation was introduced by Einstein in 1925, so calling it 'the Palatini approach' is a historical misattribution. — Supplies the chapter's central tool and its most elegant derivation. (DIV §11.1 p.187 (pdf 202))
- **Erwin Schrödinger (1950):** The authors state that their variational treatment follows Schrödinger's short book Space-time Structure. — Identifies the pedagogical lineage of the chapter's sequence (identities, indirect variation, first-order Lagrangian, Palatini). (DIV p.201 (pdf 216))
- **Arthur S. Eddington:** Eddington considered a gravitational theory based on the square of the Riemann tensor, set here as Exercise 11.12. — An early example of an alternative, higher-curvature action treated with independent variables. (DIV p.200 (pdf 215))
- **David Hilbert, Albert Einstein (1915):** Hilbert obtained the field equations from a variational principle in the same year Einstein published them; this chapter calls sqrt(-g) R the Einstein Lagrangian and leaves the attribution to DIV ch13 §13.3. — Explains why the Lagrangian is commonly called Einstein-Hilbert in other texts. (DIV §11.4 p.190 (pdf 205))

## Notation and conventions

- **Asterisk over equals sign:** An equation marked with a starred equals sign holds only in a particular coordinate system, here geodesic coordinates at P. (DIV §11.1 p.187 (pdf 202))
- **Riemann and Ricci index conventions:** R^a_bcd = partial_c Gamma^a_bd - partial_d Gamma^a_bc + Gamma^e_bd Gamma^a_ec - Gamma^e_bc Gamma^a_ed (6.40); Ricci by contracting first and third indices, R_bd = R^a_bad. — With these conventions and signature (+,-,-,-) the action uses +sqrt(-g) R and the field equations read G^ab = kappa T^ab with positive kappa. (DIV §11.1 p.187 (pdf 202))
- **Gothic (fraktur) letters:** A gothic tensor means the tensor multiplied by sqrt(-g), a weight +1 density; e.g. gothic g^ab = sqrt(-g) g^ab. (DIV §11.3 p.189 (pdf 204))
- **Comma derivatives:** g_ab,c = partial_c g_ab and g_ab,cd for second derivatives; partial L / partial g_ab,cd means differentiation with respect to the second-derivative variable. — In (11.43) and (11.44) the ', c' is typeset as a subscript under the superscript of gothic g^ab in the denominator. It means differentiation with respect to gothic g^ab_,c. The OCR reading copy prints it as a loose ', c'. (DIV §11.4 p.191 (pdf 206))
- **Variational notation:** delta X is a first-order variation; delta L / delta g_ab is the Euler-Lagrange derivative; an identically-equal sign marks results valid for all metrics. (DIV §11.2 p.188 (pdf 203))
- **Subscripts on Lagrangians:** L_G for the gravitational (Einstein) Lagrangian, L_M for matter, L-bar_G for the first-order equivalent Lagrangian. (DIV §11.4 p.190 (pdf 205))
- **Action normalization and energy-momentum tensor:** I = integral of (L_G + 2 kappa L_M) dOmega and T^ab = (2/sqrt(-g)) delta L_M / delta g_ab; equivalently sqrt(-g) T_ab = -2 delta L_M / delta g^ab. — Some authors drop the 2 in both places, which leaves G^ab = kappa T^ab unchanged. (DIV §11.8 p.197 (pdf 212))
- **Signature and sign of the scalar-field Lagrangian:** With the book's signature (+,-,-,-) the scalar field Lagrangian in Exercise 11.13 is written -(1/2) g^cd phi_,c phi_,d + V(phi), and the canonical tensor carries a minus sign on its derivative term, so that Theta_00 is the positive energy density. — This is the overall negative of the usual particle-physics Lagrangian in the same signature; mixing sources can flip the sign of T_ab. (DIV p.200 (pdf 215))
- **Integration measure:** dOmega denotes the coordinate volume element and dS_b the coordinate surface element; densities of weight +1 supply the sqrt(-g). (DIV §11.2 p.188 (pdf 203))

## Teaching gems

### Derive the contracted Bianchi identity from nothing but coordinate invariance of an unspecified action, before choosing any Lagrangian. · DIV §11.2 p.188 (pdf 203)

**Why it works:** It turns a mysterious curvature identity into an inevitable consequence of general covariance and explains in one stroke why Einstein's equations have built-in redundancy and why the source must be conserved.

**App idea:** Interactive 2D region: learner sketches a smooth bump vector field X inside a boundary; the app deforms the grid, plots delta g_ab = -(nabla_a X_b + nabla_b X_a) as a colour map, and steps through integration by parts with the boundary flux counter reading zero, ending at a highlighted nabla_b L^ab = 0.

### Prove the Palatini identity in two lines by computing in geodesic coordinates and then promoting via tensor character. · DIV §11.1 p.187 (pdf 202)

**Why it works:** Models a reusable proof strategy and shows why tensoriality of the difference of connections matters.

**App idea:** Step-through derivation widget where each term carries a 'tensor?' badge; switching to geodesic coordinates hides Gamma terms, and the learner must mark every surviving object as tensorial before the 'holds in every chart' step unlocks.

### Warm up with the trivial Lagrangian sqrt(-g) to exercise the whole framework on a case whose answer is obvious. · DIV §11.3 p.190 (pdf 205)

**Why it works:** Learners check the machinery (determinant derivative, Euler-Lagrange tensor, identity) without drowning in algebra, and the result later reappears as the cosmological constant.

**App idea:** Lagrangian mixer: sliders for coefficients a and b in a sqrt(-g) + b sqrt(-g) R; the app displays the Euler-Lagrange tensor -b sqrt(-g) G^ab + (a/2) sqrt(-g) g^ab, and a toggle relabels it as the Einstein equation with cosmological constant Lambda = a/(2b).

### Show the full brute-force expansion of sqrt(-g) R, then abandon it for the indirect method. · DIV §11.4 p.191 (pdf 206)

**Why it works:** The visible explosion of terms makes the value of clever structure (product rule, Palatini identity, boundary terms) felt rather than asserted.

**App idea:** An 'expand' button that recursively substitutes Christoffel symbols into sqrt(-g) R with a live term counter, side by side with the six-line indirect derivation that finishes first.

### Resolve the fourth-order puzzle by splitting off a total divergence to leave a Gamma-Gamma Lagrangian. · DIV §11.6 p.193 (pdf 208)

**Why it works:** A concrete paradox gives the decomposition a purpose and teaches that Lagrangians are defined only up to boundary terms.

**App idea:** Colour-coded term tracker: terms of gothic g^ab R_ab are dragged into 'divergence' and 'first-order' bins during integration by parts, with a derivative-order meter that drops from 2 to 1 for the bulk Lagrangian.

### Let the action decide the connection (Palatini). · DIV §11.7 p.196 (pdf 211)

**Why it works:** It reverses an assumption learners have taken for granted, showing that metric compatibility can be a field equation.

**App idea:** On a sphere, the learner adds a symmetric distortion C to the Levi-Civita connection; transported vectors change length, the Palatini residual (11.48) is plotted, and the action's first variation with respect to C is shown to vanish only at C = 0.

### Define T^ab as the response of the matter action to a change of metric, with the factor 2 checked against the canonical tensor. · DIV §11.8 p.197 (pdf 212)

**Why it works:** Gives a universal recipe for energy-momentum tensors and ties it back to flat-space intuition about energy density.

**App idea:** Scalar-field playground in 1+1 dimensions: sliders for field time derivative, gradient and potential; the app computes the canonical Theta_00 and the metric-variation T_00 and shows they coincide, with a toggle that removes the factor 2 to show the mismatch.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Printed text says the generating vector X^a vanishes 'on Omega' when discarding the surface term; the boundary of Omega is meant.** — Taken literally, X would vanish everywhere and the argument would be empty. *Suggestion:* Read as 'vanishes on the boundary of Omega', consistent with the statement after (11.12).
- **In §11.7 the equation after 'the integrand must vanish' is printed without '= 0', and the step from vanishing integral to vanishing integrand needs the symmetry argument that follows.** — An expression with no equality can confuse which quantity is set to zero. *Suggestion:* Write (delta^b_c nabla_d gothic g^ad - nabla_c gothic g^ab) delta Gamma^c_ab = 0 for all symmetric delta Gamma, then symmetrize.
- **(11.26) is printed with a variational delta on the left, while Exercise 11.2 and §11.6 use the partial derivative for the same quantity.** — Confuses the Euler-Lagrange derivative with an ordinary partial derivative. *Suggestion:* Use partial L_G / partial g_ab,cd.
- **The text after (11.34) cites (11.31), but the step substituting delta g^ab actually uses its solved form (11.32).** — Minor; a reader searching for the substitution may be puzzled. *Suggestion:* Point to (11.32) and (11.35) explicitly.
- **Exercise 11.10 contains misprints: (iii) should read gothic g^ab_,b = -gothic g^bc Gamma^a_bc (contract (11.41) on b and c); in (v) delta Gamma^a_ac should be delta Gamma^c_ac; in (vi) the minus sign between the two Kronecker-delta terms inside the bracket should be a plus, otherwise the bracket is antisymmetric in a,b and disagrees with (11.43). Exercise 11.13 also writes Theta^c_a where Theta^c_b is meant.** — Students attempting the guided derivation will fail to reproduce the printed intermediate results. *Suggestion:* Provide corrected statements in any tutor or app version; checking index balance catches (iii) immediately.
- **Discarding the boundary term in §11.5 requires delta Gamma, hence first derivatives of delta g_ab, to vanish on the boundary, not only delta g_ab; the chapter simply says variations vanish on the surface. The Gibbons-Hawking-York boundary term that makes the metric variational problem well posed is not mentioned.** — Readers may later meet the GHY term or the ADM action and not see why it is needed. *Suggestion:* Add a short note: for fixed boundary metric only, a boundary term involving the extrinsic curvature must be added; the first-order Gamma-Gamma Lagrangian sidesteps this.
- **§11.4 simply asserts that sqrt(-g) R is the next simplest choice after sqrt(-g). It gives no argument that this choice is forced, and no mention of alternatives such as the curvature-squared Lagrangians that Exercise 11.12 hints at. (Own note.) The standard justification is Lovelock's theorem: in four dimensions, the only symmetric, divergence-free tensors built from the metric and its first two derivatives are combinations a G^ab + b g^ab.** — Learners may think the Einstein Lagrangian is simply the first thing to try, rather than essentially the only choice that gives second-order equations. *Suggestion:* When presenting §11.4, state Lovelock's result: together with the §11.3 result it explains why the most general answer is R plus a cosmological constant.
- **In the Palatini section the metric variation is said to give R_ab = 0 before the connection is identified; for an arbitrary symmetric connection R_ab need not be symmetric, and only its symmetric part is constrained by varying the symmetric gothic g^ab.** — Could suggest the vacuum equations hold for any connection, or hide the logical order of the two variations. *Suggestion:* Present the connection variation first, conclude Gamma is Christoffel, then note R_(ab) = 0 becomes the usual vacuum equations.
- **The Palatini deduction from (11.48) to nabla sqrt(-g) = 0 uses the determinant of gothic g^ab, which equals -1 identically in two dimensions; the argument works in any dimension above two, but the book never flags this dependence. Non-symmetric connections (projective freedom, torsion) are excluded without comment.** — Advanced readers generalizing the method may be misled. *Suggestion:* State the n > 2 and torsion-free assumptions explicitly; mention projective invariance for general connections as an aside.
- **Partial derivatives with respect to symmetric variables such as g_ab are used without discussing how off-diagonal components are counted; Exercise 11.3 quietly symmetrizes.** — Factor-of-2 errors in off-diagonal components when computing by hand or with computer algebra. *Suggestion:* Adopt the convention that derivatives are symmetrized, or treat g_ab and g_ba as independent and symmetrize at the end.
- **Noether's theorem is not named, and there is no discussion of how the matter field equations plus diffeomorphism invariance of L_M imply nabla_b T^ab = 0.** — Misses the most important physical payoff of the identity: consistency of G^ab = kappa T^ab with matter conservation. *Suggestion:* Apply the §11.2 argument to L_M alone, using the matter field equations to kill the matter-variation term, to obtain conservation of T^ab.
- **In the same signature, the scalar-field Lagrangian of Exercise 11.13 has the opposite overall sign to the usual particle-physics form. DIV ch12 (12.63) uses the same sign as the exercise, so the book is internally consistent. Chapter 11 never explains the overall sign choices behind (11.49).** — Learners combining this with other textbooks may obtain negative energy densities. *Suggestion:* Check the sign by evaluating T_00 for a constant field: it must come out as +V(phi). For a general configuration it should be (1/2)(phi-dot^2 + |grad phi|^2) + V.

## Tutor notes

- Check prerequisites first: can the learner vary a determinant, integrate a total divergence to a boundary term, and write the Euler-Lagrange equations for a field? If not, revisit DIV ch07 §7.3-§7.5 before starting.
- Open with a motivating question: 'Chapter 10 guessed Einstein's equations. If all we know is that the action must be a scalar, what can we say about the field equations before we pick a Lagrangian?' Let the learner suspect there must be constraints.
- Suggested order: Palatini identity (as a tool), action and Euler-Lagrange tensor, identity from coordinate invariance, sqrt(-g) warm-up, indirect derivation of G^ab, then the second-order puzzle, then Palatini, then matter coupling. The brute-force expansion (11.24) can be shown briefly for effect only.
- When teaching the Palatini identity, have the learner say out loud why delta Gamma is a tensor; this is the step most often skipped and it is essential to the promotion argument.
- For the identity derivation, stress 'identically': ask 'does this hold for a metric that does not solve the field equations?' A correct answer (yes) shows understanding of off-shell versus on-shell.
