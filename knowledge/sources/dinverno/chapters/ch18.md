# DIV ch18 · Maximal extension and conformal compactification

> Defines maximal and geodesically complete spacetimes, builds Kruskal's maximal analytic extension of Schwarzschild by reparametrising the two radial null coordinates, reads off the four-region Kruskal diagram and the Einstein-Rosen bridge, then invents Penrose diagrams by conformally squeezing Minkowski spacetime into a finite diamond of the Einstein static universe, and ends with the Kruskal and collapse Penrose diagrams and the idea of a trapped surface.

**Pages:** printed 343–354 · pdf 358–369 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 4/5, novice-friendliness 2/5

The algebra is elementary (chain rule, exponentials, trigonometric identities) but many steps are left as exercises and the Kruskal compactification is only stated. The conceptual load is high: maximality, topology versus geometry, conformal boundaries and trapped surfaces all arrive in about ten pages, with coordinate names reused (t', r', w) and a few inconsistencies (printed Kruskal compactification range, pinch-off time t' = 1 versus (18.8), a sign-flipped metric in a parenthetical). The diagrams carry the argument, so learners who cannot yet read spacetime diagrams fluently will struggle.

## Role in the book

Second chapter of Part D. Chapter 17 showed that the Schwarzschild exterior can be continued through r = 2m in two inequivalent ways (advanced and retarded Eddington-Finkelstein charts, black hole and white hole). This short, diagram-heavy chapter asks whether those continuations can be carried further, answers with the unique Kruskal extension, and then supplies the global tool (conformal compactification and Penrose diagrams, with scri and the points i+, i0, i-) that Chapter 19 uses for Reissner-Nordstrom, Chapter 20 for Kerr and the singularity theorems, and Part F for the conformal structure of cosmological models. It also plants trapped surfaces as the ingredient behind the singularity theorems of §20.13.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can state what it means for a spacetime to be maximal and to be geodesically complete, and explain why complete implies maximal but not conversely, using Minkowski and Kruskal as examples.
- Reader can rewrite the Schwarzschild metric in double null coordinates (v, w) and explain why the (t, r) 2-metric is automatically conformally flat.
- Reader can explain why reparametrising v and w separately keeps the 2-metric in conformally flat null form, and carry out Kruskal's exponential choice to obtain (18.8)-(18.9).
- Reader can sketch the Kruskal diagram, locate the horizons, the spacelike future and past singularities, lines of constant r and t, and identify regions I, II, I', II' with the exterior, black hole, second exterior and white hole.
- Reader can describe the Einstein-Rosen bridge on the t' = 0 slice, explain how the throat evolves and pinches off, and distinguish it from a single-universe Schwarzschild wormhole with the same local geometry.
- Reader can explain the physical/unphysical metric construction, why a conformal factor leaves light cones and causal structure unchanged, and how arctan-type maps bring infinity to finite coordinate values.
- Reader can carry out the conformal compactification of Minkowski spacetime step by step to (18.27) and recognise the result as a finite diamond on the Einstein static universe cylinder.
- Reader can name and locate i+, i-, i0, scri+ and scri- on the Minkowski Penrose diagram and state where timelike, null and spacelike geodesics begin and end.
- Reader can read the Kruskal Penrose diagram: which infinities belong to regions I and I', where ingoing and outgoing radial light rays from any point end up, and why region II is inescapable.
- Reader can define a trapped surface via the areas of the two families of wave fronts from an illuminated 2-sphere, classify spheres in regions I, II and II', and explain why closedness is essential.
- Reader can explain why the Penrose diagram of realistic spherical collapse contains only parts of regions I and II and no second universe or white hole.

## Teaching approach

A compact, coordinate-driven chapter whose real content lives in its diagrams. Each global statement is obtained by choosing clever coordinates for the radial 2-space, then drawn: first a Kruskal diagram with straight 45-degree null lines, then embedding pictures of the t' = constant slices, then Penrose diagrams. Minkowski space is used as a fully worked prototype before the harder Kruskal compactification is simply stated with its picture. Many algebraic steps are deferred to the exercises, and the chapter closes on physical interpretation (trapped surfaces, collapse) rather than on computation.

**Style:** diagram-driven, components-first, spiral-revisit, geometric-first, survey-overview

**Narrative arc**

1. Motivating question: the Eddington-Finkelstein charts continue Schwarzschild past r = 2m; can the manifold be continued even further?
2. Make the question precise: define maximal and geodesically complete spacetimes, note Minkowski is complete, Schwarzschild and EF are not maximal, Kruskal is maximal and unique but incomplete.
3. Model: put both advanced and retarded null coordinates on the radial 2-space, observe conformal flatness, and isolate the freedom to relabel each null family separately.
4. Formalism: Kruskal's exponential relabelling gives a metric with a regular conformal factor, r defined implicitly by a hyperbola equation.
5. Picture: Kruskal diagram with four regions, two spacelike singularities, horizons as asymptotes, and an infalling particle whose signals escape only before crossing r = 2m.
6. Interpretation: embed the t' = 0 slice to reveal the Einstein-Rosen bridge, follow its time evolution to pinch-off, and contrast with a wormhole of different topology to stress that field equations fix local geometry only.
7. New question: the Kruskal diagram still hides infinity. Introduce a physical and an unphysical metric related by a conformal factor that preserves null geodesics.
8. Prototype computation: compactify Minkowski with null coordinates and arctan, arriving at the Einstein static universe metric on a finite diamond.
9. Classify the conformal boundary (scri+-, i+-, i0) and state where each type of geodesic starts and ends; define the Penrose diagram.
10. Application: state the Kruskal compactification and present its Penrose diagram, reading off the fate of radial light rays in each region.
11. Physical payoff: illuminate a 2-sphere to define trapped surfaces, relate them to singularity theorems, and show the Penrose diagram of a collapsing star.

**Signature moves**

- Frames extension as a sharpened question with formal definitions (maximal versus geodesically complete) before any coordinates are chosen.
- Uses the residual freedom v -> v'(v), w -> w'(w) of double null coordinates as the single idea behind both the Kruskal chart and the Penrose compactification.
- Treats Minkowski spacetime as a worked prototype for conformal compactification, then transfers the recipe to Kruskal with details omitted.
- Constantly reminds that each point of a 2D diagram stands for a 2-sphere, and recovers suppressed dimensions by rotating diagrams about an axis (Figs 18.2, 18.3, 18.8).
- Separates local geometry from topology by showing two embeddings of the same slice geometry (bridge versus folded-sheet wormhole).
- Defines trapped surfaces operationally through the areas of the two envelopes of Huygens wavelets from an illuminated sphere, then gives a counterexample to show closedness matters.
- Leaves several decisive steps (metric transformations, drawing constant-coordinate curves, the fate of observers from I and I', what collapse does to I' and II') to short exercises.

## Section by section

### §18.1 Maximal analytic extensions — p.343 (pdf 358)

Recalls that the Eddington-Finkelstein charts extend Schwarzschild inward because radial infall passes smoothly through r = 2m, and asks whether further extension is possible. Makes this precise: a spacetime is maximal if every geodesic either runs to infinite affine parameter both ways or ends at an intrinsic singularity, and geodesically complete if every geodesic runs to infinite affine parameter. Minkowski space is complete; Schwarzschild and both EF charts are not maximal; Kruskal's extension is maximal and unique yet incomplete because of its singularities, and is built by straightening both families of radial null geodesics at once.

**Concepts:** maximal spacetime, geodesic completeness, intrinsic singularity, maximal analytic extension, Kruskal extension

**Key moves**
- Uses the geodesic behaviour at r = 2m (radial timelike geodesics continue to r = 0) as evidence that the Schwarzschild chart is incomplete.
- Replaces the vague question 'can we extend further?' by the affine-parameter criterion for maximality.
- Notes the logical relation: completeness implies maximality, but a maximal spacetime may still have geodesics ending at singularities.
- Announces the strategy for Kruskal: straighten ingoing and outgoing radial null geodesics simultaneously (each EF chart straightens only one family).

### §18.2 The Kruskal solution — p.343 (pdf 358)

Writes Schwarzschild in the double null coordinates v = t + r*, w = t - r* with the tortoise coordinate r*, so the radial 2-metric is (1 - 2m/r) dv dw and hence conformally flat. Any separate relabelling of v and w preserves this null form, giving a general F^2 (dt'^2 - dx'^2). Kruskal's choice v' = exp(v/4m), w' = -exp(-w/4m) yields a conformal factor F^2 = (16m^2/r) exp(-r/2m) that is regular at r = 2m, with r fixed implicitly by t'^2 - x'^2 = -(r - 2m) exp(r/2m). The resulting diagram has 45-degree light cones everywhere, r = constant hyperbolae, horizons along the diagonals, spacelike future and past singularities at r = 0, and four regions: I (exterior), II (black hole), II' (white hole) and a surprising second exterior I'.

**Concepts:** double null coordinates, tortoise coordinate, conformal flatness of two-dimensional metrics, null coordinate reparametrisation freedom, Kruskal coordinates, Kruskal diagram, event horizon, black hole region, white hole region, second asymptotically flat region, spacelike singularity

**Key moves**
- Combine the advanced and retarded EF null coordinates into one chart (18.1)-(18.4).
- Invoke the theorem that every 2D metric is conformally flat, exhibited by t~ = (v+w)/2, x~ = (v-w)/2.
- Observe that v -> v'(v), w -> w'(w) is the most general change keeping the 2-metric in conformally flat double null form.
- Choose exponentials so that the factor (1 - 2m/r) is cancelled by the Jacobian, leaving a factor regular at r = 2m, (18.6)-(18.9).
- Read global structure off (18.8): each r labels a pair of hyperbolae; r = 2m gives the diagonals; r = 0 gives two spacelike boundary hyperbolae.
- Match regions to earlier charts: I and II are the advanced EF chart, I and II' the retarded EF chart, I' is new.
- Trace the radial timelike geodesic drawn from (r = 4m, t' = 0) into region II: the outward signals it emits escape only while it is still outside r = 2m.

### §18.3 The Einstein-Rosen bridge — p.346 (pdf 361)

Restores the suppressed 2-spheres by examining the spatial slice t' = 0. Along the x' axis r falls from infinity to a minimum of 2m at x' = 0 and rises again, so the equatorial slice embeds as two asymptotically flat sheets joined by a throat of radius 2m: the Einstein-Rosen bridge. At later Kruskal times the throat narrows, pinches off where the slice meets r = 0 and leaves two separate universes each with a singularity; the evolution is time symmetric. The authors flag the physical status of region I' as open, emphasise that the field equations fix local geometry but not topology, and show a wormhole embedding that joins two distant parts of one universe with identical local geometry.

**Concepts:** Einstein-Rosen bridge, embedding diagram, dynamical pinch-off of the Einstein-Rosen bridge, local geometry versus global topology, Schwarzschild wormhole

**Key moves**
- Induce the spatial metric (18.10) on t' = 0 and restrict to the equator (18.11).
- Use (18.8) with t' = 0 to see r has a minimum 2m at x' = 0: two exteriors meet at a minimal sphere.
- Embed the 2-geometry in flat 3-space to draw the bridge (Fig. 18.2).
- Follow t' = constant slices: the minimal radius shrinks below 2m, reaches zero at the singularity, then the sheets separate (Fig. 18.3).
- Show a second, topologically different embedding of the same local geometry (Fig. 18.4) to separate geometry from topology.
- Motivate the next tool: the Kruskal diagram does not show infinity.

### §18.4 Penrose diagram for Minkowski space-time — p.347 (pdf 362)

Introduces a physical metric g and an unphysical metric Omega^2 g; because conformally related metrics share null geodesics, the unphysical metric has the same light cones and causal structure, while a suitable Omega can bring infinity to finite coordinates via arctan-type maps. For Minkowski space, null coordinates v = t + r, w = t - r (with v >= w) are squashed by p = arctan v, q = arctan w; the metric becomes a conformal factor times the unphysical metric 4 dp dq - sin^2(p - q) dOmega^2. With t' = p + q, r' = p - q this is the Einstein static universe metric, so all of Minkowski space maps onto a finite diamond of that cylinder. Its boundary consists of the null surfaces scri+ and scri- and the points i+, i0, i-, which serve as the endpoints of timelike, null and spacelike geodesics. The picture of this compactified spacetime is the Penrose diagram, and the method extends to Penrose's simple spacetimes.

**Concepts:** conformal transformation, physical and unphysical metrics, conformal invariance of null geodesics, compactifying coordinate map, conformal compactification, Einstein static universe, 3-sphere, compactified Minkowski spacetime, future and past null infinity, future and past timelike infinity, spatial infinity, Penrose diagram, asymptotically simple spacetime, coordinate singularity

**Key moves**
- State the conformal rescaling (18.12) and its key property: light cones are unchanged.
- Show arctan as the model map from an infinite line to a finite interval (Fig. 18.5).
- Write Minkowski in null coordinates (18.15) with the half-plane constraint v >= w (18.16).
- Apply p = arctan v, q = arctan w and factor out 1/4 sec^2 p sec^2 q to expose the unphysical metric (18.20)-(18.21).
- Rotate to t' = p + q, r' = p - q to recognise the Einstein static universe (18.27) on the restricted range (18.24)-(18.26).
- Picture the ESU as a cylinder via an embedding in 5D flat space and shade the diamond that is Minkowski space (Fig. 18.7).
- List the boundary pieces in (p, q) and assign each as the start or end point of a class of geodesics (Fig. 18.8), with a warning that accelerated curves need not obey the rules.
- Define the Penrose diagram and draw constant-t curves (spacelike slices meeting at i0) and constant-r curves (timelike sphere histories from i- to i+) in it, with p = const and q = const as the -45 and +45 degree radial light rays (Fig. 18.9).

### §18.5 Penrose diagram for the Kruskal solution — p.351 (pdf 366)

Compactifies Kruskal by applying arctan to the Kruskal null coordinates scaled by (2m)^(1/2), states coordinate ranges, omits the computation and presents the Penrose diagram: a flattened hexagon in which regions I and I' each have their own i+, i0, i-, scri+ and scri-, and horizontal wavy edges mark the future and past singularities. From a point outside r = 2m the outgoing radial light ray reaches scri+ while the ingoing one hits the future singularity; inside r = 2m both hit the singularity.

**Concepts:** Penrose diagram of the Kruskal spacetime, black hole region, future and past null infinity

**Key moves**
- Reuse the Minkowski recipe: arctan of null coordinates, here v'' = arctan(v'/(2m)^(1/2)), w'' = arctan(w'/(2m)^(1/2)).
- Read causal fate from 45-degree lines in the diagram rather than from equations.
- Point out that each asymptotic region carries a complete set of conformal infinities.

### Trapped surfaces and the Penrose diagram of collapse (end of §18.5) — p.351 (pdf 366)

Returns to the 2-spheres behind each diagram point. Light flashed from every point of a sphere S0 produces two envelopes, one outgoing and one ingoing. In region I the outgoing envelope has larger area and the ingoing one smaller; in region II both envelopes have smaller area than S0. A closed 2-surface whose light rays converge in both directions is a trapped surface; spheres in II' are time-reversed trapped surfaces. Trapped surfaces are the key input to singularity theorems. The authors defend Penrose's term over 'closed trapped surface' with a Minkowski counterexample: the intersection of two past light cones is convergent in both directions but is not closed and not trapped. The chapter ends with the Penrose diagram of a collapsing spherical star.

**Concepts:** trapped surface, singularity theorems, Penrose diagram of spherical gravitational collapse

**Key moves**
- Replace a diagram point by its 2-sphere and apply Huygens' construction to get two wave fronts (Fig. 18.11).
- Compare areas of the ingoing and outgoing wave fronts with the original sphere to diagnose trapping.
- Classify spheres: normal in I, trapped in II, anti-trapped in II'.
- Use a Minkowski counterexample to argue that convergence alone is not trapping; compactness is required.
- Show the collapse Penrose diagram, where the star interior replaces the second exterior and white hole (Fig. 18.12).

## Concepts

### maximal spacetime

*definition · introduced* · also: maximal manifold, inextendible spacetime

A manifold with affine or metric geometry in which every geodesic, followed in both directions, either continues to arbitrarily large affine parameter or ends at an intrinsic singularity; nothing can be added to it.

**How introduced:** Introduced to turn the question 'can the Eddington-Finkelstein charts be extended further?' into a precise criterion based on the affine parameter of geodesics.

**Prerequisites:** affine parameter, geodesic, intrinsic singularity

**Notes:** The book's criterion is the geodesic one; modern treatments define maximality as inextendibility of the manifold (no isometric embedding as a proper open subset of a larger spacetime).

**Where:** DIV §18.1 p.343 (pdf 358)

### geodesic completeness

*definition · introduced* · also: geodesically complete manifold

A spacetime is geodesically complete if every geodesic can be extended to infinite affine parameter in both directions. Complete spacetimes are maximal; maximal spacetimes need not be complete.

**How introduced:** Defined immediately after maximality as the special case with no singular endpoints; Minkowski is the trivial example, Kruskal the example of maximal but incomplete.

**Prerequisites:** maximal spacetime, affine parameter

**Where:** DIV §18.1 p.343 (pdf 358)

### intrinsic singularity

*definition · revisited* · also: curvature singularity, essential singularity, physical singularity

A place where the geometry itself breaks down (for Schwarzschild, curvature invariants blow up at r = 0), which cannot be removed by any change of coordinates; geodesics that reach it cannot be continued.

**How introduced:** Recalled from §17.2 as the allowed way for a geodesic to end in a maximal spacetime; r = 0 becomes the two hyperbolic boundaries of the Kruskal diagram.

**Prerequisites:** coordinate singularity

**Where:** DIV §18.1 p.343 (pdf 358); DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360)

### coordinate singularity

*definition · mention* · also: coordinate degeneracy

A place where a particular chart fails (metric components vanish or blow up, or coordinates stop being one-to-one) although the geometry is perfectly regular there, such as r = 2m in Schwarzschild coordinates or r' = 0, pi and theta = 0, pi on the Einstein static universe.

**How introduced:** Mentioned when listing the ESU coordinate ranges, where the poles of the 3-sphere chart are called coordinate singularities; implicitly behind the whole Kruskal construction.

**Where:** DIV §18.4 p.350 (pdf 365)

### maximal analytic extension

*technique · introduced* · also: analytic extension

The process of continuing a spacetime known on one chart to the largest spacetime into which it embeds with an analytic metric; for Schwarzschild the result is unique and is the Kruskal spacetime.

**How introduced:** Posed as the goal of the chapter after the EF charts were found to be non-maximal; Kruskal is credited with finding the unique answer.

**Prerequisites:** maximal spacetime, Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates

**Where:** DIV §18.1 p.343 (pdf 358)

### Kruskal extension

*solution-or-model · core* · also: Kruskal solution, Kruskal spacetime, Kruskal-Szekeres spacetime, maximally extended Schwarzschild spacetime

The unique maximal analytic extension of the vacuum Schwarzschild geometry: a spacetime with two asymptotically flat exterior regions, a black hole region, a white hole region, horizons at r = 2m, and spacelike singularities at r = 0.

**How introduced:** Built in §18.2 by straightening both families of radial null geodesics, then interpreted with the Kruskal diagram, embedding diagrams and finally a Penrose diagram.

**Prerequisites:** Kruskal coordinates, maximal analytic extension, Schwarzschild solution

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = \frac{16m^2}{r}e^{-r/2m}(\mathrm{d}t'^2-\mathrm{d}x'^2) - r^2\mathrm{d}\Omega^2
$$

**Notes:** Maximal but not geodesically complete. The book does not name Szekeres, who found the same extension independently.

**Where:** DIV §18.1 p.343 (pdf 358); DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360); DIV §18.5 p.351 (pdf 366)

### Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: advanced and retarded Eddington-Finkelstein solutions, EF charts

Charts for Schwarzschild built on the ingoing null coordinate v = t + r* (advanced, covering exterior plus black hole) or the outgoing null coordinate w = t - r* (retarded, covering exterior plus white hole), in which one family of radial light rays is straight and the metric is regular at r = 2m.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapter 17 as the partial extensions that motivate the search for a maximal one; the Kruskal diagram shows each as a pair of its four regions.

**Prerequisites:** tortoise coordinate, event horizon

$$
v = t + r^*,\quad w = t - r^*
$$

**Where:** DIV §18.1 p.343 (pdf 358); DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360)

### double null coordinates

*technique · core* · also: null coordinates, advanced and retarded null coordinates used together

Coordinates (v, w) on a two-dimensional Lorentzian space whose coordinate lines are both families of null curves, so the 2-metric takes the form f dv dw with no dv^2 or dw^2 terms.

**How introduced:** Used first on the Schwarzschild radial 2-space (18.1) and then on Minkowski space (18.13)-(18.15); both Kruskal and Penrose constructions start from them.

**Prerequisites:** null geodesic, light cone

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = (1-2m/r)\,\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}w - r^2\mathrm{d}\Omega^2
$$

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = \mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}w - \tfrac14(v-w)^2\mathrm{d}\Omega^2
$$

**Where:** DIV §18.2 p.343 (pdf 358); DIV §18.4 p.348 (pdf 363)

### tortoise coordinate

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: tortoise radial coordinate, Regge-Wheeler coordinate, r*

The radial function r* = r + 2m ln(r - 2m) (for r > 2m) chosen so that radial light rays satisfy t = +-r* + constant; it tends to minus infinity as r approaches 2m, reflecting the infinite coordinate time taken to reach the horizon.

**How introduced:** Named in passing when writing v and w symmetrically as t +- r*.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild solution

$$
r^* = r + 2m\ln(r-2m)
$$

**Notes:** The logarithm's argument should be made dimensionless, e.g. ln(r/2m - 1); the constant shift is harmless but matters for the numerical scale of Kruskal coordinates.

**Where:** DIV §18.2 p.344 (pdf 359)

### conformal flatness of two-dimensional metrics

*theorem · developed* · also: 2D conformal flatness theorem

Every two-dimensional metric can locally be written as a function times a flat metric; for a Lorentzian 2-space this is realised by double null coordinates, ds^2 = F^2 (dt^2 - dx^2).

**How introduced:** Invoked from §6.13 to justify that the Schwarzschild radial 2-space (18.5) is conformally flat, made explicit with t~ = (v+w)/2, x~ = (v-w)/2.

**Prerequisites:** double null coordinates, conformal transformation

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = (1-2m/r)(\mathrm{d}\tilde t^2 - \mathrm{d}\tilde x^2)
$$

**Where:** DIV §18.2 p.344 (pdf 359)

### null coordinate reparametrisation freedom

*operation · core* · also: residual conformal gauge freedom, relabelling of null coordinates

In double null form f dv dw, replacing v by any monotonic function v'(v) and w by any w'(w) keeps the metric of the form f' dv' dw'; only the conformal factor changes. This freedom can be spent to make the factor regular or to make coordinate ranges finite.

**How introduced:** Stated as the most general transformation preserving the conformally flat null form; Kruskal's exponentials and Penrose's arctans are both particular uses of it.

**Prerequisites:** double null coordinates, conformal flatness of two-dimensional metrics

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = (1-2m/r)\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}v'}\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}w'}\mathrm{d}v'\mathrm{d}w'
$$

**Notes:** This is the unifying idea of the chapter even though the book never names it.

**Where:** DIV §18.2 p.344 (pdf 359); DIV §18.5 p.351 (pdf 366)

### Kruskal coordinates

*mathematical-object · core* · also: Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates, (t', x') coordinates, U, V coordinates

Coordinates t' = (v' + w')/2, x' = (v' - w')/2 built from v' = exp(v/4m), w' = -exp(-w/4m); in them radial light rays are 45-degree lines, the metric factor is regular at r = 2m, and r is defined implicitly by t'^2 - x'^2 = -(r - 2m) exp(r/2m).

**How introduced:** Derived as Kruskal's specific choice of null reparametrisation that cancels the zero of (1 - 2m/r) with the Jacobian of the exponential.

**Prerequisites:** null coordinate reparametrisation freedom, tortoise coordinate

$$
v' = e^{v/4m},\; w' = -e^{-w/4m}
$$

$$
t'^2 - x'^2 = -(r-2m)e^{r/2m}
$$

**Notes:** As defined, v' > 0 and w' < 0 cover only region I; the extension consists in letting (t', x') range over all values with r > 0.

**Where:** DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360)

### Kruskal diagram

*mathematical-object · core* · also: Kruskal space-time diagram, Kruskal-Szekeres diagram

The (t', x') plane of the Kruskal spacetime with angular directions suppressed: light cones are 45 degrees everywhere, r = constant curves are hyperbolae, Schwarzschild t = constant curves are straight lines through the origin, the diagonals are the horizons, and r = 0 is a pair of spacelike hyperbolae bounding the spacetime.

**How introduced:** Drawn as Fig. 18.1 immediately after the metric, with an infalling particle and its escaping signals.

**Prerequisites:** Kruskal coordinates, event horizon, spacelike singularity

**Where:** DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360); DIV §18.2 p.346 (pdf 361)

### event horizon

*phenomenon · revisited* · also: Schwarzschild horizon, r = 2m surface

The null surface r = 2m that separates events that can send signals to distant observers from those that cannot; in Kruskal coordinates it is the pair of diagonal lines t' = +-x', which cross at the bifurcation 2-sphere.

**How introduced:** Appears as the asymptotes of the r = constant hyperbolae in Fig. 18.1 and as the diagonals of the Penrose diagram Fig. 18.10.

**Prerequisites:** null surface, Kruskal diagram

$$
t' = \pm x' \;\Leftrightarrow\; r = 2m
$$

**Where:** DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360); DIV §18.5 p.351 (pdf 366)

### black hole region

*phenomenon · developed* · also: region II, future trapped region

The region of the Kruskal spacetime inside the future horizon, from which every future-directed causal curve reaches the future singularity and no signal reaches the exterior.

**How introduced:** Identified as region II of the Kruskal diagram, matching the interior part of the advanced EF chart; its inescapability is tied to the spacelike nature of the future singularity and later to trapped surfaces.

**Prerequisites:** event horizon, spacelike singularity

**Where:** DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360); DIV §18.5 p.351 (pdf 366)

### white hole region

*phenomenon · developed* · also: region II', past trapped region

The time reverse of the black hole region: the region inside the past horizon, emerging from the past singularity, from which everything must leave and which nothing can enter from outside.

**How introduced:** Identified as region II' of the Kruskal diagram, matching the interior part of the retarded EF chart; its 2-spheres are time-reversed trapped surfaces.

**Prerequisites:** black hole region, event horizon

**Where:** DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360); DIV §18.5 p.352 (pdf 367)

### second asymptotically flat region

*phenomenon · developed* · also: region I', other universe, parallel exterior

A second exterior Schwarzschild region, geometrically identical to region I and asymptotically flat, that the maximal extension forces to exist; it is causally disconnected from region I, though observers from both can meet inside the black hole.

**How introduced:** Presented as the surprise of the Kruskal diagram; its physical significance is described as an open question, and it disappears in the collapse diagram.

**Prerequisites:** Kruskal extension, Kruskal diagram

### spacelike singularity

*phenomenon · developed* · also: future singularity, past singularity

A singular boundary whose locus is spacelike, so that it lies to the future (or past) of every nearby event rather than at a place; a future spacelike singularity cannot be avoided once inside the region it bounds.

**How introduced:** Stated for the future r = 0 hyperbola in Fig. 18.1, whose spacelike character makes it unavoidable in region II; in the Penrose diagram both singularities become horizontal wavy edges.

**Prerequisites:** intrinsic singularity, light cone

**Where:** DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360); DIV §18.5 p.351 (pdf 366)

### Einstein-Rosen bridge

*phenomenon · core* · also: Schwarzschild throat, non-traversable wormhole, bridge

The geometry of the Kruskal spacetime on the slice t' = 0: two asymptotically flat spatial sheets joined through a minimal 2-sphere of area 16 pi m^2 (r = 2m).

**How introduced:** Found by inducing the metric on t' = 0, noting that r has a minimum at x' = 0, and embedding the equatorial section in flat 3-space (Fig. 18.2).

**Prerequisites:** embedding diagram, Kruskal extension, second asymptotically flat region

### embedding diagram

*technique · developed* · also: embedding of a spatial slice, Flamm paraboloid picture

A picture of the intrinsic geometry of a 2D spatial section obtained by finding a surface in flat 3D Euclidean space with the same induced line element; only the surface's intrinsic distances carry meaning.

**How introduced:** Used on the equatorial section of t' = 0 (and later slices) to visualise the bridge and the wormhole; no explicit embedding function is given.

**Prerequisites:** induced metric

### dynamical pinch-off of the Einstein-Rosen bridge

*phenomenon · developed* · also: time evolution of the bridge, throat collapse

On successive Kruskal slices t' = constant the throat radius drops below 2m, reaches zero when the slice touches the future singularity, and the two sheets then separate, each with its own singularity; the same happens toward the past by time symmetry. The throat closes too fast for any signal to cross from I to I'.

**How introduced:** Described qualitatively and summarised in the seven-panel strip Fig. 18.3, with the instruction to rotate each profile to rebuild the surface.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein-Rosen bridge, spacelike singularity

**Notes:** The book places pinch-off at t' = +-1; with (18.8) as printed it occurs at t' = +-(2m)^(1/2).

**Where:** DIV §18.3 p.347 (pdf 362)

### local geometry versus global topology

*principle · introduced* · also: topology not fixed by field equations

Einstein's equations are local differential equations: they constrain the metric near each point but do not determine how regions are glued together, so different global manifolds can share the same local geometry.

**How introduced:** Raised while discussing whether region I' is physical, illustrated by a wormhole embedding with the same local slice geometry as the bridge.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein field equations

**Where:** DIV §18.3 p.347 (pdf 362)

### Schwarzschild wormhole

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: wormhole, single-universe wormhole

A spatial geometry locally identical to the Einstein-Rosen bridge but with the two asymptotic sheets identified as distant parts of one universe, so the throat is a shortcut between them.

**How introduced:** Shown as an alternative embedding (Fig. 18.4) to make the geometry-topology point, then explicitly not pursued.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein-Rosen bridge, local geometry versus global topology

**Notes:** Not a solution of Einstein's equations globally without further identifications or matter; the book leaves it as an illustration.

**Where:** DIV §18.3 p.347 (pdf 362)

### conformal transformation

*operation · core* · also: conformal rescaling, Weyl rescaling

Replacing a metric g_ab by Omega^2 g_ab with a positive function Omega; angles and the null cone at each point are unchanged but lengths and curvature change.

**How introduced:** Introduced as the device for pulling infinity to a finite place, with Omega the conformal factor.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor

$$
\bar g_{ab} = \Omega^2 g_{ab}
$$

**Where:** DIV §18.4 p.348 (pdf 363)

### physical and unphysical metrics

*definition · introduced* · also: physical metric, unphysical metric, conformally rescaled metric

In conformal compactification the true spacetime metric is called physical and the rescaled metric Omega^2 g_ab, which is regular on the added boundary at infinity, is called unphysical; only its causal structure is to be trusted.

**How introduced:** Named at the start of §18.4 when the construction is set up.

**Prerequisites:** conformal transformation

$$
\mathrm{d}\bar s^2 = 4\,\mathrm{d}p\,\mathrm{d}q - \sin^2(p-q)\mathrm{d}\Omega^2

**Where:** DIV §18.4 p.348 (pdf 363)

### conformal invariance of null geodesics

*theorem · developed* · also: conformal invariance of causal structure

Two conformally related metrics have the same null geodesics (as unparametrised curves), hence the same light cones and the same causal relations between events.

**How introduced:** Quoted from Exercise 6.29 as the reason a conformally compactified picture faithfully displays causal structure.

**Prerequisites:** conformal transformation, null geodesic

**Notes:** Affine parametrisation of null geodesics does change under rescaling; timelike and spacelike geodesics are not preserved.

**Where:** DIV §18.4 p.348 (pdf 363)

### compactifying coordinate map

*technique · developed* · also: arctan map, tan^-1 compression

A monotonic function such as arctan that maps an infinite coordinate range onto a finite interval, used on null coordinates so that points at infinity acquire finite coordinate values.

**How introduced:** Shown graphically (Fig. 18.5) and then applied to Minkowski null coordinates and to Kruskal null coordinates.

**Prerequisites:** null coordinate reparametrisation freedom

$$
p = \tan^{-1}v,\; q = \tan^{-1}w

**Where:** DIV §18.4 p.348 (pdf 363); DIV §18.5 p.351 (pdf 366)

### conformal compactification

*technique · core* · also: conformal compactification of spacetime

Mapping a spacetime by a conformal rescaling and compactifying coordinates onto a bounded region of a larger (unphysical) spacetime, so that infinity becomes a boundary whose causal structure can be studied.

**How introduced:** Carried out explicitly for Minkowski space, where the image is a diamond in the Einstein static universe, then asserted for Kruskal.

**Prerequisites:** conformal transformation, compactifying coordinate map, conformal invariance of null geodesics

**Where:** DIV §18.4 p.350 (pdf 365); DIV §18.5 p.351 (pdf 366)

### Einstein static universe

*solution-or-model · revisited* · also: ESU, Einstein cylinder

The static spacetime R x S^3 with metric dt'^2 - dr'^2 - sin^2 r' dOmega^2, pictured as a cylinder whose axis is time and whose cross-sections are unit 3-spheres; it can be embedded as x^2 + y^2 + z^2 + w^2 = 1 in five-dimensional flat space.

**How introduced:** Recognised as the unphysical metric that Minkowski space is conformal to, then described via its cylinder embedding (Fig. 18.7).

### 3-sphere

The three-dimensional analogue of the ordinary sphere: the set of points at unit distance from the origin in four-dimensional Euclidean space, compact and without boundary, coordinatised by r', theta, phi.

### Penrose diagram of the Kruskal spacetime

### trapped surface

*definition · developed* · also: closed trapped surface

A closed (compact) spacelike 2-surface for which both the outgoing and the ingoing families of orthogonal light rays converge, so that both wave fronts have smaller area than the surface itself.

Results showing that, under energy and causality conditions, the existence of a trapped surface forces the spacetime to be null geodesically incomplete, that is, to contain a singularity.

## Key equations

### (18.1) Schwarzschild in double null coordinates · central · DIV §18.2 p.343 (pdf 358)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = \left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}w - r^2\left(\mathrm{d}\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi^2\right)
$$

With both advanced and retarded null coordinates the radial 2-metric has no squared terms; radial light rays are v = const or w = const.

**Symbols:** v advanced null coordinate, w retarded null coordinate, r(v, w) areal radius, m mass in geometric units

### (18.2) Implicit radius · supporting · DIV §18.2 p.344 (pdf 359)

$$
\tfrac12(v-w) = r + 2m\ln(r-2m)
$$

Determines r as a function of the null coordinates.

**Symbols:** v, w null coordinates

### (18.3) Null coordinates from Schwarzschild t · supporting · DIV §18.2 p.344 (pdf 359)

$$
v = t + r^*,\qquad w = t - r^*
$$

Symmetric definition of the ingoing and outgoing null coordinates.

**Symbols:** t Schwarzschild time, r* tortoise coordinate

### (18.4) Tortoise coordinate · supporting · DIV §18.2 p.344 (pdf 359)

$$
r^* = r + 2m\ln(r-2m)\quad (r>2m)
$$

Radial coordinate in which radial light rays move at unit coordinate speed; diverges logarithmically at the horizon.

**Symbols:** r areal radius

### (18.5) Radial 2-metric · supporting · DIV §18.2 p.344 (pdf 359)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = (1-2m/r)\,\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}w
$$

Metric of the theta, phi = const 2-space; conformally flat by the 2D theorem.

### Conformally flat form · derivation-step · DIV §18.2 p.344 (pdf 359)

$$
\tilde t = \tfrac12(v+w),\; \tilde x = \tfrac12(v-w):\quad \mathrm{d}s^2 = (1-2m/r)(\mathrm{d}\tilde t^2-\mathrm{d}\tilde x^2)
$$

Exhibits conformal flatness of the Schwarzschild radial 2-space explicitly.

### Residual freedom of null coordinates · central · DIV §18.2 p.344 (pdf 359)

$$
v\to v'(v),\; w\to w'(w):\quad \mathrm{d}s^2 = (1-2m/r)\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}v'}\frac{\mathrm{d}w}{\mathrm{d}w'}\mathrm{d}v'\mathrm{d}w' = F^2(t',x')(\mathrm{d}t'^2-\mathrm{d}x'^2)
$$

Independent relabelling of each null family preserves double null form and changes only the conformal factor; t' = (v'+w')/2, x' = (v'-w')/2.

**Symbols:** F conformal factor

### (18.6) Kruskal advanced coordinate · central · DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360)

$$
v' = \exp(v/4m)
$$

Exponential relabelling of v chosen to cancel the zero of 1 - 2m/r.

### (18.7) Kruskal retarded coordinate · central · DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360)

$$
w' = -\exp(-w/4m)
$$

Companion relabelling of w; the minus sign keeps v' increasing to the future while w' < 0 in region I.

### (18.8) Kruskal hyperbola relation · central · DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360)

$$
t'^2 - x'^2 = -(r-2m)\exp(r/2m)
$$

Defines r implicitly: r = const curves are hyperbolae, r = 2m is t' = +-x', r = 0 is t'^2 - x'^2 = 2m.

**Symbols:** t', x' Kruskal coordinates

### Kruskal conformal factor · supporting · DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360)

$$
F^2 = \frac{16m^2}{r}\exp(-r/2m)
$$

Regular and positive for all r > 0, including r = 2m, which is why the chart crosses the horizon.

### (18.9) Kruskal line element · central · DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = \frac{16m^2}{r}e^{-r/2m}\,\mathrm{d}t'^2 - \frac{16m^2}{r}e^{-r/2m}\,\mathrm{d}x'^2 - r^2\left(\mathrm{d}\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi^2\right)
$$

Maximal analytic extension of Schwarzschild; radial light rays are 45-degree lines and the only singularity is r = 0.

**Symbols:** r(t', x') from (18.8)

### Location of the Kruskal singularities (derived from 18.8, not printed) · derivation-step · DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360)

$$
r=0 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; t'^2 - x'^2 = 2m
$$

Two spacelike hyperbolae bounding the spacetime; on the axis x' = 0 they sit at t' = +-(2m)^{1/2}, which is where the bridge pinches off for the normalisation of (18.8).

### (18.10) Induced metric on t' = 0 · supporting · DIV §18.3 p.346 (pdf 361)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = -F^2\mathrm{d}x'^2 - r^2\left(\mathrm{d}\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi^2\right)
$$

Spatial geometry of the Kruskal slice through the bifurcation sphere; r has minimum 2m at x' = 0.

### (18.11) Equatorial section of the bridge · supporting · DIV §18.3 p.346 (pdf 361)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = -\left(F^2\mathrm{d}x'^2 + r^2\mathrm{d}\phi^2\right)
$$

2D geometry that is embedded in flat 3-space to draw the Einstein-Rosen bridge.

### (18.12) Conformal rescaling · central · DIV §18.4 p.348 (pdf 363)

$$
\bar g_{ab} = \Omega^2 g_{ab}
$$

Unphysical metric conformal to the physical one; same null geodesics and light cones.

**Symbols:** Omega conformal factor, g physical metric, g-bar unphysical metric

### (18.13)-(18.14) Minkowski null coordinates · supporting · DIV §18.4 p.348 (pdf 363)

v = t + r,\qquad w = t - r

Advanced and retarded null coordinates of flat spacetime.

### (18.15) Minkowski in double null form · supporting · DIV §18.4 p.348 (pdf 363)

\mathrm{d}s^2 = \mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}w - \tfrac14(v-w)^2\left(\mathrm{d}\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi^2\right)

Flat metric with r = (v - w)/2.

### (18.16) Half-plane condition · derivation-step · DIV §18.4 p.348 (pdf 363)

Only half of the (v, w) plane is physical.

### (18.17)-(18.18) Arctan compactification · central · DIV §18.4 p.349 (pdf 364)

p = \tan^{-1}v,\qquad q = \tan^{-1}w

Squeezes each infinite null coordinate into (-pi/2, pi/2).

p \ge q

Image of r >= 0.

\mathrm{d}s^2 = \tfrac14\sec^2p\,\sec^2q\left[4\,\mathrm{d}p\,\mathrm{d}q - \sin^2(p-q)\left(\mathrm{d}\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi^2\right)\right]

### Kruskal compactification · central · DIV §18.5 p.351 (pdf 366)

v'' = \tan^{-1}\!\left[v'/(2m)^{1/2}\right],\qquad w'' = \tan^{-1}\!\left[w'/(2m)^{1/2}\right]

### Printed range for the Kruskal compactification (needs correction) · supporting · DIV §18.5 p.351 (pdf 366)

$$
-\tfrac{\pi}{2}<v''<\tfrac{\pi}{2},\quad -\tfrac{\pi}{2}<w''<\tfrac{\pi}{2},\quad -\pi<v''+w''<\pi
$$

As printed, the third inequality follows from the first two and does not cut off the singularities. ## Worked examples

### Example · standard · DIV §18.2 p.344 (pdf 359)

**Problem:** Find coordinates in which the Schwarzschild metric is regular across both the future and past horizons and radial light rays are straight.

**Method:** Write the radial 2-metric in double null form (1 - 2m/r) dv dw; note that separate relabellings of v and w keep the null form; pick v' = exp(v/4m), w' = -exp(-w/4m); compute the product v'w' in terms of r and the Jacobian factor; define t', x' as half sum and difference.

**Key insight:** The exponential Jacobian vanishes exactly as fast as 1 - 2m/r near the horizon, so their ratio, the new conformal factor, stays finite and positive.

**Result:** ds^2 = (16m^2/r) e^{-r/2m}(dt'^2 - dx'^2) - r^2 dOmega^2 with t'^2 - x'^2 = -(r - 2m) e^{r/2m} (18.8)-(18.9).

**Concepts:** Kruskal coordinates, null coordinate reparametrisation freedom, double null coordinates

### Example · intro · DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360)

**Problem:** Follow the radial timelike geodesic that the text starts at r = 4m on the slice t' = 0, together with the outward light signals it emits.

**Method:** Draw the worldline in the Kruskal diagram using the 45-degree light cones; send outgoing 45-degree signals from successive points.

**Key insight:** Signals sent before the particle crosses the diagonal r = 2m reach large r; later signals are confined to region II and end on the spacelike singularity along with the particle.

**Result:** Qualitative: finite proper time to r = 0, escape cut off at the horizon.

**Concepts:** Kruskal diagram, event horizon, black hole region, spacelike singularity

### Example · standard · DIV §18.3 p.346 (pdf 361)

**Problem:** Determine the spatial geometry of the Kruskal slice t' = 0.

**Method:** Set dt' = 0 in (18.9) to get (18.10); set t' = 0 in (18.8) so that -(r - 2m)e^{r/2m} = -x'^2, and see that r decreases from infinity to 2m as x' goes to 0 and then increases again; restrict to the equator and embed.

**Key insight:** Because r has a minimum rather than reaching zero on this slice, the slice joins two exterior regions through a smallest sphere.

**Result:** Two asymptotically flat sheets linked by a throat of radius 2m (Fig. 18.2); later slices have narrower throats and pinch off at the singularity.

**Concepts:** Einstein-Rosen bridge, embedding diagram

### Example · standard · DIV §18.4 p.348 (pdf 363)

**Problem:** Conformally compactify Minkowski spacetime.

**Method:** Use v = t + r, w = t - r to get dv dw - (v - w)^2 dOmega^2/4 with v >= w; set p = arctan v, q = arctan w and use dv = sec^2 p dp and v - w = sin(p - q) sec p sec q to factor out sec^2 p sec^2 q /4; define t' = p + q, r' = p - q.

**Key insight:** After factoring, what remains is a perfectly regular metric that is recognisably the Einstein static universe, defined on a bounded range; the conformal factor carries all the infinity.

**Result:** ds^2 = (1/4) sec^2 p sec^2 q [dt'^2 - dr'^2 - sin^2 r' dOmega^2], with -pi < t' +- r' < pi, r' >= 0 (18.20)-(18.27).

**Concepts:** conformal compactification, Einstein static universe, compactified Minkowski spacetime

### Example · standard · DIV §18.4 p.350 (pdf 365)

**Problem:** Identify the boundary of compactified Minkowski space and where geodesics of each type begin and end.

**Method:** Take the limits p -> +-pi/2, q -> +-pi/2 allowed by p >= q; classify the resulting sets as null segments or points; follow straight lines t = t0 + u r (|u| < 1, = 1, > 1) through the arctan map.

**Key insight:** Different causal types of geodesic reach infinity at different parts of the boundary, so 'infinity' in a Lorentzian spacetime has structure.

**Result:** scri+ at p = pi/2, scri- at q = -pi/2, i+ at (pi/2, pi/2), i0 at (pi/2, -pi/2), i- at (-pi/2, -pi/2); timelike from i- to i+, null from scri- to scri+, spacelike from i0 to i0.

**Concepts:** future and past null infinity, future and past timelike infinity, spatial infinity

### Example · intro · DIV §18.5 p.351 (pdf 366)

**Problem:** Read from the Kruskal Penrose diagram where radial light rays from a given event end.

**Method:** Draw both 45-degree lines through the event in Fig. 18.10 and see which boundary edge each meets first.

**Key insight:** Outside the horizon one ray reaches scri+ and the other the singularity; inside, both lines meet the horizontal singularity because it lies entirely to the future.

**Concepts:** Penrose diagram of the Kruskal spacetime, black hole region

### Example · standard · DIV §18.5 p.351 (pdf 366)

**Problem:** Decide whether a round 2-sphere in each Kruskal region is trapped.

**Method:** Flash the sphere, construct the inner and outer envelopes of the wavelets (Fig. 18.11), and compare their areas 4 pi r^2 with that of the original sphere, using the direction in which r must change along each null direction in the region.

**Key insight:** Trapping is about both null directions reducing area, which in region II follows because r must decrease along every future-directed causal direction.

**Result:** Region I: outer front larger, inner smaller (normal). Region II: both smaller (trapped). Region II': both larger (time-reversed trapped).

**Concepts:** trapped surface, black hole region, white hole region

### Example · challenging · DIV §18.5 p.352 (pdf 367)

**Problem:** Show that convergence of light rays in both directions does not by itself make a surface trapped.

**Method:** Take two past light cones in Minkowski space and intersect them; check that the wave fronts from the intersection converge but that the surface is not closed.

**Key insight:** Compactness is essential to trapping; an unbounded convergent surface exists even in flat spacetime.

**Result:** The intersection is convergent but not trapped, which is why the authors see no need for the adjective 'closed'.

**Concepts:** trapped surface


## Analogies and intuitions

### Squeezing the whole real line into a finite interval with arctan → conformal compactification · strong · DIV §18.4 p.348 (pdf 363)

Shows in one graph that relabelling can bring 'infinity' to a finite coordinate value while preserving order, which is then applied to each null coordinate.

**Where it breaks down:** A coordinate change alone cannot make distances finite; a conformal factor must also be discarded, and the arctan choice is not unique.

**App idea:** Rubber ruler: stretch-and-squeeze animation of evenly spaced ticks under arctan, then applied to both axes of a light-cone grid.

### Each point of the 2D diagram is really a whole sphere → suppressed angular dimensions · useful · DIV §18.3 p.346 (pdf 361)

Reminds readers that the Kruskal and Penrose diagrams hide 2-spheres of area 4 pi r^2, which is needed for the bridge and trapped-surface arguments.

**Where it breaks down:** Only valid for spherically symmetric spacetimes; the sphere's radius differs from point to point.

**App idea:** Hover any diagram point to pop up a 3D sphere scaled to its r.

### Rotating a profile curve about an axis to generate a surface → Einstein-Rosen bridge time evolution · useful · DIV §18.3 p.347 (pdf 362)

The seven flat profiles in Fig. 18.3 become surfaces of revolution, turning a cheap strip of drawings into a movie of the throat.

**Where it breaks down:** Embedding pictures show slice geometry only; the evolution depends on how slices are chosen and does not show causal structure.

**App idea:** Lathe tool: learner spins a profile to build the throat surface and scrubs time.

### A cylinder x^2 + y^2 = 1 in 3D as a stand-in for the Einstein static universe → Einstein static universe · useful · DIV §18.4 p.350 (pdf 365)

Suppressing two dimensions, the 3-sphere cross-section becomes a circle and the ESU becomes an ordinary cylinder with time along its length.

**Where it breaks down:** The cylinder is drawn in Euclidean space, but the relevant geometry is Lorentzian; the book's parenthetical even writes the signature with opposite sign.

### The 3-sphere as the next member of the family circle, 2-sphere, ... → 3-sphere · useful · DIV §18.4 p.350 (pdf 365)

Lets readers accept S^3 cross-sections of the ESU without formal topology.

**Where it breaks down:** Visual intuition fails in three dimensions; poles r' = 0, pi are coordinate artefacts.

**App idea:** Stereographic slicing of S^3 into nested 2-spheres controlled by r'.

### Huygens wavelets from a flashing sphere form an inner and an outer wave front → trapped surface · strong · DIV §18.5 p.351 (pdf 366)

Turns the abstract condition on null expansions into a picture of two envelopes whose areas can be compared with the original sphere.

**Where it breaks down:** The flat-picture intuition that the outer front is always bigger is exactly what fails in region II; the picture itself does not show why.

**App idea:** Flash-the-sphere animation with area bars that change sign across the horizon.

### A folded sheet with a tube connecting two layers → local geometry versus global topology · useful · DIV §18.3 p.347 (pdf 362)

Keeps the throat geometry identical while changing how the far regions connect, making vivid that field equations do not fix topology.

**Where it breaks down:** The folded embedding is not a vacuum solution with time evolution; it is a static cartoon of one slice.

### Eddington-Finkelstein charts straighten one family of radial light rays; Kruskal straightens both at once → Kruskal coordinates · useful · DIV §18.1 p.343 (pdf 358)

Presents the Kruskal construction as completing a job the previous chapter did halfway.

**Where it breaks down:** Straightness of null lines is chart dependent and does not by itself guarantee regularity; the conformal factor must also be finite.

**App idea:** Three-panel comparison: Schwarzschild, advanced EF, Kruskal, with the same light rays drawn.


## Misconceptions addressed

### A maximal spacetime must be free of singularities (maximal and geodesically complete mean the same thing). · DIV §18.1 p.343 (pdf 358)

**Correction:** Maximality allows geodesics to end at intrinsic singularities; completeness forbids any end. Kruskal is maximal but incomplete.

**Why tempting:** Both notions are phrased in terms of extending geodesics to infinite affine parameter.

### Einstein's equations determine the topology of spacetime, so the Kruskal region I' must exist wherever a black hole exists. · DIV §18.3 p.347 (pdf 362)

**Correction:** Field equations are local and fix geometry near each point; different global manifolds can share it, and the physical status of I' is open (and in collapse it is absent).

**Why tempting:** Kruskal is presented as the unique maximal analytic extension, which sounds like physical necessity.

### The Einstein-Rosen bridge is a static tunnel through which one could travel from region I to region I'. · DIV §18.3 p.347 (pdf 362)

**Correction:** The throat opens and closes in Kruskal time; it pinches off when slices reach the singularity, and any causal path from I into the throat ends in region II before reaching I'.

**Why tempting:** The single t' = 0 embedding picture looks like a permanent static tube.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The future singularity at r = 0 is a point at the centre that an infalling observer might steer around. · DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360)

**Correction:** In Kruskal and Penrose diagrams r = 0 is spacelike, a moment in the future of every event in region II, so it cannot be avoided.

**Why tempting:** In flat-space intuition r = 0 is a location in space.

### Penrose diagrams preserve distances or times, so the size of a region indicates how big it is. · DIV §18.4 p.348 (pdf 363)

**Correction:** The conformal factor distorts lengths enormously (near the boundary Omega goes to zero); only light-cone directions and causal relations are faithful.

**Why tempting:** They look like ordinary spacetime diagrams with coordinates.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Every timelike curve ends at i+ and every lightlike-looking curve ends on scri+. · DIV §18.4 p.350 (pdf 365)

**Correction:** The endpoint rules hold for geodesics; a uniformly accelerated observer, for example, asymptotes to a null direction and ends on scri+ rather than i+.

**Why tempting:** The rules are stated crisply and seem to classify all curves.

### Spatial infinity i0 is a circle (or sphere) because it is drawn as the rim of a double cone. · DIV §18.4 p.351 (pdf 366)

**Correction:** In the compactification all spacelike directions reach the same point i0; the rim is an artefact of drawing one extra dimension.

**Why tempting:** Fig. 18.8 shows the rim as a large ellipse.

### Any surface whose light rays converge in both directions is trapped. · DIV §18.5 p.352 (pdf 367)

**Correction:** The surface must also be closed; the intersection of two past light cones in Minkowski space converges both ways but is not trapped.

**Why tempting:** Convergence is the part of the definition that seems physically decisive.

### A real astrophysical black hole contains a white hole in its past and a second universe. · DIV §18.5 p.352 (pdf 367)

**Correction:** The Penrose diagram of collapse (Fig. 18.12) replaces regions I' and II' with the star interior; they belong only to the idealised eternal vacuum solution.

**Why tempting:** The maximal extension is presented first and at length, so it is easy to treat it as the generic black hole.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Because Kruskal diagrams have 45-degree light cones everywhere, spacetime is flat in these coordinates. · DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360)

**Correction:** Only the radial 2-metric is conformally flat; curvature is carried by the non-constant conformal factor and by r(t', x').

**Why tempting:** Straight 45-degree null lines are the hallmark of Minkowski diagrams.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The horizon r = 2m is a singular place where something physically breaks down. · DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360)

## Thought experiments

### Signals from an infalling particle · DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360)

**Setup:** A particle on a radial geodesic starting at r = 4m, t' = 0 in region I (drawn as if it begins at rest) keeps emitting outward light pulses as it approaches and crosses r = 2m.

**Lesson:** Pulses before the horizon crossing escape to distant observers; pulses after it are trapped in region II and end on the singularity with the emitter.

**App idea:** Kruskal-diagram signal launcher with escape/no-escape colouring.

### Meeting a visitor from the other universe · DIV §18.2 p.352 (pdf 367)

**Setup:** An observer in region I and one in region I' each decide to meet the other (posed as Exercise 18.4).

**Lesson:** They can only meet inside the black hole region II, and both then end at the future singularity; the two exteriors never exchange signals.

**App idea:** Two-player Penrose-diagram game where each player steers a worldline and the app shows the earliest possible meeting point and its fate.

### Trying to cross the Einstein-Rosen bridge · DIV §18.3 p.347 (pdf 362)

**Setup:** Picture a light ray sent from region I toward the throat seen on the t' = 0 slice, aiming for region I'.

**Lesson:** The throat closes before the ray can pass through; crossing would need a spacelike path, so the bridge is non-traversable.

**App idea:** Race animation: throat radius versus photon position on successive slices.

### Flashing a sphere · DIV §18.5 p.351 (pdf 366)

**Setup:** Light is emitted from every point of a 2-sphere at one instant, first in region I, then in region II, then in region II'.

**Lesson:** Whether the two resulting wave fronts grow or shrink diagnoses normal, trapped, and anti-trapped surfaces, the key input to singularity theorems.

**App idea:** See Fig. 18.11 redesign.

### A shortcut between distant places · DIV §18.3 p.347 (pdf 362)

**Setup:** Identify the two sheets of the bridge as far-apart regions of one universe (Fig. 18.4).

**Lesson:** Identical local geometry can have different global topology; Einstein's equations alone cannot tell which is realised.


## Applications and observations

- **Spherical collapse of a star to a black hole** (astrophysical-system, DIV §18.5 p.352 (pdf 367)): The Penrose diagram of collapse shows that a realistic black hole formed from a star has one exterior, an event horizon that forms inside the star before its surface crosses r = 2m, trapped surfaces behind the horizon, and a spacelike singularity; the idealised second universe and white hole are absent.
- **Lifetime of the Einstein-Rosen throat (own estimate, not in the text)** (numerical-estimate, DIV §18.3 p.347 (pdf 362)): The throat centre follows a radial geodesic released from rest at r = 2m, whose proper time to r = 0 is pi m; a signal cannot cross in that time, so the bridge is non-traversable. Key numbers: tau = pi GM/c^3; for one solar mass about 1.5e-5 s (m = 1.48 km).
- **Proper time for the infalling particle of Fig. 18.1 (own estimate, not in the text)** (numerical-estimate, DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360)): Assuming the particle of Fig. 18.1 starts from rest (the text only says it starts at r = 4m, t' = 0), radial free fall from rest at r0 reaches r = 0 after proper time (pi/2) r0^(3/2)/(2m)^(1/2) in geometric units; with r0 = 4m this is about 8.9m. Key numbers: About 8.9 GM/c^3, roughly 4.4e-5 s for a solar-mass hole or about 12 hours for a 1e9 solar-mass hole.
- **Size of the bridge throat (own estimate, not in the text)** (numerical-estimate, DIV §18.3 p.346 (pdf 361)): On t' = 0 the minimal sphere has r = 2m, so its area is 16 pi m^2, the horizon area of the black hole. Key numbers: Area about 110 km^2 for one solar mass (r = 2.95 km).

## Historical notes

- **Martin Kruskal (1960 (not stated in the text)):** Found the maximal analytic extension of the Schwarzschild solution by straightening both families of radial null geodesics; the book credits him alone and asserts uniqueness. — Settled the global structure of the eternal Schwarzschild black hole and introduced the second exterior region and white hole. George Szekeres found the same extension independently, which the book does not mention. (DIV §18.1 p.343 (pdf 358))
- **Albert Einstein, Nathan Rosen (1935 (not stated in the text)):** Their name is attached to the throat connecting two exterior sheets of the Schwarzschild geometry. — The Einstein-Rosen bridge is the prototype of a wormhole and of the question whether mathematically allowed topology is physical. (DIV §18.3 p.346 (pdf 361))
- **Roger Penrose (1960s (not stated in the text)):** Introduced conformal compactification, the diagrams that bear his name, the notion of simple (asymptotically simple) spacetimes, and the term 'trapped surface'. — Made infinity and causal structure tractable and supplied the concept that drives the singularity theorems. (DIV §18.4 p.350 (pdf 365))
- **Stephen Hawking, George Ellis (1973):** Their monograph The Large Scale Structure of Space-Time is named as the main source for the chapter; the authors also contrast their term 'closed trapped surface' with Penrose's. — Standard reference for global methods in GR. (DIV p.353 (pdf 368))

## Notation and conventions

- **Metric signature:** Book uses signature (+, -, -, -), so timelike intervals have ds^2 > 0 and spatial slices carry negative-definite induced metrics as in (18.10)-(18.11). — The parenthetical cylinder metric on p.350 is written with the opposite overall sign (-dt^2 + dx^2 + dy^2); treat it as a slip. (DIV §18.4 p.350 (pdf 365))
- **Signature as a number:** A flat 5D metric with one plus and four minus signs is described as having signature -3 (sum of the diagonal signs). (DIV §18.4 p.350 (pdf 365))
- **Units:** Geometric units with G = c = 1; m is the mass parameter (GM/c^2) and the horizon is at r = 2m. — Logarithms of dimensional quantities such as ln(r - 2m) implicitly fix a length unit; t' = +-1 as pinch-off time also presupposes a choice of units. (DIV §18.2 p.344 (pdf 359))
- **Null coordinates:** v advanced (ingoing, constant on ingoing radial light rays), w retarded (outgoing). Primes mark Kruskal relabellings v', w'; double primes v'', w'' mark their compactified versions. (DIV §18.2 p.344 (pdf 359))
- **Kruskal coordinates:** t' = (v' + w')/2 and x' = (v' - w')/2; Kruskal regions are labelled I (our exterior), II (black hole), I' (other exterior), II' (white hole). — Other books use U, V or T, X, and label regions I-IV differently. (DIV §18.2 p.345 (pdf 360))
- **Reuse of primed symbols:** t' is reused in §18.4 for the Einstein static universe time p + q and r' for p - q, unrelated to the Kruskal t', x' of §18.2; w is also reused as a fifth Cartesian coordinate in the ESU embedding. — Rename in teaching materials (e.g. T, X for Kruskal; tau, chi for ESU). (DIV §18.4 p.349 (pdf 364))
- **Conformal rescaling:** Physical metric g_ab, unphysical metric g-bar_ab = Omega^2 g_ab; for Minkowski Omega^-2 = (1/4) sec^2 p sec^2 q. (DIV §18.4 p.348 (pdf 363))
- **Conformal infinity symbols:** Script I (pronounced 'scri') with + or - for future or past null infinity; i+, i- for timelike infinity; i0 for spatial infinity. (DIV §18.4 p.350 (pdf 365))
- **Angular element:** The book writes d theta^2 + sin^2 theta d phi^2 explicitly; this dossier abbreviates it as dOmega^2. (DIV §18.2 p.343 (pdf 358))

## Gaps and pitfalls

- **The printed coordinate range for the Kruskal compactification includes -pi < v'' + w'' < pi, which is implied by the other two inequalities and does not exclude the region beyond the singularities. With (18.8), r = 0 corresponds to v'w' = 2m, i.e. tan v'' tan w'' = 1, i.e. v'' + w'' = +-pi/2.** — A learner plotting the printed region gets a full diamond rather than the hexagon of Fig. 18.10 and cannot locate the singularities. *Suggestion:* State the range as -pi/2 < v'' + w'' < pi/2 and show that the singularities become straight horizontal lines at T'' = v'' + w'' = +-pi/2.
- **The text says the bridge pinches off at t' = 1, and Fig. 18.3 is labelled with t' = +-1, but with (18.8) as printed r = 0 on the axis x' = 0 occurs at t'^2 = 2m.** — Learners who check numerically find a mismatch and may doubt their algebra. *Suggestion:* Either work in units 2m = 1 explicitly or normalise (18.8) as t'^2 - x'^2 = (1 - r/2m) e^{r/2m}; then pinch-off is at t' = +-1.
- **Logarithms of dimensional quantities: r* = r + 2m ln(r - 2m).** — Hidden choice of length unit feeds into the scale of Kruskal coordinates and the (2m)^(1/2) factors of §18.5. *Suggestion:* Use r* = r + 2m ln(r/2m - 1) and point out that additive constants in r* rescale v' and w'.
- **The actual act of extension is not spelled out: v' = exp(v/4m) > 0 and w' = -exp(-w/4m) < 0 cover only region I; the other regions arise by allowing t', x' to take all values with r > 0 in the analytic metric.** — Readers may think the coordinate change itself creates the new regions or be unsure why the extension is legitimate. *Suggestion:* Show the quadrant v' > 0, w' < 0 first, then note the metric (18.9) is analytic in (t', x') wherever r > 0 and extend; mark which sign combinations give II, I', II'.
- **Derivation of F^2 and of (18.8) is left entirely to Exercise 18.2, and the Kruskal Penrose diagram computation is omitted.** — The key cancellation of the horizon zero is never seen, weakening understanding of why Kruskal works. *Suggestion:* Show the two-line computation dv dw = -16m^2 dv' dw'/(v'w') and v'w' = -(r - 2m) e^{r/2m}.
- **Coordinate symbols are reused with different meanings (t' for Kruskal time and ESU time; r' vs x'; w as retarded coordinate and as a fifth embedding coordinate).** — Confusion when comparing formulas across sections. *Suggestion:* Use distinct symbols in app and tutor materials, e.g. (T, X) for Kruskal and (tau, chi) for the Einstein cylinder.
- **The role of the conformal factor vanishing on the boundary (Omega = 2 cos p cos q -> 0 at infinity) is not stated, nor that the boundary is not part of physical spacetime.** — Learners may think points of scri are reachable events or that the unphysical metric is physically meaningful. *Suggestion:* Show Omega -> 0 on p, q = +-pi/2 and explain that infinite physical distances correspond to a finite unphysical distance only because of this factor.
- **The embedding of the t' = 0 slice is drawn without giving the embedding function.** — Learners cannot reproduce or quantify Fig. 18.2. *Suggestion:* Give the Flamm paraboloid z = +-(8m(r - 2m))^(1/2) in Schwarzschild r and show how both signs give the two sheets.
- **Trapped surfaces are defined only by wave-front areas; no expansion scalars or precise closure conditions are given, and the singularity theorems are merely mentioned.** — The definition is intuitive but not usable in calculations. *Suggestion:* Add the null expansions theta+ and theta- of a 2-sphere (both negative for trapped) and forward-link to optical scalars (ch23) and singularity theorems (ch20).
- **The parenthetical cylinder line element is written as -dt^2 + dx^2 + dy^2, opposite to the book's (+,-,-,-) signature used one line earlier.** — Sign-convention confusion for careful readers. *Suggestion:* Write dt^2 - dx^2 - dy^2 for consistency.
- **In describing the Minkowski Penrose diagram (Fig. 18.9) the text calls the t = constant curves 'timelike slices'; they are spacelike hypersurfaces (constant-time slices), while the r = constant curves are the timelike histories of 2-spheres.** — A careful reader may confuse which family of curves is timelike, exactly the distinction the Penrose diagram is meant to make visible. *Suggestion:* Say that t = constant curves are spacelike slices that all run into i0, and r = constant curves are timelike worldtubes running from i- to i+; have learners check with the 45-degree light cones.
- **The Schwarzschild time t labels on Fig. 18.1 are not explained: in region I t is related to Kruskal coordinates by t'/x' = tanh(t/4m), and t is not a time coordinate inside region II.** — Learners may try to read t = constant lines in regions II or I' as ordinary time slices. *Suggestion:* Give the tanh relation and explain that t becomes spacelike inside the horizon.
- **The claim that the physical significance of region I' is 'still an open' question is dated in emphasis; modern consensus is that astrophysical black holes formed by collapse have no second exterior, and the bridge is non-traversable for classical matter.** — Learners may take other universes as a live prediction of GR for real black holes. *Suggestion:* Pair the statement with the collapse diagram and the non-traversability argument; mention that wormhole traversability requires exotic matter.
- **Szekeres's independent discovery of the extension is not mentioned, and uniqueness is asserted without explanation.** — Minor historical incompleteness; learners meeting 'Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates' elsewhere may not connect them. *Suggestion:* Add the alias and a one-line history note.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'Schwarzschild coordinates broke at r = 2m, and Eddington-Finkelstein fixed it for infalling things. What would it take to be sure we have the whole spacetime?' Use the answer to motivate maximality and completeness.
- Before any formulas, check the learner can read a Minkowski spacetime diagram (45-degree light cones, future and past cones) and recall that an EF chart straightens one family of radial light rays.
- Explain in this order: double null form -> freedom to relabel each null coordinate -> Kruskal's exponential choice -> Kruskal diagram -> regions -> bridge -> conformal factor idea -> Minkowski compactification -> boundary pieces -> Kruskal Penrose diagram -> trapped surfaces -> collapse diagram.
- Check for understanding after the Kruskal diagram: ask the learner to point to where an observer in region I can receive signals from, and where observers from I and I' could meet (only in region II).
- When introducing Penrose diagrams, stress repeatedly that only light-cone directions are faithful; ask 'is region II bigger than region I?' to catch the distance misconception.
- Use simpler symbols than the book: (T, X) for Kruskal coordinates and (tau, chi) for the Einstein cylinder, and warn if the learner is reading the text that primes are reused.
- If the learner computes numbers, flag that the book's t' = 1 pinch-off and the printed Kruskal compactification range assume a normalisation; use t'^2 - x'^2 = (1 - r/2m)e^{r/2m} and the range |v'' + w''| < pi/2.
- Common question: 'Is the other universe real?' Answer: it is part of the idealised eternal vacuum solution; collapse of a real star removes it (Fig. 18.12), and even in Kruskal it cannot be reached or signalled from region I.
- Common question: 'Can I travel through the Einstein-Rosen bridge?' Answer with the pinch-off story and the Penrose diagram: every causal path into the throat ends on the future singularity.
- Common question: 'Why is the singularity a line at the top rather than a point in the middle?' Explain that r = 0 is spacelike: it is a future moment for everyone in region II.
- For trapped surfaces, first ask what happens to the areas of wave fronts from a flashing sphere in flat space, then ask what must change inside a black hole where r must decrease along any future direction.
- For advanced learners, connect scri to gravitational-wave radiation at null infinity (DIV ch23) and trapped surfaces to Penrose's singularity theorem (DIV ch20 §20.13).
- Quick check at the end: have the learner sketch the Minkowski Penrose triangle from memory, label i+, i0, i-, scri+ and scri-, then draw the Kruskal hexagon and the collapse diagram and name what differs.
