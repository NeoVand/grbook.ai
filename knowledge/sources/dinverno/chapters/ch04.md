# DIV ch04 · The elements of relativistic mechanics

> Carries Newtonian dynamics over to special relativity by postulating a speed-dependent mass, extracts m = gamma m0 from a symmetric inelastic collision, reads kinetic energy out of its low-speed expansion to motivate E = mc^2 and the energy-momentum invariant, and then applies the machinery to photons, closing with a Doppler argument that E/nu is the same in every inertial frame.

**Pages:** printed 49–61 · pdf 64–76 · **Difficulty:** math 2/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

Mathematics is elementary: vectors, a quadratic, binomial expansions and simple substitutions. The conceptual load is moderate: the reader must keep track of which velocity (particle or frame) enters each gamma, accept explicitly ad hoc assumptions, and follow a quick analogy from invariant to transformation law. Several steps are marked '(exercise)' or '(check)', including the key simplification to gamma. The relativistic-mass framing is dated and can build habits that later chapters must undo. The exercises range from one-line checks to the demanding Exercise 4.8.

## Role in the book

This is the last chapter of Part A (special relativity). Chapters 2 and 3 were purely kinematic (k-calculus, Lorentz transformations, time dilation, velocity composition, Doppler effect); this short chapter supplies the minimum dynamics the rest of the book needs: rest mass, relativistic energy and momentum, the invariant (E/c)^2 - p^2 = (m0 c)^2, the Lorentz transformation of (E/c, p), and the photon relations E = h nu, p = h nu/c. It also records Newtonian gravity in field form (Poisson and Laplace equations) and flags the logical weakness of Newton's definitions of mass and force, both of which are picked up again in Part C. The ad hoc, non-covariant route taken here is deliberately provisional: the tensor formalism of Part B and the covariant, variational treatment of relativistic mechanics in Chapter 8 later replace it.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can state Newton's second law in momentum form and explain why F = ma holds only for constant mass.
- Reader can explain how mutual accelerations of two isolated bodies calibrate mass ratios, and why the definitions of force and mass in Newtonian mechanics are entangled with the laws themselves.
- Reader can write Newtonian gravity both as the inverse-square force law and as Poisson's equation inside matter (Laplace's equation outside).
- Reader can derive conservation of total linear momentum for an isolated n-particle system from Newton's second and third laws.
- Reader can reproduce the symmetric inelastic-collision argument (lab frame plus centre-of-momentum frame plus velocity composition) that yields m(u) = gamma(u) m0, including the choice of root and the algebra that simplifies U/(u-U) to gamma.
- Reader can identify which assumptions (speed-dependent mass, conservation of mass and momentum) are inputs rather than consequences, and that experiment is the final arbiter.
- Reader can expand gamma m0 c^2 at low speed, identify the rest energy and the Newtonian kinetic energy, and show that relativistic mass and momentum conservation reduce to Newtonian energy and momentum conservation.
- Reader can state E = mc^2, the rest energy m0 c^2 and p = m u, and derive the invariant (E/c)^2 - p^2 = (m0 c)^2.
- Reader can write the Lorentz transformation of energy and momentum for standard configuration, invert it, and check it by transforming from the instantaneous rest frame.
- Reader can show that a particle moving at c must have zero rest mass and derive E = pc and p = (h nu/c) n-hat for a photon.
- Reader can combine the Doppler shift with the energy transformation to show that the ratio of a light flash's energy to its frequency is the same for all inertial observers.
- Reader can use energy-momentum conservation plus the invariant to solve one-dimensional relativistic collision, absorption and emission problems (Exercises 4.2-4.12).

## Teaching approach

A compact bridging chapter that refuses to pretend relativistic dynamics follows from kinematics alone. It first reviews Newton with an eye on operational definitions, then keeps Newtonian concepts (mass, momentum, conservation) and changes only one thing, letting mass depend on speed. A single, cleverly symmetric thought-experiment collision plus the Chapter 2 velocity composition law fixes that dependence. Everything after is obtained by expansion (Newtonian limits), analogy with the interval (energy and momentum transform like time and position), and consistency checks (rest frame, photon limit, Doppler). Justifications that need four-vectors are explicitly postponed to the tensor chapters.

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, derivation-heavy, thought-experiment, components-first, historical-narrative

**Narrative arc**

1. Review Newton's second and third laws, then ask how force and mass could ever be measured without already assuming those laws.
2. Show mass calibration by mutual accelerations; note force is either read off from ma or supplied by an independent law such as universal gravitation, which is also written as Poisson's equation for later use.
3. Derive conservation of total momentum for an isolated Newtonian system as the template to carry into relativity.
4. Admit that relativistic dynamics needs new assumptions; hypothesize a speed-dependent mass and retain conservation of mass and of momentum.
5. Analyse a symmetric perfectly inelastic collision from the lab and centre-of-momentum frames, use velocity composition, and solve for m(u) = gamma m0; plot its divergence at c.
6. Expand m(u) at low speed to find the classical kinetic energy inside mc^2, verify that relativistic conservation laws reduce to Newtonian energy and momentum conservation, and promote E = mc^2 (better, Delta E = Delta(mc^2)).
7. Generalize to three dimensions, derive the invariant (E/c)^2 - p^2 = (m0 c)^2, compare with the interval, adopt the Lorentz transformation of (E/c, p), and confirm it from the instantaneous rest frame.
8. Turn to light: history of quanta, zero photon rest mass, E = pc, and p = h nu/c.
9. Finish by showing E/nu is frame independent from the Doppler formula, identify it with h, and hand off to the tensor formalism.

**Signature moves**

- Minimal-modification strategy: keep the Newtonian definitions of momentum and conservation, change only the speed dependence of mass, and say openly that the result is an assumption vindicated by experiment rather than a theorem.
- Symmetry-plus-composition collision: the centre-of-momentum frame makes the two incoming particles mirror images, so the lab-frame speed u is just U composed with U, which turns an unknown function m(u) into an algebra problem.
- Correspondence by Taylor expansion at every stage: gamma m0 c^2 contains (1/2) m0 u^2; mass conservation contains kinetic-energy conservation; relativistic momentum conservation contains Newtonian momentum conservation.
- Transformation law guessed from a shared invariant quadratic form: (E/c)^2 - p^2 mirrors (ct)^2 - x^2, so (E/c, p) is taken to transform like (ct, x), with rigorous justification deferred to tensors.
- Consistency check by boosting from the rest frame, which regenerates the very formulae m = gamma m0, E = mc^2, p = mv the derivation started from.
- Recycling earlier kinematics for new physics: the Chapter 3 Doppler formula is reused to connect relativistic mechanics with the quantum relation E = h nu.
- Laws stated in labelled grey boxes (N2, N3, UG), echoing the book's postulate style.

## Section by section

### §4.1 Newtonian theory — p.49 (pdf 64)

Revisits Newton's mechanics as the starting point for relativistic dynamics. Force is introduced as a push or pull, inertia as resistance to changes of motion quantified by mass, and momentum as mass times velocity. The second law (rate of change of momentum equals force) and third law (action equals minus reaction) are boxed; the momentum form reduces to F = ma when mass is constant. The authors then press on the operational problem: force and mass must be measurable, but any measurement uses the laws. Mass ratios can be fixed by letting two isolated bodies pull on each other and comparing accelerations against a unit standard, a technique used for elementary particles. Force can be read off from ma (consistent but circular) or given by an independent law, with universal gravitation as the example, then also written for continuous matter as Poisson's equation inside and Laplace's equation outside. Special relativity is framed as physics without gravitation, and a fuller critique of Newton is postponed to Part C.

**Concepts:** Newton's second law, Newton's third law, inertial mass, linear momentum, operational measurement of mass by mutual accelerations, operational status of force in Newtonian mechanics, Newton's law of universal gravitation, Poisson's equation for the gravitational potential, Laplace's equation, Newton's first law, force

**Key moves**
- Write N2 as F = dp/dt so that a changing mass is allowed; constant mass gives F = ma.
- Isolate two bodies, apply N2 to each and N3 to the pair to get m1 a1 = -m2 a2, so measured accelerations fix mass ratios relative to one chosen unit mass.
- Observe that defining force as ma is self-consistent but empty unless force has independent content, e.g. a force law such as UG or the N3 property.
- Record Newtonian gravity for a point pair (4.4) and for a mass density (Poisson (4.5), Laplace (4.6)) as the background later matched by general relativity.

### §4.2 Isolated systems of particles in Newtonian mechanics — p.51 (pdf 66)

A short derivation of Newtonian momentum conservation for n particles of constant mass. The total force on each particle is split into an external part and a sum of pairwise internal forces (with self-force set to zero). Summing N2 over all particles, the double sum of internal forces cancels in pairs by N3, so the rate of change of total momentum equals the total external force. For an isolated system the total momentum is constant, giving P_initial = P_final, the form of the law that Section 4.3 carries into relativity.

**Concepts:** linear momentum, internal and external forces, isolated system, conservation of linear momentum, Newton's third law

**Key moves**
- Decompose F_i = F_i^ext + sum_j F_ij with F_ii = 0.
- Sum N2 over i and cancel the internal double sum using F_ij = -F_ji.
- Set F^ext = 0 to conclude total momentum is a constant vector, hence initial equals final total momentum.

### §4.3 Relativistic mass — p.52 (pdf 67)

The authors warn that passing to relativistic dynamics needs extra, ad hoc assumptions; their policy is to stay as close to Newtonian definitions as possible and let experiment (notably particle physics) decide. Since lengths and times depend on the observer, they postulate that mass does too, m = m(u), with rest mass m0 = m(0). Two identical particles collide and stick in one dimension. In the lab frame S one is at rest and the other moves at u; the merged body moves at U. In the centre-of-momentum frame S' the particles approach with equal and opposite speeds and the product is at rest, so S' moves at U relative to S. Conserving mass and momentum in S gives m(u) = m0 U/(u - U). Composing U with U must return u, which gives a quadratic for U; the root that stays finite as u -> 0 is chosen, and substitution gives m = gamma(u) m0. The factor has the same form as the time-dilation factor but depends on the particle's velocity, not a frame's. The graph of m against u rises without bound as u approaches c. The section closes by stressing that the result is not derivable a priori.

**Concepts:** relativistic mass, rest mass, perfectly inelastic collision, centre-of-momentum frame, conservation of relativistic mass, relativistic velocity addition, Lorentz factor, time dilation, speed of light as a limiting speed for massive bodies

**Key moves**
- Postulate observer-dependent mass m = m(u) and name m(0) = m0 the rest mass.
- Choose a perfectly inelastic collision of identical particles so that the centre-of-momentum frame is fixed by symmetry: speeds U and -U before, product at rest after, and S' moves at U relative to S.
- Impose conservation of relativistic mass and of momentum in S, eliminate M(U) to get m(u) = m0 U/(u - U).
- Relate u and U through the composition law u = 2U/(1 + U^2/c^2), solve the quadratic U^2 - (2c^2/u)U + c^2 = 0, and keep the minus root so U -> 0 as u -> 0.
- Simplify (left to the reader): with the minus root, u - U = (c^2/u) sqrt(1 - u^2/c^2) [1 - sqrt(1 - u^2/c^2)] while U = (c^2/u)[1 - sqrt(1 - u^2/c^2)], so U/(u - U) = gamma(u).
- Contrast gamma(u), built from a particle's speed, with the time-dilation factor beta(v), built from a frame's speed, and note m -> infinity as u -> c.

### §4.4 Relativistic energy — p.54 (pdf 69)

Expanding gamma m0 for u much less than c shows that c^2 times the relativistic mass equals a constant m0 c^2 plus, to leading order, the Newtonian kinetic energy. For a general two-body collision (rest masses m0 and m0-bar, velocities before and after), conservation of relativistic mass reduces at low speed to conservation of kinetic energy, since the rest-mass terms cancel. Because energy is fixed only up to an additive constant, the authors take E = mc^2 as the energy of a particle and stress that it asserts the equivalence of mass and energy, most safely stated as Delta E = Delta(mc^2). The enormous size of c^2 means a tiny mass change carries huge energy, with an allusion to nuclear weapons and power. m0 c^2 is named the rest energy, and relativistic momentum conservation is shown (as an exercise) to reduce to Newtonian momentum conservation.

**Concepts:** Newtonian limit of relativistic mechanics, relativistic kinetic energy, conservation of relativistic mass, relativistic energy, mass-energy equivalence, rest energy, relativistic momentum, conservation of linear momentum

**Key moves**
- Binomial expansion m(u) = m0 + (1/c^2)(m0 u^2/2) + O(u^4/c^4).
- Multiply by c^2 and recognize the Newtonian kinetic energy riding on the constant m0 c^2.
- Apply mass conservation to a generic two-body collision (4.15); the m0 and m0-bar terms cancel, leaving kinetic-energy balance (4.16).
- Use the freedom to shift energy by a constant to define E = mc^2 and reinterpret it as Delta E = Delta(mc^2).
- Show relativistic momentum conservation m(u)u reduces to (4.18) at low speed.

### Energy-momentum invariant and its Lorentz transformation (within §4.4) — p.56 (pdf 71)

The one-dimensional results are extended to three dimensions: a particle with velocity u has m = gamma m0, E = mc^2, p = m u. Straightforward algebra gives (E/c)^2 minus the squared momentum components equal to (m0 c)^2, the same for every inertial observer. Its resemblance to the interval (ct)^2 - x^2 - y^2 - z^2 suggests that (E/c, p_x, p_y, p_z) transforms exactly like (ct, x, y, z). Instead of proving this (tensors will do it properly in Part C), the authors assume it and write the standard-configuration transformation and its inverse. As a check, putting S' in the particle's instantaneous rest frame (p' = 0, E' = m0 c^2) and transforming back reproduces m = gamma m0, E = mc^2 and p = m v.

**Concepts:** relativistic momentum, energy-momentum relation, energy-momentum four-vector, Lorentz transformation of energy and momentum, invariance of the spacetime interval, standard configuration, instantaneous rest frame

**Key moves**
- Combine m = gamma m0, E = mc^2, p = mu to get gamma^2 m0^2 c^2 (1 - u^2/c^2) = (m0 c)^2.
- Match the invariant against the interval (3.12) and infer that (E/c, p) is transformed like (ct, x).
- Write E' = beta(E - v p_x), p_x' = beta(p_x - vE/c^2), and invert by swapping primes and v -> -v.
- Take S' as the instantaneous rest frame and confirm that the inverse transformation regenerates (4.19).

### §4.5 Photons — p.57 (pdf 72)

A brief history: conflicts over radiation in cavities led Planck to energy quanta with E = h nu; Einstein used the idea for the photoelectric effect; light is now described as having both wave and particle aspects. Treating light as a stream of photons and putting u = c into m0 = m/gamma forces the rest mass to vanish, which is unproblematic because no inertial observer can find a photon at rest. With momentum p along a unit vector n-hat, the invariant collapses to (E/c)^2 = p^2, so E = pc, and with Planck's hypothesis p = (h nu/c) n-hat. The authors remark that relativity, born from a clash in the kinematics of light and matter, also unifies their mechanics.

**Concepts:** photon, Planck's relation, photoelectric effect, wave-particle duality, masslessness of the photon, photon energy-momentum relation, energy-momentum relation

**Key moves**
- Set u = c in m0 = gamma^{-1} m to conclude m0 = 0 for a photon, interpreting rest mass as a notional quantity for light.
- Put m0 = 0 in the invariant to get E = pc (taking positive roots).
- Combine with E = h nu to give p = (h nu/c) n-hat.

### Planck's relation from the Doppler effect (end of §4.5) — p.58 (pdf 73)

The chapter ends by arguing that E proportional to nu is already built into special relativity. The receding-source Doppler formula (3.26) is rewritten in terms of frequency using c = lambda nu. A flash of energy E0 emitted by the source travels in the negative x-direction of both frames, so its momentum in the source frame is p_x' = -E0/c. Transforming the energy with (4.22) gives exactly the same square-root factor as the frequency shift. Hence E0/nu0 = E/nu for any pair of inertial observers, and the authors identify the frame-independent ratio with Planck's constant. A closing paragraph announces the move to tensors as the language for general relativity.

**Concepts:** relativistic Doppler effect, Lorentz transformation of energy and momentum, Lorentz invariance of the energy-to-frequency ratio of light, Planck's relation

**Key moves**
- Convert the wavelength Doppler ratio into a frequency ratio nu0/nu = sqrt((1 + v/c)/(1 - v/c)).
- Use E = pc with the flash moving along negative x to set p_x' = -E0/c.
- Transform: E = beta(E0 - vE0/c) = E0 sqrt((1 - v/c)/(1 + v/c)).
- Divide the two ratios to find E0/nu0 = E/nu and call the invariant ratio h.

## Concepts

### Newton's second law

*law · revisited* · also: N2, F = dp/dt

The net force on a body equals the time rate of change of its linear momentum, F = d(mv)/dt, and points along the force; if the mass is constant this becomes F = ma.

**How introduced:** Stated in a grey box as N2 during the review of Newtonian mechanics, after force is described informally as a push or pull; written in momentum form first so the constant-mass special case can be singled out.

**Prerequisites:** linear momentum, inertial mass

$$
\boldsymbol F = \frac{d\boldsymbol p}{dt} = \frac{d(m\boldsymbol v)}{dt}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol F = m\boldsymbol a
$$

**Notes:** The momentum form is the one that survives in relativity (Exercise 4.6 uses F = d(mu)/dt with relativistic mass).

**Where:** DIV §4.1 p.49 (pdf 64); DIV §4.1 p.50 (pdf 65)

### Newton's third law

*law · revisited* · also: N3, action and reaction

For internal forces between bodies of a system, the force on body i due to body j is equal in size and opposite in direction to the force on j due to i: F_ij = -F_ji.

**How introduced:** Boxed as N3 and described as less general than N2 because it only concerns internal forces; then used twice, to calibrate mass and to cancel internal forces in the momentum-conservation proof.

**Prerequisites:** internal and external forces

$$
\boldsymbol F_{ij} = -\boldsymbol F_{ji}
$$

**Where:** DIV §4.1 p.49 (pdf 64); DIV §4.2 p.52 (pdf 67)

### Newton's first law

*law · mention* · also: law of inertia

A body on which no force acts moves in a straight line at constant velocity (or stays at rest).

**How introduced:** Recalled in one sentence from Chapter 2 as the starting point of the Newtonian review.

**Where:** DIV §4.1 p.49 (pdf 64)

### force

*physical-quantity · revisited* · also: Newtonian force, push or pull

Whatever causes a body's velocity (more precisely its momentum) to change; in Newtonian mechanics it is measured through F = dp/dt, and it has content beyond that definition only when a separate force law or the action-reaction property is supplied.

**How introduced:** Named in bold at the opening of §4.1 as the agent behind any change of velocity, provisionally pictured as a tangible push or pull; the authors then ask how it could be measured and conclude that reading it off from ma is circular unless an independent law such as universal gravitation is given.

**Prerequisites:** linear momentum

$$
\boldsymbol F = \frac{d\boldsymbol p}{dt}
$$

**Notes:** The book also mentions the 'standard approach' of treating force as fundamental, with force laws stated separately or derived from other considerations.

**Where:** DIV §4.1 p.49 (pdf 64); DIV §4.1 p.50 (pdf 65); DIV §4.1 p.51 (pdf 66)

### inertial mass

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: mass, inertia

A number attached to a body measuring its resistance to changes in its state of motion; operationally, the ratio of two inertial masses is minus the inverse ratio of the accelerations they induce in each other when isolated.

**How introduced:** Motivated by everyday experience that heavier bodies are harder to set moving; the authors hedge that this is provisional ('for the moment') and then give an operational calibration via mutual accelerations.

**Prerequisites:** Newton's second law, Newton's third law

$$
m_1\boldsymbol a_1 = -m_2\boldsymbol a_2
$$

**Notes:** Distinction between inertial and gravitational mass is not made here; it is taken up in DIV ch09 §9.3-§9.4.

**Where:** DIV §4.1 p.49 (pdf 64); DIV §4.1 p.50 (pdf 65)

### operational measurement of mass by mutual accelerations

*technique · introduced* · also: mass calibration by mutually induced accelerations

Isolate two bodies interacting only with each other; by N2 and N3 their accelerations are antiparallel with magnitudes inversely proportional to their masses, so choosing one body as the unit fixes the other's mass, and the procedure can be chained to calibrate any body.

**How introduced:** Offered as the answer to the question of how mass can be given an operational meaning inside Newtonian theory, illustrated by Fig. 4.1 and noted to be used for comparing elementary particle masses; imperfect isolation is handled by keeping other influences roughly constant.

**Prerequisites:** Newton's second law, Newton's third law, inertial mass

$$
m_2 = -m_1\,\frac{a_1}{a_2}
$$

**Where:** DIV §4.1 p.50 (pdf 65)

### operational status of force in Newtonian mechanics

*definition · introduced* · also: circularity of force and mass definitions

In Newtonian mechanics force and mass are not independently defined before the laws: the laws themselves provide their operational definitions, so defining force as ma is consistent but circular, and force acquires independent physical content only through separate force laws (e.g. gravitation) and properties such as action-reaction.

**How introduced:** Raised as a surprising logical feature after noting that position, velocity and acceleration can be measured with rulers and clocks but force and mass cannot without invoking the laws; the full critique is deferred to Part C.

**Prerequisites:** Newton's second law, Newton's third law, inertial mass

**Notes:** Exercise 4.1 asks the reader to consider force (a standard weight) as the primitive quantity instead.

**Where:** DIV §4.1 p.50 (pdf 65); DIV §4.1 p.51 (pdf 66)

### Newton's law of universal gravitation

*law · mention* · also: UG, inverse-square law of gravitation

Two point masses attract each other with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of their separation: F = -G m1 m2 r-hat / r^2, with G approximately 6.67 x 10^-11 m^3 kg^-1 s^-2.

**How introduced:** Given as the standard example of a force law that gives force meaning independent of N2, with Fig. 4.2 showing the separation vector and the attractive force.

**Prerequisites:** inertial mass

$$
\boldsymbol F = -G\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}\hat{\boldsymbol r}
$$

**Notes:** The boxed verbal statement on printed p.50 says 'inversely proportional to the distance'; the square is missing there, though (4.4) is correct.

**Where:** DIV §4.1 p.50 (pdf 65); DIV §4.1 p.51 (pdf 66)

### Poisson's equation for the gravitational potential

*law · mention* · also: Newtonian field equation

A mass density rho(x, y, z, t) generates a Newtonian gravitational potential phi obeying nabla^2 phi = 4 pi G rho at points inside the matter.

**How introduced:** Stated for completeness, since special relativity excludes gravitation; it is the Newtonian statement that the general relativistic field equations must reproduce later.

**Prerequisites:** Newton's law of universal gravitation

$$
\nabla^2\phi = 4\pi G\rho
$$

$$
\nabla^2 = \partial_x^2 + \partial_y^2 + \partial_z^2
$$

**Notes:** Matched against the field equations in DIV ch10 §10.6.

**Where:** DIV §4.1 p.51 (pdf 66)

### Laplace's equation

*law · mention* · also: vacuum Newtonian field equation

Outside a mass distribution, where rho = 0, the Newtonian gravitational potential satisfies nabla^2 phi = 0.

**How introduced:** Obtained as the exterior special case of Poisson's equation.

**Prerequisites:** Poisson's equation for the gravitational potential

$$
\nabla^2\phi = 0
$$

**Notes:** Newtonian analogue of the vacuum field equations of DIV ch10 §10.4.

**Where:** DIV §4.1 p.51 (pdf 66)

### linear momentum

*physical-quantity · revisited* · also: momentum, Newtonian momentum

For a body of mass m moving with velocity v, the vector p = m v; for a system, the total momentum is the vector sum over its particles.

**How introduced:** Defined in the Newtonian review so that N2 can be written as dp/dt = F; later carried over unchanged in form but with relativistic mass.

**Prerequisites:** inertial mass

$$
\boldsymbol p = m\boldsymbol v
$$

$$
\boldsymbol P = \sum_i \boldsymbol p_i
$$

**Where:** DIV §4.1 p.49 (pdf 64); DIV §4.2 p.51 (pdf 66)

### internal and external forces

*definition · introduced* · also: action and reaction forces

In a system of bodies, internal forces are those exerted on a body by other members of the system (F_ij on i due to j); external forces arise from fields or agents outside the system.

**How introduced:** Introduced with N3, which applies only to internal forces, and then used to split the total force on each particle in the momentum-conservation proof.

$$
\boldsymbol F_i = \boldsymbol F_i^{\text{ext}} + \sum_{j} \boldsymbol F_{ij},\quad \boldsymbol F_{ii} = 0
$$

**Where:** DIV §4.1 p.49 (pdf 64); DIV §4.2 p.52 (pdf 67)

### isolated system

*definition · introduced* · also: closed system

A system of particles on which the total external force vanishes, so only mutual internal forces act.

**How introduced:** Defined at the end of the momentum-conservation derivation as the condition F^ext = 0.

**Prerequisites:** internal and external forces

$$
\boldsymbol F^{\text{ext}} = \sum_i \boldsymbol F_i^{\text{ext}} = 0
$$

**Where:** DIV §4.2 p.52 (pdf 67)

### conservation of linear momentum

*law · developed* · also: momentum conservation

The total linear momentum of an isolated system is constant in time, so it is the same before and after any interaction; in relativity the same statement holds with p = gamma m0 u.

**How introduced:** Derived in Newtonian mechanics from N2 plus N3 by cancelling internal forces pairwise; then simply assumed in the relativistic collision of §4.3 and shown to reduce to the Newtonian law at low speed.

**Prerequisites:** Newton's second law, Newton's third law, isolated system, linear momentum

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol P} = \boldsymbol F^{\text{ext}}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol P_{\text{initial}} = \boldsymbol P_{\text{final}}
$$

### relativistic mass

*physical-quantity · core* · also: m(u), velocity-dependent mass, gamma m0

The quantity m = gamma(u) m0 = m0 (1 - u^2/c^2)^(-1/2) assigned to a particle moving with speed u relative to an inertial observer, chosen so that p = m u and the sum of m over particles are both conserved; it equals E/c^2 and is therefore observer dependent.

### rest mass

*physical-quantity · core* · also: m0, invariant mass, proper mass

The mass of a particle measured in a frame where it is at rest, m0 = m(0); it is the same for all inertial observers and equals sqrt(E^2 - p^2 c^2)/c^2 for any particle or system.

**How introduced:** Named as the value of the speed-dependent mass at zero speed during the collision argument; later identified as the Lorentz-invariant combination in (4.20). For composite bodies it is not additive (Exercise 4.2).

### conservation of relativistic mass

*principle · developed* · also: mass conservation in relativity

The assumption that the total relativistic mass sum of gamma_i m0_i of an isolated system is unchanged by collisions; since E = mc^2 it is the same statement as conservation of total energy, and at low speed it reduces to conservation of Newtonian kinetic energy (rest masses unchanged) or of total rest mass.

**How introduced:** Adopted as one of two explicit assumptions (with momentum conservation) in the §4.3 collision; in §4.4 its low-speed limit is shown to contain kinetic-energy conservation, which justifies reading mc^2 as energy.

**Prerequisites:** relativistic mass, conservation of linear momentum

$$
m(u) + m_0 = M(U)

$$

**Notes:** Rest mass is not conserved in inelastic processes (Exercise 4.2); only the relativistic mass (energy) is.

Expanding relativistic expressions in powers of u/c and keeping leading terms recovers Newtonian formulae, e.g. gamma m0 c^2 ~ m0 c^2 + m0 u^2/2 and gamma m0 u ~ m0 u; relativistic conservation laws thereby reduce to Newtonian ones.

### relativistic energy

### mass-energy equivalence

*principle · core* · also: equivalence of mass and energy, E = mc^2

U^2 - \left(\frac{2c^2}{u}\right)U + c^2 = 0
$$

Rearranged composition law.

### Roots for U · derivation-step · DIV §4.3 p.54 (pdf 69)

$$
U = \frac{c^2}{u} \pm \left[\left(\frac{c^2}{u}\right)^2 - c^2\right]^{1/2} = \frac{c^2}{u}\left[1 \pm \left(1 - \frac{u^2}{c^2}\right)^{1/2}\right]
$$

The minus sign is the physical root (finite, and tending to u/2, as u -> 0).

### (4.11) Relativistic mass · central · DIV §4.3 p.54 (pdf 69)

$$
m(u) = \gamma m_0
$$

Mass of a particle moving at u relative to an inertial observer.

**Symbols:** gamma Lorentz factor of the particle

### (4.12) Lorentz factor of a particle · central · DIV §4.3 p.54 (pdf 69)

$$
\gamma(u) := (1 - u^2/c^2)^{-1/2}
$$

Defines gamma; same form as the frame factor beta(v) of time dilation.

### (4.13) Low-speed expansion of relativistic mass · supporting · DIV §4.4 p.54 (pdf 69)

$$
m(u) = m_0 + \frac{1}{c^2}\left(\tfrac12 m_0 u^2\right) + O\!\left(\frac{u^4}{c^4}\right)
$$

Binomial expansion of gamma m0 for u << c.

### (4.14) Kinetic energy inside mc^2 · supporting · DIV §4.4 p.55 (pdf 70)

$$
mc^2 = m_0c^2 + \tfrac12 m_0 u^2 + \cdots \simeq \text{constant} + \text{k.e.}
$$

Apart from the constant rest energy, mc^2 is the Newtonian kinetic energy to leading order.

### (4.15) Mass conservation in a two-body collision · supporting · DIV §4.4 p.55 (pdf 70)

$$
m_0(1 - v_1^2/c^2)^{-1/2} + \bar m_0(1 - \bar v_1^2/c^2)^{-1/2} = m_0(1 - v_2^2/c^2)^{-1/2} + \bar m_0(1 - \bar v_2^2/c^2)^{-1/2}
$$

Relativistic mass (energy) balance for the collision of Fig. 4.5 with unchanged rest masses.

**Symbols:** barred quantities belong to the second particle; subscripts 1 before, 2 after

### (4.16) Newtonian kinetic energy conservation · derivation-step · DIV §4.4 p.55 (pdf 70)

$$
\tfrac12 m_0v_1^2 + \tfrac12 \bar m_0\bar v_1^2 = \tfrac12 m_0v_2^2 + \tfrac12 \bar m_0\bar v_2^2
$$

Leading nontrivial order of (4.15) when all speeds are small.

### (4.17) Mass-energy relation · central · DIV §4.4 p.55 (pdf 70)

$$
E = mc^2
$$

Energy of a particle, with the additive constant fixed so a particle at rest has m0 c^2.

**Symbols:** m relativistic mass

### Energy change equals mass change · central · DIV §4.4 p.55 (pdf 70)

$$
\Delta E = \Delta(mc^2)
$$

Preferred statement of mass-energy equivalence since only energy differences are fixed.

### (4.18) Newtonian momentum conservation from the relativistic law · supporting · DIV §4.4 p.56 (pdf 71)

$$
m_0v_1 + \bar m_0\bar v_1 = m_0v_2 + \bar m_0\bar v_2
$$

Low-speed limit of relativistic momentum conservation for the Fig. 4.5 collision.

### (4.19) Mass, energy and momentum of a particle · central · DIV §4.4 p.56 (pdf 71)

$$
m = \gamma m_0,\quad E = mc^2,\quad \boldsymbol p = m\boldsymbol u
$$

Three-dimensional summary of relativistic particle dynamics.

**Symbols:** u velocity relative to S

### (4.20) Energy-momentum invariant · central · DIV §4.4 p.56 (pdf 71)

$$
(E/c)^2 - p_x^2 - p_y^2 - p_z^2 = (m_0 c)^2
$$

Frame-independent combination of energy and momentum fixed by the rest mass.

### Interval, quoted as (3.12) · supporting · DIV §4.4 p.56 (pdf 71)

$$
(ct)^2 - x^2 - y^2 - z^2 = s^2
$$

Coordinate invariant whose form (4.20) copies, suggesting (E/c, p) transforms like (ct, x).

**Symbols:** s interval

### (4.21) Lorentz transformation of energy-momentum · central · DIV §4.4 p.56 (pdf 71)

$$
E' = \beta(E - vp_x),\quad p_x' = \beta(p_x - vE/c^2),\quad p_y' = p_y,\quad p_z' = p_z
$$

Energy and momentum seen from S' in standard configuration at speed v.

**Symbols:** beta = (1 - v^2/c^2)^{-1/2} frame factor

### (4.22) Inverse transformation · supporting · DIV §4.4 p.56 (pdf 71)

$$
E = \beta(E' + vp_x'),\quad p_x = \beta(p_x' + vE'/c^2),\quad p_y = p_y',\quad p_z = p_z'
$$

Obtained by swapping primes and v -> -v; used for the rest-frame check and the Doppler argument.

### Rest-frame consistency check · derivation-step · DIV §4.4 p.57 (pdf 72)

$$
E = \beta E' = \frac{m_0c^2}{(1 - v^2/c^2)^{1/2}} = mc^2,\qquad \boldsymbol p = (\beta vE'/c^2, 0, 0) = (mv, 0, 0) = m\boldsymbol v
$$

Transforming from the instantaneous rest frame reproduces (4.19) with u = v.

### (4.23) Planck's hypothesis · central · DIV §4.5 p.57 (pdf 72)

$$
E = h\nu
$$

Energy of a radiation quantum is proportional to its frequency.

**Symbols:** h Planck's constant, nu frequency

### (4.24) Photon rest mass · supporting · DIV §4.5 p.57 (pdf 72)

$$
m_0 = \gamma^{-1}m = (1 - u^2/c^2)^{1/2}m = 0
$$

At u = c the rest mass must vanish.

### Photon momentum direction · derivation-step · DIV §4.5 p.57 (pdf 72)

$$
\boldsymbol p = (p_x, p_y, p_z) = p\hat{\boldsymbol n}
$$

Momentum of magnitude p along the unit propagation direction.

**Symbols:** n-hat unit vector of travel

### Null invariant · derivation-step · DIV §4.5 p.57 (pdf 72)

$$
(E/c)^2 - p^2 = 0
$$

(4.20) with m0 = 0.

### (4.25) Photon energy-momentum relation · central · DIV §4.5 p.58 (pdf 73)

$$
E = pc
$$

Massless particles have energy equal to c times momentum magnitude.

### (4.26) Photon energy and momentum · central · DIV §4.5 p.58 (pdf 73)

$$
E = h\nu,\quad \boldsymbol p = (h\nu/c)\hat{\boldsymbol n}
$$

Mechanical properties of a photon combining relativity with Planck's hypothesis.

### Doppler shift in wavelength, quoted as (3.26) · derivation-step · DIV §4.5 p.58 (pdf 73)

$$
\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_0} = \left(\frac{1 + v/c}{1 - v/c}\right)^{1/2}
$$

Receding source: received wavelength lengthened.

**Symbols:** lambda0 source-frame wavelength, lambda observer-frame wavelength

### (4.27) Doppler shift in frequency · supporting · DIV §4.5 p.58 (pdf 73)

$$
\frac{\nu_0}{\nu} = \left(\frac{1 + v/c}{1 - v/c}\right)^{1/2}
$$

Same shift expressed with frequencies via c = lambda nu.

### Transformed flash energy · derivation-step · DIV §4.5 p.58 (pdf 73)

$$
E = \beta(E_0 - vE_0/c) = \frac{E_0(1 - v/c)}{(1 - v^2/c^2)^{1/2}} = E_0\left(\frac{1 - v/c}{1 + v/c}\right)^{1/2}
$$

Energy received in S from a flash of energy E0 in the source frame, with p_x' = -E0/c.

### (4.28) Energy ratio of a light flash · supporting · DIV §4.5 p.58 (pdf 73)

$$
\frac{E_0}{E} = \left(\frac{1 + v/c}{1 - v/c}\right)^{1/2}
$$

Energy of light scales between frames by exactly the Doppler factor.

### Invariant energy-to-frequency ratio · supporting · DIV §4.5 p.59 (pdf 74)

$$
\frac{E_0}{\nu_0} = \frac{E}{\nu}
$$

Combining (4.27) and (4.28); the frame-independent ratio is identified with h.

### Relativistic N2 (Exercise 4.6) · supporting · DIV p.59 (pdf 74)

$$
\boldsymbol F = \frac{d}{dt}(m\boldsymbol u),\qquad \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{d(m\boldsymbol u)}{dt}\cdot\boldsymbol u = \frac{m_0}{(1 - u^2/c^2)^{3/2}}\,u\frac{du}{dt}
$$

Power delivered by the force; rewriting the right side as c^2 dm/dt and integrating gives E = mc^2 + constant.

### Inelastic merger (Exercise 4.7) · supporting · DIV p.60 (pdf 75)

$$
m_3^2 = m_1^2 + m_2^2 + 2m_1m_2\gamma_1\gamma_2(1 - u_1u_2/c^2),\qquad u_3 = \frac{m_1\gamma_1u_1 + m_2\gamma_2u_2}{m_1\gamma_1 + m_2\gamma_2}
$$

Rest mass and velocity of the composite formed when two collinear particles stick together.

**Symbols:** m_i rest masses, gamma_i = (1 - u_i^2/c^2)^{-1/2}

### Head-on elastic collision (Exercise 4.8) · supporting · DIV p.60 (pdf 75)

$$
p = \frac{p_0(m_0^2c^2 - M^2c^2)}{2Me_0 + M^2c^2 + m_0^2c^2},\qquad P = \frac{2p_0M(e_0 + Mc^2)}{2Me_0 + M^2c^2 + m_0^2c^2}
$$

Final momenta of projectile and target; the book prints m where m0 is meant in the numerator of p and in part (iii).

**Symbols:** m0, e0, p0 projectile rest mass, initial energy, momentum; M target rest mass

### Recoil-corrected emission frequency (Exercise 4.12) · supporting · DIV p.61 (pdf 76)

$$
\nu = \frac{e}{h}\left(1 - \frac{e}{2m_0c^2}\right)
$$

Photon frequency from an atom at rest that loses rest energy e, reduced slightly by recoil.

**Symbols:** m0 initial atomic mass

## Worked examples

### Example · intro · DIV §4.1 p.50 (pdf 65)

**Problem:** Give an operational procedure for assigning mass to a body using only Newton's laws and measurements of acceleration.

**Method:** Take two bodies influenced only by each other; apply N2 to each and N3 to the pair so that m1 a1 = -m2 a2; declare one body the unit and repeat against others.

**Key insight:** Mass ratios are ratios of mutually induced accelerations, which shows mass is defined through the very laws it enters.

**Result:** m2/m1 = -a1/a2 (as magnitudes, |a1|/|a2|).

**Concepts:** operational measurement of mass by mutual accelerations, Newton's third law, inertial mass

### Example · intro · DIV §4.2 p.51 (pdf 66)

**Problem:** Show that the total momentum of an isolated system of n particles is conserved.

**Method:** Split each particle's force into external and pairwise internal parts, sum N2 over all particles, cancel internal pairs using N3, then set the external force to zero.

**Key insight:** Momentum conservation for the whole system is the collective shadow of action-reaction between pairs.

**Result:** dP/dt = F^ext, so P is constant when F^ext = 0: P_initial = P_final.

**Concepts:** conservation of linear momentum, internal and external forces, isolated system

### Example · standard · DIV §4.3 p.53 (pdf 68)

**Problem:** Assuming mass depends on speed and that mass and momentum are conserved, find m(u) for a particle moving at u.

**Method:** Collide a moving particle with an identical one at rest so they stick. In the lab, mass and momentum conservation give m(u) = m0 U/(u - U). In the centre-of-momentum frame the particles move at +U and -U, so velocity composition gives u = 2U/(1 + U^2/c^2). Solve the quadratic for U, keep the root that vanishes as u -> 0, and substitute; the algebra reduces U/(u - U) to (1 - u^2/c^2)^(-1/2).

**Key insight:** Symmetry in the centre-of-momentum frame plus relativistic velocity composition is enough to fix the whole speed dependence of mass from one special collision.

**Result:** m(u) = gamma(u) m0 with gamma = (1 - u^2/c^2)^(-1/2).

**Concepts:** relativistic mass, rest mass, centre-of-momentum frame, relativistic velocity addition, conservation of relativistic mass, perfectly inelastic collision

### Example · intro · DIV §4.4 p.54 (pdf 69)

**Problem:** Interpret gamma m0 c^2 at low speed.

**Method:** Binomially expand (1 - u^2/c^2)^(-1/2) to second order in u/c and multiply by c^2.

**Key insight:** The relativistic mass already contains the classical kinetic energy, which suggests that mc^2 is an energy.

**Result:** mc^2 = m0 c^2 + m0 u^2/2 + m0 c^2 O(u^4/c^4); the constant is the rest energy and the next term is the Newtonian kinetic energy.

**Concepts:** Newtonian limit of relativistic mechanics, relativistic kinetic energy, relativistic energy

### Example · intro · DIV §4.4 p.55 (pdf 70)

**Problem:** Show that, for slow particles, conservation of relativistic mass and momentum in a two-body collision (Fig. 4.5) reduces to conservation of Newtonian kinetic energy and momentum.

**Method:** Expand each gamma factor in (4.15); the zeroth-order rest-mass terms cancel on both sides, leaving the order-u^2 terms. For momentum, gamma m0 v -> m0 v at leading order. The details are left as Exercise 4.4.

**Key insight:** A single relativistic conservation law (of mass, i.e. energy) contains both rest-mass conservation and kinetic-energy conservation as successive orders.

**Result:** (4.16) and (4.18).

**Concepts:** conservation of relativistic mass, Newtonian limit of relativistic mechanics, conservation of linear momentum

### Example · intro · DIV §4.4 p.56 (pdf 71)

**Problem:** Derive the energy-momentum invariant from m = gamma m0, E = mc^2 and p = mu.

**Method:** Compute (E/c)^2 - p^2 = gamma^2 m0^2 (c^2 - u^2) = gamma^2 m0^2 c^2 (1 - u^2/c^2) = m0^2 c^2 (left as Exercise 4.5).

**Key insight:** The combination depends only on the rest mass, so it is the same for every observer, just as the interval is.

**Result:** (E/c)^2 - p_x^2 - p_y^2 - p_z^2 = (m0 c)^2.

**Concepts:** energy-momentum relation, rest mass, relativistic momentum

### Example · intro · DIV §4.4 p.56 (pdf 71)

**Problem:** Check the assumed Lorentz transformation of energy and momentum.

**Method:** Let S' be the particle's instantaneous rest frame, so p' = 0 and E' = m0 c^2; apply the inverse transformation (4.22).

**Key insight:** Boosting the rest-frame values must regenerate the moving-particle formulae; it does, so the assumed law is consistent with (4.19).

**Result:** E = beta m0 c^2 = mc^2 and p = (mv, 0, 0).

**Concepts:** Lorentz transformation of energy and momentum, instantaneous rest frame, rest energy

### Example · intro · DIV §4.5 p.57 (pdf 72)

**Problem:** What rest mass, and what energy-momentum relation, must a particle moving at c have?

**Method:** Put u = c into m0 = m/gamma, then set m0 = 0 in (4.20) and take positive roots; combine with Planck's hypothesis.

**Key insight:** Light fits into relativistic mechanics as the massless limit, with energy and momentum proportional.

**Result:** m0 = 0, E = pc, p = (h nu/c) n-hat.

**Concepts:** masslessness of the photon, photon energy-momentum relation, Planck's relation, photon

### Example · standard · DIV §4.5 p.58 (pdf 73)

**Problem:** Show that the ratio of a light flash's energy to its frequency is the same in the source frame and in the frame of an observer from whom the source recedes.

**Method:** Rewrite the Doppler wavelength ratio as a frequency ratio. For the flash, E = pc and travel along negative x give p_x' = -E0/c; insert into E = beta(E' + v p_x') and simplify to the same square-root factor. Divide the two ratios.

**Key insight:** Energy and frequency of light pick up identical Doppler factors under a boost, so E/nu is Lorentz invariant.

**Result:** E0/nu0 = E/nu, which the authors identify with the universal constant h.

**Concepts:** relativistic Doppler effect, Lorentz transformation of energy and momentum, Lorentz invariance of the energy-to-frequency ratio of light, Planck's relation


## Analogies and intuitions

### Mass as the reluctance of a body to be pushed: heavier things are harder to get moving. → inertial mass · useful · DIV §4.1 p.49 (pdf 64)

Opens the chapter as an everyday anchor before the operational definition by mutual accelerations.

**Where it breaks down:** Suggests mass is a fixed property of a body, which the relativistic sections then revise; also mixes up weight and inertia for many learners.

**App idea:** Push the same force on carts of different masses and compare accelerations, then repeat at high speed to foreshadow that pushing gets harder near c.

### Since lengths and times depend on the observer, mass might too. → relativistic mass · weak · DIV §4.3 p.52 (pdf 67)

Plausibility argument for the hypothesis m = m(u) before the collision calculation.

**Where it breaks down:** Rest mass, the quantity most physicists now call mass, is invariant; what really depends on the observer is energy.

### The factor in m = gamma m0 has the same shape as the time-dilation factor, but one uses a particle's speed and the other a frame's speed. → Lorentz factor · useful · DIV §4.3 p.54 (pdf 69)

Connects the new dynamical result to familiar kinematics while warning that the velocities differ.

**Where it breaks down:** Structural resemblance only; mixing the two velocities in problems with several frames causes errors.

**App idea:** Side-by-side gauges: a moving clock's slowed tick rate and a moving particle's energy, driven by the same slider but labelled with the relevant velocity.

### Newtonian kinetic energy is hiding inside the relativistic mass, revealed by expanding in u/c. → relativistic energy · strong · DIV §4.4 p.55 (pdf 70)

The main argument for interpreting mc^2 as energy.

**Where it breaks down:** Only identifies energy up to an additive constant; the rest energy's physical reality needs processes like annihilation or nuclear reactions.

**App idea:** A series-expansion stacker: tap to add successive terms of gamma m0 c^2 and watch the approximation hug the exact curve up to larger speeds.

### c^2 as an enormous exchange rate: a small change in mass corresponds to a vast amount of energy. → mass-energy equivalence · useful · DIV §4.4 p.56 (pdf 71)

Explains why mass changes go unnoticed in daily life but matter for nuclear energy.

**Where it breaks down:** Encourages the misleading image of mass being 'converted' rather than energy carrying mass.

**App idea:** Mass-to-energy converter slider with everyday comparisons (paperclip, raisin) expressed in household-years of electricity.

### Energy-momentum is to (ct, x) as its invariant (m0 c)^2 is to the interval s^2. → energy-momentum four-vector · strong · DIV §4.4 p.56 (pdf 71)

Motivates assuming that energy and momentum transform like time and position under boosts.

**Where it breaks down:** A matching invariant does not by itself prove the transformation law; the justification comes from four-velocity and proper time.

**App idea:** Twin Minkowski planes: events sliding on hyperbolae of constant interval, and momenta sliding on mass-shell hyperbolae of constant rest mass, both driven by one rapidity slider.

### A photon's rest mass is like a property measured in a situation that can never occur, so calling it zero is harmless. → masslessness of the photon · useful · DIV §4.5 p.57 (pdf 72)

Defuses the surprise of zero rest mass for something carrying energy.

**Where it breaks down:** Better stated as: light's four-momentum is null; learners may still think zero mass means zero energy or momentum.

**App idea:** Shrink m0 toward zero on a mass-shell plot and watch the hyperbola collapse onto the light cone while E = pc emerges.


## Misconceptions addressed

### Newton's laws rest on independently defined notions of force and mass. · DIV §4.1 p.50 (pdf 65)

**Correction:** Force and mass get their operational meaning from the laws: mass is calibrated via N2 and N3, and force read off from ma is circular unless supplemented by independent force laws.

**Why tempting:** School physics presents mass and force as obvious primitives measured by scales and spring balances.

### Special relativity already covers gravity. · DIV §4.1 p.51 (pdf 66)

**Correction:** Special relativity describes bodies and light where gravitation is absent; gravity requires general relativity.

**Why tempting:** Relativity is popularly associated with gravity and curved spacetime.

### The formula m = gamma m0 follows logically from the Lorentz transformations alone. · DIV §4.3 p.54 (pdf 69)

**Correction:** Its derivation needs extra dynamical assumptions (speed-dependent mass, conservation of mass and momentum); experiment is what ultimately confirms it.

**Why tempting:** The derivation looks like pure algebra built on the velocity composition law.

### The gamma in m = gamma m0 and the factor in time dilation are the same velocity-dependent quantity. · DIV §4.3 p.54 (pdf 69)

**Correction:** They share a form, but the mass factor uses the particle's velocity relative to the observer while time dilation uses the relative velocity of two frames.

**Why tempting:** Identical algebraic expressions invite being treated as one object.

### A fast particle gains more 'stuff': its intrinsic mass, and hence e.g. its tendency to form a black hole, increases. · DIV §4.3 p.54 (pdf 69)

**Correction:** The rest mass is invariant; only energy (called relativistic mass here) is observer dependent, and a particle cannot be made a black hole by boosting the observer.

**Why tempting:** The name 'relativistic mass' and the plot of m against u invite a literal reading.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### E = mc^2 is a formula relating two different things, and the absolute value of E is physically meaningful. · DIV §4.4 p.55 (pdf 70)

**Correction:** Mass and energy are equivalent concepts; since energy is fixed only up to a constant, the safe statement is that energy changes equal mass changes times c^2.

**Why tempting:** Popular accounts describe mass 'turning into' energy as if they were distinct substances.

### Relativistic mass conservation is an additional law, independent of energy conservation. · DIV §4.4 p.55 (pdf 70)

**Correction:** At low speed it contains kinetic-energy conservation; with E = mc^2 it is energy conservation.

**Why tempting:** Newtonian mechanics treats mass and energy conservation as separate principles.

### Rest masses add: the composite formed in a sticking collision has rest mass equal to the sum of the parts. · DIV p.59 (pdf 74)

**Correction:** The composite's rest mass is larger, because the kinetic energy in the centre-of-momentum frame becomes internal energy and therefore rest mass.

**Why tempting:** In Newtonian mechanics mass is additive and kinetic energy lost in inelastic collisions just 'disappears' as heat.

### A photon has zero mass, so it cannot have momentum or energy; or zero rest mass is a contradiction. · DIV §4.5 p.57 (pdf 72)

**Correction:** Zero rest mass simply means E = pc; photons carry energy h nu and momentum h nu/c, and no observer can ever see one at rest.

**Why tempting:** Newtonian p = mv makes momentum proportional to mass.

### Relativistic dynamics is just F = gamma m0 a. · DIV p.59 (pdf 74)

**Correction:** The relativistic second law is F = d(gamma m0 u)/dt; since gamma changes with speed, force and acceleration are generally not parallel and there is no single 'mass' relating them.

**Why tempting:** Once mass is called gamma m0, substituting it into F = ma looks natural.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Special relativity by itself proves that light is quantized. · DIV §4.5 p.59 (pdf 74)

**Correction:** The Doppler argument shows only that E/nu is the same for all observers of a given signal; that the ratio is one universal constant for all light is extra, quantum input.

**Why tempting:** The book presents the argument as deriving Planck's hypothesis.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The Newtonian momentum formula p = m0 v is exactly conserved in all inertial frames. · DIV §4.3 p.53 (pdf 68)

**Correction:** With relativistic velocity composition, m0 v is not conserved in every frame; only gamma m0 v is, and it reduces to m0 v at low speed.

**Why tempting:** Newtonian momentum conservation is taught as a universal law.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Two isolated bodies weighing each other by acceleration · DIV §4.1 p.50 (pdf 65)

**Setup:** Two bodies in empty space interact only with each other; their accelerations are recorded.

**Lesson:** Mass ratios follow from acceleration ratios via N2 and N3, which reveals that Newtonian mass is defined through the laws themselves.

**App idea:** Space-station puck experiment where learners calibrate an unknown mass against a unit puck.

### Sticky collision seen from lab and centre-of-momentum frames · DIV §4.3 p.53 (pdf 68)

**Setup:** A particle moving at u hits an identical particle at rest and they stick. The same event is described in the lab frame and in the frame where the particles approach each other with equal speeds.

**Lesson:** Requiring mass and momentum conservation in the lab while respecting relativistic velocity composition forces m = gamma m0.

**App idea:** Frame-slider collision animation with live conservation checks under Newtonian and relativistic mass models (see Fig. 4.3 redesign).

### A receding lamp emits a single flash · DIV §4.5 p.58 (pdf 73)

**Setup:** A source moving away from an observer emits a light flash of energy E0 and frequency nu0 in its own frame; the observer measures energy E and frequency nu.

**Lesson:** Energy and frequency drop by the same Doppler factor, so their ratio is frame independent, consistent with E = h nu.

**App idea:** A pulse leaving a receding lamp with a colour shift and a shrinking energy bar; both ratios shown updating together as the speed slider moves.


## Applications and observations

- **Mass comparison of elementary particles** (experiment, DIV §4.1 p.50 (pdf 65)): The authors note that comparing mutually induced accelerations is in practice used to compare masses of elementary particles, with other influences held nearly constant.

Key numbers: Our estimate for illustration: an electron at 0.99c has gamma ~ 7.1, so its energy is about 7 times its rest energy of 0.511 MeV.
- **Energy equivalent of a small mass** (numerical-estimate, DIV §4.4 p.56 (pdf 71)): The book remarks that c^2 is huge in conventional units, so a small mass change corresponds to enormous energy, with nuclear applications alluded to. Key numbers: Our estimate: 1 g of mass corresponds to about 9 x 10^13 J (roughly 21 kilotons of TNT).
- **Photoelectric effect** (experiment, DIV §4.5 p.57 (pdf 72)): Ejection of electrons from a metal by incident (especially ultraviolet) light, cited as strong support for Planck's hypothesis.
- **Momentum of visible photons** (numerical-estimate, DIV §4.5 p.58 (pdf 73)): Application of E = h nu and p = h nu/c to visible light (our own illustration, not in the book). Key numbers: Our estimate: a 500 nm photon has E ~ 2.5 eV and p ~ 1.3 x 10^-27 kg m/s.

## Notation and conventions

- **Law labels N2, N3, UG:** Newton's second law, third law and universal gravitation are stated in grey boxes with these tags. (DIV §4.1 p.49 (pdf 64))
- **Vectors and unit vectors:** Bold symbols for vectors (F, p, v, a, r); a hat marks a unit vector (r-hat, n-hat); an overdot is a time derivative. (DIV §4.1 p.51 (pdf 66))
- **F_ij:** Force on particle i due to particle j, with F_ii = 0; superscript 'ext' for external forces; capital P for total momentum. — The book writes P = sum P_i where lower-case p_i is meant. (DIV §4.2 p.52 (pdf 67))
- **m, m0, M(U), M0:** m or m(u) is relativistic (speed-dependent) mass; subscript 0 marks rest values (m0 rest mass, M0 composite rest mass, E0 rest energy). — Modern texts use m for rest mass and avoid relativistic mass. (DIV §4.3 p.53 (pdf 68))
- **gamma versus beta:** gamma(u) = (1 - u^2/c^2)^(-1/2) for a particle's speed u; beta(v) = (1 - v^2/c^2)^(-1/2) for the relative speed v of frames S and S'. — Opposite to the common modern use of beta for v/c; flag when mixing sources. (DIV §4.3 p.54 (pdf 69))
- **Defining symbol :=:** Used for definitions such as (4.12). (DIV §4.3 p.54 (pdf 69))
- **'Non-relativistic units':** Means conventional units with c written explicitly, as opposed to Chapter 2's relativistic units with c = 1; formula (2.6) is restated with c restored. — Does not mean a low-speed approximation. (DIV §4.3 p.54 (pdf 69))
- **Order symbol O(u^4/c^4):** Stands for all terms of fourth and higher order in u/c. (DIV §4.4 p.54 (pdf 69))
- **Barred quantities:** m0-bar, v1-bar, v2-bar refer to the second particle in the two-body collision; subscripts 1 and 2 denote before and after. (DIV §4.4 p.55 (pdf 70))
- **Signature of invariants:** Invariants are written time part minus space part, (E/c)^2 - p^2 = (m0 c)^2, matching the (+,-,-,-) interval of Chapter 3. — Opposite to Schutz and MTW, where p.p = -m^2. (DIV §4.4 p.56 (pdf 71))
- **Frames S, S' in standard configuration:** Primed quantities are measured in S', which moves at v along the x-axis of S; inverse transformations by swapping primes and v -> -v. (DIV §4.4 p.56 (pdf 71))
- **Doppler subscripts:** Subscript 0 on lambda, nu, E denotes values in the source's rest frame; unsubscripted values are measured by the observer. — (4.27) is printed with a stray double equals sign. (DIV §4.5 p.58 (pdf 73))
- **Units for G:** SI units, G quoted in m^3 kg^-1 s^-2. — Printed with capital 'Kg'. (DIV §4.1 p.51 (pdf 66))

## Teaching gems

### Fix the unknown function m(u) with a single symmetric sticking collision: in the centre-of-momentum frame the setup is a mirror image, so the lab speed is U composed with U, and conservation in the lab then yields gamma. · DIV §4.3 p.53 (pdf 68)

**Why it works:** Uses only tools the learner already has (velocity composition and conservation), shows precisely where the assumptions enter, and produces gamma from a concrete picture rather than from four-vector formalism.

**App idea:** Collision theatre with a frame slider and a toggle between constant mass and gamma m0; conservation meters turn red in boosted frames under the Newtonian model and stay green under the relativistic one.

### Find the Newtonian kinetic energy sitting inside gamma m0 c^2 by expansion, and use it to argue that mc^2 is the energy. · DIV §4.4 p.54 (pdf 69)

**Why it works:** Connects a famous but mysterious formula to a familiar quantity, and shows how a relativistic definition earns its name through the correspondence principle.

**App idea:** Term-by-term Taylor stacker over the exact gamma curve, with a speed marker showing where each truncation fails by more than 1 percent.

### Show that one relativistic conservation law (of gamma m0, i.e. energy) contains, order by order, both the conservation of rest mass and of Newtonian kinetic energy. · DIV §4.4 p.55 (pdf 70)

**Why it works:** Unifies two separate Newtonian conservation laws and explains why energy conservation 'looked' separate at low speeds.

**App idea:** Collision sandbox with a shrinkable speed of light: bars for rest-mass sum, kinetic-energy sum and total energy show which are conserved as c is lowered.

### Place (E/c)^2 - p^2 = (m0 c)^2 beside (ct)^2 - x^2 = s^2 and read off that (E/c, p) must transform like (ct, x). · DIV §4.4 p.56 (pdf 71)

**Why it works:** Transfers all the geometric intuition built for spacetime diagrams to momentum space in one move, preparing four-vectors.

**App idea:** Linked twin Minkowski planes (events and momenta); one boost slider moves a worldline tick along its hyperbola and a particle's momentum along its mass shell in lockstep.

### Check any transformation law by boosting from the rest frame and seeing whether it rebuilds the moving-particle formulae. · DIV §4.4 p.57 (pdf 72)

**Why it works:** Models a habit of self-verification and makes the physical meaning of E', p' concrete.

**App idea:** A momentum-space point at (m0 c, 0) that the learner drags along the mass shell with a boost slider; readouts compare transformed values with gamma m0 c^2 and gamma m0 v.

### Treat light as the zero-rest-mass limit: set u = c to get m0 = 0, then E = pc and p = h nu/c. · DIV §4.5 p.57 (pdf 72)

**Why it works:** Integrates photons into the same energy-momentum framework instead of treating them as a special case, and defuses the 'massless but energetic' puzzle.

**App idea:** Mass-shell hyperbola with an m0 slider collapsing onto the light cone; a photon pulse shows its momentum arrow length tied to its colour.

### Energy of a light flash and its frequency pick up exactly the same Doppler factor under a boost, so E/nu is frame independent. · DIV §4.5 p.58 (pdf 73)

**Why it works:** Recycles the previous chapter's Doppler result to reveal a hidden consistency between relativity and quantum theory.

**App idea:** Receding-lamp demo where a colour bar (frequency) and an energy bar shrink together as the recession speed grows, with their ratio displayed as a constant.

### Measure mass by letting two isolated bodies accelerate each other, and notice that this makes mass depend on the laws it appears in. · DIV §4.1 p.50 (pdf 65)

**Why it works:** Invites learners to question 'obvious' concepts, the same habit needed later for the equivalence principle.

**App idea:** Unknown-mass puzzle with pucks on a frictionless track, scored by how accurately learners infer mass ratios from observed accelerations.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **The chapter is built on relativistic mass m = gamma m0 with E = mc^2, a framing most modern textbooks and researchers have abandoned in favour of invariant mass m and E = gamma m c^2.** — Learners mixing sources may think mass literally grows with speed, misapply F = gamma m a, or be confused by the modern E = mc^2 for rest energy only. *Suggestion:* Teach energy and momentum first (E = gamma m0 c^2, p = gamma m0 u), present 'relativistic mass' as a historical name for E/c^2, and connect to four-momentum early.
- **Printed error: the boxed verbal statement of universal gravitation says the force is inversely proportional to the distance, omitting the square; (4.4) correctly has r^2.** — A reader who trusts the boxed statement may learn a 1/r law. *Suggestion:* State the inverse-square law explicitly and point out the misprint; a quick check with doubling distance helps.
- **The key algebra from (4.10) with the minus root to gamma(u) is not shown; the reader is only told to 'check'.** — Many learners get lost in the nested square roots and lose confidence in the derivation. *Suggestion:* Show that u - U = (c^2/u) sqrt(1 - u^2/c^2)[1 - sqrt(1 - u^2/c^2)], so U/(u - U) = gamma, or rationalize U = u/(1 + sqrt(1 - u^2/c^2)).
- **Only conservation of mass and of momentum in S are named as assumptions, but the argument also quietly uses: mass depends on speed alone, not direction (so both particles in S' carry the same m(U)); momentum is conserved in S' too, which is what puts the merged body at rest there and makes S' move at U; and the principle of relativity, so the same function m(u) applies in both frames. The composite mass M(U) is eliminated, so no law for it is needed.** — Learners may believe the result is more forced than it is, or be unable to reconstruct the logic. *Suggestion:* Put the complete list of inputs on screen before the algebra, marking which frame each one is used in, and let the learner switch individual assumptions off to see where the derivation breaks.
- **The step from (4.15) to (4.16) quietly takes the rest masses to be the same before and after, so it only covers elastic collisions. The §4.3 sticking collision obeys the same mass-conservation law, yet Newtonian kinetic energy is not conserved there. The text never reconciles the two cases.** — A learner may conclude that relativistic mass conservation always implies kinetic-energy conservation at low speed, which contradicts the inelastic collision used a page earlier. *Suggestion:* Expand both sides of the mass balance without assuming equal rest masses. The zeroth-order terms then give the change in rest mass, and the order-u^2 terms show that the kinetic energy lost equals c^2 times the rest mass gained (link to Exercise 4.2).
- **The Lorentz transformation of energy and momentum (4.21) is assumed from the resemblance of two invariants; an invariant quadratic form alone does not determine how the components transform.** — Weakens the logical chain and leaves learners unsure why energy mixes with momentum. *Suggestion:* Derive it from p = gamma m0 u and the velocity transformation, or introduce four-velocity dx/dtau and multiply by m0.
- **The claim to 'derive' Planck's hypothesis overreaches: the Doppler argument shows E/nu is invariant for a given signal, not that it equals one constant for all light, and a classical electromagnetic pulse obeys the same scaling.** — Learners may believe quantization follows from relativity. *Suggestion:* Present it as a consistency result: relativity permits E proportional to nu for every observer; quantum physics supplies the universal constant h.
- **The geometry of the Doppler step (why the flash travels in the negative x-direction and p_x' = -E0/c) relies on recalling Fig. 3.11 from the previous chapter.** — Sign errors or confusion about which frame is the source frame. *Suggestion:* Redraw the configuration: observer at the origin of S, source at rest in S' receding along +x, light returning along -x.
- **The photon rest-mass argument substitutes u = c into m0 = m/gamma, which presumes the photon's relativistic mass is finite; strictly the massive-particle formula m = gamma m0 does not apply at u = c.** — Careful learners notice the 0 times infinity issue and may reject the argument. *Suggestion:* Argue instead from E^2 = p^2c^2 + m0^2c^4 with the requirement that speed pc^2/E equals c.
- **Notation clashes: beta for the frame Lorentz factor and gamma for the particle factor, plus 'non-relativistic units' meaning c not set to 1.** — Confusion with modern sources where beta = v/c, and with low-speed approximations. *Suggestion:* Use gamma_frame and gamma_particle (or gamma_v, gamma_u) consistently in the app and explain the unit terminology once.
- **Typographical slips: (4.27) printed with '==', Exercise 4.8 (iii)-(iv) writes m where m0 is intended, and §4.2 writes P_i for p_i.** — Minor confusion when checking algebra, especially in Exercise 4.8. *Suggestion:* Provide corrected versions in the tutor's worked solutions.
- **No relativistic force law, no discussion of kinetic energy (gamma - 1) m0 c^2 as a named quantity, and no worked elastic-collision example in the text; these appear only in exercises.** — Learners reach tensor chapters without practice solving collision problems or understanding force in relativity. *Suggestion:* Add a worked two-body decay or Compton-like example and a short treatment of F = dp/dt with parallel and perpendicular forces.
- **Newton's third law in its instantaneous action-reaction form is not compatible with relativity for forces at a distance, but the chapter does not say what replaces it.** — Learners may assume N3 carries over and be puzzled later by field momentum. *Suggestion:* Note that in relativity conservation of total momentum (including fields) is the fundamental statement, not pairwise action-reaction.

## Tutor notes

- Opening question: 'If moving clocks run slow and moving rods shrink, should momentum still be m v?' Let the learner propose that something must change, then introduce the collision test.
- Before the collision derivation, make sure the learner can use the velocity composition law u = (w + V)/(1 + wV/c^2) and knows what the centre-of-momentum frame is.
- Suggested order: Newtonian momentum conservation -> failure of m0 v conservation under relativistic velocity addition -> symmetric sticking collision -> gamma -> low-speed expansion -> E = gamma m0 c^2 -> invariant -> four-vector transformation -> photons -> Doppler consistency.
- Use modern language by default (rest mass m, energy E = gamma m c^2, momentum p = gamma m u) and translate: 'the book's relativistic mass m is our E/c^2'. Flag this explicitly whenever quoting the book.
- When deriving gamma from (4.10), offer the simplification U = u/(1 + sqrt(1 - u^2/c^2)); then u - U = u sqrt(1 - u^2/c^2)/(1 + sqrt(1 - u^2/c^2)) and the ratio is immediate.
- Check for understanding: ask which velocity enters gamma in m = gamma m0 and which enters beta in the energy-momentum transformation; many learners conflate them.
- Check for understanding: ask the learner to compute the rest mass of the product in the §4.3 collision (answer m0 sqrt(2(1 + gamma))) and to explain why it exceeds 2 m0.
- Common learner question: 'Does an object moving fast become a black hole?' Answer: no; rest mass and all invariant properties are unchanged, and in its own frame nothing happens.
- Common learner question: 'How can light have momentum without mass?' Use E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4 with m = 0 and mention radiation pressure and solar sails.
- Common learner question: 'Is E = mc^2 about mass turning into energy?' Emphasize that energy has mass: a hot object or a compressed spring weighs very slightly more; nuclear reactions redistribute energy and rest mass.
- When presenting the Planck argument, frame it as a consistency check, not a proof of quantization, and ask the learner what extra assumption makes h universal.
- Mention the book's printed slip in the verbal gravitation law (1/r instead of 1/r^2) if a learner is reading along.
- For advanced learners, preview the Chapter 8 route: four-velocity u^a = dx^a/dtau, four-momentum p^a = m u^a, and p_a p^a = m^2 c^2 in the (+,-,-,-) signature.
- Numerical feel: 1 g of mass is about 9 x 10^13 J; a visible photon carries about 2-3 eV; an electron at 0.99c has gamma about 7.
