# DIV ch24 · Relativistic cosmology

> Builds the Robertson-Walker spacetime from the cosmological principle and Weyl's postulate, feeds a perfect fluid into Einstein's equations to get the Friedmann equations, and then derives cosmological redshift, a luminosity distance and the (approximate) Hubble law, after warming up with Olbers' paradox and a discrete Newtonian model.

**Pages:** printed 479–510 · pdf 494–525 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

Mathematically lighter than the preceding black-hole and radiation chapters: the heaviest steps are the eigenvector/Einstein-space argument and the 3D Riemann decomposition, while the Friedmann equations and several transformations are left as exercises. Conceptually the challenge lies in the modelling assumptions (averaging, privileged observers, cosmic time) and the several distance definitions. The Newtonian warm-up and embedding pictures make it accessible, but notational overloading (R for scale factor and Ricci scalar; d_A and d_L conventions differing from modern usage) and some dated numbers can confuse.

## Role in the book

Opens Part F (Cosmology) after the long treatment of black holes and gravitational radiation. It is the bridge from local exact solutions to a model of the whole universe: it assembles the symmetry assumptions and the kinematics (metric, redshift, distance, H and q) that Chapter 25 uses to classify and solve the classical Friedmann models and that Chapter 26 updates with multi-component LambdaCDM, CMB and inflation. It reuses tensor machinery from Chapter 6 (Weyl tensor in 3D), the perfect fluid and energy conditions of Chapter 12, the cosmological term and conservation laws of Chapter 13, and the spherically symmetric ansatz from Chapter 15.

## Learning objectives

- State Olbers' paradox, reproduce the shell argument that a static, infinite, eternal, uniformly starry universe has a bright sky, and explain why finite age or expansion removes the paradox.
- Build the discrete Newtonian cosmological model: impose radial scaling r_i(t)=S(t)r_i(t_0), derive Hubble's law v=Hr, and turn total energy conservation into an equation of the Friedmann form with k = +1, 0, -1.
- State the cosmological principle and translate it into geometry: a foliation by spacelike slices that are homogeneous (transitive isometries) and isotropic about every point.
- State Weyl's postulate and explain why it yields a perfect-fluid substratum, comoving coordinates and a cosmic time that is the proper time of fundamental observers.
- Argue from similar triangles that time can enter the spatial metric only through an overall scale factor, g_{alpha beta}=S^2(t) h_{alpha beta}(x).
- Show that isotropy about every point forces G^a_b proportional to the identity (an Einstein space), and that in three dimensions this means constant curvature.
- Solve for the spherically symmetric 3-metric of constant curvature, obtain dr^2/(1-Kr^2)+r^2 d Omega^2, rescale to k=+1,0,-1, and write the Robertson-Walker line element in both the unbarred and conformally flat forms.
- Describe the three spatial geometries: the 3-sphere embedded in Euclidean R^4 (finite volume 2 pi^2 R^3), flat R^3, and the hyperboloid embedded in Minkowski space (infinite volume), including how the area of geodesic spheres grows with radius in each.
- Derive the two Friedmann equations for a perfect fluid with Lambda, recover energy conservation dE + p dV = 0 from them, and obtain the acceleration equation.
- Use the acceleration equation and a tangent-line argument to prove the FRW singularity theorem and to bound the age of a decelerating universe by the Hubble time.
- Integrate the dust case to Friedmann's equation Rdot^2 = C/R + Lambda R^2/3 - k and recognize it as identical to the Newtonian analogue.
- Derive the cosmological redshift 1+z = R(t_0)/R(t_1) from two successive radial null rays, and distinguish it from a special-relativistic Doppler shift.
- Distinguish absolute (proper) distance, angular-size distance and luminosity distance; explain the two factors of (1+z) in the flux, and derive z ~ H_0 d_L and the second-order correction involving q_0.
- Define the deceleration parameter and relate q, H and rho through q = (4 pi rho/3 - Lambda/3)/H^2.

## Teaching approach

A classic Bondi-style introduction: the authors first make the reader feel why cosmology needs dynamics (Olbers' paradox) and how far Newtonian reasoning can go (a discrete model that already yields Hubble's law and a Friedmann-type energy equation). They then replace ad hoc Newtonian assumptions by carefully stated principles (cosmological principle, Weyl's postulate, general relativity) and derive, step by step, the metric, the dynamics and the observational relations. Geometry is made concrete through embeddings and by tracking how sphere areas grow with radius. The chapter ends by deducing, not assuming, Hubble's law, and calibrates the theory against rough observational numbers.

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, axiomatic-mathematical, derivation-heavy, historical-narrative, diagram-driven, observational-astrophysics

**Narrative arc**

1. Frame cosmology as modelling a unique system by smoothing out all structure; preview the hot big bang and caution that local physics is being extrapolated (§24.1).
2. Motivating puzzle: Olbers' shell argument shows a static infinite eternal universe would have a sky as bright as a stellar surface; resolutions point to youth or expansion (§24.2).
3. Newtonian warm-up: a finite set of galaxies moving radially with a common scale factor gives Hubble's law, and energy conservation (with a Lambda force) gives an equation of exactly the Friedmann form (§24.3).
4. Principles: state the cosmological principle, define homogeneity and isotropy precisely, review evidence (CMB), and survey alternatives (Bianchi, Kasner, hierarchical) before adopting it on pragmatic grounds (§24.4).
5. Weyl's postulate: galaxies as fluid particles on non-intersecting timelike geodesics give a unique velocity field, hence a perfect fluid (§24.5).
6. Kinematics: hypersurface orthogonality gives comoving coordinates and cosmic time; similar-triangle argument gives g=S^2(t)h(x); world map vs world picture (§24.6).
7. Geometry: isotropy everywhere implies Einstein space, which in 3D implies constant curvature; solve the spherically symmetric ansatz and rescale to Robertson-Walker form (§24.7).
8. Visualize k=+1,0,-1 via embedding in Euclidean or Minkowski 4-space, areas, volumes and topology (§24.8).
9. Dynamics: insert the perfect fluid into the field equations, obtain the two Friedmann equations, extract energy conservation, the acceleration equation and the FRW singularity theorem, then specialize to dust to get Friedmann's equation and note its identity with the Newtonian one (§24.9).
10. Observables: radial null geodesics give redshift 1+z=R_0/R_1 and a linear z-distance law for nearby sources (§24.10).
11. Distance: reject absolute and angular-size distances as impractical, define luminosity distance with two (1+z) factors, mention magnitudes and standard candles (§24.11).
12. Payoff: compute d_L=r_1 R_0 from homogeneity, deduce z~H_0 d_L, introduce Hubble time, deceleration parameter and its second-order effect, compare with Sandage's data, and link q to density and Lambda (§24.12).

**Signature moves**

- Newtonian model first, then show the relativistic Friedmann equation is formally identical, while warning that the meaning of the terms differs.
- Principles stated as highlighted postulates (cosmological principle, Weyl's postulate), each translated into a precise geometric condition.
- Similar-triangles thought experiment to justify that the spatial metric only scales with time.
- Eigenvector argument: isotropy leaves no preferred eigen-directions, so G^a_b must be a multiple of the identity.
- Embedding diagrams of S^3 and the hyperboloid with one dimension suppressed, plus tracking the area of spheres versus radius to make curvature tangible.
- Tangent-line (concavity) argument on the graph of R(t) to prove a finite-past big bang and bound the age by the Hubble time.
- Successive-light-ray construction on a spacetime diagram to derive redshift without any velocity concept.
- Physical bookkeeping of flux: photon arrival rate and photon energy each reduced by (1+z).
- Explicit honesty about foundations: the cosmological principle is adopted because data are too sparse for anything weaker, not because it is proven.

## Section by section

### §24.1 Preview — p.481 (pdf 496)

Defines cosmology as the dynamics of the universe treated as a smoothed-out whole, anticipates that symmetry reduces the dynamics to a first-order ODE (Friedmann's equation), and notes that Newtonian cosmology was constructed only after GR. Contrasts the perfect cosmological principle and steady state with the hot big bang, sketches the thermal history (radiation era, particle and element formation, then stars and galaxies), and stresses two epistemic caveats: local laws are extrapolated (Lambda is an example of a possible cosmic-scale interaction) and the universe is a unique, unrepeatable experiment.

**Concepts:** cosmology, Friedmann models, cosmological principle, perfect cosmological principle, steady-state theory, de Sitter solution, hot big bang model, cosmological constant, uniqueness of the universe

**Key moves**
- Smoothing assumption: ignore solar system, galaxy and cluster detail to capture only gross dynamics.
- Newtonian and relativistic cosmology share the same starting simplicity principle.
- Hot big bang timeline from radiation bath to galaxies; physics claimed reliable after about 1e-10 s (standard model) or 1e-43 s (GR plus GUTs).
- No controlled repetition in cosmology: the system studied is unique.

### §24.2 Olbers' paradox — p.483 (pdf 498)

Under Olbers' assumptions (Euclidean, infinite, static, eternal, uniform average stellar density and luminosity) every spherical shell contributes the same intensity, so the naive total diverges; including blocking by foreground stars caps it at the surface brightness of a star, still contradicting the dark sky. Thermodynamic equilibrium and line-of-sight arguments give the same conclusion. An absorbing gas does not help because it heats up and re-radiates, and curvature or finiteness of space does not change the conclusion. The resolution requires either a young universe (stars switched on recently) or expansion, whose redshift degrades distant light. The book notes a catch in the young-universe option: a short enough luminous history may conflict with the ages of the oldest stars.

**Concepts:** Olbers' paradox, lookback time, cosmological redshift

**Key moves**
- Shell of radius r, thickness dr: luminosity 4 pi r^2 dr l spread over area 4 pi r^2 gives l dr independent of r (24.1).
- Sum of shells diverges; occultation by stars reduces it to stellar-surface brightness.
- Equivalent arguments: equilibrium, every sightline ends on a star.
- Half the radiation would come from beyond about 1e20 light years.
- Absorbing gas refuted by thermal equilibrium; resolution by finite age or expansion.

### §24.3 Newtonian cosmology — p.484 (pdf 499)

A finite system of n galaxies with pairwise Newtonian attraction plus a cosmological force F_i = Lambda m_i r_i/3. Spherical symmetry about an origin makes all motion radial, and preserving the pattern forces a universal scale factor S(t), so velocities obey Hubble's law v = H r with H = Sdot/S. Substituting into the conserved energy gives E = A Sdot^2 - B/S - D S^2 with positive constants; interpretation: with Lambda = 0 expansion must decelerate, positive Lambda repels, negative attracts. Rescaling S to R = mu S with mu^2 = A/|E| produces Rdot^2 = C/R + Lambda R^2/3 - k with k in {+1,0,-1}, the same form as the relativistic Friedmann equation, although the model only applies to a finite (arbitrarily large) universe.

**Concepts:** Newtonian cosmology, scale factor, Hubble parameter, Hubble's law, Hubble constant, cosmological constant, curvature index k, big crunch

**Key moves**
- Radial motion from spherical symmetry (24.2).
- Cosmological force and its potential energy (24.4)-(24.5).
- Only uniform scaling is compatible with homogeneity: r_i(t)=S(t) r_i(t_0) (24.7).
- Differentiate to get Hubble's law (24.10).
- Energy equation (24.11) with A, B, D; note D = Lambda A/3.
- Rescale by mu to normalize the energy constant into k = +1, 0, -1 (24.17)-(24.18).

### §24.4 The cosmological principle — p.487 (pdf 502)

States the cosmological principle as a generalized Copernican principle and makes it precise: there is a cosmic time whose slices are homogeneous (an isometry group maps any point to any other) and isotropic about each point; global isotropy implies homogeneity. Homogeneity is meant only after averaging over cells larger than about 1e9 light years, bigger than superclusters (about 3e8 ly). Evidence: isotropy of radio galaxies, X-ray sources and quasars to better than 15%, supernova Hubble-constant measurements, and above all the CMB (2.7 K, isotropic to about 1 part in 1e5 per Planck); galaxy counts and a linear Hubble law support homogeneity. Surveys alternatives: Bianchi (homogeneous anisotropic) models, the vacuum Bianchi I Kasner metric with cigar and pancake singularities, whimper singularities, BKL, and Charlier's hierarchical model. The principle is adopted because weaker assumptions leave too many unknown functions for the available data.

**Concepts:** cosmological principle, Copernican principle, homogeneity, isotropy, cosmic time, cosmic microwave background, Bianchi models, Kasner solution, cigar and pancake singularities, whimper singularity, BKL singularity, hierarchical cosmology, fluid (smoothing) approximation

**Key moves**
- Formal definitions: homogeneity via transitive isometry group; isotropy as no preferred direction (spherical symmetry) about a point.
- Global isotropy implies homogeneity.
- Homogeneity holds only for a smeared-out universe averaged over >1e9 ly cells.
- Three motivations for anisotropic/inhomogeneous models: galaxy formation, generic singularity structure (BKL), and washing-out mechanisms like inflation.
- Kasner exponents satisfy sum p_i = sum p_i^2 = 1.
- Pragmatic adoption: symmetric models are the only ones data can constrain.

### §24.5 Weyl's postulate — p.489 (pdf 504)

Weyl (1923) confronted the tension between a generally covariant theory and a unique universe by singling out privileged observers who move with the smoothed galactic motion, justified by small local peculiar velocities. In Robertson's formulation, the world-lines of the substratum form a family of timelike geodesics that spread out from a single past point (finite or infinitely remote); since they do not cross (except at past/future singular points), each event has a unique fluid velocity and the substratum is a perfect fluid. Peculiar velocities below about 1e-3 c are neglected. The postulate is a useful starting point rather than a necessity.

**Concepts:** Weyl's postulate, substratum, fundamental observers, timelike geodesic congruence, perfect fluid

**Key moves**
- Uniqueness of the universe motivates a privileged class of observers.
- Non-intersecting geodesic congruence gives a single velocity at each event.
- Single velocity field justifies a perfect-fluid source.
- Peculiar velocities (<1e-3 c) negligible compared with expansion velocities near c at large distances.

### §24.6 Standard models of relativistic cosmology — p.490 (pdf 505)

The standard models rest on three assumptions: cosmological principle, Weyl's postulate, general relativity. Isotropy forces the substratum geodesics to be orthogonal to the slices (otherwise the tangent picks out a direction), giving comoving spatial coordinates constant along the geodesics and a cosmic time t with ds^2 = dt^2 - g_{alpha beta} dx^alpha dx^beta. Cosmic time defines simultaneity: the world map is the distribution of events on a slice, the world picture is what an observer sees on its past light cone. A small triangle of particles must evolve into a similar triangle with a position-independent magnification, so g_{alpha beta} = S^2(t) h_{alpha beta}(x); S must be real, and each slice must have constant curvature.

**Concepts:** standard models of relativistic cosmology, hypersurface-orthogonal congruence, comoving coordinates, cosmic time, world map, world picture, scale factor

**Key moves**
- Orthogonality of fluid world-lines to slices follows from isotropy.
- Choose t so g_00 = 1 and g_0 alpha = 0.
- Triangle-similarity argument forces separable time dependence (24.19).
- Reality of S preserves the spacelike character of intervals.
- Homogeneity and isotropy of slices requires constant curvature of h.

### §24.7 Spaces of constant curvature — p.492 (pdf 507)

Defines a space of constant curvature by R_abcd = K(g_ac g_bd - g_ad g_bc). For any dimension, isotropy means the linear map G^a_b has no preferred eigenvectors, so G_ab = -K g_ab with K constant by the Bianchi identities, i.e. R_ab = 2K g_ab/(n-2): an Einstein space. In three dimensions the Weyl tensor vanishes, so Riemann is fixed by Ricci and an Einstein 3-space has constant curvature. Assuming spherical symmetry about a point, the Ricci equations for e^lambda dr^2 + r^2 dOmega^2 give e^{-lambda} = 1 - K r^2. An alternative radial coordinate gives a conformally flat form. Combining with §24.6 yields the cosmological line element; rescaling r and S absorbs |K| to leave k = +1, 0, -1 and the Robertson-Walker line element (24.36)/(24.37) and slice geometry (24.38).

**Concepts:** space of constant curvature, Einstein space, isotropy, Weyl tensor, conformally flat metric, Robertson-Walker metric, curvature index k, scale factor

**Key moves**
- Isotropy => G^a_b proportional to delta^a_b.
- Contracted Bianchi identity => K constant.
- Trace: R = 2nK/(n-2); R_ab = 2K g_ab/(n-2) (24.22).
- 3D: substitute R_ab = 2K h_ab, R = 6K into Riemann-from-Ricci (24.24) to get constant curvature (24.23).
- Solve lambda'/r = 2K e^lambda to get e^{-lambda} = 1 - K r^2 (24.28).
- Coordinate change r = rbar/(1 + K rbar^2/4) gives conformally flat form (24.31).
- Normalize: k = K/|K|, r* = |K|^{1/2} r, R = S/|K|^{1/2}.

### §24.8 The geometry of 3-spaces of constant curvature — p.495 (pdf 510)

Case by case: for k = +1, r = sin chi gives R_0^2[d chi^2 + sin^2 chi dOmega^2], embedded as the 3-sphere w^2+x^2+y^2+z^2 = R_0^2 in Euclidean 4-space; geodesic 2-spheres have area 4 pi R_0^2 sin^2 chi (growing then shrinking to zero at the antipode), total volume 2 pi^2 R_0^3, closed topology, spacetime R x S^3. The embedding is a visual aid only; the physical space has no outside. For k = 0 the slice is Euclidean R^3, open topology. For k = -1, r = sinh chi gives a hyperboloid w^2 - x^2 - y^2 - z^2 = R_0^2 in Minkowski 4-space; sphere areas grow like sinh^2 chi, faster than flat, with infinite volume. Other topologies from identifications are possible but not pursued.

**Concepts:** 3-sphere, closed universe, flat space (k=0), hyperbolic 3-space, embedding diagram, radius of the universe, spatial topology of cosmological models

**Key moves**
- Coordinate r = sin chi removes the coordinate singularity at r = 1.
- Explicit embedding (24.41) and constraint (24.42).
- Area A_chi and volume 2 pi^2 R_0^3 integrals.
- Proper radial coordinate r' = R_0 chi: area close to 4 pi r'^2 for small r', then falls below it.
- k = -1 needs a Lorentzian ambient space (24.46)-(24.48).
- Topology: R x S^3 (closed) or R^4 (open), simplest choices only.

### §24.9 Friedmann's equation — p.499 (pdf 514)

Combines the Robertson-Walker metric, a perfect fluid comoving with u^a = (1,0,0,0), and G_ab - Lambda g_ab = 8 pi T_ab to obtain two independent equations. The first-order one is read as an energy integral and the second-order one as an equation of motion. Differentiating and combining yields d(rho R^3)/dt + p d(R^3)/dt = 0, i.e. dE + p dV = 0, equivalent to T^{ab}_{;b} = 0 via the Bianchi identities. Eliminating the curvature term gives Rddot/R = -(4 pi/3)(rho + 3p - Lambda/4 pi); if the bracket is positive and the universe expands now, the concave graph of R(t) must hit zero in the finite past (FRW singularity theorem; with Lambda = 0 the condition is the strong energy condition). With p about 1e-5 to 1e-6 of rho, dust is adopted; integration gives the conserved C = 8 pi R^3 rho/3 and Friedmann's equation Rdot^2 = C/R + Lambda R^2/3 - k, identical to the Newtonian analogue.

**Concepts:** Friedmann equations, perfect fluid, energy conservation in cosmology, contracted Bianchi identities, acceleration equation, strong energy condition, FRW singularity theorem, big bang, dust, Friedmann's equation, Friedmann models

**Key moves**
- Field equations reduce to (24.52) and (24.53); rho and p depend only on t.
- Combine derivative of (24.52) with (24.53) to get (24.54).
- Interpret as first law dE + p dV = 0 with V proportional to R^3.
- Acceleration equation from a linear combination of (24.52) and (24.53).
- Concavity plus present expansion => R = 0 at finite past time.
- Dust: (24.53) integrates to (24.58); constant fixed by (24.52) as (24.59).
- Friedmann's equation (24.60) matches (24.17).

### §24.10 Propagation of light — p.502 (pdf 517)

Places the observer at r = 0 and follows radial null geodesics: dt/R = +/- dr/sqrt(1-kr^2). The comoving coordinate of a source is a fixed function f(r_1) of the integral of dt/R between emission and reception, so two successive signals satisfy dt_0/R(t_0) = dt_1/R(t_1). Since t is proper time for fundamental observers, received periods are stretched by R(t_0)/R(t_1), giving 1+z = R(t_0)/R(t_1): redshift for expansion, blueshift for contraction, which the authors warn is a different thing from the special-relativistic Doppler effect. For nearby sources z ~ Rdot(t_0) r_1, a linear redshift-distance relation.

**Concepts:** radial null geodesic, cosmological redshift, cosmic time, proper time, comoving coordinates

**Key moves**
- Null condition in RW metric (24.61).
- Integral of dt/R equals f(r_1) = arcsin r_1, r_1, arcsinh r_1 (24.62)-(24.63).
- Subtract integrals for two successive rays; take R outside tiny intervals (24.64).
- Proper-time interpretation gives frequency ratio (24.65) and redshift (24.66).
- First-order Taylor expansion gives z ~ H dt and dt ~ R r_1, hence (24.68).

### §24.11 A cosmological definition of distance — p.504 (pdf 519)

Cosmic time allows a clean absolute distance: the geodesic length within a slice between two fundamental particles, R(t) times the comoving radial integral, but it is not measurable. An observational distance from known physical size and measured angular size needs the true size. Instead a luminosity-based distance is used: in an expanding universe the arrival rate of photons and the energy of each photon both drop by 1+z, motivating d_L^2 = E/(4 pi I (1+z)^2). Mentions apparent magnitude (logarithmic in received energy), the problem of unknown intrinsic luminosity, evolution of galaxies, standard candles (Cepheids, Type Ia supernovae) and calibration.

**Concepts:** proper distance, angular-size distance, luminosity distance, apparent magnitude, standard candles, galaxy evolution

**Key moves**
- Absolute distance from dt = d theta = d phi = 0 (24.69).
- Size over angle definition (24.70).
- Two separate (1+z) dimming factors: time dilation of arrivals and photon energy loss.
- Luminosity distance (24.71).
- m = const - 0.4 log E_R; standard candles and calibration problem.

### §24.12 Hubble's law in relativistic cosmology — p.505 (pdf 520)

Light emitted at P_1 is spread at reception time over a sphere through O_0 whose area, by homogeneity, equals that of the sphere of coordinate radius r_1 around O_0, namely 4 pi R_0^2 r_1^2; with the two (1+z) factors this gives d_L = r_1 R(t_0). Combining with z ~ Rdot_0 r_1 yields Hubble's law z ~ H_0 d_L, now deduced rather than assumed. Hubble time T_0 = 1/H_0 is about 1e10 years, and when rho + 3p > Lambda/4 pi the age is less than T_0. Defines the deceleration parameter q = -R Rddot/Rdot^2 (measured q_0 about -0.6 +/- 0.3, accelerating), gives the second-order law d_L = z T_0[1 - (1+q_0)z/2], shows Sandage's 1970 redshift-magnitude data, and derives q = (4 pi rho/3 - Lambda/3)/H^2.

**Concepts:** luminosity distance, Hubble's law, Hubble constant, Hubble time, age of the universe, deceleration parameter, second-order Hubble law, peculiar velocities

**Key moves**
- Homogeneity swaps the sphere centred on P_0 for one centred on O_0.
- Area 4 pi R_0^2 r_1^2 from RW angular part; flux with (1+z)^2 gives (24.72).
- Hubble law (24.74) from (24.68) and (24.72).
- Age bound via Fig. 24.8 when deceleration holds.
- Deceleration parameter (24.76) and its measured sign.
- Second-order expansion (24.78); dependence on q_0 visible in Fig. 24.13.
- Differentiate Friedmann's equation to obtain (24.79).

## Concepts

### cosmology

*definition · introduced* · also: relativistic cosmology

The study of the large-scale structure and dynamics of the universe treated as a single physical system, usually after averaging away galaxies, clusters and smaller structure.

**How introduced:** Opening framing of the chapter; contrasted with laboratory physics because the system is unique and experiments cannot be repeated.

**Where:** DIV §24.1 p.481 (pdf 496); DIV §24.1 p.482 (pdf 497)

### fluid (smoothing) approximation

*technique · developed* · also: smeared-out universe, averaging over large cells

Modelling the matter of the universe as a continuous distribution obtained by averaging over regions large enough (in the book, cells over about 1e9 light years) to contain many clusters, so that homogeneity can hold on average though not in detail.

**How introduced:** Stated at the start as smoothing out all irregularities, and later quantified when explaining in what sense the universe is homogeneous (like a gas).

**Notes:** The book compares this to the homogeneity of a gas.

**Where:** DIV §24.1 p.481 (pdf 496); DIV §24.4 p.487 (pdf 502); DIV §24.4 p.488 (pdf 503)

### uniqueness of the universe

*principle · introduced* · also: cosmology as a unique system

The epistemic point that cosmology studies a single system which cannot be re-run under controlled conditions, so models must rely on extrapolating locally verified physics plus simplicity assumptions.

**How introduced:** Raised in the preview and again as Weyl's motivation for privileged observers.

**Where:** DIV §24.1 p.482 (pdf 497); DIV §24.5 p.489 (pdf 504)

### hot big bang model

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: big bang cosmology

The model in which the universe expanded from an extremely hot, dense early state (a singularity in classical models); as it cooled, particles, light nuclei and atoms formed, then stars and galaxies.

### Olbers' paradox

*phenomenon · developed* · also: dark night sky paradox

The contradiction between the dark night sky and the prediction that a static, infinite, eternal universe uniformly filled with stars would have a sky everywhere as bright as the surface of a star.

**How introduced:** Motivating puzzle of §24.2: equal-intensity shells argument, then occultation, thermodynamic equilibrium and line-of-sight versions, then attempted and accepted resolutions.

**Prerequisites:** inverse-square law

$$
\frac{(4\pi r^2 dr)\ell}{4\pi r^2}=\ell\,dr
$$

**Notes:** The book gives two escapes. (i) A young universe in which stars switched on recently: the book notes that making this short enough can conflict with the ages of the oldest stars. (ii) Expansion, where redshift drains energy from distant light; the book calls this the accepted resolution. Modern analyses (e.g. Harrison) find that finite age and finite stellar lifetimes are the dominant factor.

**Where:** DIV §24.2 p.483 (pdf 498); DIV §24.2 p.484 (pdf 499)

### scale factor

### cosmological principle

*principle · core* · also: Copernican cosmological principle

### homogeneity

*definition · core* · also: spatial homogeneity

A spatial slice is called homogeneous when its isometry group acts transitively: for any two of its points some symmetry of the slice's metric sends one to the other, so no location is geometrically special.

### isotropy

### Weyl's postulate

*principle · core* · also: substratum postulate

Robertson's version of Weyl's assumption: the world-lines of the smoothed-out galaxies form a family of timelike geodesics, one through each event, which fan out from a common origin in the past (possibly infinitely far back) and meet nowhere else except perhaps at a final singular point. **How introduced:** Motivated by how a covariant theory can describe a unique universe; stated in Robertson's form as a highlighted postulate.

**Prerequisites:** timelike geodesic congruence, substratum

$$

**Where:** DIV §24.7 p.493 (pdf 508); DIV §24.9 p.500 (pdf 515)

### acceleration equation

*law · developed* · also: Raychaudhuri equation for FRW

The combination of the two Friedmann equations from which both Rdot and the curvature index k drop out: Rddot/R = -(4 pi/3)(rho + 3p - Lambda/4 pi). Density and pressure (through rho + 3p) slow the expansion, while a positive Lambda speeds it up.

**How introduced:** Obtained by a linear combination of (24.52) and (24.53) to motivate the singularity theorem.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann equations

$$
\frac{\ddot R}{R}=-\frac{4\pi}{3}\left(\rho+3p-\frac{\Lambda}{4\pi}\right)
$$

**Notes:** The book's instruction for forming the combination has the roles swapped (see gaps). The reading copy also lost overdots on this page ('eliminate R', 'so that R < 0'); the render of pdf p.500 shows Rdot and Rddot.

**Where:** DIV §24.9 p.500 (pdf 515)

### strong energy condition

*principle · mention*

For a perfect fluid, the requirement rho + 3p >= 0 (together with rho + p >= 0), meaning gravity is attractive for timelike congruences.

**How introduced:** Identified as the Lambda = 0 version of the condition for guaranteed deceleration.

**Prerequisites:** perfect fluid

$$
\rho+3p>0
$$

**Where:** DIV §24.9 p.501 (pdf 516)

### FRW singularity theorem

*theorem · developed* · also: big bang theorem

If Lambda = 0 and rho + 3p > 0 (more generally rho + 3p > Lambda/4 pi) and the universe is expanding today, then R(t) vanished at a finite time in the past: there was an initial big bang singularity.

**How introduced:** Proved graphically: Rddot < 0 means the graph of R lies below each tangent; the present tangent crosses the t axis at finite time, so R must reach zero even sooner (Fig. 24.8).

**Prerequisites:** acceleration equation, strong energy condition

**Where:** DIV §24.9 p.500 (pdf 515); DIV §24.9 p.501 (pdf 516)

### dust

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: pressureless matter, incoherent matter

A perfect fluid with p = 0, appropriate for the present universe where pressure is about 1e-5 to 1e-6 of the matter energy density.

**How introduced:** Adopted in §24.9 after estimating the pressure-to-density ratio, turning (24.54) into mass conservation.

**Prerequisites:** perfect fluid

$$
p=0
$$

$$
\rho R^3=\text{const}
$$

**Where:** DIV §24.9 p.501 (pdf 516)

### Friedmann's equation

*law · core* · also: Friedmann equation for dust

For pressure-free matter, Rdot^2 = C/R + Lambda R^2/3 - k with C = (8 pi/3) rho R^3 constant; its solutions are the Friedmann models.

**How introduced:** Obtained by integrating the second Friedmann equation with p = 0 and fixing the constant from the first; noted to coincide with the Newtonian result (24.17).

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann equations, dust

$$
\dot R^2=\frac{C}{R}+\frac13\Lambda R^2-k
$$

$$
C=\frac83\pi R^3\rho
$$

**Where:** DIV §24.9 p.501 (pdf 516); DIV §24.3 p.487 (pdf 502); DIV §24.12 p.507 (pdf 522)

### Friedmann models

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: FRW models, Friedmann-Robertson-Walker models, classical cosmological models

The family of homogeneous isotropic cosmologies obtained by solving Friedmann's equation for different k, Lambda and C.

**How introduced:** Named in the preview and at the end of §24.9; solved in Chapter 25.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann's equation

**Where:** DIV §24.1 p.481 (pdf 496); DIV §24.9 p.501 (pdf 516)

### big bang

*phenomenon · developed* · also: initial singularity

The finite-past event at which the scale factor vanishes and density diverges in Friedmann models satisfying the singularity theorem conditions.

**How introduced:** Qualitative in the preview; made precise via the FRW singularity theorem.

**Prerequisites:** FRW singularity theorem

**Where:** DIV §24.1 p.482 (pdf 497); DIV §24.9 p.501 (pdf 516)

### radial null geodesic

*mathematical-object · developed*

A light ray moving at fixed theta and phi; in the Robertson-Walker metric it obeys dt/R(t) = +/- dr/sqrt(1-kr^2).

**How introduced:** Set ds = d theta = d phi = 0 with the observer at the origin (allowed by homogeneity).

**Prerequisites:** Robertson-Walker metric

$$
\frac{dt}{R(t)}=\pm\frac{dr}{(1-kr^2)^{1/2}}
$$

**Where:** DIV §24.10 p.502 (pdf 517)

### comoving distance function f(r)

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: chi coordinate distance

The dimensionless comoving radial distance f(r_1) = arcsin r_1, r_1, or arcsinh r_1 (for k = +1, 0, -1), equal to the integral of dt/R along a radial light ray from the source to the observer.

**How introduced:** Result of integrating the null condition (24.62)-(24.63).

**Prerequisites:** radial null geodesic

$$
\int_{t_1}^{t_0}\frac{dt}{R}=f(r_1)
$$

**Where:** DIV §24.10 p.502 (pdf 517)

### cosmological redshift

*phenomenon · core* · also: expansion redshift, Doppler shift (book's loose term)

The fractional decrease in frequency of light from a comoving source, 1+z = R(t_0)/R(t_1), due to the change in scale factor between emission and reception; a blueshift in a contracting universe.

**How introduced:** Derived from two successive null rays and the proper-time meaning of t (Fig. 24.10); distinguished from the special-relativistic Doppler effect.

**Prerequisites:** radial null geodesic, cosmic time

$$
1+z=\frac{\nu_1}{\nu_0}=\frac{R(t_0)}{R(t_1)}
$$

**Where:** DIV §24.2 p.484 (pdf 499); DIV §24.10 p.502 (pdf 517); DIV §24.10 p.503 (pdf 518)

### proper distance

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: absolute distance d_A, distance at fixed cosmic time

The geodesic length within a slice of constant cosmic time between two fundamental particles: R(t) times the comoving radial integral.

**How introduced:** Defined as the mathematically natural distance and immediately set aside as unmeasurable (Fig. 24.11).

**Prerequisites:** cosmic time, comoving coordinates

$$
d_A=R(t)\int_0^{r_1}\frac{dr}{(1-kr^2)^{1/2}}
$$

**Notes:** The book's d_A means absolute distance, not the angular-diameter distance usually denoted d_A today.

**Where:** DIV §24.11 p.504 (pdf 519)

### angular-size distance

*physical-quantity · mention* · also: observational distance d_O, angular diameter distance

A distance defined by the ratio of an object's true linear diameter to its observed angular diameter.

**How introduced:** Offered as an operational alternative but rejected because true sizes are unknown.

$$
d_O=\alpha/\beta
$$

**Where:** DIV §24.11 p.504 (pdf 519)

### luminosity distance

*physical-quantity · core* · also: d_L

A distance inferred from how bright a source of known power appears; in the book it is defined with the redshift losses divided out, d_L^2 = E/(4 pi I (1+z)^2), which in a Robertson-Walker universe gives d_L = r_1 R(t_0).

**How introduced:** Motivated by counting two (1+z) losses (arrival rate and photon energy), then computed from the area of the sphere over which light spreads by the time of reception (Fig. 24.12).

**Prerequisites:** cosmological redshift, Robertson-Walker metric

$$
d_L^2=\frac{E}{4\pi I(1+z)^2}
$$

$$
d_L=r_1R(t_0)
$$

**Notes:** Modern convention defines d_L^2 = E/(4 pi I), giving d_L = (1+z) r_1 R(t_0); the book's d_L equals the modern transverse comoving distance.

**Where:** DIV §24.11 p.504 (pdf 519); DIV §24.11 p.505 (pdf 520); DIV §24.12 p.506 (pdf 521)

### apparent magnitude

*convention · mention* · also: m

A logarithmic brightness scale for received energy, m = constant - 0.4 log10 E_R (brighter objects have smaller m).

**How introduced:** Mentioned loosely as the astronomers' unit; used on the axis of Fig. 24.13.

$$
m=\text{const}-0.4\log_{10}E_R
$$

**Where:** DIV §24.11 p.505 (pdf 520); DIV §24.12 p.508 (pdf 523)

### standard candles

*technique · mention*

Classes of sources believed to have nearly the same intrinsic luminosity at all places and times (e.g. Cepheid variables, Type Ia supernovae), allowing luminosity distances once calibrated.

**How introduced:** Introduced after noting galaxies evolve, so they are unreliable as equal-luminosity sources.

**Prerequisites:** luminosity distance

**Where:** DIV §24.11 p.505 (pdf 520)

### galaxy evolution

*phenomenon · mention* · also: evolutionary universe

Distant galaxies are seen when younger, so their average luminosity need not equal that of nearby galaxies, biasing distance estimates that assume equal luminosity.

**How introduced:** Raised as a flaw in treating all galaxies as equally luminous.

**Prerequisites:** lookback time

**Where:** DIV §24.11 p.505 (pdf 520)

### Hubble time

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: T_0 = 1/H_0

The reciprocal of the Hubble constant, a characteristic expansion timescale; for a decelerating universe it is an upper bound on the age.

**How introduced:** Defined after Hubble's law; the book quotes about 1e10 years within a factor of 2.

**Prerequisites:** Hubble constant

$$
T=1/H
$$

$$
T_0\approx10^{10}\,\text{yr}
$$

**Where:** DIV §24.12 p.506 (pdf 521); DIV §24.12 p.507 (pdf 522)

### age of the universe

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: time since the big bang

The cosmic time elapsed since R = 0; if rho + 3p > Lambda/4 pi throughout, it is less than the Hubble time.

**How introduced:** Read off Fig. 24.8: the tangent at t_0 meets the axis a Hubble time earlier, the curve meets it later.

**Prerequisites:** FRW singularity theorem, Hubble time

**Where:** DIV §24.1 p.482 (pdf 497); DIV §24.12 p.507 (pdf 522)

### deceleration parameter

*physical-quantity · core* · also: q

The dimensionless quantity q = -R Rddot/Rdot^2: positive when expansion slows, negative when it speeds up; related to density and Lambda by q = (4 pi rho/3 - Lambda/3)/H^2 for dust.

**How introduced:** Defined in §24.12, given observational value about -0.6 +/- 0.3, used in the second-order Hubble law, and linked to Friedmann's equation.

**Prerequisites:** Hubble parameter, Friedmann's equation

$$
q=-\frac{R\ddot R}{\dot R^2}
$$

$$
q=\frac{\frac43\pi\rho-\frac13\Lambda}{H^2}
$$

**Where:** DIV §24.12 p.507 (pdf 522); DIV §24.12 p.508 (pdf 523)

### second-order Hubble law

*law · developed* · also: redshift-distance relation to O(z^2)

Expanding the scale factor to second order around t_0 gives d_L = z T_0 [1 - (1+q_0) z/2 + ...] (with the book's d_L), so deviations from linearity at larger z measure q_0.

**How introduced:** Stated with derivation left to Exercise 24.17 (trapezium rule); compared with Sandage's data (Fig. 24.13).

**Prerequisites:** deceleration parameter, luminosity distance

$$
d_L=zT_0[1-\tfrac12(1+q_0)z+\cdots]
$$

**Notes:** With the modern luminosity distance the bracket is [1 + (1-q_0)z/2].

**Where:** DIV §24.12 p.507 (pdf 522); DIV p.510 (pdf 525)

### peculiar velocities

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: random motions of galaxies

Motions of galaxies relative to the smoothed Hubble flow, below about 1e-3 c, which dominate the observed redshift of very nearby galaxies.

**How introduced:** Neglected in Weyl's postulate; revisited as the reason Hubble's law fails for the closest objects.

**Prerequisites:** substratum

**Where:** DIV §24.5 p.490 (pdf 505); DIV §24.12 p.507 (pdf 522)

### dominant energy condition

*principle · mention* · also: energy conditions (12.64)

The requirement from Chapter 12 that the cosmic fluid be physically reasonable: energy density non-negative and energy flux never spacelike, which for a perfect fluid means rho >= |p|.

**How introduced:** Imposed in passing, right after the two Friedmann equations, as an extra condition that rho and p must satisfy; the stronger-looking condition rho + 3p > 0 used for the singularity theorem is then identified separately as the strong energy condition.

**Prerequisites:** perfect fluid

$$
\rho\ge|p|
$$

**Notes:** Dominant and strong conditions are logically independent; learners often conflate them.

**Where:** DIV §24.9 p.500 (pdf 515)

## Key equations

### (24.1) Olbers shell contribution · central · DIV §24.2 p.483 (pdf 498)

$$
\frac{(4\pi r^2\,dr)\,\ell}{4\pi r^2}=\ell\,dr
$$

A thin shell of stars contributes an intensity at its centre independent of its radius, so an infinite static universe gives a divergent (or, with occultation, stellar-surface) brightness.

**Symbols:** \ell: number density of stars times mean luminosity; r, dr: shell radius and thickness

### (24.2) radial motion · derivation-step · DIV §24.3 p.484 (pdf 499)

$$
\mathbf r_i(t)=r_i(t)\,\hat{\mathbf r}
$$

Spherical symmetry about O forces purely radial motion of each galaxy.

**Symbols:** \mathbf r_i: position of galaxy i

### (24.3) Newtonian potential energy · derivation-step · DIV §24.3 p.485 (pdf 500)

$$
V=-G\sum_{i<j}\frac{m_im_j}{|\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j|}
$$

Total gravitational potential energy, each pair counted once.

### (24.4) cosmological force · supporting · DIV §24.3 p.485 (pdf 500)

$$
\mathbf F_i=\tfrac13\Lambda m_i\mathbf r_i
$$

Newtonian stand-in for the cosmological term: a force proportional to distance from O, repulsive for Lambda>0.

**Symbols:** \Lambda: cosmological constant

### (24.5) cosmological potential energy · derivation-step · DIV §24.3 p.485 (pdf 500)

$$
V_c=-\tfrac16\Lambda\sum_i m_ir_i^2
$$

Potential whose negative gradient gives (24.4).

### (24.6) total energy · supporting · DIV §24.3 p.485 (pdf 500)

$$
E=\tfrac12\sum_i m_i\dot r_i^2-G\sum_{i<j}\frac{m_im_j}{|\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_j|}-\tfrac16\Lambda\sum_i m_ir_i^2
$$

Conserved total energy of the discrete Newtonian model.

### (24.7) uniform scaling · central · DIV §24.3 p.485 (pdf 500)

$$
r_i(t)=S(t)\,r_i(t_0)
$$

The only motion compatible with homogeneity and isotropy is a common rescaling of all distances.

**Symbols:** S(t): scale factor, S(t_0)=1

### (24.8) · derivation-step · DIV §24.3 p.485 (pdf 500)

$$
\dot r_i(t)=\dot S(t)r_i(t_0)=\frac{\dot S}{S}r_i(t)
$$

Velocity of each galaxy follows from differentiating the scaling law.

### (24.9) Hubble parameter · central · DIV §24.3 p.485 (pdf 500)

$$
H(t)=\dot S(t)/S(t)
$$

Fractional expansion rate, units of inverse time.

### (24.10) Hubble's law (Newtonian) · central · DIV §24.3 p.485 (pdf 500)

$$
\dot r_i(t)=H(t)\,r_i(t)
$$

Recession speed proportional to distance at a given epoch.

### (24.11) Newtonian cosmological energy equation · central · DIV §24.3 p.486 (pdf 501)

$$
E=A\dot S^2-\frac{B}{S}-DS^2
$$

Energy conservation in terms of the scale factor alone; with Lambda=0, expansion lowers |potential| so kinetic term must fall: deceleration.

**Symbols:** A,B,D>0 constants from (24.12)-(24.14)

### (24.12)-(24.14) coefficients · derivation-step · DIV §24.3 p.486 (pdf 501)

$$
A=\tfrac12\sum_i m_i r_i(t_0)^2,\quad B=G\sum_{i<j}\frac{m_im_j}{|\mathbf r_i(t_0)-\mathbf r_j(t_0)|},\quad D=\tfrac16\Lambda\sum_i m_ir_i(t_0)^2=\tfrac13\Lambda A
$$

Constants fixed by the configuration at the reference epoch; A is half the moment of inertia about O.

### (24.15) · derivation-step · DIV §24.3 p.486 (pdf 501)

$$
\dot S^2=\frac{B}{A}\frac1S+\tfrac13\Lambda S^2+\frac{E}{A}
$$

Energy equation solved for the expansion rate.

### (24.16) · derivation-step · DIV §24.3 p.486 (pdf 501)

$$
R(t)=\mu S(t)
$$

Rescaling the scale factor by a constant.

### (24.17) Newtonian Friedmann equation · central · DIV §24.3 p.487 (pdf 502)

$$
\dot R^2=\frac{C}{R}+\tfrac13\Lambda R^2-k,\qquad C=B\mu^3/A,\; k=-\mu^2E/A
$$

Newtonian model yields exactly the form of the relativistic Friedmann equation; k is minus the sign of the total energy.

### (24.18) normalization · derivation-step · DIV §24.3 p.487 (pdf 502)

$$
\mu^2=A/|E|\quad(E\neq0)
$$

Choice of mu making k = +1 or -1; mu arbitrary if E = 0 (k = 0).

### Kasner metric · supporting · DIV §24.4 p.488 (pdf 503)

$$
ds^2=dt^2-t^{2p_1}dx^2-t^{2p_2}dy^2-t^{2p_3}dz^2,\qquad p_1+p_2+p_3=p_1^2+p_2^2+p_3^2=1
$$

Vacuum Bianchi I anisotropic cosmology with one free exponent; singular at t = 0 with cigar or pancake behaviour.

### synchronous comoving form · supporting · DIV §24.6 p.491 (pdf 506)

$$
ds^2=dt^2-g_{\alpha\beta}(t,x)\,dx^\alpha dx^\beta
$$

Consequence of orthogonal geodesic congruence: cosmic time with no cross terms.

**Symbols:** Greek indices 1-3

### (24.19) separable spatial metric · central · DIV §24.6 p.491 (pdf 506)

$$
g_{\alpha\beta}=S^2(t)\,h_{\alpha\beta}(x^\gamma)
$$

Time enters the spatial metric only as an overall magnification, required by similar-triangle evolution.

### (24.20) constant curvature · central · DIV §24.7 p.492 (pdf 507)

$$
R_{abcd}=K(g_{ac}g_{bd}-g_{ad}g_{bc})
$$

Defining property of a space of constant curvature K.

### (24.21) isotropy condition on Einstein tensor · central · DIV §24.7 p.493 (pdf 508)

$$
G^a{}_b=-K\delta^a{}_b\;\Rightarrow\;G_{ab}=-Kg_{ab}
$$

No preferred eigen-directions forces G to be a multiple of the metric; Bianchi identities make K constant.

### (24.22) Einstein space · central · DIV §24.7 p.493 (pdf 508)

$$
R_{ab}=\frac{2K}{n-2}g_{ab},\qquad R=\frac{2nK}{n-2}
$$

Isotropy about every point implies an Einstein space in any dimension n>2.

### (24.23) 3D constant curvature · central · DIV §24.7 p.493 (pdf 508)

$$
R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}=K(h_{\alpha\gamma}h_{\beta\delta}-h_{\alpha\delta}h_{\beta\gamma})
$$

In 3D an Einstein space has constant curvature.

### (24.24) 3D Riemann from Ricci · supporting · DIV §24.7 p.493 (pdf 508)

$$
R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}=h_{\alpha\gamma}R_{\beta\delta}+h_{\beta\delta}R_{\alpha\gamma}-h_{\alpha\delta}R_{\beta\gamma}-h_{\beta\gamma}R_{\alpha\delta}+\tfrac12(h_{\alpha\delta}h_{\beta\gamma}-h_{\alpha\gamma}h_{\beta\delta})R
$$

Vanishing Weyl tensor in 3D; substituting R_{alpha beta} = 2K h, R = 6K gives (24.23).

### (24.25) spherically symmetric 3-metric · derivation-step · DIV §24.7 p.493 (pdf 508)

$$
d\sigma^2=e^{\lambda(r)}dr^2+r^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)
$$

Ansatz justified by isotropy about the origin.

### (24.26) Ricci components · derivation-step · DIV §24.7 p.494 (pdf 509)

$$
R^{(3)}_{11}=\lambda'/r,\qquad R^{(3)}_{22}=1+\tfrac12re^{-\lambda}\lambda'-e^{-\lambda},\qquad R^{(3)}_{33}=\sin^2\theta\,R^{(3)}_{22}
$$

Non-zero Ricci components of the ansatz (24.25), written here in corrected form. The printed line (checked on the page render) lists R_22 as cosec^2 theta and attaches the bracketed expression to R_33, a misprint; (24.27) only makes sense if that expression is the theta-theta component, with the phi-phi one carrying an extra sin^2 theta.

### (24.27) · derivation-step · DIV §24.7 p.494 (pdf 509)

$$
\lambda'/r=2Ke^{\lambda},\qquad 1+\tfrac12re^{-\lambda}\lambda'-e^{-\lambda}=2Kr^2
$$

Einstein-space condition R_{alpha beta} = 2K h_{alpha beta} applied to (24.26).

### (24.28) · derivation-step · DIV §24.7 p.494 (pdf 509)

$$
e^{-\lambda}=1-Kr^2
$$

Solution of (24.27).

### (24.29) constant-curvature 3-metric · central · DIV §24.7 p.494 (pdf 509)

$$
d\sigma^2=\frac{dr^2}{1-Kr^2}+r^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)
$$

Spatial geometry of homogeneous isotropic slices.

### (24.30)-(24.31) conformally flat form · supporting · DIV §24.7 p.494 (pdf 509)

$$
r=\frac{\bar r}{1+\frac14K\bar r^2},\qquad d\sigma^2=\frac{d\bar r^2+\bar r^2d\Omega^2}{(1+\frac14K\bar r^2)^2}

Change of radial coordinate displaying the 3-metric as conformal to flat space.

### (24.32)-(24.33) · supporting · DIV §24.7 p.494 (pdf 509)

$$
ds^2=dt^2-S^2(t)\left(\frac{dr^2}{1-Kr^2}+r^2d\Omega^2\right)=dt^2-S^2(t)\frac{d\bar r^2+\bar r^2d\Omega^2}{(1+\frac14K\bar r^2)^2}

Cosmological line element before normalizing K.

### (24.34)-(24.35) rescaled radial coordinate · derivation-step · DIV §24.7 p.494 (pdf 509)

$$
r^*=|K|^{1/2}r,\qquad ds^2=dt^2-\frac{S^2}{|K|}\left(\frac{dr^{*2}}{1-kr^{*2}}+r^{*2}d\Omega^2\right)

Absorbing |K| into the coordinate leaves only its sign k.

**Symbols:** k=K/|K|

### normalized scale factor · derivation-step · DIV §24.7 p.495 (pdf 510)

$$
R(t)=S(t)/|K|^{1/2}\ (K\neq0),\qquad R(t)=S(t)\ (K=0)
$$

Definition of R(t).

### (24.36) Robertson-Walker line element (unbarred) · central · DIV §24.7 p.495 (pdf 510)

$$
ds^2=dt^2-R^2(t)\left(\frac{dr^2}{1-kr^2}+r^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)\right)
$$

The standard metric of homogeneous isotropic cosmology, k = +1, 0, -1.

**Symbols:** r dimensionless comoving radial coordinate; R has dimensions of length

### (24.37) Robertson-Walker line element (barred) · central · DIV §24.7 p.495 (pdf 510)

$$
ds^2=dt^2-R^2(t)\frac{d\bar r^2+\bar r^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)}{(1+\frac14k\bar r^2)^2}
$$

Conformally flat form, the one the book names Robertson-Walker; repeated as (24.49).

### (24.38) slice geometry · supporting · DIV §24.7 p.495 (pdf 510)

$$
d\sigma^2=R_0^2\left(\frac{dr^2}{1-kr^2}+r^2d\Omega^2\right)
$$

Geometry of the slice t = t_0.

**Symbols:** R_0=R(t_0)

### (24.39)-(24.40) 3-sphere metric · central · DIV §24.8 p.495 (pdf 510)

$$
r=\sin\chi,\qquad d\sigma^2=R_0^2[d\chi^2+\sin^2\chi(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)]
$$

k = +1 slice in hyperspherical coordinates, regular at r = 1.

**Symbols:** 0\le\chi\le\pi

### (24.41)-(24.42) 3-sphere embedding · supporting · DIV §24.8 p.496 (pdf 511)

$$
(w,x,y,z)=R_0(\cos\chi,\ \sin\chi\sin\theta\cos\phi,\ \sin\chi\sin\theta\sin\phi,\ \sin\chi\cos\theta),\qquad w^2+x^2+y^2+z^2=R_0^2
$$

The k = +1 slice is a round 3-sphere in Euclidean 4-space.

### geodesic 2-sphere area (k=+1) · supporting · DIV §24.8 p.496 (pdf 511)

A_\chi=4\pi R_0^2\sin^2\chi=4\pi R_0^2\sin^2(r'/R_0)
$$

Area of spheres at proper radius r' = R_0 chi grows, peaks at the equator, returns to zero at the antipode.

### (24.43) volume of the 3-sphere · central · DIV §24.8 p.496 (pdf 511)

V=\int_0^\pi\!\int_0^\pi\!\int_0^{2\pi}R_0^3\sin^2\chi\sin\theta\,d\phi\,d\theta\,d\chi=2\pi^2R_0^3

Finite spatial volume of the closed universe; motivates calling R the radius of the universe.

### flat slice · supporting · DIV §24.8 p.497 (pdf 512)

### (24.44)-(24.45) hyperbolic 3-metric · central · DIV §24.8 p.498 (pdf 513)

d\sigma^2=-dw^2+dx^2+dy^2+dz^2,\quad (w,x,y,z)=R_0(\cosh\chi,\sinh\chi\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sinh\chi\sin\theta\sin\phi,\sinh\chi\cos\theta),\quad w^2-x^2-y^2-z^2=R_0^2

The k = -1 slice is one sheet of a hyperboloid in Minkowski 4-space (signature +2 ambient metric).

### geodesic 2-sphere area (k=-1) · supporting · DIV §24.8 p.499 (pdf 514)

A_\chi=4\pi R_0^2\sinh^2\chi
$$

Area grows exponentially for large chi, faster than in flat space; volume is infinite.

### (24.50) perfect fluid · supporting · DIV §24.9 p.499 (pdf 514)

$$
T_{ab}=(\rho+p)u_au_b-pg_{ab},\qquad u^a\overset{*}{=}(1,0,0,0)

Source demanded by Weyl's postulate, comoving in cosmic-time coordinates.

### (24.51) field equations with Lambda · supporting · DIV §24.9 p.499 (pdf 514)

$$
G_{ab}-\Lambda g_{ab}=8\pi T_{ab}
$$

Einstein's equations in relativistic units with cosmological term.

### (24.52) first Friedmann equation · central · DIV §24.9 p.499 (pdf 514)

$$
3\frac{\dot R^2+k}{R^2}-\Lambda=8\pi\rho
$$

Energy-type constraint relating expansion rate, curvature, Lambda and density.

### (24.53) second Friedmann equation · central · DIV §24.9 p.499 (pdf 514)

$$
\frac{2R\ddot R+\dot R^2+k}{R^2}-\Lambda=-8\pi p
$$

Equation of motion for the scale factor involving pressure.

### intermediate continuity step · derivation-step · DIV §24.9 p.500 (pdf 515)

$$
\dot\rho+3p\frac{\dot R}{R}=-\frac{3}{8\pi}\frac{\dot R}{R}\left(\frac{3\dot R^2}{R^2}+\frac{3k}{R^2}-\Lambda\right)=-3\rho\frac{\dot R}{R}
$$

Time derivative of (24.52) combined with (24.53).

### (24.54) continuity equation · central · DIV §24.9 p.500 (pdf 515)

$$
\frac{d}{dt}(\rho R^3)+p\frac{d}{dt}(R^3)=0
$$

Energy conservation for a comoving volume.

### (24.55) first law · central · DIV §24.9 p.500 (pdf 515)

$$
dE+p\,dV=0
$$

With E = rho V and V proportional to R^3: pressure does work in the expansion.

### (24.56) conservation law · supporting · DIV §24.9 p.500 (pdf 515)

$$
T^{ab}{}_{;b}=0
$$

Same content as (24.55), guaranteed by the contracted Bianchi identities.

### acceleration equation · central · DIV §24.9 p.500 (pdf 515)

$$
\frac{\ddot R}{R}=-\frac{4\pi}{3}\left(\rho+3p-\frac{\Lambda}{4\pi}\right)
$$

Deceleration from rho + 3p, acceleration from positive Lambda. Obtained as 3 x (24.53) minus (24.52); the text's verbal recipe reverses the roles.

### (24.57) dust · supporting · DIV §24.9 p.501 (pdf 516)

$$
p=0
$$

Present-epoch approximation (p/rho about 1e-5 to 1e-6).

### (24.58)-(24.59) first integral for dust · supporting · DIV §24.9 p.501 (pdf 516)

$$
R(\dot R^2+k)-\tfrac13\Lambda R^3=C,\qquad C=\tfrac83\pi R^3\rho
$$

Integral of (24.53) with p = 0; C is twice the Euclidean mass of a sphere of radius R.

### (24.60) Friedmann's equation · central · DIV §24.9 p.501 (pdf 516)

$$
\dot R^2=\frac{C}{R}+\tfrac13\Lambda R^2-k
$$

Governing ODE for the scale factor of pressure-free models; identical in form to (24.17).

### (24.61) radial null geodesic · supporting · DIV §24.10 p.502 (pdf 517)

$$
\frac{dt}{R(t)}=\pm\frac{dr}{(1-kr^2)^{1/2}}
$$

Light-ray equation in RW, plus for outgoing, minus for incoming.

### (24.62)-(24.63) comoving distance of source · central · DIV §24.10 p.502 (pdf 517)

$$
\int_{t_1}^{t_0}\frac{dt}{R(t)}=\int_0^{r_1}\frac{dr}{(1-kr^2)^{1/2}}=f(r_1)=\begin{cases}\sin^{-1}r_1&k=+1\\ r_1&k=0\\ \sinh^{-1}r_1&k=-1\end{cases}
$$

The conformal-time interval of a ray equals a fixed function of the source's comoving coordinate.

### (24.64) · derivation-step · DIV §24.10 p.503 (pdf 518)

$$
\frac{dt_0}{R(t_0)}=\frac{dt_1}{R(t_1)}
$$

Successive signals: received interval stretched by the expansion factor.

### (24.65)-(24.66) cosmological redshift · central · DIV §24.10 p.503 (pdf 518)

$$
\frac{\nu_1}{\nu_0}=\frac{R(t_0)}{R(t_1)},\qquad z:=\frac{\nu_1-\nu_0}{\nu_0},\qquad 1+z=\frac{R(t_0)}{R(t_1)}
$$

Redshift measures the ratio of scale factors at reception and emission.

**Symbols:** \nu_1: emitted frequency; \nu_0: observed frequency

### (24.67) · derivation-step · DIV §24.10 p.503 (pdf 518)

$$
1+z=\frac{R(t_0)}{R(t_0-dt)}\simeq1+\frac{\dot R(t_0)}{R(t_0)}dt
$$

First-order redshift for nearby sources.

### (24.68) linear redshift-distance relation · supporting · DIV §24.10 p.504 (pdf 519)

$$
z\simeq\dot R(t_0)\,r_1
$$

Nearby: redshift proportional to comoving coordinate distance.

### (24.69) absolute distance · supporting · DIV §24.11 p.504 (pdf 519)

$$
d_A=R(t)\int_0^{r_1}\frac{dr}{(1-kr^2)^{1/2}}
$$

Proper distance at fixed cosmic time.

### (24.70) observational (angular-size) distance · supporting · DIV §24.11 p.504 (pdf 519)

$$
d_O=\alpha/\beta
$$

True diameter over angular diameter.

### (24.71) luminosity distance (book's definition) · central · DIV §24.11 p.505 (pdf 520)

$$
d_L^2=\frac{E}{4\pi I(1+z)^2}
$$

Flux-based distance with both redshift dimming factors removed.

**Symbols:** E: emitted power; I: received flux

### apparent magnitude · supporting · DIV §24.11 p.505 (pdf 520)

$$
m=\text{const}-0.4\log_{10}E_R
$$

Logarithmic brightness scale.

### received flux in RW · derivation-step · DIV §24.12 p.506 (pdf 521)

$$
I=\frac{E}{4\pi R^2(t_0)r_1^2(1+z)^2}
$$

Energy spread over a sphere of area 4 pi R_0^2 r_1^2, reduced twice by (1+z).

### (24.72) luminosity distance in RW · central · DIV §24.12 p.506 (pdf 521)

$$
d_L=r_1R(t_0)
$$

With the book's definition, d_L equals present-day areal radius of the sphere through the observer.

### (24.73)-(24.74) relativistic Hubble law · central · DIV §24.12 p.506 (pdf 521)

$$
H(t)=\frac{\dot R}{R},\qquad z\simeq H(t_0)\,d_L
$$

Hubble's law deduced to first order.

### (24.75) Hubble time · supporting · DIV §24.12 p.506 (pdf 521)

$$
T_0=1/H_0\approx10^{10}\ \text{yr}
$$

Book's quoted value, uncertain by a factor of 2.

### (24.76) deceleration parameter · central · DIV §24.12 p.507 (pdf 522)

$$
q(t)=-\frac{R\ddot R}{\dot R^2}
$$

Dimensionless measure of deceleration.

### (24.77) · supporting · DIV §24.12 p.507 (pdf 522)

$$
q_0=-0.6\pm0.3
$$

Quoted observational value: the expansion is accelerating.

### (24.78) second-order Hubble law · central · DIV §24.12 p.507 (pdf 522)

$$
d_L=zT_0\left[1-\tfrac12(1+q_0)z+\cdots\right]
$$

Curvature of the redshift-distance relation measures q_0 (book's d_L; modern d_L has 1 + (1-q_0)z/2).

### (24.79) q from dynamics · central · DIV §24.12 p.507 (pdf 522)

$$
q=\frac{\frac43\pi\rho-\frac13\Lambda}{H^2}
$$

Links deceleration, expansion rate, mean density and Lambda for dust models.

## Worked examples

### Example · intro · DIV §24.2 p.483 (pdf 498)

**Problem:** Estimate the sky brightness at a point in a static, infinite, eternal, Euclidean universe uniformly filled with stars.

**Method:** Divide space into concentric shells, compute each shell's contribution as luminosity over area, sum over all shells, then account for occultation.

**Key insight:** Every shell contributes the same amount, so distance does not dim the sky in aggregate; only finite age, finite extent of luminous history, or redshift can.

**Result:** Naive sum \int_0^\infty \ell\,dr diverges; with blocking, sky brightness equals a stellar surface.

**Concepts:** Olbers' paradox

### Example · standard · DIV §24.3 p.485 (pdf 500)

**Problem:** Derive the scale-factor equation of a finite Newtonian universe of point galaxies with a cosmological force.

**Method:** Impose spherical symmetry (radial motion), deduce uniform scaling, substitute into total energy, collect configuration-dependent sums into constants, and rescale S by mu^2 = A/|E|.

**Key insight:** Homogeneity reduces n-body dynamics to a single function S(t); energy conservation then plays the role of Friedmann's equation, with k = -sign(E).

**Result:** \dot R^2 = C/R + \Lambda R^2/3 - k, k \in \{+1,0,-1\}

**Concepts:** Newtonian cosmology, scale factor, Hubble's law, curvature index k

### Example · challenging · DIV §24.7 p.492 (pdf 507)

**Problem:** Show that a space isotropic about every point has constant curvature in three dimensions.

**Method:** Treat G^a_b as a linear map; isotropy forbids preferred eigenvectors so G^a_b = -K delta; Bianchi identity makes K constant; trace to get R_ab = 2K g_ab/(n-2); in 3D use the Weyl-free Riemann decomposition.

**Key insight:** A symmetry argument on eigenvectors does most of the work; dimension three is special because Ricci fixes Riemann.

**Result:** R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}=K(h_{\alpha\gamma}h_{\beta\delta}-h_{\alpha\delta}h_{\beta\gamma})

**Concepts:** isotropy, Einstein space, space of constant curvature, Riemann tensor determined by Ricci in three dimensions

### Example · standard · DIV §24.7 p.493 (pdf 508)

**Problem:** Find the spherically symmetric 3-metric of constant curvature.

**Method:** Use the ansatz e^lambda dr^2 + r^2 dOmega^2, compute its Ricci tensor, impose R_ab = 2K h_ab and solve the resulting ODEs; then rescale r and S to reduce K to its sign.

**Key insight:** One radial function suffices, and it is fixed algebraically: e^{-lambda} = 1 - K r^2.

**Result:** d\sigma^2=\frac{dr^2}{1-Kr^2}+r^2d\Omega^2 \;\to\; \text{Robertson-Walker (24.36)}

**Concepts:** space of constant curvature, Robertson-Walker metric

### Example · standard · DIV §24.8 p.496 (pdf 511)

**Problem:** Compute the area of geodesic 2-spheres and the total volume of the k = +1 slice, and interpret.

**Method:** Embed via (24.41), check the induced metric, integrate the angular part for area and the full volume element over 0<=chi<=pi.

**Key insight:** Areas grow more slowly than 4 pi r'^2, peak at the equator, and shrink to zero at the antipode: a finite unbounded space.

**Result:** A_\chi=4\pi R_0^2\sin^2\chi,\quad V=2\pi^2R_0^3

**Concepts:** 3-sphere, closed universe, radius of the universe

### Example · standard · DIV §24.8 p.498 (pdf 513)

**Problem:** Embed and characterize the k = -1 slice.

**Method:** Substitute r = sinh chi, embed in Minkowski space with w = R_0 cosh chi etc., verify w^2 - x^2 - y^2 - z^2 = R_0^2, compute sphere areas.

**Key insight:** Negative curvature needs a Lorentzian ambient space; sphere areas grow like sinh^2, so volume diverges.

**Result:** A_\chi=4\pi R_0^2\sinh^2\chi,\ V=\infty

**Concepts:** hyperbolic 3-space, embedding diagram

### Example · standard · DIV §24.9 p.500 (pdf 515)

**Problem:** Show that the Friedmann equations imply energy conservation for the cosmic fluid.

**Method:** Differentiate (24.52), combine with (24.53) to eliminate Rddot, reuse (24.52), multiply by R^3.

**Key insight:** The field equations already contain the first law dE + p dV = 0, as they must by the contracted Bianchi identities.

**Result:** \frac{d}{dt}(\rho R^3)+p\frac{d}{dt}R^3=0

**Concepts:** energy conservation in cosmology, contracted Bianchi identities

### Example · intro · DIV §24.9 p.500 (pdf 515)

**Problem:** Prove that an expanding universe with rho + 3p > Lambda/4 pi began at a finite past time, and bound its age.

**Method:** Form the acceleration equation, note Rddot < 0 so the graph is concave and below every tangent; the tangent at t_0 meets R = 0 at t_0 - R_0/Rdot_0.

**Key insight:** A geometric concavity argument replaces solving any ODE; the same picture bounds the age by the Hubble time.

**Result:** t_0 - t_{\rm bang} < 1/H_0

**Concepts:** FRW singularity theorem, acceleration equation, Hubble time

### Example · standard · DIV §24.9 p.501 (pdf 516)

**Problem:** Obtain Friedmann's equation for dust.

**Method:** Set p = 0; observe that (24.53) times Rdot R^2 is a total derivative; integrate to (24.58); fix the constant using (24.52).

**Key insight:** The integration constant C is essentially the conserved mass in a comoving sphere, and the result matches the Newtonian model term by term.

**Result:** \dot R^2=\frac{C}{R}+\frac13\Lambda R^2-k,\ C=\frac83\pi\rho R^3

**Concepts:** Friedmann's equation, dust

### Example · standard · DIV §24.10 p.502 (pdf 517)

**Problem:** Derive the cosmological redshift from the Robertson-Walker metric.

**Method:** Integrate the radial null condition for two successive signals; since both integrals equal f(r_1), the small end intervals must match; interpret dt as proper time of comoving observers.

**Key insight:** Redshift is a ratio of scale factors, not a velocity effect: no relative velocity was ever used.

**Result:** 1+z=R(t_0)/R(t_1)

**Concepts:** cosmological redshift, radial null geodesic, comoving distance function f(r)

### Example · intro · DIV §24.10 p.504 (pdf 519)

**Problem:** Show redshift is proportional to distance for nearby galaxies.

**Method:** Taylor expand R(t_0 - dt) to first order, and approximate the integral of dt/R by dt/R_0 and f(r_1) by r_1.

**Key insight:** Linear Hubble behaviour emerges from any smooth R(t) at small separations.

**Result:** z\simeq\dot R(t_0)\,r_1

**Concepts:** Hubble's law, cosmological redshift

### Example · standard · DIV §24.12 p.505 (pdf 520)

**Problem:** Express the luminosity distance of a galaxy in Robertson-Walker coordinates and deduce Hubble's law.

**Method:** Use homogeneity to equate the area of the wavefront sphere around the source with the sphere of radius r_1 around the observer, read area 4 pi R_0^2 r_1^2 from the metric, include (1+z)^2 dimming, compare with the definition of d_L, combine with z ~ Rdot_0 r_1.

**Key insight:** Homogeneity turns an awkward source-centred sphere into one centred on the observer; the relativistic Hubble law is then a theorem.

**Result:** d_L=r_1R(t_0),\quad z\simeq H_0 d_L

**Concepts:** luminosity distance, Hubble's law, homogeneity

### Example · intro · DIV §24.12 p.507 (pdf 522)

**Problem:** Relate the deceleration parameter to density and Lambda in dust models.

**Method:** Differentiate Friedmann's equation, divide appropriately to form -R Rddot/Rdot^2, then substitute C = 8 pi rho R^3/3 and H = Rdot/R.

**Key insight:** Measuring q_0 and H_0 constrains the combination of mean density and Lambda; a negative q_0 demands Lambda > 4 pi rho.

**Result:** q=(\tfrac43\pi\rho-\tfrac13\Lambda)/H^2

**Concepts:** deceleration parameter, Friedmann's equation


## Analogies and intuitions

### Newtonian ball of galaxies with total energy E → Friedmann's equation · strong · DIV §24.3 p.486 (pdf 501)

The relativistic scale-factor equation is shown to have the same form as energy conservation for a self-gravitating cloud: kinetic term, gravitational term, Lambda term and a constant fixed by the energy sign (k).

**Where it breaks down:** The meaning differs: in GR k is spatial curvature, not energy; the Newtonian model needs a centre and a finite extent, and pressure enters GR dynamics (rho + 3p) with no Newtonian counterpart.

**App idea:** Split-screen: a Newtonian expanding ball with an energy bar chart beside an FRW model with a curvature indicator; the R(t) curves coincide for matched parameters.

### First-order equation as energy integral, second-order as equation of motion → Friedmann equations · useful · DIV §24.9 p.500 (pdf 515)

Explains why (24.52) is called an energy equation and (24.53) an equation of motion, borrowing mechanics intuition.

**Where it breaks down:** Only heuristic; both are components of one tensor equation and are related by the Bianchi identity.

### Homogeneity of a gas → fluid (smoothing) approximation · useful · DIV §24.4 p.487 (pdf 502)

Just as a gas is homogeneous only on scales containing many molecules, the universe is homogeneous only after averaging over cells over about 1e9 light years.

**Where it breaks down:** Unlike a gas, the universe has structure on a continuous range of scales and gravitationally driven clustering; the averaging problem in GR is nontrivial (averaging then solving differs from solving then averaging).

**App idea:** Zoom-out slider over a galaxy distribution: density fluctuations in cells of adjustable size shrink as the cell grows.

### Galaxies as particles of a fluid (substratum) → Weyl's postulate · strong · DIV §24.5 p.490 (pdf 505)

Treats galaxies like molecules carried by a smooth flow with a unique velocity at each point, justifying a perfect fluid.

**Where it breaks down:** Real galaxies have peculiar velocities and merge; dark matter and radiation are also part of the cosmic fluid.

### Copernican principle generalized → cosmological principle · useful · DIV §24.4 p.487 (pdf 502)

Extends the demotion of the Earth from the centre of the solar system to the claim that no place in the universe is special.

**Where it breaks down:** The Copernican idea is about our location; the cosmological principle is a strong claim about all locations and about isotropy, which requires observational support (CMB, surveys).

### Evolving triangle stays similar → scale factor · strong · DIV §24.6 p.491 (pdf 506)

Three nearby particles form a triangle; homogeneity and isotropy force it to grow into a similar triangle with a location-independent magnification, which is exactly an overall factor S^2(t) in the metric.

**Where it breaks down:** Local argument about small triangles; it presumes cosmic time and comoving particles already defined.

**App idea:** Drag triangle vertices on a comoving grid; run time forward under isotropic expansion (similar) vs anisotropic Kasner expansion (distorted).

### Ordinary 2-sphere with circles of latitude → 3-sphere · strong · DIV §24.8 p.497 (pdf 512)

The area of spheres around a point in S^3 behaves like the circumference of latitude circles on a globe: nearly Euclidean near the pole, maximal at the equator, zero at the opposite pole.

**Where it breaks down:** One must add a dimension mentally; the embedding space is not physical, and the analogy says nothing about time dependence.

**App idea:** Globe with adjustable latitude circle and live plot of circumference vs geodesic radius, then 'promote' to S^3 with areas.

### Graph lying below its tangent → FRW singularity theorem · strong · DIV §24.9 p.500 (pdf 515)

Uses concavity of a curve and a straight tangent line to argue that R reaches zero within a finite past time shorter than 1/H_0.

**Where it breaks down:** Needs deceleration at all past times; with a positive Lambda dominating at late times the curve is convex now and the simple bound fails for today's universe.

### First law of thermodynamics dE + p dV = 0 → energy conservation in cosmology · useful · DIV §24.9 p.500 (pdf 515)

Reads the continuity equation as an adiabatically expanding gas doing work with its pressure.

**Where it breaks down:** There is no external surrounding to do work on; global energy conservation is not generally well-defined in GR, and for radiation the 'lost' energy statement is subtle.

### C as twice the mass of a Euclidean ball → Friedmann's equation · useful · DIV §24.9 p.501 (pdf 516)

Mnemonic: the constant C = 8 pi R^3 rho/3 equals twice the mass of a sphere of radius R and density rho, linking to the 2GM/R form of an escape-speed condition.

**Where it breaks down:** Only a memory device; R is not a physical radius in the flat or open cases.

### Radius of the universe → scale factor · useful · DIV §24.8 p.496 (pdf 511)

Because the closed slice has volume 2 pi^2 R^3, R is pictured as the radius of the 3-sphere.

**Where it breaks down:** Only meaningful for k = +1; for k = 0 the normalization of R is arbitrary and for k = -1 R is a curvature radius, not a size.


## Misconceptions addressed

### Newtonian cosmology was developed first and general relativity merely refined it. · DIV §24.1 p.481 (pdf 496)

**Correction:** Serious dynamical cosmology began with general relativity; the Newtonian analogue was constructed afterwards and gives Friedmann's form with different interpretations of the terms.

**Why tempting:** Newtonian physics is historically older, so its cosmology is assumed older too.

### An intervening absorbing gas can explain the dark night sky. · DIV §24.2 p.484 (pdf 499)

**Correction:** In a static eternal universe the gas heats until it re-emits as much as it absorbs, so the radiation density is not reduced.

**Why tempting:** Dust dimming works on short timescales in everyday astronomy.

### Olbers' paradox disappears if space is curved or finite. · DIV §24.2 p.484 (pdf 499)

**Correction:** The paradox persists for non-Euclidean homogeneous space and for open or closed geometries; what matters is finite age or expansion.

**Why tempting:** Finite volume sounds like it limits the number of stars.

### The universe is homogeneous in detail. · DIV §24.4 p.488 (pdf 503)

**Correction:** Homogeneity holds only for averages over cells larger than about 1e9 light years; below that there are galaxies, clusters and superclusters.

**Why tempting:** The word 'homogeneous' is absolute in everyday language.

### The cosmological principle is an established fact. · DIV §24.4 p.489 (pdf 504)

**Correction:** It is adopted mainly because it makes the model testable with limited data; alternatives (Bianchi, hierarchical) are studied and its validity is checked observationally.

**Why tempting:** It is stated like a law and underpins all standard models.

### Isotropy about our location alone implies the universe is homogeneous. · DIV §24.4 p.487 (pdf 502)

**Correction:** Homogeneity follows from isotropy about every point; isotropy about one point is compatible with a spherically symmetric inhomogeneous universe centred on us, so the Copernican assumption is essential.

**Why tempting:** The CMB looks isotropic to us, which feels like proof of uniformity.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The embedding space of the 3-sphere or hyperboloid is physically real, so the universe has an outside or a centre in the fourth dimension. · DIV §24.8 p.497 (pdf 512)

**Correction:** The embedding is a visual device; the slice is all of space at that epoch, with no boundary and no external points.

**Why tempting:** Diagrams show a sphere sitting in a larger space.

### The sign of k fixes the global topology uniquely (k = +1 closed, otherwise infinite). · DIV §24.8 p.499 (pdf 514)

**Correction:** k fixes local geometry; identifications can produce other topologies, e.g. finite flat or hyperbolic spaces.

**Why tempting:** The book (and many texts) pair k with closed/open for simplicity.

### 'Einstein space' means a solution of Einstein's field equations. · DIV §24.7 p.493 (pdf 508)

**Correction:** In geometry an Einstein space is any metric with Ricci proportional to the metric, a purely geometric notion (though such a spacetime is a vacuum solution with Lambda).

**Why tempting:** The name suggests a connection to Einstein's equations.

### Cosmological redshift is the special-relativistic Doppler shift of galaxies flying through space. · DIV §24.10 p.503 (pdf 518)

**Correction:** It is set by the ratio of scale factors at emission and reception, 1+z = R_0/R_1, derived without assigning any velocity; it differs from the SR Doppler formula at large z.

**Why tempting:** At small z both give z ~ v/c, and the book itself sometimes calls it a Doppler shift.

### Luminosity distance is just the inverse-square distance from observed flux. · DIV §24.11 p.504 (pdf 519)

**Correction:** In an expanding universe both the photon arrival rate and each photon's energy are reduced by (1+z), and the relevant sphere area is set by R(t_0) r_1, so flux is not E/(4 pi d^2) for any single naive d.

**Why tempting:** Inverse-square law works locally.

### The book's d_L is the same quantity called luminosity distance in modern cosmology papers. · DIV §24.11 p.505 (pdf 520)

**Correction:** Modern usage defines d_L^2 = E/(4 pi I), giving d_L = (1+z) R_0 r_1 and Hubble-law expansion z/H_0 [1 + (1-q_0)z/2]; the book's d_L divides out (1+z)^2 and equals the transverse comoving distance.

**Why tempting:** Same name and symbol.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Hubble's law v = H d is exact at all distances in relativistic cosmology. · DIV §24.12 p.507 (pdf 522)

**Correction:** The linear law holds only to first order in z (or distance); higher orders depend on q_0 and the model; very nearby it is also masked by peculiar velocities.

**Why tempting:** Taught early as a simple proportionality.

### The age of the universe is always less than the Hubble time. · DIV §24.12 p.507 (pdf 522)

**Correction:** This holds only if the expansion has always decelerated (rho + 3p > Lambda/4 pi); with the measured negative q_0 the late-time expansion accelerates and the age can approach or exceed 1/H_0 (in LambdaCDM they are nearly equal).

**Why tempting:** The book's Fig. 24.8 argument is presented right before the Hubble time value.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Galaxies recede from a centre, like an explosion into pre-existing space. · DIV §24.3 p.485 (pdf 500)

**Correction:** Uniform scaling means every galaxy sees all others receding with v proportional to distance; the Newtonian origin O is arbitrary, and comoving coordinates show galaxies at rest while the metric scales.

**Why tempting:** The Newtonian model explicitly uses an origin O and spherical symmetry about it.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Olbers' shells · DIV §24.2 p.483 (pdf 498)

**Setup:** Surround an observer by thin concentric shells of stars in a static, infinite, eternal universe and add their light; then note every sightline eventually meets a stellar surface.

**Lesson:** Distance does not help: without finite age or expansion the sky would blaze like a stellar surface.

**App idea:** See Fig. 24.1 redesign (sightline casting).

### Evolving triangle of particles · DIV §24.6 p.491 (pdf 506)

**Setup:** Mark a small triangle of fundamental particles at one cosmic time and look at the triangle they form later.

**Lesson:** Isotropy and homogeneity force the later triangle to be similar with a location-independent scale, so g = S^2(t)h(x).

**App idea:** Comoving grid with draggable triangle under isotropic vs anisotropic expansion.

### Growing spheres in a closed universe · DIV §24.8 p.497 (pdf 512)

**Setup:** From a point P at fixed epoch, measure the areas of spheres of increasing proper radius.

**Lesson:** Areas start Euclidean, fall behind 4 pi r'^2, peak at the equator, and shrink to zero at the antipode; a finite space without edge.

**App idea:** See Fig. 24.5 redesign (area meter).

### Two successive light pulses · DIV §24.10 p.502 (pdf 517)

**Setup:** A comoving galaxy emits two pulses a short proper time apart; follow both to a comoving observer.

**Lesson:** The reception interval is stretched by R(t_0)/R(t_1), giving the redshift as a pure geometry effect.

**App idea:** See Fig. 24.10 redesign (crest stretcher).

### Flash spreading to a sphere through the observer · DIV §24.12 p.505 (pdf 520)

**Setup:** A galaxy flashes at t_1; by t_0 the light lies on a sphere through us; use homogeneity to find its area.

**Lesson:** The flux we receive is set by the present areal size of that sphere plus two redshift losses, which defines and computes luminosity distance.

**App idea:** See Fig. 24.12 redesign.


## Applications and observations

- **Olbers' radiation budget** (numerical-estimate, DIV §24.2 p.483 (pdf 498)): In the paradoxical static model, half of the sky's radiation would come from regions so far away that light has only a 50% chance of avoiding occultation; local estimates place this beyond about 1e20 light years. Key numbers: about 1e20 light years
- **Scale of homogeneity** (observation, DIV §24.4 p.488 (pdf 503)): Averaging cells must exceed the largest structures (superclusters) for homogeneity to hold. Key numbers: cells over 1e9 ly; superclusters about 3e8 ly
- **Isotropy of discrete source counts** (observation, DIV §24.4 p.488 (pdf 503)): Counts of quasars, radio galaxies and X-ray sources across the sky show no anisotropy above roughly the 15% level; Hubble-constant measurements from Type Ia supernovae in different directions give a comparable limit. Key numbers: better than 15%
- **Cosmic microwave background** (detection-event, DIV §24.4 p.488 (pdf 503)): Thermal radiation bath discovered in 1965; Planck satellite shows isotropy to high precision; interpreted as big-bang relic. Key numbers: T = 2.7 K; isotropic to about 1 part in 100,000
- **Peculiar velocities of galaxies** (observation, DIV §24.5 p.490 (pdf 505)): Random motions relative to the Hubble flow are small, justifying the substratum idealization; for very nearby galaxies they obscure Hubble's law. Key numbers: below about 1e-3 c
- **Pressure of cosmic matter today** (numerical-estimate, DIV §24.9 p.501 (pdf 516)): All forms of pressure (random stellar/galactic motions, thermal, radiation) are tiny compared with matter energy density, so dust is a good present-day model. Key numbers: p/rho about 1e-5 to 1e-6
- **Hubble time** (observation, DIV §24.12 p.506 (pdf 521)): Quoted observational inverse Hubble constant; gives an upper bound on the age for decelerating models. Key numbers: T_0 about 1e10 yr, within a factor of 2. Modern value (our addition): H_0 about 67-73 km/s/Mpc, T_0 about 13.4-14.6 Gyr, age about 13.8 Gyr.
- **Deceleration parameter** (observation, DIV §24.12 p.507 (pdf 522)): Most measurements indicate accelerating expansion; later chapter discusses details. Key numbers: q_0 = -0.6 +/- 0.3 (flat LambdaCDM with Omega_m about 0.3 gives q_0 about -0.55; our estimate)
- **Sandage 1970 Hubble diagram** (data-analysis, DIV §24.12 p.508 (pdf 523)): Redshift against corrected apparent magnitude shows a tight linear law out to z of a few tenths; model curves for q_0 = 0, 1, 2 diverge only at the top where data are sparse. Velocity-distance law usable to about 18th magnitude. Key numbers: z from about 0.003 to about 0.5 on the plot; about 18th magnitude
- **Hubble's 1929 data** (observation, DIV §24.12 p.507 (pdf 522)): Hubble proposed the law from eighteen nearby galaxies, a tiny corner of later diagrams. Key numbers: 18 galaxies
- **Standard candles** (technology, DIV §24.11 p.505 (pdf 520)): Cepheid variables and Type Ia supernovae are used as sources of near-uniform luminosity, subject to calibration of absolute distances.
- **Early-universe reliability claims** (numerical-estimate, DIV §24.1 p.483 (pdf 498)): Some cosmologists claim understanding after the Planck-era time with GR plus GUTs; more conservatively after the electroweak era with GR plus the standard model. Big bang placed about 1e10 years ago. Key numbers: 1e-43 s; 1e-10 s; about 1e10 yr

## Historical notes

- **Albert Einstein (1917):** Added the cosmological term Lambda g_ab to the field equations (in the context of a static universe). — Cited as an example of a possible interaction that only shows up on cosmological scales; kept throughout the chapter. (DIV §24.1 p.482 (pdf 497))
- **Heinrich Olbers (1826):** Gave a precise formulation of the dark-night-sky paradox (earlier authors had similar ideas). — Shows that a static eternal universe was already problematic before GR. (DIV §24.2 p.483 (pdf 498))
- **C. V. I. Charlier (1908):** Proposed a hierarchical universe of clusters of clusters without a smooth average. — Main historical alternative to homogeneity; observations suggest the hierarchy stops at superclusters. (DIV §24.4 p.489 (pdf 504))
- **Hermann Weyl, H. P. Robertson (1923):** Weyl proposed a privileged class of observers moving with the smoothed galactic flow; Robertson later gave the postulate its geodesic-congruence form. — Foundation for cosmic time, comoving coordinates and the perfect-fluid source. (DIV §24.5 p.489 (pdf 504))
- **Alexander Friedmann:** Derived the dynamical equation for the scale factor that bears his name. — Solutions (Friedmann models) became the standard models of cosmology. (DIV §24.9 p.501 (pdf 516))
- **H. P. Robertson, A. G. Walker:** First obtained the homogeneous isotropic line element. — The book names the metric after them. (DIV §24.7 p.495 (pdf 510))
- **Arno Penzias, Robert Wilson (1965):** Discovered the 2.7 K cosmic microwave background. — Strongest evidence for isotropy and for the hot big bang; later refined by Planck to about 1 part in 1e5. (DIV §24.4 p.488 (pdf 503))
- **V. Belinski, I. Khalatnikov, E. Lifshitz:** Analysed the structure of generic cosmological singularities (BKL). — Suggests the isotropic Friedmann singularity is not generic, a motivation for anisotropic models. (DIV §24.4 p.488 (pdf 503))
- **Edwin Hubble (1929):** Proposed the velocity-distance law from about eighteen nearby galaxies. — The book stresses how tiny that dataset is on the scale of later compilations. (DIV §24.12 p.507 (pdf 522))
- **Allan Sandage (1970):** Review compiling redshift versus corrected magnitude and discussing q_0. — Source of Fig. 24.13; illustrates the difficulty of measuring deceleration. (DIV §24.12 p.507 (pdf 522))
- **Hermann Bondi (1961):** Wrote the classic text Cosmology on which this chapter is mainly based. — Explains the chapter's principle-first, Weyl-postulate structure. (DIV p.510 (pdf 525))

## Notation and conventions

- **Metric signature:** (+,-,-,-): ds^2 = dt^2 - R^2(t)(...); spatial metrics d sigma^2 and h_{alpha beta} are written positive definite and enter with a minus sign. — The k = -1 embedding uses an auxiliary Minkowski metric of signature +2 (-dw^2 + dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2). (DIV §24.6 p.491 (pdf 506))
- **Units:** Relativistic units G = c = 1; field equations G_ab - Lambda g_ab = 8 pi T_ab. — The Newtonian model keeps G explicitly. (DIV §24.9 p.499 (pdf 514))
- **Index ranges:** Latin a, b, ... run 0-3 (or 1..n in general-dimension arguments); Greek alpha, beta, ... run 1-3 for spatial components. (DIV §24.6 p.491 (pdf 506))
- **Curvature constants:** K: actual constant curvature in R_abcd = K(g_ac g_bd - g_ad g_bc); k = K/|K| in {+1, -1} or 0 after rescaling. — With the book's Ricci convention a round 3-sphere has R_{alpha beta} = 2K h_{alpha beta} with K > 0. (DIV §24.7 p.492 (pdf 507))
- **Scale factors:** S(t): unnormalized (Newtonian or with K); R(t) = mu S or S/|K|^{1/2}; R_0 = R(t_0). — Do not confuse the scale factor R(t) with the Ricci scalar R used in §24.7. (DIV §24.7 p.495 (pdf 510))
- **Radial coordinates:** r (unbarred, dimensionless after rescaling), rbar (conformally flat), r* (rescaled, then starred dropped), chi (hyperspherical: r = sin chi or sinh chi), r' = R_0 chi (proper radius). (DIV §24.7 p.494 (pdf 509))
- **Robertson-Walker naming:** The book applies the name Robertson-Walker to the barred conformally flat form (24.37)/(24.49), but uses the unbarred form (24.36) for light propagation. (DIV §24.7 p.495 (pdf 510))
- **Star-equals:** u^a with an asterisk over the equals sign: equality valid only in the particular (comoving) coordinate system. — Book-wide DIV convention. (DIV §24.9 p.499 (pdf 514))
- **Energy-momentum tensor:** Perfect fluid T_ab = (rho + p)u_a u_b - p g_ab with u^a u_a = +1. (DIV §24.9 p.499 (pdf 514))
- **Redshift:** z = (nu_1 - nu_0)/nu_0 with nu_1 emitted and nu_0 received; subscript 1 for emission event, 0 for observation (now). (DIV §24.10 p.503 (pdf 518))
- **Distances:** d_A = absolute (proper) distance at fixed cosmic time; d_O = alpha/beta observational size distance; d_L = luminosity distance defined with (1+z)^2 removed. — Modern texts use d_A for angular-diameter distance and d_L without the (1+z)^2 division; beware of clashes. (DIV §24.11 p.504 (pdf 519))
- **Hubble and deceleration parameters:** H = Rdot/R, H_0 = H(t_0), T = 1/H (Hubble time, T_0 today), q = -R Rddot/Rdot^2. (DIV §24.12 p.506 (pdf 521))
- **Dots and primes:** Dot = d/dt with respect to cosmic time; prime = d/dr in §24.7. (DIV §24.7 p.494 (pdf 509))
- **Newtonian ell:** ell = (number of stars per unit volume) x (mean stellar luminosity). (DIV §24.2 p.483 (pdf 498))

## Teaching gems

### Newtonian ball of galaxies reproduces Friedmann's equation exactly, with k emerging as minus the sign of the total energy after rescaling. · DIV §24.3 p.486 (pdf 501)

**Why it works:** Learners can reach the central cosmological ODE with only first-year mechanics before touching curvature, and later recognize the relativistic result as familiar.

**App idea:** Energy-landscape widget: potential -C/R - Lambda R^2/3 plotted with a horizontal 'energy' line at -k; a ball rolls along R(t) and the learner sees bound (recollapse), marginal, and unbound histories.

### Eigenvector argument: if no direction is special, the Einstein tensor viewed as a matrix must have every vector as eigenvector, so it is a multiple of the identity. · DIV §24.7 p.492 (pdf 507)

**Why it works:** Converts an abstract symmetry assumption into a one-line linear-algebra fact.

**App idea:** Show a 3x3 symmetric matrix acting on a sphere of unit vectors; generic matrices deform the sphere into an ellipsoid with principal axes (preferred directions); isotropy slider shrinks the eigenvalue differences until the ellipsoid is a sphere.

### Similar-triangle evolution forces the metric to scale uniformly in time. · DIV §24.6 p.491 (pdf 506)

**Why it works:** Physical, visual, and exactly captures what homogeneity plus isotropy mean for time dependence.

**App idea:** Draggable triangles on an expanding grid; switching to anisotropic (Kasner) expansion distorts shapes and reveals why the cosmological principle rules it out.

### Tracking the area of spheres versus radius distinguishes k = +1, 0, -1 intrinsically. · DIV §24.8 p.497 (pdf 512)

**Why it works:** Gives a measurable, embedding-free test of spatial curvature and ties directly to observations (source counts, luminosity distance).

**App idea:** Three overlaid curves A(r') = 4 pi R_0^2 sin^2, 4 pi r'^2, 4 pi R_0^2 sinh^2 with a draggable radius; a 'galaxy count' mode shows numbers of evenly spaced galaxies inside each sphere.

## Gaps and pitfalls

- **The two Friedmann equations (24.52)-(24.53), the central result, are left entirely as an exercise without even the Christoffel symbols.** — Readers may treat the equations as given and miss how k, Rdot and Rddot arise from curvature of slices and time dependence. *Suggestion:* Provide a guided derivation (Christoffels, R_00, R_ij) or a symbolic-algebra demo; point to the Newtonian analogue as a check.
- **Printed component list (24.26) is garbled: it gives R_22 = cosec^2 theta and labels the remaining expression R_33.** — Someone checking Exercise 24.7 will find inconsistent results. *Suggestion:* State R_22 = 1 + (1/2) r e^{-lambda} lambda' - e^{-lambda} and R_33 = sin^2 theta R_22.
- **Several small printed slips: Case 2 substitution writes x = R_0 r cos theta (should be z); maximum area said to occur at r' = pi/2 (should be r' = pi R_0/2); verbal recipe for the acceleration equation says 3 x (24.52) minus (24.53) (correct is 3 x (24.53) minus (24.52)); 'dt_1 and dt_2' should be dt_1 and dt_0; Fig. 24.12 labels O's world-line r = r_0 instead of r = 0; Exercise 24.17(i) has lower-case r(t_0) in a denominator.** — Careful readers can lose time reconciling algebra with the text. *Suggestion:* Flag corrections inline in the app and in tutor answers.
- **The book's luminosity distance divides out (1+z)^2, so d_L = r_1 R_0 and the second-order law has -(1+q_0)/2; modern literature defines d_L = (1+z) r_1 R_0 with +(1-q_0)/2.** — Formulas will disagree by a factor of 1+z with papers, supernova analyses and Chapter-26-style data; q_0 inferred from data could have the wrong sign convention in mind. *Suggestion:* Teach both, label the book's quantity as transverse comoving (proper-motion) distance, and show how the flux relation changes.
- **Dated or tension-prone numbers: T_0 about 1e10 yr (factor 2), q_0 = -0.6 +/- 0.3 alongside a Sandage 1970 plot, big bang 'some 1e10 years ago', and a success of big-bang theory attributed to heavy-element abundances.** — Learners may memorize outdated values or think the big bang explains heavy elements. *Suggestion:* Update to H_0 about 67-73 km/s/Mpc, age 13.8 Gyr, q_0 about -0.55 for LambdaCDM; clarify nucleosynthesis makes light elements (H, He, Li) while heavy elements are stellar.
- **Having just shown the age is below 1/H_0 when rho + 3p > Lambda/4 pi, the text quotes a negative q_0 without noting that this condition then fails today.** — Creates a hidden inconsistency; learners may conclude the age bound always applies. *Suggestion:* Pair (24.79) with the observation: q_0 < 0 implies Lambda > 4 pi rho now, so the tangent argument cannot be applied at t_0; in LambdaCDM H_0 t_0 is close to 0.95.
- **The resolution of Olbers' paradox is credited mainly to expansion redshift, and the lost photon energy is said to do the work of expansion.** — Modern analyses (e.g. Harrison) find finite age and finite stellar lifetimes dominate; energy-conservation claims for photons in expanding space are subtle and contested. *Suggestion:* Present both factors quantitatively (sky brightness with finite age vs with redshift) and treat the energy remark as a heuristic.
- **The argument that isotropy implies an Einstein space is applied in n dimensions of any signature, but for spacetime itself the cosmological principle only asserts spatial isotropy; the derivation is used only for the 3-slices, which is not clearly stated.** — Readers may wrongly conclude RW spacetime is itself an Einstein space (it is not unless it is de Sitter-like). *Suggestion:* Emphasize the argument is applied to h_{alpha beta} on each slice.
- **Newtonian model's origin O and finite extent are not reconciled with the claim that there is no centre; the continuum infinite case is said not to work.** — Learners may picture an explosion from a centre. *Suggestion:* Show that uniform scaling looks identical from any galaxy (change origin: velocities remain proportional to relative position).
- **Chapter cross-references are stale: modern cosmology, inflation and new H_0 measurements are said to be in Chapter 25 but live in Chapter 26.** — Navigation confusion. *Suggestion:* Use DIV ch26 §26.2, §26.5, §26.7 links.
- **Absolute distance is labelled d_A, clashing with the standard use of d_A for angular-diameter distance; the angular distance d_O is dismissed without its relativistic expression d_O = R(t_1) r_1.** — Later reading will mismatch symbols; the non-monotonic angular size effect is missed. *Suggestion:* Add the RW angular-diameter distance and show that angular size can increase with z.
- **The book's first-law argument (24.55) and the remark that the energy lost to redshift powers the expansion treat E = rho V as a conserved global energy, but for Lambda the same equation says vacuum energy in a comoving volume grows (p = -rho).** — Learners may generalize 'energy is conserved' to the whole universe and be puzzled once Lambda or radiation is included. *Suggestion:* Work out dE = -p dV for dust, radiation and Lambda side by side, and stress that it is a local statement contained in T^{ab}_{;b} = 0, not a global energy budget.

## Tutor notes

- Open with 'Why is the night sky dark?' and let the learner reason through Olbers' shells before revealing that the answer involves the age and expansion of the universe.
- Before any tensors, run the Newtonian ball-of-galaxies model: ask what the only homogeneity-preserving motion is (uniform scaling) and let the learner derive v = H r.
- Check understanding of the cosmological principle by asking: 'If the CMB looks the same in all directions from Earth, what extra assumption is needed to conclude the universe is homogeneous?' (isotropy about every point / Copernican assumption).
- Explain the order: principles (cosmological principle, Weyl) -> kinematics (cosmic time, comoving coordinates, S(t)) -> geometry (constant curvature, RW metric) -> dynamics (Friedmann) -> observables (z, distances, H, q). Keep a visible roadmap because the dynamics only appear two-thirds of the way through.
- When introducing k, always state what it changes: local geometry of slices (area of spheres, triangle angle sums), not necessarily global topology; and in the Newtonian picture it corresponds to the sign of total energy.
- Common learner question: 'Where is the centre of expansion?' Answer with the uniform-scaling argument from any galaxy's viewpoint and the comoving-grid picture.
- Common learner question: 'Is redshift a Doppler shift?' Derive 1+z = R_0/R_1 from two pulses and note agreement with v/c only at small z.
- Warn explicitly about the book's d_L convention (equals R_0 r_1) versus modern d_L = (1+z) R_0 r_1, and about symbol reuse of R (scale factor vs Ricci scalar) and d_A (absolute vs angular-diameter).
- Quick checks: compute V = 2 pi^2 R_0^3; show that p = 0 gives rho R^3 constant; show that q_0 < 0 requires Lambda > 4 pi rho in (24.79); estimate 1/H_0 for H_0 = 70 km/s/Mpc (about 14 Gyr).
- For the singularity theorem, have the learner sketch R(t) with its tangent before any algebra; then ask what happens to the argument when Lambda dominates.
- Update numbers when citing observations: CMB 2.725 K, H_0 about 67-73 km/s/Mpc, age 13.8 Gyr, q_0 about -0.55 in flat LambdaCDM; note the book's values are rough.
- For advanced learners, connect the acceleration equation to the Raychaudhuri equation for the geodesic congruence of Weyl's postulate, and preview horizons via the integral of dt/R.
