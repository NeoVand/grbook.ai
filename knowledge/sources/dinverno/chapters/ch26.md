# DIV ch26 · Modern cosmology

> Rewrites Friedmann's equation in terms of present-day density parameters, maps every FRW model onto the (Ωm, ΩΛ) plane with a particle-in-a-potential analysis, then shows how H0, the CMB, nucleosynthesis, rotation curves, supernovae and CMB anisotropies locate our universe at the flat ΛCDM point, before running the clock back through the thermal history, motivating inflation, discussing anthropic arguments and closing the book with the open questions of cosmology.

**Pages:** printed 539–572 · pdf 554–587 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 4/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

Section 26.1 needs only ODE separation, calculus on a potential and some algebra (the cubic step is skipped); §26.7's scalar-field part needs the book's variational machinery. The conceptual load is high because the chapter moves quickly across observational astronomy, statistical physics, particle physics and philosophy, with many results quoted rather than derived. Printed slips (the R̃ argument in (26.21)-(26.22), the black-body peak condition, 'Ωm = 0.7' in the age, the t_dec coefficient, T_eq, the exponent in (26.62) and the 8π in (26.71)) and inconsistent epoch numbers can trip careful readers. Observational values are dated relative to current precision cosmology.

## Role in the book

Last chapter of Part F and of the whole book, and a chapter added in the second edition (Chapter 1 announces it as the new, parameter-measuring treatment of cosmology). Chapter 24 set up the Robertson-Walker geometry, Friedmann's equations, redshift, the book's luminosity distance and the deceleration parameter; Chapter 25 solved single-component dust and radiation models and studied horizons and conformal structure. This chapter changes viewpoint from 'solve for R(t) given C, Λ, k' to the observational cosmologist's language of Ω parameters, so that theory can be confronted with data and the concordance ΛCDM model identified. From §26.3 on it deliberately imports physics outside general relativity (black-body radiation, atomic and nuclear energy scales, particle dark matter candidates, scalar fields), flagged as Level 2 and to be taken partly on trust. Its closing section ties cosmology back to the horizons and singularities of Part D, the gravitational-wave detectors of Part E, and the book-long aim of preparing readers for Hawking and Ellis.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can define the critical density and the density parameters Ωr, Ωm, ΩΛ and Ωc, and explain why they sum to exactly one at the present epoch.
- Reader can rewrite the multi-component Friedmann equation in the dimensionless variables R̃ = R/R0 and t̃ = H0 t and interpret it as motion of a unit mass in the effective potential V(R̃) with energy E = Ωc/2.
- Reader can derive the dividing lines of the (Ωm, ΩΛ) plane: closed/open along ΩΛ = 1 − Ωm, accelerating/decelerating along ΩΛ = Ωm/2, and the big-bang/bounce boundary from V_max = E.
- Reader can express the age of an FRW universe as an integral over R̃ and evaluate it for a flat matter-plus-Λ model, getting H0 t0 ≈ 0.96 for Ωm = 0.3.
- Reader can explain why H0 must be measured with distant objects and how peculiar velocities, standard candles and the calibration problem lead to a cosmic distance ladder.
- Reader can obtain ε = αT⁴ from the Planck spectrum, turn the CMB temperature into Ωr ≈ 10⁻⁴, and show that T ∝ 1/R keeps a black-body spectrum thermal as the universe expands.
- Reader can make the crude decoupling estimate from the hydrogen ionization energy and explain the two reasons the real decoupling temperature (≈3000 K) is about ten times lower.
- Reader can list the independent routes to Ωm (star counts, nucleosynthesis, rotation curves, supernova redshift-magnitude data, CMB anisotropies) and explain how crossing SN and CMB constraints give Ωm ≈ 0.3, ΩΛ ≈ 0.7 and a flat universe.
- Reader can derive the exact luminosity distance-redshift relation for matter plus Λ, with its sin, linear and sinh forms for k = +1, 0, −1.
- Reader can place matter-radiation equality, decoupling, nucleosynthesis-era temperatures and the quark-hadron transition in time using T ∝ 1/R with R ∝ t^{2/3} or t^{1/2}.
- Reader can state the flatness problem (|1 − Ω| ∝ t^{2/3} in the matter era) and the horizon problem (particle horizon 2t in the radiation era) quantitatively and show how exponential expansion removes both.
- Reader can derive φ̈ + 3Hφ̇ + V'(φ) = 0 and the scalar-field energy-momentum tensor, and explain why slow roll makes T_ab ≈ V g_ab act like a cosmological constant.
- Reader can distinguish the book's weak and strong anthropic principles and summarize the objections raised against the strong form.
- Reader can list the unresolved questions behind ΛCDM: the nature of dark matter, the origin of dark energy and the vacuum-energy mismatch, the physics of the inflaton, the averaging problem, and the status of the initial singularity.

## Teaching approach

A survey chapter that starts with explicit, calculus-level manipulation and progressively hands over to physics the reader is asked to accept on trust. Section 26.1 is the mathematical core: Friedmann's equation is made dimensionless, reinterpreted as a particle moving in a potential, and used with elementary calculus on V(R̃) to carve the (Ωm, ΩΛ) plane into regions of qualitatively different universes. Each later section supplies one number or one constraint for that plane: H0 from distance ladders, Ωr from the CMB temperature, baryons from nucleosynthesis, dark matter from rotation curves, and the crossing supernova and CMB strips that single out ΛCDM. The authors then run the model backwards with two scaling laws and order-of-magnitude arithmetic, introduce inflation as a cure computed from the very formulas that expose the flatness and horizon problems, and finish in a discursive, partly philosophical register (anthropic principles, arrows of time, Wheeler's U) with an honest list of what is still unexplained.

**Style:** survey-overview, observational-astrophysics, computational-recipe, diagram-driven, spiral-revisit, analogy-driven

**Narrative arc**

1. Motivating question: Chapter 25 treated radiation, matter and vacuum one at a time; how do we handle a universe containing all three, in terms that observations can pin down?
2. Model: define the critical density and Ω's, treat Λ as a fluid with p = −ρ, write the Friedmann equation in R̃ and t̃, and derive the sum rule Ωr + Ωm + ΩΛ + Ωc = 1.
3. Reinterpret as a unit mass in a potential; conclude that four numbers fix the model and, neglecting Ωr, that the (Ωm, ΩΛ) plane classifies all models.
4. Formalism: derive the dividing lines (closed/open, accelerating, big bang or bounce, recollapse) and the age integral.
5. Measurement I: H0 via standard candles and distance ladders; the CMB as a black body giving Ωr and T ∝ 1/R; decoupling and the last scattering surface.
6. Measurement II: mass census (stars, nucleosynthesis, rotation curves), then the exact d_L(z) relation and CMB anisotropies, whose nearly perpendicular constraints fix Ωm ≈ 0.3, ΩΛ ≈ 0.7.
7. Application: the ΛCDM model, its exact sinh^{2/3} solution and age, and an audit of its poorly understood ingredients and of dissenting views.
8. Check by extrapolation: run the model backwards to matter-radiation equality, decoupling and the first second, summarized in a table of epochs.
9. New puzzles and a resolution: quantify the flatness and horizon problems; show exponential expansion removes both; realize it with a slowly rolling scalar field that also seeds perturbations.
10. Wider frame and farewell: anthropic reasoning as an alternative explanation, arrows of time, open questions about fundamental physics, and links back to singularities, horizons, gravitational waves and Hawking-Ellis.

**Signature moves**

- Non-dimensionalizes before analysing (R̃ = R/R0, t̃ = H0 t, densities over ρcrit) so that H0 drops out as a pure scale and the Ω's are the only shape parameters.
- Reuses the energy-equation idea from Chapter 25 with an explicit potential V(R̃) = −½(Ωm/R̃ + Ωr/R̃² + ΩΛR̃²) and E = Ωc/2, then turns the condition V_max = E into a curve in parameter space.
- Builds one master diagram (the (Ωm, ΩΛ) plane) from theory, and later overlays observational confidence regions on identical axes so that data and theory share a picture.
- Crude estimate followed by a diagnosis of its failure: the decoupling temperature from mean photon energy is ten times too high, and the two physical corrections are named.
- Chains simple scaling laws (T ∝ 1/R, R ∝ t^{2/3} or t^{1/2}) with a few anchor numbers to produce a thermal history without solving the full equations.
- Evaluates the same particle-horizon integral for R ∝ t^{1/2} and for R ∝ e^{Ht}, so the contrast between linear and exponential growth carries the whole horizon-problem argument.
- Reads the homogeneous scalar-field equation as a ball rolling in V(φ) with Hubble friction, echoing the particle-in-a-potential picture of §26.1.
- Explicitly flags non-GR material as Level 2 and repeatedly names the limits of the standard model (unknown dark matter, dark energy mismatch, SN evolution, averaging, inflaton physics).

## Section by section

### §26.1 Multi-component models — p.539 (pdf 554)

Starts from the Λ = 0 Friedmann equation to define the critical density 3H0²/8π (of order 10⁻²⁶ kg m⁻³) and the density parameter Ω, whose size relative to 1 fixes the sign of k. Treats Λ as a constant-density fluid with p = −ρ and writes the Friedmann equation with radiation, matter and vacuum densities scaling as R⁻⁴, R⁻³ and R⁰, then non-dimensionalizes with R̃ and t̃. Evaluating at t0 gives Ωr + Ωm + ΩΛ + Ωc = 1. The equation becomes energy conservation for a particle in V(R̃), so four parameters determine the model and H0 only sets the scale. With Ωr neglected, the (Ωm, ΩΛ) plane is divided into closed/open, accelerating/decelerating and big bang/no big bang regions (the last via V_max = E and a cubic), giving Fig. 26.2. Ends with the general age integral and its closed form for flat matter-plus-Λ models.

**Concepts:** critical density, density parameter, component density parameters, curvature density parameter, cosmic sum rule, vacuum energy as a perfect fluid, multi-component Friedmann equation, scaling of densities with the scale factor, dimensionless Friedmann equation, effective potential for the scale factor, four-parameter specification of FRW models, (Ωm, ΩΛ) classification plane, deceleration parameter, big bang versus bounce criterion, age of the universe

**Key moves**
- Set k = 0 in H² = 8πρ/3 − k/R² to define ρcrit and compare the actual density with it to decide open/flat/closed.
- Move Λg_ab to the source side: ρΛ = Λ/8π with p = −ρ, constant in time.
- Express ρm, ρr, ρΛ as ρcrit Ω R̃⁻³, ρcrit Ω R̃⁻⁴, ρcrit ΩΛ and rescale time by the Hubble time.
- Evaluate the dimensionless equation at R̃ = 1 (where dR̃/dt̃ = 1) to get the sum rule and hence Ωc from measurable Ω's.
- Recast as ½(dR̃/dt̃)² + V(R̃) = E; integrate by quadrature; rescale by R0 = 1/(H0|Ωc|^{1/2}).
- Neglect Ωr (≈10⁻⁴) and analyse three questions: sign of Ωc, sign of q0 = Ωm/2 − ΩΛ, and whether the potential barrier V_max exceeds E.
- Maximize V at R̃³ = Ωm/2ΩΛ, impose V_max = E, cube to a cubic in ΩΛ and identify the physical roots through (0,1) and (1,0).
- Separate variables once more to write H0 t0 as a definite integral from R̃ = 0 to 1.

### §26.2 Measuring the Hubble constant — p.545 (pdf 560)

Quotes H0 = 72 ± 8 km/s/Mpc and its h-parameterization, introduces parsecs and megaparsecs with a table of cosmic distance scales, and explains why the Hubble relation z ≈ H0 d is hard to exploit: nearby galaxies are dominated by peculiar velocities, distant ones are beyond parallax and need luminosity distances. Those require standard candles (main-sequence stars, Cepheid variables, Type Ia supernovae) whose absolute brightness must be calibrated, which astronomers do by overlapping methods forming a distance ladder.

**Concepts:** Hubble constant, parsec and megaparsec, peculiar velocity, standard candle, cosmic distance ladder, Hubble's law

**Key moves**
- State H0 with its uncertainty and introduce h to carry that uncertainty through formulas.
- Give distance units and a table spanning the Sun to the edge of the observable universe.
- Identify the tension: nearby objects have measurable distances but large peculiar velocities; distant objects have clean Hubble flow but no direct distances.
- Resolve with luminosity distances from standard candles, then name the calibration problem and the ladder solution.

### §26.3 The cosmic microwave background radiation — p.547 (pdf 562)

Announces that much of the rest of the chapter uses physics beyond GR (marked Level 2). Recounts the prediction and discovery of the CMB and quotes T0 = 2.725 K with isotropy better than one part in 10⁴. From the Planck spectrum it extracts the peak and mean photon energies and ε = αT⁴, giving ΩCMB ≈ 5×10⁻⁵ and Ωr ≲ 10⁻⁴. Combining ε ∝ T⁴ with ρr ∝ R⁻⁴ yields T ∝ 1/R, and a black-body spectrum stays black-body because ν/T is unchanged. A hydrogen-plus-photons model gives a crude decoupling temperature of 50,000 K, corrected to about 3000 K (R̃ ≈ 10⁻³) by the spectral tail and the photon excess. Defines the observer-centred last scattering surface (radius of order 6000 Mpc), follows the photon frequency from 1.6×10¹⁴ Hz down to 160 GHz, and contrasts the black-body success with the steady-state prediction.

**Concepts:** cosmic microwave background, black-body spectrum, radiation energy density law, component density parameters, temperature-redshift relation, preservation of the black-body form under expansion, decoupling, photon-to-baryon ratio, last scattering surface, steady-state theory

**Key moves**
- Flag Level 2 content and say which results matter most: the scaling laws (26.36) and (26.38).
- Differentiate the Planck spectrum to locate its peak; quote the mean photon energy ≈ 3k_BT.
- Integrate the spectrum to get αT⁴, insert T0, convert to mass density and divide by ρcrit.
- Equate ε ∝ T⁴ with ρr ∝ R⁻⁴ to obtain T ∝ 1/R; check that the thermal form is invariant because ν and T redshift together.
- Estimate decoupling by 3k_BT = 14 eV, then correct for the high-energy tail and for the many photons per hydrogen atom.
- Interpret the CMB as coming from a sphere around each observer, not a special place, and track its frequency redshift by ~1000.

### §26.4 How heavy is the universe? — p.551 (pdf 566)

Builds up Ωm in stages: luminous stars give about 0.01; light-element nucleosynthesis fixes baryons at about 0.04; flat galactic rotation curves, analysed with Newtonian circular orbits v = √(GM(r)/r), reveal dark halos and motivate cold dark matter. The decisive evidence comes from two elongated constraints in the (Ωm, ΩΛ) plane: the exact luminosity distance-redshift relation for Type Ia supernovae, derived from the dimensionless Friedmann equation for k = +1, 0, −1, and the multipole spectrum of CMB anisotropies, whose peak positions depend on geometry and content. The two strips cross almost at right angles near Ωm ≈ 0.3, ΩΛ ≈ 0.7, implying Ωc ≈ 0: the ΛCDM model.

**Concepts:** baryon density parameter, big bang nucleosynthesis, galaxy rotation curve, dark matter, luminosity distance, comoving radial coordinate χ, exact luminosity distance-redshift relation, CMB anisotropies and angular power spectrum, ΛCDM model

**Key moves**
- Show star counts undercount matter (hot X-ray gas in clusters) and nucleosynthesis gives the baryon total.
- Balance gravity against centripetal acceleration to predict v ∝ r^{−1/2}; flat observed curves imply unseen mass.
- Replace the approximate Hubble law by an exact d_L(z): χ = ∫dt/R rewritten as an integral over R̃ with the potential inside the square root.
- Describe the CMB temperature correlation C(θ) and its multipole decomposition qualitatively.
- Overlay SN and CMB constraints on the model plane; their near-orthogonality gives a sharp intersection.

### §26.5 The ΛCDM model of cosmology — p.555 (pdf 570)

Lists the model's successes (expansion history, CMB, large-scale structure, light-element abundances) and writes its exact matter-plus-Λ solution as R ∝ sinh^{2/3}(t/tΛ), which grows like t^{2/3}, decelerates, then accelerates towards de Sitter expansion; the age comes out near 14 billion years, easing tension with the oldest objects. It then audits the model: near-flatness needs explaining (inflation), roughly nine-tenths of the matter is non-baryonic, and 70% of the content is dark energy. Dark matter candidates (primordial black holes, MACHOs, massive neutrinos, axions, WIMPs) and dark energy interpretations (a constant like G, vacuum zero-point energy with a gigantic theoretical mismatch, quintessence, modified gravity) are surveyed, together with doubts about SN Ia luminosity evolution and about averaging the non-linear Einstein equations.

**Concepts:** ΛCDM model, de Sitter expansion, age of the universe, dark matter candidates, dark energy, cosmological constant problem, quintessence, averaging problem in cosmology

**Key moves**
- Recast the Chapter 25 solution (25.5) in Ω language (Exercise 26.10) and read off early t^{2/3} and late e^{H0√ΩΛ t} behaviour.
- Evaluate the age formula (26.30) for the concordance parameters.
- Convert the three unexplained ingredients (flatness, dark matter, dark energy) into explicit questions.
- Eliminate astrophysical dark matter candidates using the nucleosynthesis bound and lensing searches; list particle candidates.
- Contrast geometric and source-side readings of Λ and quote the vacuum-energy discrepancy.
- Record observational and theoretical objections to the need for Λ.

### §26.6 The early Universe — p.558 (pdf 573)

Radiation, negligible today, dominates early because ρr/ρm = (Ωr/Ωm)/R̃. With Ωr = 7×10⁻⁵ and Ωm = 0.3 equality occurs at R̃ ≈ 2×10⁻⁴. Combining T ∝ 1/R̃ with R ∝ t^{2/3} after equality dates decoupling to about 10¹³ s and equality to about 10¹² s; switching to R ∝ t^{1/2} before equality gives T ≈ 1.5×10¹⁰ K/√t, so at one second thermal energies are about 1 MeV (nuclear scale) and the quark-hadron transition is near 10⁻⁴ s. Table 26.2 and Fig. 26.6 summarize the epochs.

**Concepts:** scaling of densities with the scale factor, matter-radiation equality, temperature-redshift relation, temperature-time relation in the radiation era, thermal history of the universe, quark-hadron phase transition, decoupling

**Key moves**
- Take the ratio of the radiation and matter scaling laws to locate equality in R̃.
- Use two scaling laws together, T/T0 = (t0/t)^{2/3}, to convert temperatures into times after equality.
- Change the exponent to 1/2 before equality and renormalize at t_eq, T_eq.
- Translate temperatures into energies with E = k_BT to identify which particles are free at each epoch.

### §26.7 Inflationary cosmology — p.560 (pdf 575)

Introduces a time-dependent critical density and shows |1 − ρ/ρcrit(t)| = |k|/(HR)² grows as t^{2/3} in the matter era, so present near-flatness implies extreme early tuning (flatness problem). Computes the particle horizon Δr = R(t)∫dt/R, equal to 2t for radiation domination, and argues that CMB photons from opposite directions left causally disconnected points (horizon problem; connected patches span only about 2°). Exponential expansion R ∝ e^{Ht} makes the horizon grow exponentially and drives |1 − Ω| to zero as e^{−2Ht}, curing both. A scalar inflaton with action (26.64) obeys φ̈ + 3Hφ̇ + V' = 0; on a flat stretch of potential Hubble damping freezes φ, so T_ab ≈ V(φ*)g_ab behaves like a cosmological constant and gives de Sitter expansion until φ rolls to the minimum. Quantum fluctuations δφ produce δρ ≈ V'δφ, which are inflated into CMB anisotropies and the seeds of structure.

**Concepts:** flatness problem, critical density, particle horizon, horizon problem, cosmic inflation, inflaton scalar field, Hubble friction, slow-roll inflation, de Sitter expansion, primordial density perturbations from inflation

**Key moves**
- Divide the Friedmann equation by H² to isolate |1 − Ω| = |k|/(HR)² and insert R ∝ t^{2/3}, H ∝ 1/t.
- Extrapolate today's 30% bound back to equality and the quark-hadron epoch.
- Set ds² = 0 on a radial ray, integrate dr = dt/R and multiply by R(t) to get the proper horizon distance; evaluate for R ∝ t^{1/2}.
- Use past light cones of two antipodal last-scattering points to state the horizon problem.
- Re-evaluate both formulas for R ∝ e^{Ht}: horizon (e^{Ht} − 1)/H, flatness deviation ∝ e^{−2Ht}.
- Vary the scalar-field action to get the field equation and T_ab; specialize to RW; argue slow roll from the damping term.
- Identify T_ab ≈ V g_ab with a cosmological constant and borrow the de Sitter solution; add fluctuations δρ ≈ V'δφ.

### §26.8 The anthropic principle — p.565 (pdf 580)

Presents anthropic reasoning (our existence constrains what we can observe) as a rival style of explanation for cosmic smoothness. The weak form confines life to suitable regions and is used to account for the universe's great age via the slow chain of galaxy formation, stellar nucleosynthesis, planet formation and evolution. Quantum cosmology and the Everett-Wheeler many-worlds picture motivate the strong form, where only a few of many universes permit life and fine-tuned constants are read as selection rather than design. Objections: unobservable universes can be ignored, accessible regions reduce the strong form to the weak one, and it reverses the Copernican lesson of the cosmological principle. Inflation plus the weak form may explain present appearances; arrows of time are mentioned; the section ends with Wheeler's 'big U' and a callback to Lillian Lieber's book from Chapter 1.

**Concepts:** anthropic principle, weak anthropic principle, strong anthropic principle, quantum cosmology, many-worlds interpretation, fine-tuning of fundamental constants, Copernican principle, arrows of time, cosmic inflation

**Key moves**
- State the general principle, then the weak and strong versions in highlighted panels.
- Give the age-of-the-universe argument as the weak version's showcase.
- Link the strong version to many-worlds quantum mechanics and to fine-tuned constants.
- Raise three objections and settle on inflation plus the weak form.

### §26.9 Final questions — p.567 (pdf 582)

Summarizes inflation plus ΛCDM as a precise fit to data, mentions structure formation by gravitational instability (simulations require dark matter), and lists the questions that remain: what the inflaton is and why its potential suits inflation, what dark matter is, and why there is dark energy, with possible input from string theory, conformal cyclic cosmology, accelerators and space gravitational-wave detectors. Recaps cosmic history from singularity through inflation and radiation, matter and Λ eras towards de Sitter, links horizons and singularities to Part D, cites the singularity theorems against a bounce, stresses that the big bang is in principle observable, heralds multi-messenger astronomy and LISA, and closes the book by recommending Hawking and Ellis as the next step, after a short farewell that looks back on the book's three promised destinations (black holes, gravitational waves and cosmology).

**Concepts:** gravitational instability, ΛCDM model, cosmic inflation, dark matter, dark energy, big bang singularity, singularity theorems, multi-messenger astronomy, conformal cyclic cosmology

**Key moves**
- Convert cosmological successes into questions for fundamental physics.
- Give the full ΛCDM + inflation timeline in one paragraph.
- Argue from the singularity theorems that the initial singularity is generic, not an artefact of symmetry.
- Point to observation (multi-messenger, LISA) as the arbiter and to Hawking-Ellis as the reader's next destination.

## Concepts

### critical density

*definition · core* · also: rho_crit, ρcrit

The density that makes the spatial sections of a Friedmann-Robertson-Walker model exactly flat for a given expansion rate, ρ = 3H²/8π in geometric units (3H²/8πG in SI). The book reserves the name for today's value 3H0²/8π, of order 10⁻²⁶ kg m⁻³, and in §26.7 reintroduces the time-dependent version ρcrit(t) = 3H(t)²/8π.

**How introduced:** Read off from the Λ = 0 Friedmann equation H² = 8πρ/3 − k/R² by asking which density kills the curvature term; frozen at t0 to give one number, then compared with the actual density to decide k.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann equation, Hubble parameter, curvature index k

$$
\rho_{\rm crit}=\frac{3H_0^2}{8\pi}
$$

$$
\rho_{\rm crit}(t)=\frac{3H^2(t)}{8\pi}
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 26.2 turns 1/H0 ≈ 10¹⁰ yr into about 1.8×10⁻²⁶ kg m⁻³ (my evaluation). For H0 = 72 km/s/Mpc it is 9.7×10⁻²⁷ kg m⁻³.

### density parameter

*physical-quantity · core* · also: Omega, Ω

Present mean density divided by the critical density, Ω = 8πρ(t0)/3H0². When Λ = 0, Ω > 1, Ω = 1 and Ω < 1 correspond to k = +1, 0 and −1.

**How introduced:** Introduced immediately after ρcrit as the standard bookkeeping device of cosmology, then split into components once Λ is included.

**Prerequisites:** critical density

$$
\Omega=\frac{8\pi\rho(t_0)}{3H_0^2}
$$

**Where:** DIV §26.1 p.539 (pdf 554); DIV §26.1 p.540 (pdf 555)

### component density parameters

*physical-quantity · core* · also: Omega_r, Omega_m, Omega_Lambda, (26.9)

Present densities of radiation, matter and vacuum energy, each divided by ρcrit: Ωr = ρr(t0)/ρcrit, Ωm = ρm(t0)/ρcrit, ΩΛ = ρΛ/ρcrit = Λ/3H0². Together with H0 they specify an FRW model; observed values are roughly Ωr ≈ 10⁻⁴, Ωm ≈ 0.3, ΩΛ ≈ 0.7.

**How introduced:** Defined to make the densities in the multi-component Friedmann equation dimensionless; afterwards they serve as coordinates of the model plane and as the targets of observation.

**Prerequisites:** critical density, scaling of densities with the scale factor, vacuum energy as a perfect fluid

$$
\Omega_r=\frac{\rho_r(t_0)}{\rho_{\rm crit}},\ \Omega_m=\frac{\rho_m(t_0)}{\rho_{\rm crit}},\ \Omega_\Lambda=\frac{\rho_\Lambda}{\rho_{\rm crit}}
$$

**Where:** DIV §26.1 p.540 (pdf 555); DIV §26.3 p.549 (pdf 564); DIV §26.4 p.554 (pdf 569)

### curvature density parameter

*definition · developed* · also: Omega_c, Ω_k in most modern texts

Ωc = −k/(H0²R0²): the curvature term of the Friedmann equation written as if it were a density fraction. It is positive for open (k = −1), zero for flat and negative for closed (k = +1) models, and equals twice the 'energy' E of the particle analogy.

**How introduced:** Named so that evaluating the dimensionless Friedmann equation at the present epoch produces a sum rule, making curvature measurable from the contents.

**Prerequisites:** dimensionless Friedmann equation, curvature index k

$$
\Omega_c=-\frac{k}{H_0^2R_0^2}
$$

$$
R_0=\frac{1}{H_0|\Omega_c|^{1/2}}\ (k\neq0)
$$

**Notes:** Sign convention matches the modern Ω_k.

**Where:** DIV §26.1 p.541 (pdf 556); DIV §26.4 p.553 (pdf 568)

### cosmic sum rule

*identity · core* · also: closure relation, Ωr + Ωm + ΩΛ + Ωc = 1

At the present epoch the density parameters of all components plus the curvature parameter add to exactly one, so the spatial curvature follows from the contents: Ωc = 1 − (Ωr + Ωm + ΩΛ).

**How introduced:** Obtained by setting t = t0 in the dimensionless Friedmann equation, where R̃ = 1 and dR̃/dt̃ = Ṙ/(R0H0) = 1 (Exercise 26.5).

**Prerequisites:** component density parameters, curvature density parameter, dimensionless Friedmann equation

$$
\Omega_r+\Omega_m+\Omega_\Lambda+\Omega_c=1
$$

**Where:** DIV §26.1 p.541 (pdf 556)

### vacuum energy as a perfect fluid

*definition · core* · also: cosmological constant as a source, rho_Lambda, equation of state p = −ρ

Moving Λg_ab to the source side of Einstein's equations makes the cosmological constant equivalent to a perfect fluid of constant density ρΛ = Λ/8π and pressure p = −ρΛ. Such a fluid does not dilute as space expands.

**How introduced:** Read off by matching the Λ terms of Friedmann's equations (24.52)-(24.53) against density and pressure (Exercise 26.3). Reused in §26.5 to interpret dark energy as quantum zero-point energy and in §26.7 for the slow-roll stress tensor.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann equation, perfect fluid energy-momentum tensor

$$
\rho_\Lambda=\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi},\quad p_\Lambda=-\rho_\Lambda
$$

**Where:** DIV §26.1 p.540 (pdf 555); DIV §26.5 p.557 (pdf 572)

### multi-component Friedmann equation

*law · core* · also: (26.6), Friedmann equation with radiation, matter and Λ

Ṙ²/R² = (8π/3)(ρr + ρm + ρΛ) − k/R²: the squared expansion rate is fixed by the total density of all components together with the spatial curvature term.

**How introduced:** Obtained by rewriting (24.52) with the Λ term absorbed as a fluid; generalizes the one-component treatments of Chapter 25.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann equation, vacuum energy as a perfect fluid

### scaling of densities with the scale factor

*law · developed* · also: ρm ∝ R⁻³, ρr ∝ R⁻⁴, ρΛ constant

In an expanding FRW universe pressure-free matter dilutes as R⁻³, radiation as R⁻⁴ (dilution plus redshift of each quantum) and vacuum energy stays constant. Hence radiation dominates at early times, matter at intermediate times and Λ at late times, with ρr/ρm ∝ 1/R.

**How introduced:** Quoted from energy conservation in Chapter 25 to express each density as ρcrit Ω R̃⁻ⁿ; used again in §26.3 to derive T ∝ 1/R and in §26.6 to locate matter-radiation equality.

**Prerequisites:** energy conservation in FRW models

$$
\rho_m=\rho_{\rm crit}\Omega_m\tilde R^{-3},\ \rho_r=\rho_{\rm crit}\Omega_r\tilde R^{-4},\ \rho_\Lambda=\rho_{\rm crit}\Omega_\Lambda

### dimensionless Friedmann equation

*technique · core* · also: (26.13), normalized scale factor R̃, Hubble-time units

Friedmann's equation in R̃ = R/R0 and t̃ = H0 t: (dR̃/dt̃)² = Ωm/R̃ + Ωr/R̃² + ΩΛR̃² + Ωc. The Ω's alone fix the shape of R̃(t̃); H0 and, for k ≠ 0, R0 only rescale time and length.

**Prerequisites:** multi-component Friedmann equation, component density parameters, Hubble time

### Hubble time

*physical-quantity · revisited* · also: t_H, 1/H0

The reciprocal of the Hubble constant, the natural expansion timescale; about 13.6 Gyr for H0 = 72 km/s/Mpc. Cosmic ages are naturally quoted as multiples of it (H0t0).

### effective potential for the scale factor

*technique · core* · also: particle-in-a-potential analogy, V(R̃), (26.17)-(26.19)

Writing the dimensionless Friedmann equation as ½(dR̃/dt̃)² + V(R̃) = E with V = −½(Ωm/R̃ + Ωr/R̃² + ΩΛR̃²) and E = Ωc/2 turns cosmic expansion into one-dimensional motion of a unit mass. Where V > E the motion is forbidden; where V = E the expansion rate vanishes (a turning point).

**How introduced:** Offered as an aid for analysing (26.13); used to decide which models start from R = 0, to derive the no-big-bang boundary, and inside the square root of the exact luminosity-distance integral.

**Prerequisites:** dimensionless Friedmann equation, energy conservation in one-dimensional motion

$$
\tfrac12\left(\frac{d\tilde R}{d\tilde t}\right)^2+V(\tilde R)=E
$$

$$
V(\tilde R)=-\tfrac12\left(\frac{\Omega_m}{\tilde R}+\frac{\Omega_r}{\tilde R^2}+\Omega_\Lambda\tilde R^2\right)
$$

$$
2E=\Omega_c
$$

**Notes:** E is bookkeeping, not a physical energy; the analogy reappears in §26.7 for the inflaton.

**Where:** DIV §26.1 p.541 (pdf 556); DIV §26.1 p.543 (pdf 558); DIV §26.1 p.544 (pdf 559); DIV §26.4 p.553 (pdf 568)

### four-parameter specification of FRW models

*principle · core* · also: cosmological parameters Ωr, Ωm, ΩΛ, H0

A homogeneous isotropic model containing radiation, matter and a cosmological constant is completely fixed by Ωr, Ωm, ΩΛ and H0: E follows from the sum rule, R̃(t̃) from a quadrature, and the physical R(t) = R0 R̃(H0 t) with R0 = 1/(H0|Ωc|^{1/2}) when k ≠ 0.

**How introduced:** Stated in a highlighted panel after the quadrature and rescaling; justifies reducing cosmology to measuring four numbers, and, with Ωr small, to a plane.

**Prerequisites:** dimensionless Friedmann equation, cosmic sum rule

$$

**Where:** DIV §26.1 p.542 (pdf 557)

With radiation neglected, each point (Ωm, ΩΛ) is an FRW model. ### open, flat and closed universes

*definition · revisited* · also: k = −1, 0, +1, spatial curvature of FRW models

Spatial sections of negative, zero or positive constant curvature. In Ω language a model is closed if Ωr + Ωm + ΩΛ > 1, flat if the sum is exactly 1 and open if it is less. With Λ ≠ 0, geometry no longer decides whether the universe recollapses.

**How introduced:** First via ρ0 versus ρcrit (26.3), then generalized with Λ to the line ΩΛ = 1 − Ωm in the model plane.

### deceleration parameter

*physical-quantity · revisited* · also: q, q0

q = −RR̈/Ṙ², positive when expansion slows and negative when it speeds up. For matter plus Λ at the present epoch q0 = Ωm/2 − ΩΛ, so the model accelerates above the line ΩΛ = Ωm/2; for (0.3, 0.7), q0 = −0.55.

**How introduced:** Takes (24.79) and substitutes ρm(t0) = Ωmρcrit and Λ = 8πρΛ.

**Where:** DIV §26.1 p.543 (pdf 558)

### big bang versus bounce criterion

*technique · developed* · also: no-big-bang region, V_max = E boundary, recollapse boundary

For Ωm + ΩΛ > 1 (E < 0) and ΩΛ > 0 the potential has a maximum V_max = −(3/4)(2Ωm²ΩΛ)^{1/3} at R̃³ = Ωm/2ΩΛ. If V_max < E the solution climbs over the barrier from R = 0 (a big bang). If V_max > E there are two turning points: either expansion from R = 0 to a maximum and recollapse, or contraction from large R to a minimum and re-expansion with no big bang. The condition V_max = E defines boundary curves in the (Ωm, ΩΛ) plane.

**How introduced:** Differentiate V, substitute the stationary point, equate to E and cube to a cubic in ΩΛ with three real roots; the root through (0, 1) bounds the no-big-bang region, the root through (1, 0) separates eternal expansion from recollapse, and the third is unphysical.

**Prerequisites:** effective potential for the scale factor, (Ωm, ΩΛ) classification plane

$$
V_{\max}=-\tfrac34(2\Omega_m^2\Omega_\Lambda)^{1/3}
$$

$$
1-(\Omega_m+\Omega_\Lambda)=-\tfrac32(2\Omega_m^2\Omega_\Lambda)^{1/3}
$$

**Notes:** The argument that E ≥ 0 rules out turning points assumes ΩΛ ≥ 0; with ΩΛ < 0 the potential rises at large R̃ and every model recollapses. In the printed discussion the turning-point condition is Ṙ = 0 (dR̃/dt̃ = 0); the reading copy drops the overdots, and one printed instance ('no points where R = 0') also lacks the dot.

**Where:** DIV §26.1 p.543 (pdf 558); DIV §26.1 p.544 (pdf 559); DIV §26.1 p.545 (pdf 560)

### age of the universe

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: H0 t0, (26.29)-(26.30)

Cosmic time elapsed since R = 0: H0t0 = ∫₀¹ dR̃ [ΩrR̃⁻² + ΩmR̃⁻¹ + ΩΛR̃² + Ωc]^{−1/2}. For a flat matter-plus-Λ model this integrates to (2/3)(1 − Ωm)^{−1/2} sinh⁻¹[((1 − Ωm)/Ωm)^{1/2}], about 0.96 Hubble times for Ωm = 0.3, compared with 2/3 for the Einstein-de Sitter model.

**How introduced:** Separating variables in the dimensionless Friedmann equation and integrating from R̃ = 0 to 1; §26.5 uses the result to argue that Λ relieves the tension with ages of old stars.

H_0t_0=\int_0^1\frac{d\tilde R}{[\Omega_r\tilde R^{-2}+\Omega_m\tilde R^{-1}+\Omega_\Lambda\tilde R^2+\Omega_c]^{1/2}}

A class of source assumed to have the same intrinsic luminosity everywhere and at every epoch, so its apparent brightness yields its luminosity distance. The book names main-sequence stars, Cepheid variables and Type Ia supernovae.

### cosmic microwave background

*phenomenon · core* · also: CMB, relic radiation

Nearly perfectly isotropic black-body radiation at T0 = 2.725 K filling the universe: thermal radiation released when the universe became transparent and redshifted by about a factor 1000 since. It is evidence for a hot big bang and for large-scale isotropy, and it fixes Ωr.

**How introduced:** Introduced as the gateway to Ωr and as observational justification of the Robertson-Walker metric, with the story of its prediction and detection.

**Prerequisites:** black-body spectrum, temperature-redshift relation

$$
T_0=2.725\pm0.001\,{\rm K}
$$

### black-body spectrum

*law · developed* · also: Planck distribution, (26.35)

Energy density per unit frequency of radiation in thermal equilibrium, f(ν) = (8πh/c³) ν³/(e^{hν/k_BT} − 1). In x = hν/k_BT it peaks at x ≈ 2.82; the mean photon energy is about 2.7 k_BT.

**How introduced:** Imported from statistical physics (Level 2) with a dimensionless plot; its peak and mean are used for the decoupling estimate.

**Prerequisites:** thermal energy k_BT, photon energy E = hν

f(\nu)=\frac{8\pi h}{c^3}\frac{\nu^3}{e^{h\nu/k_BT}-1}

**Notes:** The book quotes E_max ≈ 2.7k_BT and E_mean ≈ 3k_BT, and its printed stationarity condition lacks a factor e^x.

### radiation energy density law

*law · developed* · also: Stefan-Boltzmann law, ε = αT⁴, radiation constant α

Integrating the Planck spectrum over frequency gives total radiation energy density ε = αT⁴ with α = π²k_B⁴/(15ħ³c³) = 7.565×10⁻¹⁶ J m⁻³ K⁻⁴.

**How introduced:** Derived by the substitution y = hν/k_BT (Exercise 26.8) and applied at T0 to give ε ≈ 4.17×10⁻¹⁴ J m⁻³ and ΩCMB ≈ 5×10⁻⁵.

\epsilon_{\rm rad}=\alpha T^4,\ \alpha=\frac{\pi^2k_B^4}{15\hbar^3c^3}

**Where:** DIV §26.3 p.549 (pdf 564)

*law · core* · also: T ∝ 1/R, cosmic cooling

Because radiation energy density falls as R⁻⁴ and equals αT⁴, the radiation temperature scales as 1/R: the universe was 1000 times hotter when 1000 times smaller, and T/T0 = 1 + z.

**How introduced:** Obtained by combining (26.36) with the R⁻⁴ law; singled out by the authors as one of the two results to retain from the Level 2 material.

### preservation of the black-body form under expansion

*theorem · developed* · also: thermal spectrum invariance

A thermal spectrum stays thermal as the universe expands: every frequency and the temperature both scale as 1/R, so hν/k_BT is unchanged, while the ν³ prefactor rescales exactly like inverse volume. A black body at decoupling is therefore a black body today at a lower temperature.

**How introduced:** Raised as a worry (the temperature changes, so why should old radiation still look thermal?) and resolved by the scaling argument; used to explain why the CMB is such a perfect black body.

**Prerequisites:** temperature-redshift relation, redshift

**Where:** DIV §26.3 p.550 (pdf 565); DIV §26.3 p.551 (pdf 566)

### decoupling

*phenomenon · core* · also: recombination, epoch of last scattering

The rather quick change, at T ≈ 3000 K when the universe was about a thousandth of its present size, from an opaque ionized plasma in which photons scatter constantly to a transparent gas of neutral atoms through which CMB photons travel freely.

**How introduced:** Modelled with hydrogen plus photons; a crude estimate from 3k_BT = 14 eV (about 50,000 K) is corrected by the high-energy tail of the spectrum and the large number of photons per atom.

**Prerequisites:** black-body spectrum, temperature-redshift relation, photon-to-baryon ratio

$$
T_{\rm dec}\simeq3000\,{\rm K}
$$

**Notes:** Standard texts distinguish recombination (atoms form) from photon decoupling; the book merges them and rounds hydrogen's 13.6 eV ionization energy to 14 eV.

**Where:** DIV §26.3 p.550 (pdf 565); DIV §26.3 p.551 (pdf 566); DIV §26.6 p.558 (pdf 573)

### photon-to-baryon ratio

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: photon excess

Number of photons per baryon, very large in our universe (about 10⁹, a figure not given in the book). Because of it, the rare photons in the spectral tail can keep hydrogen ionized until k_BT is far below the ionization energy.

**How introduced:** Given as the second reason the crude decoupling estimate is too high by about ten.

**Prerequisites:** black-body spectrum

**Where:** DIV §26.3 p.550 (pdf 565)

### last scattering surface

*definition · developed* · also: surface of last scattering

The sphere, centred on a given observer, on which the CMB photons now arriving were last scattered at decoupling. It is not a physical shell: every point emitted at decoupling, and observers at other places or epochs see other spheres. For us its radius is thousands of Mpc.

**How introduced:** Introduced right after decoupling with an emphasis on its observer dependence; used in §26.7 to state the horizon problem.

**Prerequisites:** decoupling, past light cone

### baryon density parameter

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: Omega_baryon

Fraction of the critical density in ordinary matter made of protons and neutrons, about 0.04 from nucleosynthesis: four times the 0.01 in stars but about a seventh of Ωm ≈ 0.3.

**How introduced:** Used to show that most ordinary matter is dark and that most matter is non-baryonic.

**Prerequisites:** big bang nucleosynthesis

$$
\Omega_{\rm baryon}\simeq0.04

**Notes:** Current value ≈ 0.049.

**Where:** DIV §26.4 p.552 (pdf 567); DIV §26.5 p.556 (pdf 571)

### galaxy rotation curve

*experiment-or-observation · developed* · also: flat rotation curves

Orbital speed of stars and gas plotted against radius r in a galaxy. Newtonian circular orbits give v = √(GM(r)/r), so outside the visible mass v should fall like r^{−1/2}; observed curves stay roughly flat, implying M(r) ∝ r in an unseen halo.

**How introduced:** Derived from F = ma for a circular orbit as the first clue to non-luminous matter.

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian circular orbits

$$
v=\sqrt{GM(r)/r}
$$

**Where:** DIV §26.4 p.552 (pdf 567)

### dark matter

*phenomenon · core* · also: cold dark matter, CDM

Matter detected through gravity but emitting no light. Its favoured form, cold dark matter, is non-baryonic, moves slowly compared with light, and interacts with ordinary matter essentially only gravitationally; it supplies most of Ωm ≈ 0.3.

**How introduced:** Motivated by rotation curves and by the gap between Ω_baryon and Ωm; candidates are surveyed in §26.5 and its role in structure formation is noted in §26.9.

**Prerequisites:** galaxy rotation curve, baryon density parameter

**Where:** DIV §26.4 p.552 (pdf 567); DIV §26.5 p.556 (pdf 571); DIV §26.5 p.557 (pdf 572); DIV §26.9 p.567 (pdf 582)

### dark matter candidates

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: primordial black holes, MACHOs, hot dark matter, axions, WIMPs

Proposed constituents of dark matter: primordial black holes (they must predate nucleosynthesis to escape the baryon bound), massive compact halo objects (seen in microlensing towards the Large Magellanic Cloud but too few), light massive neutrinos (relativistic, hence 'hot', and limited by mass bounds), axions (hypothetical light particles also motivated by QCD) and supersymmetric weakly interacting massive particles (sought by direct detection and accelerators). None is confirmed.

**How introduced:** Surveyed in §26.5, first astrophysical then particle-physics options, ending with the admission that the answer may be something not yet imagined.

**Prerequisites:** dark matter, big bang nucleosynthesis

**Where:** DIV §26.5 p.556 (pdf 571); DIV §26.5 p.557 (pdf 572)

### dark energy

*phenomenon · core* · also: Λ as dark energy

Whatever accounts for ΩΛ ≈ 0.7 and the present acceleration. It may be a geometric constant Λ needing no more explanation than G, the quantum vacuum's zero-point energy (a fluid with p = −ρ), a dynamical scalar field, or a sign of modified gravity.

**How introduced:** Introduced in the audit of ΛCDM with the two readings of the cosmological term (left or right side of Einstein's equations).

**Prerequisites:** vacuum energy as a perfect fluid

**Where:** DIV §26.5 p.556 (pdf 571); DIV §26.5 p.557 (pdf 572); DIV §26.9 p.568 (pdf 583)

### cosmological constant problem

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: vacuum energy discrepancy

If Λ is quantum zero-point energy, standard particle physics predicts a vacuum density more than a hundred orders of magnitude larger than observed.

**How introduced:** Stated as the main difficulty of the vacuum-energy interpretation, with the hope that supersymmetry or string theory might fix it.

**Prerequisites:** dark energy, vacuum energy as a perfect fluid

**Notes:** Often quoted as about 120 orders of magnitude.

**Where:** DIV §26.5 p.557 (pdf 572)

### quintessence

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: dynamical dark energy

An alternative to a strict cosmological constant in which dark energy is a slowly evolving scalar field φ in a potential V(φ).

**How introduced:** Mentioned among dark energy options, foreshadowing the scalar-field treatment of inflation.

**Prerequisites:** dark energy, inflaton scalar field

### averaging problem in cosmology

*phenomenon · mention* · also: backreaction

Because Einstein's equations are non-linear, the spacetime obtained from a smoothed matter distribution need not equal the average of the true lumpy spacetime, so fitting a Robertson-Walker model to an inhomogeneous universe could bias inferred parameters such as ΩΛ.

**How introduced:** Raised, citing Ellis (2011), as a theoretical objection to the inference of a cosmological constant.

**Prerequisites:** Robertson-Walker metric, cosmological principle

**Where:** DIV §26.5 p.557 (pdf 572)

### ΛCDM model

*solution-or-model · core* · also: concordance model, standard cosmological model

Spatially flat FRW model with a positive cosmological constant and cold dark matter, Ωm ≈ 0.3 and ΩΛ ≈ 0.7, radiation negligible except early, preceded by inflation. Once radiation is unimportant R̃ = (Ωm/ΩΛ)^{1/3} sinh^{2/3}(t/tΛ), passing from t^{2/3} deceleration to exponential de Sitter-like acceleration.

**How introduced:** Identified in §26.4 where supernova and CMB constraints cross; §26.5 lists its successes, exact solution and unexplained ingredients; §26.9 recaps its full history.

**Prerequisites:** (Ωm, ΩΛ) classification plane, dark matter, dark energy, exact luminosity distance-redshift relation, CMB anisotropies and angular power spectrum

$$
\tilde R(t)=(\Omega_m/\Omega_\Lambda)^{1/3}\sinh^{2/3}(t/t_\Lambda)

$$
t_\Lambda=\frac{2}{3H_0\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda}}

**Where:** DIV §26.4 p.554 (pdf 569); DIV §26.5 p.555 (pdf 570); DIV §26.5 p.556 (pdf 571); DIV §26.9 p.568 (pdf 583)

### de Sitter expansion

*solution-or-model · revisited* · also: exponential expansion, R ∝ e^{Ht}

Expansion with constant Hubble rate, R ∝ e^{Ht}, produced when a cosmological constant (or anything with T_ab ∝ g_ab) dominates. It is the late-time fate of ΛCDM with H = H0√ΩΛ and the form of inflationary expansion.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapter 25 for the far future of ΛCDM and for slow-roll inflation.

**Prerequisites:** vacuum energy as a perfect fluid

R\propto e^{Ht}

### matter-radiation equality

*phenomenon · developed* · also: t_eq, R̃_eq

The epoch when radiation and matter densities are equal. Since ρr/ρm = (Ωr/Ωm)/R̃, it happens at R̃_eq = Ωr/Ωm ≈ 2×10⁻⁴, around 10¹² s (tens of thousands of years); radiation dominates before, matter after.

**How introduced:** Found from the ratio of the two scaling laws with Ωr = 7×10⁻⁵, Ωm = 0.3.

**Prerequisites:** scaling of densities with the scale factor, component density parameters

\tilde R_{\rm eq}=\Omega_r/\Omega_m\simeq2\times10^{-4}

### thermal history of the universe

*phenomenon · developed* · also: epochs of the early universe, Table 26.2

The sequence of states implied by T ∝ 1/R: free quarks and leptons before about 10⁻⁴ s, free nucleons until about 1 s (≈1 MeV), nuclei in a radiation-dominated plasma until about 10¹² s, a matter-dominated plasma until about 10¹³ s, and neutral atoms with free-streaming photons thereafter.

**How introduced:** Built from two scaling laws and a few anchor values, then tabulated and illustrated.

**Prerequisites:** temperature-redshift relation, matter-radiation equality, decoupling

**Where:** DIV §26.6 p.558 (pdf 573); DIV §26.6 p.559 (pdf 574); DIV §26.6 p.560 (pdf 575)

### temperature-time relation in the radiation era

*law · developed* · also: T ≈ 1.5×10¹⁰ K/√t

During radiation domination R ∝ t^{1/2} and T ∝ 1/R, so T ∝ t^{−1/2}; normalized at equality the book finds T ≈ 1.5×10¹⁰ K (t/1 s)^{−1/2}, i.e. about 1 MeV when the universe is one second old.

**How introduced:** Obtained by changing the exponent in the matter-era relation and matching at t_eq; Exercise 26.9 derives it independently from ρr c² = αT⁴ and H = 1/2t.

**Prerequisites:** temperature-redshift relation, radiation-dominated expansion

T\simeq\frac{1.5\times10^{10}}{\sqrt{t}}

**Where:** DIV §26.6 p.559 (pdf 574)

### quark-hadron phase transition

*phenomenon · mention*

Transition at energies of a few hundred MeV (T ~ 2×10¹² K, t ~ 10⁻⁴ s) above which protons and neutrons dissolve into free quarks.

**How introduced:** Mentioned as the earliest epoch reached by the scaling argument.

**Prerequisites:** thermal history of the universe

**Where:** DIV §26.6 p.559 (pdf 574); DIV §26.7 p.561 (pdf 576)

### flatness problem

*phenomenon · core* · also: fine-tuning of Ω

In decelerating expansion |1 − ρ/ρcrit(t)| = |k|/(HR)² grows with time (∝ t^{2/3} when matter dominates, ∝ t for radiation), so a universe near critical density today must have started absurdly close to it; the problem is to explain that tuning rather than assume it.

**How introduced:** Derived from a time-dependent critical density and extrapolated back from today's bound to matter-radiation equality and the quark-hadron epoch.

**Prerequisites:** critical density, multi-component Friedmann equation

$$
\left|1-\frac{\rho_{\rm total}}{\rho_{\rm crit}(t)}\right|=\frac{|k|}{H^2R^2}
$$

**Where:** DIV §26.7 p.560 (pdf 575); DIV §26.7 p.561 (pdf 576)

### particle horizon

*definition · revisited* · also: horizon distance

The greatest proper distance from which a signal emitted at the big bang can have reached a given observer by time t, Δr = R(t)∫₀ᵗ dt'/R(t'). It equals 2t for R ∝ t^{1/2} but grows like e^{Ht}/H under exponential expansion.

**How introduced:** Revisited from Chapter 25 by integrating a radial null ray in a flat Robertson-Walker metric.

**Prerequisites:** comoving radial coordinate χ

$$
\Delta r=R(t)\int_0^t\frac{dt'}{R(t')}
$$

**Where:** DIV §26.7 p.561 (pdf 576); DIV §26.7 p.562 (pdf 577); DIV §26.7 p.563 (pdf 578)

### horizon problem

*phenomenon · core* · also: causality problem

CMB photons arriving from opposite directions left points on the last scattering surface whose past light cones, traced back to the big bang, do not overlap; nothing could have brought them to a common temperature, yet they agree to one part in 10⁴. In a standard hot big bang only patches within about 2° on the sky shared any causal past.

**How introduced:** Introduced through the everyday idea that homogeneity needs mixing, then the particle horizon integral and a light-cone picture.

**Prerequisites:** particle horizon, last scattering surface, past light cone

**Where:** DIV §26.7 p.561 (pdf 576); DIV §26.7 p.562 (pdf 577)

### cosmic inflation

*solution-or-model · core* · also: inflation, inflationary epoch

A hypothesized very early phase (as early as ~10⁻³⁴ s) of nearly exponential expansion that blows a small, causally connected, thermalized patch up beyond the present observable universe, dilutes irregularities, drives Ω towards 1, and turns quantum fluctuations into the seeds of structure.

**How introduced:** Proposed as the common cure of the flatness and horizon problems by re-evaluating the very formulas that expose them for R ∝ e^{Ht}.

**Prerequisites:** flatness problem, horizon problem, de Sitter expansion

$$
R\propto e^{\tilde Ht}
$$

**Where:** DIV §26.7 p.562 (pdf 577); DIV §26.7 p.563 (pdf 578); DIV §26.7 p.565 (pdf 580); DIV §26.8 p.567 (pdf 582)

### inflaton scalar field

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: scalar field φ, inflaton

A scalar field with Lagrangian density √−g[−½g^{ab}∇aφ∇bφ + V(φ)] (book's sign convention, signature +−−−). It obeys □φ + V'(φ) = 0, reduces for a homogeneous field in an FRW background to φ̈ + 3Hφ̇ + V' = 0, and has energy-momentum tensor T_ab = ∇aφ∇bφ + g_ab[−½(∇φ)² + V].

**How introduced:** Introduced as one possible engine for inflation using the book's variational methods, with the field equation and T_ab left as Exercise 26.12.

**Prerequisites:** variational derivation of the energy-momentum tensor, Robertson-Walker metric

$$
\ddot\phi+3H\dot\phi+V'(\phi)=0
$$

$$
T_{ab}=\nabla_a\phi\nabla_b\phi+g_{ab}\left[-\tfrac12g^{cd}\nabla_c\phi\nabla_d\phi+V(\phi)\right]
$$

**Where:** DIV §26.7 p.563 (pdf 578); DIV §26.7 p.564 (pdf 579)

### Hubble friction

*phenomenon · developed* · also: Hubble damping

The 3Hφ̇ term in the homogeneous scalar-field equation, which acts like friction on a particle rolling in the potential V(φ); when H is large compared with the slope of V it keeps φ̇ small.

**How introduced:** Pointed out by reading (26.66) as a damped particle equation.

**Prerequisites:** inflaton scalar field

**Where:** DIV §26.7 p.564 (pdf 579)

### slow-roll inflation

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: slow roll, reheating

Inflation driven by a scalar field on a nearly flat stretch of its potential, where Hubble friction keeps φ ≈ φ*. Its stress tensor is then T_ab ≈ V(φ*)g_ab, an effective cosmological constant giving de Sitter expansion, until φ reaches the steep part, falls to the minimum and the released energy reheats the universe into radiation domination.

**How introduced:** Derived from the damped field equation and illustrated with a plateau-then-well potential.

**Prerequisites:** inflaton scalar field, Hubble friction, vacuum energy as a perfect fluid

$$
T_{ab}\simeq V(\phi_*)g_{ab}
$$

**Notes:** The book gives H = (V/3)^{1/2}; with ρ = V and H² = 8πρ/3 one expects H = (8πV/3)^{1/2}. Reheating appears only as a figure label.

**Where:** DIV §26.7 p.564 (pdf 579); DIV §26.7 p.565 (pdf 580)

### primordial density perturbations from inflation

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: inflaton fluctuations

Quantum fluctuations δφ of the inflaton create density fluctuations δρ ≈ V'(φ*)δφ that inflation stretches to cosmic scales; they appear later as CMB anisotropies and seed galaxies, and the predicted spectrum shape agrees with data and constrains V.

**How introduced:** Sketched briefly as a bonus of inflation beyond its original motivation.

**Prerequisites:** cosmic inflation, CMB anisotropies and angular power spectrum

$$
\delta\rho\simeq V'(\phi_*)\delta\phi
$$

**Where:** DIV §26.7 p.565 (pdf 580)

### gravitational instability

*phenomenon · mention* · also: structure formation

Growth of density contrasts: slightly overdense regions attract matter and grow denser, underdense regions empty out. Simulations reproduce observed galaxy clustering only when dark matter is included.

**How introduced:** Mentioned in the closing section as the mechanism the book has not treated.

**Prerequisites:** primordial density perturbations from inflation, dark matter

### anthropic principle

*principle · introduced*

The explanatory stance that observed features of the universe are constrained by the requirement that observers like us can exist in it.

### weak anthropic principle

*principle · introduced* · also: WAP

In the book's version: only some regions or epochs of the universe allow life to develop, so we necessarily observe one of them. Example: we find the universe many billions of years old because galaxies, heavy elements, planets and evolution take that long.

**How introduced:** Stated in a highlighted panel and illustrated by the age argument.

**Prerequisites:** anthropic principle

### strong anthropic principle

*principle · introduced* · also: SAP

In the book's version: of many separate universes (quantum branches, or disjoint regions with their own initial conditions or even laws) only a few allow life. Fine-tuned constants are then read as a selection effect rather than design.

**How introduced:** Motivated through quantum cosmology and many-worlds, then subjected to three objections.

**Prerequisites:** anthropic principle, many-worlds interpretation

**Notes:** Differs from Barrow and Tipler's usage (the universe must have properties allowing life); the book follows Hawking (1988).

### many-worlds interpretation

*historical-idea · mention* · also: Everett-Wheeler interpretation

Reading of quantum mechanics in which the universe continually splits into many non-interacting branches, one for each possible outcome.

**How introduced:** Mentioned, with a branching-tree figure, as one route to many universes for the strong anthropic principle.

**Prerequisites:** quantum cosmology

**Where:** DIV §26.8 p.566 (pdf 581)

### fine-tuning of fundamental constants

*phenomenon · mention* · also: argument from design

The observation that unexplained constants appear finely adjusted: small changes would give universes unable to support life. It is interpreted either as evidence of design or as support for anthropic selection.

**How introduced:** Raised as the evidence behind the strong anthropic principle.

**Prerequisites:** strong anthropic principle

**Where:** DIV §26.8 p.566 (pdf 581)

### Copernican principle

*principle · mention* · also: mediocrity principle

The view that we occupy no privileged position: a typical planet, star, galaxy and cluster. The book frames this through the cosmological principle and uses it against the human-centred strong anthropic principle.

**How introduced:** Cited as the historical trend that the strong anthropic principle would reverse.

### arrows of time

Distinct time directions: the thermodynamic arrow of increasing entropy, cosmological (world) time defined by expansion, and psychological time as experienced; anthropic reasoning is suggested as a way to ask why they agree.

## Key equations

### (26.1) Friedmann equation with Λ = 0 · central · DIV §26.1 p.539 (pdf 554)

$$
H^2=\frac{8\pi}{3}\rho-\frac{k}{R^2}
$$

Squared Hubble rate balances density against spatial curvature; the starting point for defining the critical density.

**Symbols:** H = Ṙ/R; ρ density; k curvature index; R scale factor (G = c = 1)

### (26.2) Critical density · central · DIV §26.1 p.539 (pdf 554)

$$
\rho_{\rm crit}=\frac{3H_0^2}{8\pi}
$$

Present density that would make k = 0; of order 10⁻²⁶ kg m⁻³.

**Symbols:** H0 present Hubble constant

### (26.3) Density and spatial geometry · supporting · DIV §26.1 p.539 (pdf 554)

$$
\rho_0>\rho_{\rm crit}\Rightarrow k=+1,\quad \rho_0=\rho_{\rm crit}\Rightarrow k=0,\quad \rho_0<\rho_{\rm crit}\Rightarrow k=-1
$$

For Λ = 0 the actual density compared with critical decides closed, flat or open.

**Symbols:** ρ0 = ρ(t0)

### (26.4) Density parameter · central · DIV §26.1 p.539 (pdf 554)

$$
\Omega:=\frac{\rho(t_0)}{\rho_{\rm crit}}=\frac{8\pi\rho(t_0)}{3H_0^2}
$$

Present density in units of the critical density; Ω > 1 closed, = 1 flat, < 1 open (Λ = 0).

### (26.5) Vacuum energy density · central · DIV §26.1 p.540 (pdf 555)

$$
\rho_\Lambda=\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi},\qquad p_\Lambda=-\rho_\Lambda
$$

The cosmological constant behaves as a fluid of constant density with negative pressure.

**Symbols:** Λ cosmological constant

### (26.6) Multi-component Friedmann equation · central · DIV §26.1 p.540 (pdf 555)

$$
\frac{\dot R^2}{R^2}=\frac{8\pi}{3}\left(\rho_r+\rho_m+\rho_\Lambda\right)-\frac{k}{R^2}
$$

Expansion rate set by radiation, matter and vacuum densities plus curvature.

**Symbols:** ρr, ρm, ρΛ radiation, matter, vacuum densities

### (26.7)-(26.8) Dimensionless scale factor and time · supporting · DIV §26.1 p.540 (pdf 555)

$$
\tilde R(t):=\frac{R(t)}{R(t_0)},\qquad \tilde t:=\frac{t}{t_H}=H_0t
$$

Measure size relative to today and time in Hubble times.

**Symbols:** t_H = 1/H0 Hubble time

### (26.9) Component density parameters · central · DIV §26.1 p.540 (pdf 555)

$$
\Omega_r:=\frac{\rho_r(t_0)}{\rho_{\rm crit}},\quad \Omega_m:=\frac{\rho_m(t_0)}{\rho_{\rm crit}},\quad \Omega_\Lambda:=\frac{\rho_\Lambda(t_0)}{\rho_{\rm crit}}
$$

Present fractions of critical density in radiation, matter and vacuum energy.

### (26.10)-(26.12) Densities in terms of Ω and R̃ · supporting · DIV §26.1 p.540 (pdf 555)

$$
\rho_m=\frac{\rho_{\rm crit}\Omega_m}{\tilde R^3},\qquad \rho_r=\frac{\rho_{\rm crit}\Omega_r}{\tilde R^4},\qquad \rho_\Lambda=\rho_{\rm crit}\Omega_\Lambda
$$

Matter dilutes with volume, radiation also redshifts, vacuum energy is constant. (The reading copy's OCR shows a dotted R in (26.10)-(26.11); the printed page has R̃.)

### (26.13) Dimensionless Friedmann equation · central · DIV §26.1 p.541 (pdf 556)

$$
\left(\frac{d\tilde R}{d\tilde t}\right)^2=\frac{\Omega_m}{\tilde R}+\frac{\Omega_r}{\tilde R^2}+\Omega_\Lambda\tilde R^2-\frac{k}{H_0^2R_0^2}
$$

All dynamics in terms of present-day parameters; H0 appears only in the curvature term through R0.

**Symbols:** R0 = R(t0)

### (26.14) Curvature parameter · central · DIV §26.1 p.541 (pdf 556)

$$
\Omega_c=-\frac{k}{H_0^2R_0^2}
$$

Curvature term expressed as an effective density fraction; positive for open models.

### (26.15)-(26.16) Sum rule · central · DIV §26.1 p.541 (pdf 556)

$$
\Omega_r+\Omega_m+\Omega_\Lambda+\Omega_c=1,\qquad \Omega_c=1-(\Omega_r+\Omega_m+\Omega_\Lambda)
$$

Evaluating (26.13) at R̃ = 1: curvature is determined by measurable contents.

### (26.17)-(26.19) Particle-in-a-potential form · central · DIV §26.1 p.541 (pdf 556)

$$
\tfrac12\left(\frac{d\tilde R}{d\tilde t}\right)^2+V(\tilde R)=E,\quad V(\tilde R)=-\tfrac12\left(\frac{\Omega_m}{\tilde R}+\frac{\Omega_r}{\tilde R^2}+\Omega_\Lambda\tilde R^2\right),\quad 2E=\Omega_c
$$

Expansion as one-dimensional motion of a unit mass; turning points where V = E.

**Symbols:** V effective potential; E effective energy

### (26.20) Quadrature for R̃(t̃) · supporting · DIV §26.1 p.541 (pdf 556)

$$
\tilde t=\int\frac{d\tilde R}{\left[2\left(E-V(\tilde R)\right)\right]^{1/2}}
$$

Separable integral giving time as a function of scale factor.

### (26.21)-(26.22) Restoring dimensions (corrected argument) · supporting · DIV §26.1 p.542 (pdf 557)

$$
R(t)=R_0\,\tilde R(H_0t),\qquad R_0=\frac{1}{H_0\,|1-(\Omega_r+\Omega_m+\Omega_\Lambda)|^{1/2}}\ \ (\Omega_c\neq0)
$$

H0 and R0 merely rescale the dimensionless solution. The printed equations and Exercise 26.6 write the argument as t̃/H0; since t̃ = H0t it should be H0t (or t̃).

### (26.23)-(26.24) Potential and energy with Ωr = 0 · supporting · DIV §26.1 p.542 (pdf 557)

V(\tilde R)=-\tfrac12\left(\frac{\Omega_m}{\tilde R}+\Omega_\Lambda\tilde R^2\right),\qquad 2E=\Omega_c=1-(\Omega_m+\Omega_\Lambda)

Two-parameter version used to map the (Ωm, ΩΛ) plane.

4y^3+(12x-12)y^2+(-15x^2-24x+12)y+(4x^3-12x^2+12x-4)=0

Obtained by cubing (26.27) with x = Ωm, y = ΩΛ; its roots through (0,1) and (1,0) are the physical boundaries. Cubing can introduce spurious branches, so solutions must be checked against (26.27).

**Symbols:** x = Ωm, y = ΩΛ

Time since R = 0 in Hubble times.

**Symbols:** t0 age

### (26.30) Age of a flat matter-plus-Λ universe · central · DIV §26.1 p.545 (pdf 560)

H_0t_0=\frac23\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\Omega_m}}\sinh^{-1}\left[\frac{\sqrt{1-\Omega_m}}{\sqrt{\Omega_m}}\right]\quad(\Omega_r=\Omega_c=0,\ \Omega_m<1)
$$

Closed form of (26.29) with ΩΛ = 1 − Ωm; 0.964 for Ωm = 0.3 (my evaluation), tending to 2/3 as Ωm → 1.

Recession speed per unit distance; dated estimate.

### (26.32) h-parameterization · supporting · DIV §26.2 p.547 (pdf 562)

H_0=100h\ {\rm (km/s)/Mpc},\quad h=0.72\pm0.08

Carries the uncertainty of H0 as a dimensionless factor.

T_0=2.725\pm0.001\ {\rm K}

Present black-body temperature of the background radiation.

### (26.34)-(26.35) Planck spectrum · central · DIV §26.3 p.548 (pdf 563)

$$
\epsilon_{\rm rad}=\int_0^\infty f(\nu)\,d\nu,\qquad f(\nu)=\frac{8\pi h}{c^3}\frac{\nu^3}{\exp(h\nu/k_BT)-1}
$$

Energy density of thermal radiation per unit frequency and in total.

**Symbols:** h Planck's constant; k_B Boltzmann's constant; ν frequency

### Peak of the Planck spectrum (corrected) · derivation-step · DIV §26.3 p.549 (pdf 564)

$$
3\left(1-e^{-x}\right)=x,\quad x=\frac{h\nu}{k_BT}\ \Rightarrow\ x_{\max}\simeq2.82;\qquad E_{\rm mean}\simeq2.7\,k_BT
$$

Setting f'(ν) = 0. The printed condition reads like 3(e^x − 1) = x (with a misplaced parenthesis), which has no positive root; the correct form is 3(e^x − 1) = xe^x. The book quotes ν_max ≈ 2.7k_BT/h and E_mean ≈ 3k_BT; the precise values are 2.82 and 2.70.

### (26.36) Radiation energy density · central · DIV §26.3 p.549 (pdf 564)

$$
\epsilon_{\rm rad}=\alpha T^4,\qquad \alpha=\frac{\pi^2k_B^4}{15\hbar^3c^3}=7.565\times10^{-16}\ {\rm J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-4}}
$$

Total thermal radiation energy grows as the fourth power of temperature.

**Symbols:** α radiation constant

### (26.37) Radiation density parameter · supporting · DIV §26.3 p.549 (pdf 564)

$$
\epsilon_{\rm rad}(t_0)=4.17\times10^{-14}\,{\rm J\,m^{-3}},\quad \Omega_{\rm CMB}\simeq5\times10^{-5},\quad \Omega_r\lesssim10^{-4}

CMB photons plus neutrinos (and possibly gravitons) contribute a negligible fraction today.

### (26.38) Temperature scaling · central · DIV §26.3 p.549 (pdf 564)

$$
\rho_r\propto R^{-4}\ \Rightarrow\ T\propto\frac1R

The universe cools inversely with its size.

### (26.39) Crude decoupling estimate · derivation-step · DIV §26.3 p.550 (pdf 565)

T\simeq\frac{14\,{\rm eV}}{3k_B}\simeq5\times10^4\,{\rm K}

Mean photon energy equal to hydrogen's ionization energy; about ten times too high.

$$
T_{\rm dec}\simeq3000\,{\rm K}\quad(\tilde R_{\rm dec}\simeq10^{-3})

Realistic value accounting for the spectral tail and the photon excess.

### (26.41)-(26.42) Rotation curve · central · DIV §26.4 p.552 (pdf 567)

$$
\frac{GM(r)}{r^2}=r\omega^2,\qquad v=\sqrt{GM(r)/r}
$$

Newtonian circular orbit speed; flat observed v(r) implies M(r) ∝ r.

**Symbols:** M(r) mass within r; ω angular velocity

Luminous matter versus all ordinary matter from nucleosynthesis.

### (26.43)-(26.44) Source coordinate from a light ray · supporting · DIV §26.4 p.553 (pdf 568)

$$
r_1=\begin{cases}\sin\chi&k=+1\\ \chi&k=0\\ \sinh\chi&k=-1\end{cases},\qquad \chi:=\int_{t_1}^{t_0}\frac{dt}{R(t)}
$$

Radial coordinate of an emitter from the conformal-time interval along the ray.

**Symbols:** t1 emission time

### Changing variables in χ (corrected first line) · derivation-step · DIV §26.4 p.553 (pdf 568)

$$
\chi=\int_{R(t_1)}^{R(t_0)}\frac{dR}{\dot R\,R}=\frac{1}{R_0H_0}\int_{(1+z)^{-1}}^{1}\frac{d\tilde R}{\tilde R\,(d\tilde R/d\tilde t)}=\frac{1}{R_0H_0}\int_{(1+z)^{-1}}^{1}\frac{d\tilde R}{\tilde R\left[\Omega_c-2V(\tilde R)\right]^{1/2}}
$$

Time integral turned into an integral over the scale factor using the Friedmann equation; the printed first integrand shows R̃(r)R where ṘR is meant.

### (26.45)-(26.46) Exact luminosity distance · central · DIV §26.4 p.553 (pdf 568)

$$
g(z):=\frac{1}{H_0}\int_{(1+z)^{-1}}^{1}\frac{d\tilde R}{\tilde R[\Omega_c-2V(\tilde R)]^{1/2}},\qquad d_L(z)=\begin{cases}R_0\sin(g/R_0)&k=+1\\ g(z)&k=0\\ R_0\sinh(g/R_0)&k=-1\end{cases},\quad R_0=\frac{1}{H_0|\Omega_c|^{1/2}}
$$

d_L versus z for any (Ωm, ΩΛ); comparison with Type Ia supernovae constrains the model plane. Uses the book's d_L without the usual (1+z) factor.

**Symbols:** V from (26.23); Ωc = 1 − Ωm − ΩΛ

### (26.47) ΛCDM scale factor · central · DIV §26.5 p.556 (pdf 571)

$$
R(t)=\left(\frac{\Omega_m}{\Omega_\Lambda}\right)^{1/3}\sinh^{2/3}\!\left(\frac{t}{t_\Lambda}\right),\qquad t_\Lambda:=\frac{2}{3H_0\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda}}
$$

Exact flat matter-plus-Λ solution (normalized so R = 1 today): t^{2/3} early, e^{H0√ΩΛ t} late.

**Symbols:** R here is the normalized R̃

### (26.48)-(26.49) Radiation-to-matter ratio · supporting · DIV §26.6 p.558 (pdf 573)

$$
\frac{\rho_r(t)}{\rho_m(t)}=\frac{\rho_r(t_0)}{\rho_m(t_0)}\frac{R(t_0)}{R(t)}=\frac{1}{\tilde R}\left(\frac{\Omega_r}{\Omega_m}\right)
$$

Radiation gains on matter by one power of 1/R̃ going back in time.

### (26.50) Matter-radiation equality · central · DIV §26.6 p.558 (pdf 573)

$$
\rho_m=\rho_r\ \ {\rm when}\ \ \tilde R_{\rm eq}\simeq2\times10^{-4}
$$

With Ωr = 7×10⁻⁵, Ωm = 0.3.

### (26.51) Temperature-time law, matter era · supporting · DIV §26.6 p.558 (pdf 573)

$$
\frac{T(t)}{T(t_0)}=\frac{R(t_0)}{R(t)}=\left(\frac{t_0}{t}\right)^{2/3}
$$

Combines T ∝ 1/R with R ∝ t^{2/3}; order-of-magnitude only since it ignores the Λ era.

### (26.52)-(26.53) Decoupling and equality times · supporting · DIV §26.6 p.558 (pdf 573)

$$
t_{\rm dec}\sim10^{13}\,{\rm s}\ (\sim3.5\times10^5\,{\rm yr}),\qquad t_{\rm eq}\simeq10^{12}\,{\rm s}\ (\simeq3.5\times10^4\,{\rm yr})
$$

From (26.51) with T/T0 ≈ 1000 and ≈ 5000, t0 = 4×10¹⁷ s. The printed (26.52) has a missing coefficient before ×10¹³; the scaling gives 1.3×10¹³ s ≈ 4×10⁵ yr (my evaluation).

### (26.54)-(26.55) Temperature-time law, radiation era · central · DIV §26.6 p.559 (pdf 574)

$$
\frac{T(t)}{T_{\rm eq}}=\left(\frac{t_{\rm eq}}{t}\right)^{1/2},\qquad T(t)\simeq\frac{1.5\times10^{10}}{\sqrt t}\ \ (T\ {\rm in\ K},\ t\ {\rm in\ s})
$$

About 10¹⁰ K (1 MeV) at one second. The book's T_eq ≈ 30,000 K is not consistent with R̃_eq ≈ 2×10⁻⁴ (which gives ≈14,000 K); the coefficient nevertheless agrees with a direct radiation-era calculation.

### (26.56)-(26.57) Deviation from critical density · central · DIV §26.7 p.561 (pdf 576)

$$
H^2=\frac{8\pi}{3}\rho_{\rm total}-\frac{k}{R^2}\ \Rightarrow\ \left|1-\frac{\rho_{\rm total}}{\rho_{\rm crit}(t)}\right|=\frac{|k|}{H^2(t)R^2},\quad \rho_{\rm crit}(t)=\frac{3H^2(t)}{8\pi}
$$

Since HR = Ṙ, the departure from critical density is |k|/Ṙ²: it grows whenever the expansion decelerates and shrinks when it accelerates.

### (26.58) Growth of the flatness deviation · central · DIV §26.7 p.561 (pdf 576)

$$
\left|1-\rho_{\rm total}(t)/\rho_{\rm crit}(t)\right|\propto t^{2/3}\quad(R\sim t^{2/3})
$$

Deviation grows during matter domination, so it must have been tiny early.

### (26.59) Radial null ray · supporting · DIV §26.7 p.561 (pdf 576)

$$
ds^2=dt^2-R^2(t)dr^2=0\ \Rightarrow\ \int_{r_1}^{r_2}dr=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\frac{dt}{R(t)}
$$

Coordinate distance travelled by light in a flat RW universe.

### (26.60) Proper horizon distance · central · DIV §26.7 p.562 (pdf 577)

$$
\Delta r=R(t_2)\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\frac{dt}{R(t)}
$$

With t1 = 0, t2 = t: radius of the particle horizon.

### (26.61) Horizon in a radiation era · central · DIV §26.7 p.562 (pdf 577)

$$
R=At^{1/2}\ \Rightarrow\ \Delta r(t)=At^{1/2}\int_0^t\frac{du}{Au^{1/2}}=2t
$$

Horizon grows only linearly (twice the naive ct).

### (26.62) Horizon during inflation · central · DIV §26.7 p.563 (pdf 578)

$$
R=Ae^{\tilde Ht}\ \Rightarrow\ \Delta r(t)=e^{\tilde Ht}\int_0^te^{-\tilde Hu}du=\frac{e^{\tilde Ht}-1}{\tilde H}
$$

Horizon grows exponentially. The printed intermediate bracket shows e^{H̃u} instead of e^{−H̃u}.

**Symbols:** H̃ constant inflationary Hubble rate

### (26.63) Flatness during inflation · central · DIV §26.7 p.563 (pdf 578)

$$
\left|1-\rho_{\rm total}(t)/\rho_{\rm crit}(t)\right|\propto|k|e^{-2\tilde Ht}
$$

Exponential expansion drives the universe towards critical density.

### (26.64)-(26.65) Scalar-field action and Lagrangian density · supporting · DIV §26.7 p.563 (pdf 578)

$$
I=-\int_\Omega\left[\tfrac12g^{ab}\nabla_a\phi\nabla_b\phi-V(\phi)\right]\sqrt{-g}\,d^4x,\qquad \mathcal L=(-g)^{1/2}\left[-\tfrac12g^{ab}\nabla_a\phi\nabla_b\phi+V(\phi)\right]
$$

Action for the inflaton in the book's sign conventions.

**Symbols:** Ω integration region (not a density parameter)

### Scalar field equation · supporting · DIV §26.7 p.564 (pdf 579)

$$
\Box\phi+V'(\phi)=0
$$

Euler-Lagrange equation from δℒ/δφ = 0 (Exercise 26.12(i)).

**Symbols:** □ = ∇^a∇_a

### (26.66) Homogeneous inflaton in FRW · central · DIV §26.7 p.564 (pdf 579)

$$
\ddot\phi+3H(t)\dot\phi+V'(\phi)=0
$$

Damped particle-like equation; 3Hφ̇ is Hubble friction.

### (26.67) Energy-momentum from the action · supporting · DIV §26.7 p.564 (pdf 579)

$$
T_{ab}=-\frac{2}{\sqrt{-g}}\frac{\delta\mathcal L}{\delta g^{ab}}
$$

Metric variation defines the source tensor.

### (26.68)-(26.69) Scalar-field energy-momentum tensor · supporting · DIV §26.7 p.564 (pdf 579)

$$
T_{ab}=\nabla_a\phi\nabla_b\phi+g_{ab}\left[-\tfrac12g^{cd}\nabla_c\phi\nabla_d\phi+V(\phi)\right]\ \xrightarrow{\rm RW,\ \phi(t)}\ \delta_a^0\delta_b^0\dot\phi^2+g_{ab}\left[-\tfrac12\dot\phi^2+V(\phi)\right]
$$

Gives ρ = ½φ̇² + V and p = ½φ̇² − V.

### (26.70) Slow-roll limit · central · DIV §26.7 p.564 (pdf 579)

$$
T_{ab}\simeq V(\phi_*)\,g_{ab}
$$

A frozen field acts like a cosmological constant.

**Symbols:** φ* approximately constant field value

### (26.71) Inflationary expansion rate · supporting · DIV §26.7 p.565 (pdf 580)

$$
R(t)\propto e^{\tilde Ht},\qquad \tilde H=\left(V(\phi_*)/3\right)^{1/2}
$$

de Sitter expansion from the potential energy. With ρ = V and H² = 8πρ/3 (consistent with (26.5)) one expects H̃ = (8πV/3)^{1/2}; the printed form treats V as Λ.

### (26.72) Density fluctuation from inflaton fluctuation · supporting · DIV §26.7 p.565 (pdf 580)

$$
\delta\rho=V(\phi_*+\delta\phi)-V(\phi_*)\simeq V'(\phi_*)\,\delta\phi
$$

Quantum jitter in φ becomes density perturbations that inflation stretches.

## Worked examples

### Example · intro · DIV §26.1 p.539 (pdf 554)

**Problem:** Estimate the critical density numerically.

**Method:** Insert H0 of order 100 km/s/Mpc into 3H0²/8π after restoring G (Exercise 26.2 repeats this with 1/H0 ≈ 10¹⁰ yr).

**Key insight:** The critical density is tiny in everyday terms yet comparable to the true cosmic mean, because intergalactic space is so empty.

**Result:** ρcrit ~ 10⁻²⁶ kg m⁻³ (1.9×10⁻²⁶ for h = 1; 9.7×10⁻²⁷ for h = 0.72, my evaluation)

**Concepts:** critical density

### Example · standard · DIV §26.1 p.541 (pdf 556)

**Problem:** Put the Friedmann equation with radiation, matter and Λ into dimensionless form and find the constraint on the present parameters.

**Method:** Write each density as ρcrit Ω R̃⁻ⁿ, divide by H0², multiply by R̃², call the curvature term Ωc, then evaluate at R̃ = 1 where dR̃/dt̃ = 1 (Exercises 26.4-26.5).

**Key insight:** All present-day information collapses into Ω's that must add to one, so curvature is not an independent parameter.

**Result:** (26.13) and Ωr + Ωm + ΩΛ + Ωc = 1

**Concepts:** dimensionless Friedmann equation, cosmic sum rule, curvature density parameter

### Example · standard · DIV §26.1 p.542 (pdf 557)

**Problem:** Reconstruct the physical scale factor R(t) from Ωr, Ωm, ΩΛ and H0.

**Method:** Get E = Ωc/2; integrate t̃ = ∫dR̃/[2(E − V)]^{1/2} and invert; use |Ωc| = |k|/(H0R0)² with |k| = 1 to find R0 (Exercise 26.6).

**Key insight:** H0 only sets the units of time and, for curved models, the curvature radius; the Ω's decide the shape of the history.

**Result:** R(t) = R0 R̃(H0t), R0 = 1/(H0|Ωc|^{1/2}); the printed argument t̃/H0 should read H0t

**Concepts:** four-parameter specification of FRW models, effective potential for the scale factor

### Example · intro · DIV §26.1 p.543 (pdf 558)

**Problem:** Express today's deceleration parameter through Ωm and ΩΛ.

**Method:** Take q = (4πρ/3 − Λ/3)/H² from (24.79), substitute ρm(t0) = Ωmρcrit, Λ = 8πρΛ = 8πΩΛρcrit and ρcrit = 3H0²/8π.

**Key insight:** Matter brakes the expansion with half weight, vacuum energy accelerates it with full weight.

**Result:** q0 = Ωm/2 − ΩΛ; acceleration boundary ΩΛ = Ωm/2; q0 = −0.55 for (0.3, 0.7)

**Concepts:** deceleration parameter, (Ωm, ΩΛ) classification plane

### Example · challenging · DIV §26.1 p.544 (pdf 559)

**Problem:** Find where in the (Ωm, ΩΛ) plane the barrier of the effective potential just equals the energy.

**Method:** Differentiate V = −½(Ωm/R̃ + ΩΛR̃²), solve for R̃³ = Ωm/2ΩΛ, substitute to get V_max = −¾(2Ωm²ΩΛ)^{1/3}, impose 2V_max = 1 − Ωm − ΩΛ, cube and collect a cubic in ΩΛ; keep the roots through (0, 1) and (1, 0) and discard the unphysical one.

**Key insight:** Turning-point analysis in a potential becomes a boundary between classes of universes in parameter space.

**Result:** (26.27)-(26.28): no-big-bang curve from (0, 1); expand-forever/recollapse curve from (1, 0)

**Concepts:** big bang versus bounce criterion, effective potential for the scale factor

### Example · standard · DIV §26.1 p.545 (pdf 560)

**Problem:** Compute the age of a flat universe containing matter and a cosmological constant.

**Method:** Separate dR̃/dt̃ = [Ωm/R̃ + ΩΛR̃²]^{1/2}, integrate from 0 to 1; the substitution u = R̃^{3/2} reduces it to an inverse hyperbolic sine (Exercise 26.7). §26.5 then plugs in concordance values.

**Key insight:** Λ lengthens the age compared with the Einstein-de Sitter 2/3 Hubble time, easing conflict with old stars.

**Result:** H0t0 = (2/3)(1 − Ωm)^{−1/2} sinh⁻¹√((1 − Ωm)/Ωm); for Ωm = 0.3 this is 0.964, about 13.1 Gyr at H0 = 72 (my evaluation; the book's 'Ωm = 0.7' would give 10.2 Gyr)

**Concepts:** age of the universe, ΛCDM model

### Example · standard · DIV §26.3 p.549 (pdf 564)

**Problem:** Turn the measured CMB temperature into the present radiation density parameter.

**Method:** Integrate the Planck spectrum with y = hν/k_BT to get αT⁴; evaluate at 2.725 K; divide by ρcrit c²; add neutrinos and possible gravitons.

**Key insight:** Radiation is negligible today, but because ρr/ρm ∝ 1/R̃ it dominated early.

**Result:** ε ≈ 4.17×10⁻¹⁴ J m⁻³, ΩCMB ≈ 5×10⁻⁵, Ωr ≲ 10⁻⁴

**Concepts:** radiation energy density law, component density parameters

### Example · intro · DIV §26.3 p.550 (pdf 565)

**Problem:** Estimate the temperature at which the universe became transparent.

**Method:** Set the mean black-body photon energy 3k_BT equal to hydrogen's ionization energy (≈14 eV); then argue qualitatively that the high-energy tail and the vast number of photons per atom keep hydrogen ionized to much lower temperature.

**Key insight:** A threshold process in a photon-rich gas is controlled by the rare energetic photons, not the average one.

**Result:** Crude 5×10⁴ K; realistic 3000 K, when R̃ ≈ 10⁻³; photons then at 1.6×10¹⁴ Hz, now 160 GHz

**Concepts:** decoupling, black-body spectrum, photon-to-baryon ratio

### Example · intro · DIV §26.4 p.552 (pdf 567)

**Problem:** Predict how orbital speed in a galaxy should depend on radius.

**Method:** Equate the Newtonian attraction GmM(r)/r² to the centripetal force mrω² and use v = ωr.

**Key insight:** Outside the luminous mass v should fall as r^{−1/2}; flat observed curves require mass growing linearly with r in a dark halo.

**Result:** v = √(GM(r)/r)

**Concepts:** galaxy rotation curve, dark matter

### Example · challenging · DIV §26.4 p.553 (pdf 568)

**Problem:** Derive the exact luminosity distance as a function of redshift for any (Ωm, ΩΛ).

**Method:** Write r1 = sin χ, χ or sinh χ with χ = ∫dt/R; change variable to R and then R̃ = R/R0, t̃ = H0t; replace dR̃/dt̃ by [Ωc − 2V]^{1/2}; use 1 + z = R0/R(t1) for the lower limit; apply d_L = r1R0 and R0 = 1/(H0|Ωc|^{1/2}).

**Key insight:** The whole redshift-distance curve depends on the same effective potential that governs the dynamics, so supernova data map directly onto the model plane.

**Result:** (26.45)-(26.46)

**Concepts:** exact luminosity distance-redshift relation, comoving radial coordinate χ, effective potential for the scale factor

### Example · standard · DIV §26.5 p.556 (pdf 571)

**Problem:** Rewrite the flat positive-Λ dust solution of Chapter 25 in terms of Ωm, ΩΛ and H0.

**Method:** Express C and Λ through H0, Ωm, ΩΛ and verify the proposed sinh^{2/3} form satisfies Ṙ²/R² = H0²(Ωm/R³ + ΩΛ) (Exercise 26.10); expand for small and large t.

**Key insight:** One formula shows decelerating t^{2/3} growth giving way to exponential expansion at rate H0√ΩΛ.

**Result:** R = (Ωm/ΩΛ)^{1/3} sinh^{2/3}(t/tΛ), tΛ = 2/(3H0√ΩΛ) ≈ 10.8 Gyr for H0 = 72, ΩΛ = 0.7 (my evaluation)

**Concepts:** ΛCDM model, de Sitter expansion

### Example · standard · DIV §26.6 p.558 (pdf 573)

**Problem:** Date matter-radiation equality and decoupling, and find the temperature-time law before equality.

**Method:** Use ρr/ρm = (Ωr/Ωm)/R̃ to get R̃_eq; combine T ∝ 1/R̃ with R ∝ t^{2/3} so t = t0(T0/T)^{3/2}; for earlier times use R ∝ t^{1/2} and match at equality.

**Key insight:** Two scaling laws plus two anchor numbers give a usable cosmic calendar without solving the full equations.

**Result:** R̃_eq ≈ 2×10⁻⁴; t_dec ~ 10¹³ s; t_eq ~ 10¹² s; T ≈ 1.5×10¹⁰ K/√t, about 1 MeV at 1 s

**Concepts:** matter-radiation equality, temperature-time relation in the radiation era, thermal history of the universe

### Example · standard · DIV §26.7 p.561 (pdf 576)

**Problem:** Quantify how finely tuned the early density must have been.

**Method:** Divide the Friedmann equation by H² to get |1 − Ω(t)| = |k|/(HR)²; with R ∝ t^{2/3}, H ∝ 1/t this grows as t^{2/3}; scale today's bound (0.3 at 4×10¹⁷ s) back to 10¹² s.

**Key insight:** Flatness is dynamically unstable in decelerating expansion, so near-flatness now is a clue, not a coincidence.

**Result:** Book: below 10⁻⁵ at equality and far smaller earlier (a direct scaling of 0.3 gives about 5×10⁻⁵, my evaluation)

**Concepts:** flatness problem, critical density

### Example · intro · DIV §26.7 p.562 (pdf 577)

**Problem:** Compare the particle horizon in radiation-dominated and exponentially expanding universes.

**Method:** Evaluate Δr = R(t)∫₀ᵗdu/R(u) for R = At^{1/2} and for R = Ae^{H̃t}.

**Key insight:** Replacing a power law by an exponential changes horizon growth from linear to exponential, allowing distant regions to have been in contact.

**Result:** Δr = 2t versus Δr = (e^{H̃t} − 1)/H̃; also |1 − Ω| ∝ e^{−2H̃t} during inflation

**Concepts:** particle horizon, horizon problem, cosmic inflation

### Example · challenging · DIV §26.7 p.564 (pdf 579)

**Problem:** Derive the dynamics and stress tensor of a homogeneous scalar field in an FRW universe and show when it mimics Λ.

**Method:** Vary the Lagrangian density (26.65) with respect to φ and g^{ab}; specialize to φ = φ(t) in comoving coordinates where √−g ∝ R³; argue that large Hubble friction keeps φ̇ small (Exercise 26.12).

**Key insight:** A field frozen by Hubble friction has T_ab ∝ g_ab, exactly the form of a cosmological constant.

**Result:** φ̈ + 3Hφ̇ + V' = 0; T_ab = δ⁰_aδ⁰_bφ̇² + g_ab(−½φ̇² + V); T_ab ≈ V(φ*)g_ab; R ∝ e^{H̃t}

**Concepts:** inflaton scalar field, Hubble friction, slow-roll inflation


## Analogies and intuitions

### The scale factor behaves like a bead of unit mass moving along a line in a potential V(R̃), with total 'energy' fixed by the curvature parameter. → effective potential for the scale factor · strong · DIV §26.1 p.541 (pdf 556)

Cosmic fates are read from where the E level meets the potential: no intersection means monotonic expansion from a big bang; intersections mean turning points, hence recollapse or a bounce. Also used to derive the no-big-bang boundary.

**Where it breaks down:** E is not a physical energy of any system, R̃ = 0 is a singularity rather than a wall, time is dimensionless, and the picture applies only to exactly homogeneous models.

**App idea:** Bead on a potential curve synchronized with an R̃(t̃) plot; the learner changes Ω's and sees both update.

### Ink dropped in water spreads until the glass is uniformly colored, because the parts have had time to mix. → horizon problem · useful · DIV §26.7 p.561 (pdf 576)

Sets up the expectation that uniformity requires prior interaction, which particle horizons deny to widely separated regions of the early universe.

**Where it breaks down:** A glass of water has no horizons and is static; in the universe expansion can keep regions out of contact, and inflation produces uniformity by stretching a pre-mixed patch rather than by mixing across the observed sky.

**App idea:** Split-screen: diffusion in a box versus diffusion in an expanding box with a finite signal speed, showing mixing fail when expansion outruns it.

### The inflaton is like a ball rolling down a hillside while immersed in a thick fluid whose drag is set by the Hubble rate. → slow-roll inflation · strong · DIV §26.7 p.564 (pdf 579)

Explains why φ barely moves on a flat plateau (large friction), so its potential energy acts like a cosmological constant until the slope steepens.

**Where it breaks down:** The friction coefficient 3H itself depends on the field's energy, and quantum fluctuations and spatial gradients are ignored.

**App idea:** Ball-on-potential simulator with a friction slider tied to H and a live e-fold counter.

### Standard candles as light bulbs of known wattage: how dim one looks tells how far away it is. → standard candle · useful · DIV §26.2 p.546 (pdf 561)

Explains how luminosity distances are obtained for objects beyond parallax (the book uses the term without the bulb image).

**Where it breaks down:** Real candles may evolve with cosmic age or environment, dust dims them, and in cosmology the brightness-distance relation depends on the expansion history and the definition of d_L.

**App idea:** Drag identical bulbs to different distances and read flux; then switch on evolution or dust and see the inferred H0 bias.

### A cosmic distance ladder: each rung is calibrated using the rung below it. → cosmic distance ladder · useful · DIV §26.2 p.547 (pdf 562)

Describes the chain from parallax through Cepheids to supernovae that solves the calibration problem.

**Where it breaks down:** Errors propagate and accumulate up the ladder; different rungs overlap only partially.

**App idea:** Interactive ladder where the learner adjusts a calibration error on a low rung and watches the H0 estimate shift.

### The multipole expansion of the CMB correlation function is like a Fourier series generalized to more dimensions. → CMB anisotropies and angular power spectrum · weak · DIV §26.4 p.554 (pdf 569)

Gives readers a familiar handle on why the temperature pattern is decomposed into ℓ modes.

**Where it breaks down:** The expansion is on the two-dimensional sphere in spherical harmonics (the book calls it three-dimensional), and each ℓ corresponds to an angular, not a spatial, scale.

**App idea:** Sphere viewer where the learner adds individual ℓ modes and watches the sky pattern and its power spectrum build up.

### The last scattering surface is every observer's own sphere, like the edge of visibility around a ship in fog (fog image is our addition). → last scattering surface · useful · DIV §26.3 p.551 (pdf 566)

The book stresses that the surface is not a special place but depends on where and when the observer is.

**Where it breaks down:** In cosmology the 'fog' is in the past, not merely far away: we see the surface as it was at decoupling, and it recedes as the observer ages.

**App idea:** Two observers in a 2D expanding universe each see a different circle of last-scattering points; moving one observer moves its circle.

### Wheeler's 'big U': the universe as a tube whose far end is the big bang and whose near end is an eye looking back at it. → anthropic principle · weak · DIV §26.8 p.567 (pdf 582)

Closing image for the idea that the universe produces observers who study its origin; also a nostalgic nod to Lieber's illustrated book.

**Where it breaks down:** Purely symbolic; it makes no testable claim and should not be read as a physical model.


## Misconceptions addressed

### The critical density is one fixed number valid for all cosmic time. · DIV §26.1 p.539 (pdf 554)

**Correction:** The density needed for flatness is 3H²/8π at each epoch and changes as H changes; the book attaches the name to today's value, and §26.7 uses the time-dependent ρcrit(t). Being near critical today says nothing directly about the density at other epochs.

**Why tempting:** It is quoted as a single number, like a constant of nature.

### A flat universe must have Ωm = 1. · DIV §26.1 p.542 (pdf 557)

**Correction:** With a cosmological constant, flatness means Ωm + ΩΛ (+ Ωr) = 1; ΛCDM is flat with Ωm ≈ 0.3.

**Why tempting:** Older presentations without Λ equate flatness with Ω = 1, and §26.1 begins that way.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Closed universes always recollapse and open ones always expand forever. · DIV §26.1 p.544 (pdf 559)

**Correction:** Once Λ ≠ 0, geometry and fate are decided by different curves in Fig. 26.2; a closed model with enough Λ expands forever, and an open model with negative Λ recollapses.

**Why tempting:** True for Λ = 0 dust models, which dominate introductory treatments.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The CMB comes from a special shell or edge of the universe. · DIV §26.3 p.551 (pdf 566)

**Correction:** Radiation was released everywhere at decoupling; the last scattering surface is simply the set of points whose light reaches a given observer now, and other observers see different spheres.

**Why tempting:** Diagrams show a sphere around us, and the word 'surface' suggests a physical boundary.

### Since the universe has cooled, the relic radiation should no longer have a thermal spectrum; a perfect black body today would be a coincidence. · DIV §26.3 p.550 (pdf 565)

**Correction:** Frequencies and temperature both scale as 1/R, so hν/k_BT is unchanged and the ν³ factor dilutes like 1/volume; a black body stays a black body at a lower temperature.

**Why tempting:** Changing temperature suggests changing spectral shape.

### Hydrogen recombines when the average photon energy drops below the ionization energy. · DIV §26.3 p.550 (pdf 565)

**Correction:** The average-energy estimate (≈50,000 K) is about ten times too high: photons in the high-energy tail, vastly outnumbering atoms, keep hydrogen ionized until about 3000 K.

**Why tempting:** Comparing typical energies is the usual first estimate.

### Counting the mass in stars gives the matter density of the universe. · DIV §26.4 p.551 (pdf 566)

**Correction:** Stars give Ω ≈ 0.01, ordinary matter from nucleosynthesis ≈ 0.04, and total matter ≈ 0.3; most ordinary matter is dark gas and most matter is non-baryonic.

**Why tempting:** Light is what we see, and galaxies look like collections of stars.

### Black holes formed by stellar collapse could be the non-baryonic dark matter. · DIV §26.5 p.556 (pdf 571)

**Correction:** They were made from baryons and so count against the nucleosynthesis bound; only black holes formed before nucleosynthesis (primordial ones) could evade it.

**Why tempting:** Black holes are dark and not made of 'ordinary' stuff once formed.

### Because Ωr ≈ 10⁻⁴, radiation can be ignored in cosmology at all times. · DIV §26.6 p.558 (pdf 573)

**Correction:** ρr/ρm grows as 1/R̃ into the past; radiation dominated before R̃ ≈ 2×10⁻⁴, which sets the early expansion law and thermal history.

**Why tempting:** §26.1 drops Ωr to draw the model plane.

### Everything we observe must have been in causal contact because it all started together at the big bang. · DIV §26.7 p.562 (pdf 577)

**Correction:** In decelerating expansion the particle horizon at decoupling spans only about 2° of today's sky; the past light cones of opposite sky points never meet, so their equal temperatures are unexplained without inflation.

**Why tempting:** 'Started at a point' suggests a shared origin and contact.

### Inflation settles the questions of homogeneity and flatness completely. · DIV §26.9 p.567 (pdf 582)

**Correction:** It replaces them with questions about what the inflaton is and why its potential has the right shape; the authors present it as one plausible mechanism among others.

**Why tempting:** Inflation is often taught as an established part of the standard model.

### The evidence for dark energy leaves no room for doubt. · DIV §26.5 p.557 (pdf 572)

**Correction:** Combined data strongly favour ΛCDM, but the authors note possible evolution of Type Ia supernova luminosities and the averaging problem for non-linear Einstein equations as grounds some cosmologists cite for caution.

**Why tempting:** The concordance numbers are quoted with confidence.

### Solving Einstein's equations for a smoothed-out matter distribution gives the smoothed spacetime. · DIV §26.5 p.557 (pdf 572)

**Correction:** The equations are non-linear, so averaging the source and averaging the solution need not commute.

**Why tempting:** In linear theories such as Newtonian gravity or electrostatics they do commute.

### The big bang singularity is only an artefact of perfect symmetry; real irregularities would turn it into a bounce. · DIV §26.9 p.568 (pdf 583)

**Correction:** The Hawking-Penrose singularity theorems show singularities arise generically, so there is good reason to think the real universe was singular in the past.

**Why tempting:** Symmetric collapse often focuses matter artificially; perturbing it seems likely to avoid the focus.

### The Hubble constant is constant in time. · DIV §26.2 p.545 (pdf 560)

**Correction:** H(t) = Ṙ/R evolves; 'constant' means the same everywhere at a given cosmic time, and H0 denotes its present value.

**Why tempting:** The name says constant.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The energy E in the particle analogy is the physical energy content of the universe. · DIV §26.1 p.541 (pdf 556)

**Correction:** E = Ωc/2 is a combination of curvature and expansion rate that plays the role of energy in a mathematical analogy; it is not a conserved physical energy.

**Why tempting:** The equation looks identical to kinetic plus potential energy.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Tracing two antipodal CMB photons to their origins · DIV §26.7 p.562 (pdf 577)

**Setup:** Photons reaching us from opposite directions left points P and Q on the last scattering surface; follow the past light cones of P and Q back to the big bang in a decelerating universe.

**Lesson:** The cones never intersect, so no event could have influenced both, yet P and Q have the same temperature: the horizon problem.

**App idea:** Conformal diagram where the learner places P and Q at chosen sky separation and sees when their cones overlap, with an inflation toggle.

### Running the cosmic thermometer backwards · DIV §26.6 p.559 (pdf 574)

**Setup:** Shrink the universe step by step using T ∝ 1/R: a thousandth of today's size, then the first second, then 10⁻⁴ s.

**Lesson:** The universe passes 3000 K (atoms ionize), 10¹⁰ K at one second (nuclei break up) and ~10¹² K (nucleons dissolve into quarks), organizing cosmic history by energy thresholds.

**App idea:** Rewind slider with a thermometer and a particle inventory that changes at each threshold.

### Nudging the early density away from critical · DIV §26.7 p.561 (pdf 576)

**Setup:** Start the universe with density slightly off critical at equality or earlier and let it expand with R ∝ t^{2/3} or t^{1/2}.

**Lesson:** The deviation grows with time, so to be within tens of percent today the early universe needed extraordinary tuning; exponential expansion reverses the trend.

**App idea:** Log-log plot of |1 − Ω| versus t with a draggable initial offset and an optional inflation interval.

### Letting a closed universe bounce · DIV §26.1 p.543 (pdf 558)

**Setup:** Take Ωm + ΩΛ > 1 with a potential barrier higher than E and start the universe large and contracting.

**Lesson:** It halts at a turning point and re-expands without ever reaching R = 0: some FRW models have no big bang.

**App idea:** Drop the bead on the right side of the barrier in the potential explorer.

### Retuning the constants of nature · DIV §26.8 p.566 (pdf 581)

**Setup:** Imagine altering fundamental constants such as the electron charge slightly and ask what kind of universe results.

**Lesson:** Most such universes could not support life, which is read either as design or as evidence for anthropic selection among many universes.

### Running a lumpy universe backwards · DIV §26.9 p.568 (pdf 583)

## Applications and observations

- **Size of the critical density** (numerical-estimate, DIV §26.1 p.539 (pdf 554)): Inserting H0 into 3H0²/8πG gives a density equivalent to a few hydrogen atoms per cubic metre, similar in order to the actual mean density. Key numbers: ρcrit ~ 10⁻²⁶ kg m⁻³ (H0 = 100); 9.7×10⁻²⁷ kg m⁻³ for H0 = 72 (my evaluation)
- **Hubble constant** (observation, DIV §26.2 p.545 (pdf 560)): Distance-ladder determinations with Cepheids and Type Ia supernovae; the book gives 72 ± 8 km/s/Mpc. Modern determinations disagree at the several-percent level (CMB-inferred ≈ 67-68 versus local ladder ≈ 73), a live 'Hubble tension' not discussed in the book. Key numbers: H0 = 72 ± 8 km/s/Mpc; h = 0.72 ± 0.08; 1/H0 ≈ 13.6 Gyr
- **Cosmic distance scales** (numerical-estimate, DIV §26.2 p.546 (pdf 561)): Table of rough distances from the Sun to the edge of the visible universe; 1 pc = 3.08×10¹⁶ m ≈ 3.26 ly; nearest large cluster ≈ 20 Mpc. Key numbers: 5×10⁻⁶ pc (Sun) to 1.4×10¹⁰ pc (visible universe)
- **CMB temperature and isotropy** (observation, DIV §26.3 p.548 (pdf 563)): The background is an almost perfect black body; after removing local effects its temperature varies by less than one part in 10⁴ across the sky, strong support for large-scale isotropy. Key numbers: T0 = 2.725 ± 0.001 K; ΔT/T < 10⁻⁴
- **Radiation content today** (numerical-estimate, DIV §26.3 p.549 (pdf 564)): Black-body energy density at T0 converted to a fraction of critical density; neutrinos and gravitons add little. Key numbers: ε ≈ 4.17×10⁻¹⁴ J m⁻³; ΩCMB ≈ 5×10⁻⁵; Ωr ≲ 10⁻⁴
- **Decoupling and the redshift of CMB photons** (numerical-estimate, DIV §26.3 p.551 (pdf 566)): Crude estimate 50,000 K, realistic 3000 K when the universe was 1/1000 of its present size; typical frequency then 1.6×10¹⁴ Hz, now 160 GHz; last scattering surface radius of order 6000 Mpc in the book (the comoving distance in ΛCDM is closer to 14,000 Mpc, my evaluation). Key numbers: T_dec ≈ 3000 K; z ≈ 1000; ν0 ≈ 160 GHz
- **Luminous matter and hot cluster gas** (observation, DIV §26.4 p.551 (pdf 566)): Stellar mass averaged over large volumes gives a small fraction of critical density; hot intracluster gas seen in X-rays shows extra baryons. Key numbers: Ω_stars ≈ 0.01
- **Light-element abundances** (data-analysis, DIV §26.4 p.552 (pdf 567)): Nucleosynthesis predictions for hydrogen, deuterium, helium and lithium match observations only for a particular baryon density. Key numbers: Ω_baryon ≈ 0.04 (current ≈ 0.049)
- **Flat galactic rotation curves** (observation, DIV §26.4 p.552 (pdf 567)): Orbital speeds stay roughly constant far beyond the visible disk instead of falling as r^{−1/2}, implying massive dark halos.
- **Type Ia supernova redshift-magnitude relation** (data-analysis, DIV §26.4 p.553 (pdf 568)): Standard-candle brightness versus redshift for distant supernovae compared with the exact d_L(z) of each (Ωm, ΩΛ) gives an elongated confidence region favouring ΩΛ > 0 and acceleration.
- **CMB angular power spectrum (Planck 2013)** (observation, DIV §26.4 p.554 (pdf 569)): Acoustic peak structure in D_ℓ versus ℓ constrains geometry and content; first peak near ℓ ≈ 220 (about 1°). Key numbers: Peak D_ℓ ≈ 5700 μK² at ℓ ≈ 220
- **Concordance parameters** (data-analysis, DIV §26.4 p.554 (pdf 569)): Supernova and CMB strips intersect roughly perpendicularly; structure formation and bulk flows independently require Ωm > 0.2. Key numbers: Ωm ≈ 0.3, ΩΛ ≈ 0.7, Ωc ≈ 0, q0 ≈ −0.55
- **Age of the universe in ΛCDM** (numerical-estimate, DIV §26.5 p.556 (pdf 571)): Flat age formula with concordance values gives about 14 billion years in the book, compatible with ages of old stars; without Λ the age would be uncomfortably short. Key numbers: H0t0 = 0.964 → 13.1 Gyr for H0 = 72 (my evaluation); Einstein-de Sitter 2/3 → 9.1 Gyr
- **Microlensing searches for MACHOs** (observation, DIV §26.5 p.556 (pdf 571)): Gravitational lensing surveys towards the Large Magellanic Cloud detected some compact halo objects but too few to be all the dark matter.
- **Particle dark matter searches** (experiment, DIV §26.5 p.557 (pdf 572)): Direct-detection experiments and accelerator searches for WIMPs have produced no conclusive signal; neutrino mass limits disfavour hot dark matter; axions remain undetected.
- **Vacuum energy mismatch** (numerical-estimate, DIV §26.5 p.557 (pdf 572)): Zero-point energy estimates from the standard model exceed the observed dark energy density enormously. Key numbers: More than 100 orders of magnitude
- **Cosmic calendar** (numerical-estimate, DIV §26.6 p.559 (pdf 574)): Scaling estimates of key epochs with Ωr = 7×10⁻⁵, Ωm = 0.3, H0 = 72; equality temperature quoted as 30,000 K though R̃_eq gives ≈14,000 K. Key numbers: R̃_eq ≈ 2×10⁻⁴; t_eq ≈ 10¹² s ≈ 35,000 yr; t_dec ≈ 10¹³ s ≈ 350,000 yr; T(1 s) ≈ 1.5×10¹⁰ K ≈ 1 MeV; quark-hadron ≈ 2×10¹² K at 10⁻⁴ s; t0 = 4×10¹⁷ s; inflation as early as 10⁻³⁴ s
- **Flatness and horizon fine-tuning** (numerical-estimate, DIV §26.7 p.561 (pdf 576)): Extrapolating today's bound on |1 − Ω| back in time, and the angular size of causally connected CMB patches. Key numbers: |1 − Ω| < 10⁻⁵ at t_eq (book; ≈5×10⁻⁵ by direct scaling, my evaluation); causal patches ≈ 2° (≈1.2° in a ΛCDM calculation, my evaluation)
- **Structure formation simulations** (data-analysis, DIV §26.9 p.567 (pdf 582)): Computer simulations of gravitational instability reproduce observed galaxy clustering, but only with dark matter included.
- **Multi-messenger astronomy and LISA** (technology, DIV §26.9 p.568 (pdf 583)): Gravitational-wave detections combined with radio, optical and X-ray observations; the planned space interferometer LISA may detect signals from the early universe.

## Historical notes

- **Ralph Alpher, Robert Herman (1948):** Predicted a relic thermal radiation background from a hot early universe; the prediction was later found again independently by Zel'dovich and by Dicke in the 1960s. — Shows the CMB was a theoretical consequence of the hot big bang before its discovery. (DIV §26.3 p.547 (pdf 562))
- **Arno Penzias, Robert Wilson, Robert Dicke (1964-1965):** While Dicke's Princeton colleagues were building a detector (from 1964), Penzias and Wilson found the background with an antenna built for satellite microwave communication. — Discovery of the CMB, the key evidence for the hot big bang over the steady-state theory. (DIV §26.3 p.547 (pdf 562))
- **History (2001-2018):** Space missions WMAP (2001) and Planck (the book dates it 2018, when its final results appeared; launched 2009) measured the CMB with increasing precision. — Precision CMB data turned cosmology into a parameter-measurement science. (DIV §26.3 p.547 (pdf 562))
- **Planck Collaboration (Ade et al.) (2014):** Published the 2013 CMB temperature power spectrum reproduced as Fig. 26.4. — The peak structure constrains geometry and content. (DIV §26.4 p.554 (pdf 569))
- **High-Z Supernova Search Team, Supernova Cosmology Project, BOOMERanG and MAXIMA collaborations:** Supernova redshift-magnitude contours and balloon CMB constraints, reproduced from Liddle in Fig. 26.5, jointly pointed to Ωm ≈ 0.3, ΩΛ ≈ 0.7 (late 1990s results; not dated in the book). — Established cosmic acceleration and the concordance model. (DIV §26.4 p.555 (pdf 570))
- **History:** The steady-state theory predicted a background spectrum with much more high-frequency energy than a black body. — The observed black-body CMB favours the hot big bang. (DIV §26.3 p.551 (pdf 566))
- **Sean Carroll (2001):** Gave exact formulae for the dividing curves in the (Ωm, ΩΛ) plane, which the book cites but does not reproduce. — Source for the boundary curves drawn in Fig. 26.2. (DIV §26.1 p.545 (pdf 560))
- **Stephen Hawking, Roger Penrose:** Singularity theorems showing singularities are generic, invoked for the big bang. — Supports a genuinely singular origin despite local irregularities. (DIV §26.9 p.568 (pdf 583))
- **Stephen Hawking, John Barrow, Frank Tipler (1986-1988):** Hawking's popular account (1988) is the basis of the book's anthropic discussion; Barrow and Tipler (1986) gave the technical treatment. — Frames the weak/strong anthropic distinction used in §26.8. (DIV §26.8 p.567 (pdf 582))
- **Albert Einstein:** The authors remark that Einstein, whatever his view of a personal God, held deeply that the universe could not have been made otherwise. — Illustrates the design reading of fine-tuning; historians debate how to characterize Einstein's religious views. (DIV §26.8 p.566 (pdf 581))
- **John Archibald Wheeler, Hugh Lieber, Lillian Lieber:** The book closes its anthropic discussion with Wheeler's 'big U' drawing, likened to Hugh Lieber's surreal illustrations in Lillian Lieber's relativity book that inspired d'Inverno (Chapter 1). — Brings the book full circle to its opening story. (DIV §26.8 p.567 (pdf 582))
- **Roger Penrose:** Conformal cyclic cosmology mentioned as an alternative to inflation. — Signals that the early-universe paradigm is not settled. (DIV §26.9 p.568 (pdf 583))

## Notation and conventions

- **Metric signature and units:** Signature (+, −, −, −) with G = c = 1, so the Friedmann equation carries 8πρ/3; SI factors (G, c, ħ, k_B) are restored for numerical work and in the exercises. (DIV §26.1 p.539 (pdf 554))
- **Density parameters:** Ω = ρ(t0)/ρcrit; Ωr, Ωm, ΩΛ for components; Ωc = −k/(H0²R0²) for curvature (positive for open). All are present-day values. — Ωc corresponds to Ω_k elsewhere. ΩΛ is occasionally printed as Ω_λ. Ω also denotes the integration region in (26.64). (DIV §26.1 p.540 (pdf 555))
- **Tilded dimensionless variables:** R̃ = R/R(t0), t̃ = H0t, t_H = 1/H0; R0 = R(t0). In (26.47) the symbol R stands for the normalized R̃. — H̃ in §26.7 is unrelated: it is the constant inflationary Hubble rate (the OCR copy renders it with a hat). (DIV §26.1 p.540 (pdf 555))
- **Effective potential and energy:** V(R̃) = −½(Ωm/R̃ + Ωr/R̃² + ΩΛR̃²), E = Ωc/2. — V(φ) in §26.7 is the inflaton potential, a different object. (DIV §26.1 p.541 (pdf 556))
- **Hubble parameterization:** H0 = 100h km/s/Mpc with dimensionless h. — In §26.3 h also denotes Planck's constant; the text warns about the clash. (DIV §26.2 p.547 (pdf 562))
- **Curvature index versus Boltzmann's constant:** k ∈ {−1, 0, +1} is the curvature index; k_B is Boltzmann's constant. (DIV §26.3 p.548 (pdf 563))
- **Radiation constant:** α = π²k_B⁴/(15ħ³c³) in εrad = αT⁴. — In §26.5 α also denotes the late-time exponential rate H0√ΩΛ. (DIV §26.3 p.549 (pdf 564))
- **Luminosity distance:** d_L = r1R(t0) with redshift factors removed (Chapter 24 definition); r1 = sin χ, χ, sinh χ. — Standard d_L is (1+z) times this. (DIV §26.4 p.552 (pdf 567))
- **Distances and astronomical units:** Distances in pc and Mpc; 1 pc = 3.08×10¹⁶ m ≈ 3.26 ly; H0 in km/s per Mpc. (DIV §26.2 p.546 (pdf 561))
- **Temperature-time formulas:** In (26.55) T is in kelvin and t in seconds. (DIV §26.6 p.559 (pdf 574))
- **Scalar field action and stress tensor:** Lagrangian density ℒ = √−g[−½g^{ab}∇aφ∇bφ + V(φ)], T_ab = −(2/√−g)δℒ/δg^{ab}, □φ = ∇^a∇_aφ; φ* is the slowly varying field value. — The overall sign of the action is chosen so that T_00 = ½φ̇² + V is positive with this signature. (DIV §26.7 p.564 (pdf 579))

## Gaps and pitfalls

- **(26.21), (26.22) and Exercise 26.6 write the rescaled solution as R̃(t̃/H0); since t̃ = H0t the argument should be H0t.** — Readers checking dimensions get confused about whether time is multiplied or divided by H0. *Suggestion:* Present R(t) = R0R̃(H0t) and have learners verify R(t0) = R0 at t̃0 = H0t0.
- **The argument that E ≥ 0 excludes turning points relies on V < 0, which holds only for ΩΛ ≥ 0; with ΩΛ < 0 the potential becomes positive at large R̃ and all models recollapse. The recollapse boundary for Ωm < 1 is therefore ΩΛ = 0, not the cubic root.** — Learners may apply the rule to negative-Λ models and misread the lower part of Fig. 26.2. *Suggestion:* State the ΩΛ ≥ 0 assumption explicitly and show a negative-Λ potential in the explorer.
- **The left panel of Fig. 26.1 is drawn with E = 0, although the text cites it for the E < 0 case with V_max < E.** — Mild mismatch between figure and text. *Suggestion:* Label the parameter values used for each panel (my reconstruction: roughly (0.3, 0.7) and (2, 0.02)).
- **The cubic (26.28) is obtained 'after a bit of algebra', and cubing introduces extra branches; the physical roots are identified only by reference to Fig. 26.2.** — Learners cannot reproduce the boundary curves or know which root to keep. *Suggestion:* Solve (26.27) numerically in the app, or show the parametric solution, and discard roots that fail the uncubed equation.
- **The printed stationarity condition for the Planck spectrum is wrong (it should be 3(e^x − 1) = xe^x, i.e. x ≈ 2.82); the peak and mean energies are quoted as 2.7 and 3 k_BT rather than 2.82 and 2.70.** — Anyone solving the printed equation finds no positive root. *Suggestion:* Give the corrected equation and emphasize that the peak location depends on whether one plots per frequency or per wavelength.
- **The text says most of the remaining chapter is Level 2, but the hatched bar covers only pp. 548-551, the black-body and decoupling part of §26.3.** — Unclear which material a first-time reader may skip. *Suggestion:* Mark in the app which parts need physics outside GR and provide short primers (black body, nucleosynthesis, scalar fields).
- **§26.5 says to take 'Ωm = 0.7' in (26.30); the stated age of about 14 Gyr requires Ωm = 0.3 (ΩΛ = 0.7). With H0 = 72 the formula gives 13.1 Gyr; Ωm = 0.7 would give 10.2 Gyr.** — A learner doing the calculation obtains an age that contradicts the text. *Suggestion:* Use Ωm = 0.3 and have learners compute both numbers to see the effect of Λ on age.
- **§26.5 equates 10¹³ s with about 10⁶ years (it is about 3×10⁵ years), and says the matter-plus-Λ solution holds from just after decoupling; radiation actually stops mattering dynamically after equality, while the photons stop interacting at decoupling. ## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'If you knew only what the universe contains today and how fast it is expanding, could you tell its whole past and future?' Then introduce Ω's as the answer's language.
- Before the formalism, make sure the learner recalls ρm ∝ R⁻³, ρr ∝ R⁻⁴ and ρΛ constant, and can explain each physically (dilution, plus redshift, plus no dilution).
- Teach the sum rule by evaluating the dimensionless equation at today; ask: 'Why is dR̃/dt̃ equal to 1 now?' (Because Ṙ/R = H0 and R̃ = 1.)
- Use the particle-in-a-potential picture actively: have the learner predict the fate for given Ω's by sketching V and E before showing the computed R̃(t̃).
- Check understanding with q0 = Ωm/2 − ΩΛ: ask for the minimum ΩΛ for acceleration if Ωm = 0.3 (0.15) and whether a flat universe can decelerate (yes, if ΩΛ < 1/3).
- Stress that geometry and fate decouple when Λ ≠ 0; ask for a closed universe that expands forever.
- When discussing the CMB, separate three claims: it is thermal, it is isotropic, and it is cold because of expansion; ask which observation supports each.
- Walk through the decoupling estimate as a lesson in estimation: let the learner compute 14 eV/3k_B, then ask what could make it wrong before revealing the tail and photon-number arguments.
- For dark matter, sequence the evidence: stars (0.01) < baryons (0.04) < matter (0.3); ask what each gap implies.
- Use the SN/CMB crossing picture to teach degeneracy: ask why a single probe gives a strip rather than a point.
- Flag book errata when learners reach them: R̃(H0t) not R̃(t̃/H0); age uses Ωm = 0.3; peak condition 3(e^x − 1) = xe^x; H̃² = 8πV/3; exponent sign in (26.62); d_L convention.
- For the flatness and horizon problems, derive both from the formulas already known and only then introduce inflation by changing R(t); ask the learner to predict what exponential expansion does to each formula.
- Common learner question: 'Does inflation break the speed of light?' Answer: no local signal outruns light; the comoving region in causal contact before inflation simply becomes larger than today's observable universe.
- Common learner question: 'Is dark energy the same as the cosmological constant?' Explain the geometric versus source-side readings, quintessence, and the vacuum-energy mismatch.
- Treat the anthropic section as a discussion prompt rather than physics; ask what observation could ever distinguish the weak from the strong form.
- Quote modern parameter values alongside the book's (H0 ≈ 67-73 with the tension, Ωb ≈ 0.049, Ωm ≈ 0.31, ΩΛ ≈ 0.69, T0 = 2.7255 K) and explain they are snapshots.
- Close by linking back: horizons and singularities from black holes (Part D), gravitational waves (Part E) as future cosmological probes, and Hawking-Ellis as the next text.
