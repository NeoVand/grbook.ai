# DIV ch06 · Tensor calculus

> Builds the differential side of tensor analysis on a manifold: why partial derivatives fail, the Lie derivative from dragging along a congruence, the affine connection and covariant derivative, parallel transport and geodesics, the Riemann tensor and flatness theorems, then the metric, its Levi-Civita connection, the curvature identities, Ricci and Einstein tensors, and the Weyl tensor with conformal structure.

**Pages:** printed 85–114 · pdf 100–129 · **Difficulty:** math 4/5, conceptual 4/5, novice-friendliness 2/5

Dense index calculus with many derivations delegated to exercises and no worked numerical example (no sphere, no polar coordinates) inside the text. The Lie derivative via active point transformations and the lemma proofs of §6.7 are the hardest parts; §6.8-6.12 are standard but terse. Physics motivation is entirely absent, so learners without a reason to care about curvature may lose the thread.

## Role in the book

Second of the three purely mathematical chapters (5-7) that the authors insert between special relativity and the principles of general relativity. Chapter 5 supplied tensor algebra with no derivatives and no metric; this chapter supplies every differential object the field equations need (connection, geodesic equation, Riemann, Ricci, Einstein tensor, contracted Bianchi identity) and the conformal machinery (Weyl tensor, conformal flatness) later used for gravitational radiation and Penrose diagrams. Chapter 7 then adds densities, integration, the variational derivation of the metric geodesic equation postponed here, and Killing vectors built from the Lie derivative introduced here.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can show from the chain rule that the partial derivative of a vector field picks up a second-derivative term under coordinate change and explain that this happens because a derivative subtracts components taken at two different points.
- Reader can construct the integral curves of a vector field and use them to define and compute the Lie derivative of any tensor field, including the sign rule for upper and lower indices.
- Reader can state the eight listed properties of the Lie derivative and identify the Lie derivative of a vector with the Lie bracket.
- Reader can define an affine connection through the notion of a parallel vector at a neighbouring point, write the covariant derivative of vectors, covectors and general tensors, and explain why the connection transforms inhomogeneously while the covariant derivative is a tensor.
- Reader can explain why the torsion and the difference of two connections are tensors, and why the sum of two connections is neither.
- Reader can define the absolute derivative along a curve, parallel transport, affine geodesics, and the affine parameter, and show that reparametrization changes only the right-hand side proportional to the tangent.
- Reader can compute the commutator of covariant derivatives on a vector and extract the Riemann tensor (6.40), including the torsion term for non-symmetric connections.
- Reader can construct geodesic coordinates at a point with a quadratic coordinate change and say what they do and do not achieve.
- Reader can reproduce the chain of results integrable connection iff Riemann tensor vanishes, symmetric plus integrable iff affine flat, hence flat iff Riemann tensor vanishes, including the infinitesimal-loop computation.
- Reader can define a metric, its signature, Riemannian versus Lorentzian cases, line element, norm, angle, orthogonality, null vectors, inverse metric, and raising and lowering of indices.
- Reader can write the metric geodesic equation with a general and with an interval parameter, and the null geodesic equation with an affine parameter.
- Reader can derive the metric (Levi-Civita) connection by requiring affine and metric geodesics to coincide, and prove that it is the unique symmetric connection with vanishing covariant derivative of the metric.
- Reader can prove that a metric is flat exactly when its Riemann tensor vanishes, using (6.77).
- Reader can list the algebraic symmetries of R_abcd, count independent components n^2(n^2-1)/12, write the Bianchi identities, and build the Ricci tensor, Ricci scalar, Einstein tensor and contracted Bianchi identity.
- Reader can define the Weyl tensor, state its symmetries and trace-freeness, define conformally related and conformally flat metrics, and state which quantities are conformally invariant.

## Teaching approach

A compact, proof-oriented components treatment close in style to the classical component texts such as Synge and Schild that it lists for further reading. Every derivative is motivated by the same obstacle stated at the outset, that a derivative subtracts tensors living at different points, and each new structure (a congruence, a connection, a metric) is introduced as the extra ingredient that makes that subtraction legitimate. Definitions are built from infinitesimal displacements and Taylor expansion, results are packaged as boxed lemmas and theorems with necessity and sufficiency proved separately, and many routine verifications are deferred to '(exercise)' or '(check)' markers keyed to the end-of-chapter list. Pictures are mostly schematic line drawings of curves and arrows on blob-shaped regions; only Figs 6.4 and 6.8 show a hatched or ribbon-like sheet in perspective, and no figure shows a concrete curved surface such as a sphere, and there is no physics in the chapter beyond the choice of Lorentzian signature.

**Style:** components-first, axiomatic-mathematical, derivation-heavy, diagram-driven

**Narrative arc**

1. Pose the question of which differential operations are tensorial after the algebraic ones of Chapter 5.
2. Show by the chain rule (6.1) that partial differentiation is not, and diagnose the cause as comparison of components at two points with different transformation matrices.
3. Announce the plan: three derivatives, each needing an auxiliary structure (a vector field, an affine connection, a metric).
4. Lie derivative: vector field gives a congruence of integral curves; view a coordinate shift actively to drag a tensor along a curve; subtract at the same point; derive (6.8) for a rank-2 tensor; show it reduces to an ordinary derivative in adapted coordinates; list properties and the general formula (6.17).
5. Covariant derivative: posit a parallel vector at a neighbouring point with a change bilinear in the vector and the displacement; this introduces Gamma; demand tensor character to get the inhomogeneous law (6.23); extend to covectors via Leibniz and to all tensors; note torsion and restrict to symmetric connections.
6. Transport along curves: absolute derivative, parallel propagation as a first-order ODE, affine geodesic as a self-parallel curve, reparametrization invariance of the geodesic property, affine parameter and affine length, local existence and uniqueness versus global focusing.
7. Curvature appears algebraically: commutator of covariant derivatives defines R^a_bcd; tensor character follows by the quotient argument.
8. Tool: geodesic coordinates kill Gamma at a point but not its derivatives.
9. Geometric meaning of R: path dependence of parallel transport; prove integrability iff R=0 by an integrability condition (necessity) and an infinitesimal loop plus curve deformation (sufficiency); prove symmetric plus integrable iff flat with a parallel frame whose dual covectors are gradients; combine into the flatness theorem.
10. Add a metric: signature, lengths, angles, null cone, inverse metric, index gymnastics.
11. Metric geodesics as curves of stationary interval, equation quoted with Christoffel symbols (derivation postponed to Chapter 7); null geodesics.
12. Unify: choose Gamma equal to the Christoffel symbols so that straightest and extremal curves coincide; prove metric compatibility and its converse.
13. Metric flatness equals vanishing Riemann tensor for the metric connection, with (6.77) flagged for later use.
14. Catalogue curvature: symmetries, component count, Bianchi identities, Ricci, Ricci scalar, Einstein tensor, contracted Bianchi identity, sign-convention warning.
15. Close with the Weyl tensor as the trace-free part, conformal rescalings and their invariants, and two quoted theorems on conformal flatness.

**Signature moves**

- A single diagnosis (subtracting tensors at different points) reused to motivate all three kinds of derivative.
- Active reading of an infinitesimal coordinate change x -> x + du X as a point map that drags tensors, with the Jacobian expansion (6.4)-(6.5) doing the work.
- Defining the connection through a bilinear ansatz for the change of a 'parallel' vector, with Fig. 6.5 showing the tensorial difference vector as a triangle of arrows at Q.
- Comparison of Lie and covariant derivative formulas ('compare and contrast (6.17)'), with the index position of the derivative in Gamma called out twice.
- Starred equality sign marking equations valid only in special coordinates (adapted coordinates, geodesic coordinates).
- Lemma-lemma-theorem architecture with separate necessity and sufficiency proofs for the flatness results.
- Two definitions of straight line (never turns versus extremal length) shown to be different in general and reconciled by a choice of connection.
- Heavy use of '(exercise)' and '(check)' inline, so the exercise list is the real completion of many arguments.
- Case list of Riemann component counts for n = 1, 2, 3, 4 to motivate the Weyl tensor as the part not fixed by Ricci.

## Section by section

### §6.1 Partial derivative of a tensor — p.85 (pdf 100)

Having finished algebraic tensor operations, the authors ask which derivatives preserve tensor character. Differentiating the vector transformation law shows an extra term containing second derivatives of the new coordinates, so the partial derivative of a vector is not a (1,1) tensor. The deeper reason is that a derivative subtracts values at neighbouring points, where the transformation matrices differ. The remedy previewed is to add structure: a vector field (Lie derivative), an affine connection (covariant derivative) and a metric (metric connection).

**Concepts:** non-tensorial character of the partial derivative, comparison of tensors at different points, auxiliary structure for tensorial differentiation

**Key moves**
- Chain rule on X'^a = (dx'^a/dx^b) X^b gives a homogeneous (1,1) term plus an inhomogeneous second-derivative term (6.1).
- Write the difference quotient and note that components at P and Q transform with Jacobians evaluated at different points.
- Roadmap of three derivatives, each tied to one added structure.

### §6.2 The Lie derivative — p.86 (pdf 101)

A congruence of curves determines a tangent vector field, and conversely a non-vanishing vector field determines integral curves by solving dx/du = X. The Lie derivative is defined by dragging a tensor from P to a nearby point Q on the integral curve through P using the infinitesimal point transformation x -> x + du X read actively, then subtracting the dragged tensor from the one already at Q and dividing by du. For a rank-2 contravariant tensor the Jacobian expansion gives L_X T^ab = X^c d_c T^ab - T^ac d_c X^b - T^cb d_c X^a. In coordinates adapted to X (X = d_1) the Lie derivative is simply d_1. The section closes with eight properties: linearity, Leibniz rule, type preservation, commuting with contraction, action on scalars (directional derivative), on vectors (Lie bracket), on covectors, and the general formula with a minus term per upper index and a plus term per lower index.

**Concepts:** congruence of curves, integral curves of a vector field, active versus passive transformation, Lie dragging, Lie derivative, adapted coordinates, properties of the Lie derivative, Lie bracket as a Lie derivative, Lie derivative of a covector, general Lie derivative formula

**Key moves**
- Integral curves from the ODE (6.2), existence guaranteed locally.
- Point transformation (6.3) with Jacobian delta + du d_b X^a (6.4).
- Dragged components by the tensor law to first order in du (6.5).
- Components already at Q by Taylor expansion (6.6).
- Difference quotient definition (6.7) yields (6.8).
- Adapted coordinates reduce L_X to d_1 (6.9)-(6.10), an alternative starting definition.
- Property list (6.11)-(6.17), with the index sign rule.

### §6.3 The affine connection and covariant differentiation — p.90 (pdf 105)

The naive change delta X between neighbouring points is not tensorial. The authors posit a vector at Q that is 'parallel' to X at P, differing from X(P) by a small amount assumed bilinear in X and the displacement, which defines n^3 coefficients Gamma^a_bc with a conventional minus sign. The covariant derivative is the limit of the difference between X(Q) and this parallel vector, giving nabla_c X^a = d_c X^a + Gamma^a_bc X^b. Requiring this to be a (1,1) tensor forces the inhomogeneous transformation law (6.23)/(6.24), whose extra term cancels the one in (6.1). Scalars are differentiated partially, the Leibniz rule fixes the covector formula with a minus sign, and the general formula follows. Differences of connections and the torsion are tensors; the book then restricts to symmetric (torsion-free) connections and notes that partials in any Lie derivative can then be replaced by covariant derivatives.

**Concepts:** parallel vector at a neighbouring point, affine connection, inhomogeneous transformation law of the connection, covariant derivative, affine manifold, covariant derivative of a covector, covariant derivative of a general tensor, difference of two connections is a tensor, torsion tensor, symmetric connection, Lie derivative written with covariant derivatives

**Key moves**
- Taylor expansion (6.18)-(6.19) and the non-tensorial difference.
- Bilinear ansatz (6.21) for the change of the parallel vector.
- Difference vector (6.20) made tensorial by construction (Fig. 6.5).
- Covariant derivative (6.22) with the derivative index last on Gamma.
- Demanding tensor character gives (6.23), equivalently (6.24).
- Scalar rule (6.25) plus Leibniz gives (6.26); general formula (6.27).
- Torsion as antisymmetric part; restriction to symmetric connections (6.28); (6.29).

### §6.4 Affine geodesics — p.92 (pdf 107)

Using the notation nabla_X for contraction with X, the absolute derivative D/Du along a curve of the congruence is defined, and a tensor is parallel transported when this vanishes, a first-order ODE with a unique solution for given initial data. An affine geodesic is a curve whose tangent is transported parallel to itself up to rescaling, giving the geodesic equation with a lambda term. Only the symmetric part of Gamma enters. A change of parameter preserves the form with a new lambda (6.35), so one can always choose an affine parameter s making the right-hand side vanish; affine parameters are fixed up to s -> alpha s + beta. Affine length can compare segments of one geodesic but not of different geodesics. Locally there is a unique geodesic in each direction through a point and between nearby points, but geodesics may refocus globally.

**Concepts:** directional covariant derivative, absolute derivative, parallel transport, affine geodesic, reparametrization of geodesics, affine parameter, affine length, local existence and uniqueness of geodesics, geodesic focusing

**Key moves**
- Define D/Du = nabla_X along the curve (6.31) and parallel transport (6.32).
- Self-parallel tangent condition (6.33) and its component form (6.34).
- Only the symmetric part of Gamma survives contraction with the symmetric tangent product.
- Reparametrization computation leading to (6.35)-(6.36).
- Choose the parameter to set lambda to zero: (6.37)/(6.38).
- Affine freedom s -> alpha s + beta, affine length, ODE uniqueness, focusing (Figs 6.6, 6.7).

### §6.5 The Riemann tensor — p.94 (pdf 109)

Covariant derivatives need not commute. Writing out the second covariant derivatives of a vector and subtracting, with mixed partials assumed to commute, gives a term linear in X defining R^a_bcd plus a torsion term multiplying nabla X. For symmetric connections the commutator is (1/2)R^a_bcd X^b in bracket notation, and since the left side is a tensor for arbitrary X, R is a (1,3) tensor. For symmetric connections the commutator of any tensor is expressible through R, so R = 0 is equivalent to all covariant derivatives commuting; a geometric meaning is promised for §6.7.

**Concepts:** commutator of covariant derivatives, Riemann tensor, quotient argument for tensor character

**Key moves**
- Treat nabla_c X^a as a (1,1) tensor and apply (6.27) again.
- Subtract the two orders; derivative-of-X terms cancel.
- Identify R^a_bcd (6.40) and the torsion term (6.39).
- Anti-symmetrization form (6.41) and quotient argument for tensor character.

### §6.6 Geodesic coordinates — p.95 (pdf 110)

At any chosen point P one can pick coordinates in which the (symmetric) connection vanishes at P. Placing P at the origin and adding a quadratic correction with constant symmetric coefficients Q, the Jacobian is the identity at P while the second derivatives equal Q, so the transformation law gives Gamma' = Gamma - Q at P; choosing Q equal to Gamma at P does it. The derivatives of Gamma generally do not vanish, the construction extends along a curve but not to a neighbourhood, and a coordinate system with Gamma zero everywhere defines affine flatness.

**Concepts:** geodesic coordinates, affine flatness

**Key moves**
- Quadratic coordinate change (6.42) about P at the origin.
- Jacobian is the identity at P; second derivatives are Q.
- Insert into (6.23): Gamma'(P) = Gamma(P) - Q.
- Symmetry of Gamma allows Q = Gamma(P), giving (6.43).
- Caveat: first derivatives of Gamma survive; define affine flat.

### §6.7 Affine flatness — p.96 (pdf 111)

In a general affine manifold parallel transport between two points depends on the path; a connection is integrable when it does not. Lemma 1: integrable iff Riemann tensor vanishes. Necessity uses the fact that integrability gives a covariantly constant field, whose first-order PDE requires commuting mixed partials, which by the commutator identity means R X = 0 for arbitrary X. Sufficiency expands parallel transport around an infinitesimal parallelogram to second order, finding the two routes differ by -R^a_bcd X^b dx^c Dx^d, then deforms any path into any other through infinitesimal steps on a simply connected manifold. Lemma 2: symmetric and integrable iff affine flat. Sufficiency builds n parallel, linearly independent vector fields, takes the dual covector frame, uses symmetry to show those covectors are curl-free and hence gradients of n functions, and uses those functions as coordinates in which Gamma vanishes. Together: a manifold (with symmetric connection) is affine flat iff its Riemann tensor vanishes.

**Concepts:** integrable connection, path dependence of parallel transport, integrability condition, parallel transport around an infinitesimal loop, simply connected manifold, parallel frame and dual frame, affine flatness, flatness theorem

**Key moves**
- Necessity: integrability gives nabla_c X^a = 0 everywhere (6.44); require d_d d_c X = d_c d_d X (6.45); commutator identity turns this into R X = 0.
- Sufficiency: second-order transport along two routes of an infinitesimal loop (Fig. 6.9); difference equals R^a_bdc X^b dx^c Dx^d.
- Deform one curve into another by infinitesimal stages (Fig. 6.10), assuming simple connectedness.
- Lemma 2: parallel frame X_i^a, dual frame (6.46), Gamma from the frame (6.47)-(6.48), symmetry implies curl-free dual covectors, which are gradients of f^i.
- New coordinates x'^a = f^a make the Jacobians the frames (6.49)-(6.50) and Gamma' vanish identically.
- Combine lemmas into the theorem.

### §6.8 The metric — p.100 (pdf 115)

A metric is a symmetric (0,2) tensor field with non-zero determinant. Its signature, positive minus negative eigenvalue count, is coordinate independent even though eigenvalues are not. Riemannian metrics are positive definite; relativity uses Lorentzian metrics with one positive and three negative eigenvalues (signature -2, with the +2 convention noted). The metric supplies the line element, norms, angles, orthogonality, null vectors forming a null cone in the tangent space, and an inverse metric used to raise and lower indices; index order must then be kept track of because tensors with raised and lowered indices are treated as one geometric object.

**Concepts:** metric tensor, metric determinant, signature, Riemannian metric, Lorentzian metric, line element, norm of a vector, null vector and null cone, angle between vectors, orthogonality, inverse metric, raising and lowering indices, order of mixed indices

**Key moves**
- Non-singularity via g = det g_ab != 0 (6.51).
- Signature from eigenvalue signs, with 'why?' questions left to the reader.
- Line element (6.52), norm (6.53), null vectors (6.54).
- Angle (6.55) and orthogonality (6.56); a null vector is self-orthogonal.

### §6.9 Metric geodesics — p.101 (pdf 116)

For a timelike curve the interval is the integral of the square root of g(dx/du, dx/du). A timelike metric geodesic is a curve whose interval is stationary under endpoint-fixed variations (maximum, minimum or saddle). The Euler-Lagrange equations, derived in Chapter 7, are quoted in terms of Christoffel symbols of the first and second kind, with a right-hand side depending on the parametrization; choosing the parameter linear in s removes it and gives the geodesic equation plus the normalization g(dx/ds, dx/ds) = 1. Spacelike geodesics are analogous with a sign flip. Null geodesics have zero interval, admit an affine parameter defined up to linear maps, and satisfy the same equation with a null tangent.

**Concepts:** interval along a curve, metric geodesic, Christoffel symbols of the first kind, Christoffel symbols of the second kind, proper parametrization of metric geodesics, spacelike geodesic, null geodesic, affine parameter

**Key moves**

- Linear parameter (6.66) removes the right-hand side: (6.67)-(6.68).

### §6.10 The metric connection — p.103 (pdf 118)

With both a connection and a metric a manifold has two families of 'straight' curves: affine geodesics (never change direction) and metric geodesics (extremal length), in general different (Fig. 6.11). They coincide if the connection is set equal to the Christoffel symbols of the second kind, which is automatically symmetric and transforms as a connection. From here on the book always uses this metric connection. It makes the metric covariantly constant, and conversely a symmetric connection with covariantly constant metric must be the metric connection; the Kronecker delta and inverse metric are also covariantly constant.

**Concepts:** affine versus metric geodesics, metric connection, metric compatibility, fundamental theorem for the metric connection, covariant constancy of the Kronecker delta and inverse metric

**Key moves**
- Compare (6.37) with (6.67): identify Gamma with the Christoffel symbols (6.71)-(6.72).
- Symmetry (6.73) and connection transformation law (exercise).
- Metric compatibility (6.74) and its converse as a theorem.

### §6.11 Metric flatness — p.104 (pdf 119)

At any single point a metric can be brought to diagonal form with entries +1 or -1, and by continuity and non-singularity the signature is constant. A metric is flat if one coordinate system achieves this form everywhere. Theorem: a metric is flat iff its Riemann tensor vanishes. Necessity: constant metric means vanishing Christoffel symbols everywhere, hence vanishing derivatives and vanishing R. Sufficiency: R = 0 gives coordinates with Gamma = 0 everywhere by §6.7, and metric compatibility rewritten as (6.77) then forces the metric components to be constant, so a linear change diagonalizes them. Hence for the metric connection, metric flatness and affine flatness coincide.

**Concepts:** pointwise diagonalization of the metric, invariance of signature, flat metric, metric flatness theorem, derivative of the metric in terms of the connection

**Key moves**
- Standard matrix theory diagonalizes g at a point to +-1 entries.
- Flatness defined as global existence of such coordinates.
- Necessity via constant metric and (6.72).
- Sufficiency via §6.7 theorem and (6.77).
- Equivalence of metric and affine flatness for the metric connection.

### §6.12 The curvature tensor — p.105 (pdf 120)

With the metric connection, the Riemann-Christoffel tensor depends on the metric and its first two derivatives. It is antisymmetric in its last pair, obeys a cyclic identity from symmetry of the connection, and once lowered is symmetric under exchange of pairs and antisymmetric in the first pair. These reduce the independent components from n^4 to n^2(n^2-1)/12. The differential Bianchi identities hold, most easily shown in geodesic coordinates. Contractions define the symmetric Ricci tensor and Ricci scalar, which combine into the symmetric Einstein tensor, whose divergence vanishes identically by the contracted Bianchi identities. Other sign conventions are flagged.

**Concepts:** Riemann tensor of the metric connection, algebraic symmetries of the Riemann tensor, cyclic identity, number of independent Riemann components, Bianchi identities, Ricci tensor, Ricci scalar, Einstein tensor, contracted Bianchi identities, curvature sign conventions

**Key moves**
- Antisymmetry in last pair (6.78) is immediate from (6.40).
- Cyclic identity (6.79) from symmetric Gamma.
- Pair symmetry (6.80) via geodesic coordinates, hence first-pair antisymmetry (6.81); summary (6.82).
- Counting formula.
- Bianchi identity (6.83) via geodesic coordinates.
- Contractions (6.84)-(6.86) and divergence-free Einstein tensor (6.87).

### §6.13 The Weyl tensor — p.107 (pdf 122)

The curvature tensor has 0, 1, 6 and 20 independent components for n = 1, 2, 3, 4; in four dimensions ten are carried by the Ricci tensor and ten by the Weyl tensor. The Weyl (conformal) tensor is defined for n >= 4 by subtracting Ricci and scalar pieces from R_abcd; it shares Riemann's symmetries and is in addition trace-free on every pair. Conformally related metrics (g-bar = Omega^2 g) share angles, length ratios, null geodesics and the Weyl tensor with index position (1,3), which is therefore conformally invariant, unlike g, Gamma, R^a_bcd, or the Weyl tensor with other index positions. Two theorems are quoted without proof: vanishing Weyl tensor is necessary and sufficient for conformal flatness, and every two-dimensional Riemannian manifold is conformally flat.

**Concepts:** Riemann component counts in low dimensions, Weyl tensor, trace-free property of the Weyl tensor, conformally related metrics, conformal invariance, conformally flat metric, Weyl criterion for conformal flatness, conformal flatness in two dimensions

**Key moves**
- Case list n = 1..4 motivates splitting off the Ricci part.
- Definition in n dimensions and in four dimensions (6.88).
- Symmetries (6.89) and extra trace condition (6.90), hence totally trace-free.

## Concepts

### non-tensorial character of the partial derivative

*theorem · core* · also: ordinary derivative of a tensor is not a tensor

Under a change of coordinates the partial derivatives of a vector field's components acquire, besides the (1,1) tensor term, an extra term proportional to the second derivatives of the new coordinates with respect to the old, so d_b X^a is not a tensor except under linear transformations.

**How introduced:** Opening question of the chapter: which differential operations are tensorial? Answered negatively by differentiating the Chapter 5 vector transformation law with the chain rule.

**Prerequisites:** contravariant vector, tensor transformation law, chain rule

$$
\partial'_c X'^a = \frac{\partial x'^a}{\partial x^b}\frac{\partial x^d}{\partial x'^c}\partial_d X^b + \frac{\partial^2 x'^a}{\partial x^b\partial x^d}\frac{\partial x^d}{\partial x'^c}X^b
$$

**Notes:** Generalizes the Chapter 5 observation that second partial derivatives of a scalar are not tensorial.

**Where:** DIV §6.1 p.85 (pdf 100)

### comparison of tensors at different points

*principle · core* · also: subtracting tensors at neighbouring points

Tensors at different points of a manifold live in different tangent spaces and transform with Jacobian matrices evaluated at those points, so their component difference has no invariant meaning; any tensorial derivative must first carry one tensor to the other's point by some added rule.

**How introduced:** Given as the fundamental reason behind (6.1): the difference quotient for X^a involves transformation matrices at P and at Q.

**Prerequisites:** tangent space, tensor field

**Notes:** This single diagnosis is reused to motivate the Lie derivative (drag along a congruence), the connection (parallel vector) and the metric connection.

**Where:** DIV §6.1 p.85 (pdf 100); DIV §6.1 p.86 (pdf 101)

### auxiliary structure for tensorial differentiation

*principle · introduced* · also: three types of differentiation

To differentiate tensors tensorially one must add structure to the bare manifold: a vector field gives the Lie derivative, an affine connection gives the covariant derivative, and a metric gives a preferred (metric) connection and its covariant derivative.

**How introduced:** Stated as a roadmap at the end of §6.1 before the three constructions are carried out.

**Prerequisites:** comparison of tensors at different points

**Where:** DIV §6.1 p.86 (pdf 101)

### congruence of curves

*definition · developed* · also: congruence

A family of curves filling a region of the manifold such that exactly one curve passes through each point; its tangent vectors form a vector field.

**How introduced:** Starting point of §6.2: take tangent vectors dx^a/du along each curve to obtain a vector field over the manifold (Fig. 6.1).

**Prerequisites:** parametric curve, tangent vector to a curve

$$
X^a = \frac{dx^a}{du}
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.2 p.86 (pdf 101)

### integral curves of a vector field

*definition · developed* · also: trajectories, field lines, streamlines, orbits of a vector field

The curves x^a(u) solving dx^a/du = X^a(x(u)); through each point where X is non-zero there is locally a unique such curve, so a vector field generates a local congruence.

**How introduced:** Converse of the congruence construction, explicitly likened to field lines or streamlines of vector analysis and justified by the ODE existence and uniqueness theorem (Fig. 6.2).

**Prerequisites:** congruence of curves, ordinary differential equations

$$
\frac{dx^a}{du} = X^a(x(u))
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.2 p.86 (pdf 101); DIV §6.2 p.87 (pdf 102)

### active versus passive transformation

*convention · developed* · also: point transformation, active view of a coordinate transformation

A passive transformation relabels the same point with new coordinates; an active one keeps the coordinate system and moves points, here x^a -> x^a + du X^a, so that the tensor transformation law can be reinterpreted as carrying tensors from one point to another.

**How introduced:** Flagged at the start of §6.2 as the idea on which the intricate Lie derivative argument rests; the map (6.3) sends P to Q in the same chart (Fig. 6.4).

**Prerequisites:** coordinate transformation, integral curves of a vector field

$$
\tilde{x}^a = x^a + \delta u\, X^a(x)
$$

**Notes:** Chapter 7 (§7.7) makes the active viewpoint systematic with diffeomorphisms, push-forwards and pull-backs.

**Where:** DIV §6.2 p.86 (pdf 101); DIV §6.2 p.87 (pdf 102)

### Lie dragging

*operation · developed* · also: dragged-along tensor, dragging a tensor along a congruence

Transporting a tensor from P to a nearby point Q on the integral curve of X by applying the tensor transformation law with the Jacobian of the infinitesimal point map x -> x + du X, giving components that can be compared with the tensor field's own value at Q.

**How introduced:** Pictured in Fig. 6.3 as a 'tensor' arrow at P reproduced as a dashed 'dragged-along tensor' at Q next to the actual tensor at Q; computed for T^ab in (6.5).

**Prerequisites:** active versus passive transformation, tensor transformation law

$$
\tilde{T}^{ab}(\tilde{x}) = T^{ab}(x) + [\partial_c X^a T^{cb} + \partial_c X^b T^{ac}]\delta u + O(\delta u^2)
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.2 p.87 (pdf 102); DIV §6.2 p.88 (pdf 103)

### Lie derivative

*operation · core* · also: L_X, Lie differentiation

The rate of change of a tensor field along the flow of a vector field X, defined as the limit as du -> 0 of the difference between the field's value at Q and the value dragged from P, divided by du; it needs only X (no connection or metric) and yields a tensor of the same type.

**How introduced:** Defined by the limit (6.7) after the dragging computation, with the explicit rank-2 contravariant result (6.8).

**Prerequisites:** Lie dragging, integral curves of a vector field, partial derivative

$$
\mathrm{L}_X T^{ab} = \lim_{\delta u\to 0}\frac{T^{ab}(\tilde{x}) - \tilde{T}^{ab}(\tilde{x})}{\delta u}
$$

$$
\mathrm{L}_X T^{ab} = X^c\partial_c T^{ab} - T^{ac}\partial_c X^b - T^{cb}\partial_c X^a
$$

**Notes:** Used in DIV ch07 §7.7 to define symmetries and Killing vectors via L_X g_ab = 0.

**Where:** DIV §6.2 p.88 (pdf 103); DIV §6.2 p.89 (pdf 104)

### adapted coordinates

*technique · introduced* · also: coordinates adapted to a vector field, flow-box coordinates

Near a point where X is non-zero one can choose coordinates in which the integral curve is the x^1 line and X^a = delta^a_1, so X = d_1 and the Lie derivative of any tensor is just the partial derivative of its components with respect to x^1.

**How introduced:** Stated without proof after (6.8) as an alternative starting point for the definition, using the starred equality sign.

**Prerequisites:** Lie derivative, integral curves of a vector field

$$
X^a \stackrel{*}{=} \delta^a_1
$$

$$
\mathrm{L}_X T^{ab} \stackrel{*}{=} \partial_1 T^{ab}
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.2 p.88 (pdf 103)

### properties of the Lie derivative

*theorem · developed* · also: linearity, Leibniz, type preservation, commutation with contraction

L_X is linear over constants, satisfies the product rule, maps (p,q) tensors to (p,q) tensors, commutes with contraction, and reduces on scalars to the directional derivative X^a d_a phi.

**How introduced:** Listed as consequences of the definition at the close of §6.2; commutation with contraction is proved in Exercise 6.1 via L_X delta = 0.

**Prerequisites:** Lie derivative

$$
\mathrm{L}_X(\lambda Y^a + \mu Z^a) = \lambda\mathrm{L}_X Y^a + \mu\mathrm{L}_X Z^a
$$

$$
\mathrm{L}_X(Y^a Z_{bc}) = Y^a\mathrm{L}_X Z_{bc} + (\mathrm{L}_X Y^a)Z_{bc}
$$

$$
\mathrm{L}_X\phi = X^a\partial_a\phi
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.2 p.89 (pdf 104)

### Lie bracket as a Lie derivative

*identity · developed* · also: L_X Y = [X,Y]

The Lie derivative of a vector field Y along X equals the commutator of the two vector fields viewed as derivative operators, with components X^b d_b Y^a - Y^b d_b X^a.

**How introduced:** Property 6 of the list, linking back to the Lie bracket defined algebraically in Chapter 5.

**Prerequisites:** Lie bracket, Lie derivative

$$
\mathrm{L}_X Y^a = [X,Y]^a = X^b\partial_b Y^a - Y^b\partial_b X^a
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.2 p.89 (pdf 104)

### general Lie derivative formula

*identity · core* · also: index sign rule for Lie derivatives

For any tensor, L_X T equals X^c d_c T minus one term T^{..c..} d_c X^a for each upper index plus one term T_{..c..} d_b X^c for each lower index.

**How introduced:** Stated as property 8 after the scalar, vector and covector cases (6.14)-(6.16); the covector rule appears as (6.16).

**Prerequisites:** Lie derivative, tensor of type (p,q)

$$
\mathrm{L}_X Y_a = X^b\partial_b Y_a + Y_b\partial_a X^b
$$

$$
\mathrm{L}_X T^{a\cdots}_{b\cdots} = X^c\partial_c T^{a\cdots}_{b\cdots} - T^{c\cdots}_{b\cdots}\partial_c X^a - \cdots + T^{a\cdots}_{c\cdots}\partial_b X^c + \cdots
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.2 p.89 (pdf 104)

### parallel vector at a neighbouring point

*definition · developed* · also: bar-delta X, change of a vector under parallel displacement

A vector at Q = x + dx declared parallel to X(P); its components differ from X(P) by a small non-tensorial amount assumed to vanish with X or dx and to be bilinear in them, written -Gamma^a_bc X^b dx^c.

**How introduced:** Motivated by the failure of the naive difference (6.19); the difference between X(Q) and the parallel vector is required to be tensorial (6.20), drawn as a triangle of arrows in Fig. 6.5.

**Prerequisites:** comparison of tensors at different points, Taylor expansion

$$
\bar{\delta}X^a(x) = -\Gamma^a_{bc}(x)X^b(x)\delta x^c
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.3 p.90 (pdf 105)

### affine connection

*mathematical-object · core* · also: connection, affinity, Gamma^a_bc, connection coefficients

A set of n^3 functions Gamma^a_bc on the manifold that specify parallel displacement and transform under coordinate change by the tensor law plus an inhomogeneous second-derivative term, exactly compensating the non-tensorial part of partial derivatives.

**How introduced:** Emerges from the bilinear ansatz (6.21); its transformation law (6.23)/(6.24) is derived by demanding that nabla_c X^a be a (1,1) tensor.

**Prerequisites:** parallel vector at a neighbouring point, tensor transformation law

$$
\Gamma'^a_{bc} = \frac{\partial x'^a}{\partial x^d}\frac{\partial x^e}{\partial x'^b}\frac{\partial x^f}{\partial x'^c}\Gamma^d_{ef} - \frac{\partial x^d}{\partial x'^b}\frac{\partial x^e}{\partial x'^c}\frac{\partial^2 x'^a}{\partial x^d\partial x^e}
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.3 p.90 (pdf 105); DIV §6.3 p.91 (pdf 106)

### inhomogeneous transformation law of the connection

*theorem · core* · also: Gamma is not a tensor

Because its transformation law is linear but inhomogeneous, the connection is not a tensor: it can be non-zero in one chart and zero at a point (or everywhere, if flat) in another.

**How introduced:** Read off from (6.23)-(6.24) and immediately contrasted with the tensor law; later exploited in geodesic coordinates.

**Prerequisites:** affine connection

**Where:** DIV §6.3 p.91 (pdf 106)

### covariant derivative

*operation · core* · also: nabla_c, semicolon derivative, X^a_{;c}

The tensorial derivative defined by comparing X(Q) with the vector at Q parallel to X(P), per unit coordinate displacement; for a vector it is d_c X^a + Gamma^a_bc X^b, raising the covariant rank by one.

**How introduced:** Defined by a limit following Fig. 6.5 and evaluated using (6.18) and (6.21).

**Prerequisites:** affine connection, partial derivative

$$
\nabla_c X^a = \partial_c X^a + \Gamma^a_{bc}X^b
$$

**Notes:** The book stresses that the differentiation index sits last on Gamma.

**Where:** DIV §6.3 p.90 (pdf 105); DIV §6.3 p.91 (pdf 106)

### affine manifold

*definition · introduced*

A manifold equipped with a continuous affine connection.

**How introduced:** Named right after the connection transformation law is established.

**Prerequisites:** affine connection

**Where:** DIV §6.3 p.91 (pdf 106)

### covariant derivative of a covector

*identity · developed* · also: nabla_c X_a

For a covector, nabla_c X_a = d_c X_a - Gamma^b_ac X_b, obtained by requiring that the covariant derivative of a scalar be its partial derivative and that the Leibniz rule hold for X_a Y^a.

**How introduced:** Derived as an exercise (Exercise 6.5) from (6.22), (6.25) and the product rule.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative, Leibniz rule

$$
\nabla_a\phi = \partial_a\phi
$$

$$
\nabla_c X_a = \partial_c X_a - \Gamma^b_{ac}X_b
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.3 p.91 (pdf 106)

### covariant derivative of a general tensor

*identity · core*

Partial derivative plus one +Gamma term for each upper index and one -Gamma term for each lower index, always with the derivative index last on Gamma.

**How introduced:** Written as (6.27) and explicitly compared with the Lie derivative rule (6.17), which has the opposite sign pattern and derivatives of X instead of Gamma. Exercise 6.4 shows that covariant differentiation commutes with contraction, because nabla_c delta^a_b = 0.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative, covariant derivative of a covector

$$
\nabla_c T^{a\cdots}_{b\cdots} = \partial_c T^{a\cdots}_{b\cdots} + \Gamma^a_{dc}T^{d\cdots}_{b\cdots} + \cdots - \Gamma^d_{bc}T^{a\cdots}_{d\cdots} - \cdots
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.3 p.91 (pdf 106)

### difference of two connections is a tensor

*theorem · introduced*

If Gamma and Gamma-hat are two connections on the same manifold, their difference transforms as a (1,2) tensor because the inhomogeneous terms cancel; their sum is neither a connection nor a tensor.

**How introduced:** Noted directly from the transformation law after (6.27).

**Prerequisites:** inhomogeneous transformation law of the connection

**Where:** DIV §6.3 p.91 (pdf 106)

### torsion tensor

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: torsion

The antisymmetric part T^a_bc = Gamma^a_bc - Gamma^a_cb of a connection, which is a tensor because the inhomogeneous term in the law is symmetric in b and c.

**How introduced:** Introduced by the same cancellation argument as for differences of connections, then set to zero for the rest of the book.

**Prerequisites:** affine connection, difference of two connections is a tensor

$$
T^a_{bc} = \Gamma^a_{bc} - \Gamma^a_{cb}
$$

**Notes:** Reappears as the extra term in the commutator (6.39).

**Where:** DIV §6.3 p.91 (pdf 106); DIV §6.3 p.92 (pdf 107)

### symmetric connection

*definition · core* · also: torsion-free connection

A connection with vanishing torsion, Gamma^a_bc = Gamma^a_cb; the book assumes this from §6.3 onward unless stated otherwise.

**How introduced:** Imposed as a standing restriction right after torsion is defined.

**Prerequisites:** torsion tensor

$$
\Gamma^a_{bc} = \Gamma^a_{cb}
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.3 p.91 (pdf 106); DIV §6.3 p.92 (pdf 107)

### Lie derivative written with covariant derivatives

*identity · introduced*

For a symmetric connection every partial derivative in a Lie derivative formula may be replaced by a covariant derivative, since the Gamma terms cancel pairwise.

**How introduced:** Quoted as a useful consequence of symmetry, with the vector case (6.29) left as Exercise 6.6.

**Prerequisites:** symmetric connection, general Lie derivative formula

\mathrm{L}_X Y^a = X^b\nabla_b Y^a - Y^b\nabla_b X^a

**Notes:** Shows the Lie derivative is independent of the connection and makes its tensor character manifest.

### directional covariant derivative

*operation · introduced* · also: nabla_X

The covariant derivative of a tensor contracted with a vector field X, X^c nabla_c T.

**How introduced:** Notation introduced at the start of §6.4 to write the absolute derivative compactly; linearity properties are Exercise 6.7.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative

\nabla_X T^{a\cdots}_{b\cdots} = X^c\nabla_c T^{a\cdots}_{b\cdots}

### absolute derivative

*operation · developed* · also: intrinsic derivative, D/Du

The covariant rate of change of a tensor along a curve with tangent X = dx/du, DT/Du = X^c nabla_c T; for fields defined only on the curve it equals dT/du plus Gamma terms contracted with dx/du.

**How introduced:** Defined for curves of the congruence generated by X, reusing the §6.2 link between vector fields and curves.

**Prerequisites:** directional covariant derivative, congruence of curves

\frac{D}{Du}T^{a\cdots}_{b\cdots} = \nabla_X T^{a\cdots}_{b\cdots}

**Where:** DIV §6.4 p.92 (pdf 107)

### parallel transport

*operation · core* · also: parallel propagation, parallelly propagated tensor

Carrying a tensor along a curve so that its absolute derivative vanishes; as a first-order linear ODE it has a unique solution for any initial value, defining the tensor along the whole curve.

**How introduced:** Defined by (6.32); its path dependence becomes the geometric meaning of curvature in §6.7 (Fig. 6.8).

**Prerequisites:** absolute derivative

$$
\frac{D}{Du}T^{a\cdots}_{b\cdots} = 0
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.4 p.92 (pdf 107); DIV §6.7 p.96 (pdf 111)

### affine geodesic

*definition · core* · also: autoparallel, straightest curve

A curve whose tangent vector, when parallel transported along the curve, stays proportional to the tangent; with an arbitrary parameter the tangent's absolute derivative is a multiple lambda(u) of itself.

**How introduced:** Framed as the privileged curve that never changes direction; written as (6.33) and in components (6.34), with the remark that only the symmetric part of Gamma contributes.

**Prerequisites:** parallel transport, affine connection

$$
\nabla_X X^a = \lambda X^a
$$

$$
\frac{d^2x^a}{du^2} + \Gamma^a_{bc}\frac{dx^b}{du}\frac{dx^c}{du} = \lambda\frac{dx^a}{du}
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.4 p.92 (pdf 107); DIV §6.4 p.93 (pdf 108)

### reparametrization of geodesics

*theorem · developed*

Being an affine geodesic is a property of the image curve: under u -> u-tilde(u) the equation keeps its form with a new function lambda-tilde determined by lambda and the first two derivatives of the parameter change.

**How introduced:** Explicit chain-rule computation in §6.4 ending with (6.35)-(6.36) and a '(check)'.

**Prerequisites:** affine geodesic

$$
\tilde{\lambda} = \frac{du}{d\tilde{u}}\lambda + \frac{d\tilde{u}}{du}\frac{d^2u}{d\tilde{u}^2}
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.4 p.93 (pdf 108)

### affine parameter

*definition · core* · also: s

A parameter along a geodesic for which lambda vanishes, so the tangent vector is parallel transported exactly; it is unique up to s -> alpha s + beta with constants alpha, beta. For null geodesics of a metric the same name is used for the parameter that removes the right-hand side.

**How introduced:** Obtained by choosing u-tilde in (6.35) to kill lambda-tilde; freedom shown in Exercise 6.9; reused for null metric geodesics in §6.9.

**Prerequisites:** reparametrization of geodesics, affine geodesic

$$
\frac{d^2x^a}{ds^2} + \Gamma^a_{bc}\frac{dx^b}{ds}\frac{dx^c}{ds} = 0
$$

$$
s \to \alpha s + \beta
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.4 p.93 (pdf 108); DIV §6.4 p.94 (pdf 109); DIV §6.9 p.103 (pdf 118)

### affine length

*definition · introduced*

The integral of the affine parameter between two points of one geodesic; it can compare segments on the same geodesic but not on different geodesics because each geodesic's scale alpha is arbitrary without a metric.

**How introduced:** Remark following the affine freedom s -> alpha s + beta.

**Prerequisites:** affine parameter

$$
\int_{P_1}^{P_2} ds
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.4 p.94 (pdf 109)

### local existence and uniqueness of geodesics

*theorem · introduced*

Through every point in every direction there is a unique affine geodesic, and sufficiently close points are joined by a unique geodesic, both following from ODE theory; globally, geodesics from one point may meet again.

**How introduced:** Stated with Figs 6.6 and 6.7 at the end of §6.4.

**Prerequisites:** affine geodesic, ordinary differential equations

**Where:** DIV §6.4 p.94 (pdf 109)

### geodesic focusing

*phenomenon · mention* · also: refocusing of geodesics, conjugate points (informal)

Distinct geodesics leaving a point may intersect again at another point, so the uniqueness of the geodesic joining two points holds only locally.

**How introduced:** One-sentence remark with Fig. 6.7 showing two geodesics from P meeting at Q.

**Prerequisites:** local existence and uniqueness of geodesics

**Notes:** Later relevant to gravitational lensing and to singularity theorems, not discussed here.

**Where:** DIV §6.4 p.94 (pdf 109)

### commutator of covariant derivatives

*operation · core* · also: [nabla_c, nabla_d]

The tensor nabla_c nabla_d T - nabla_d nabla_c T; for a symmetric connection it is linear in T with coefficients built from the Riemann tensor, and for a vector equals R^a_bcd X^b.

**How introduced:** Introduced by the observation that covariant derivatives, unlike partial derivatives, need not commute, and computed explicitly for a vector.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative of a general tensor

$$
\nabla_c\nabla_d X^a - \nabla_d\nabla_c X^a = R^a_{bcd}X^b + (\Gamma^e_{cd} - \Gamma^e_{dc})\nabla_e X^a
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 6.10 gives the (1,1) case; Exercise 6.11 the index-free form with the Lie bracket term.

**Where:** DIV §6.5 p.94 (pdf 109); DIV §6.5 p.95 (pdf 110)

### Riemann tensor

*mathematical-object · core* · also: curvature tensor, Riemann-Christoffel tensor, R^a_bcd

The (1,3) tensor built from the connection and its first derivatives, R^a_bcd = d_c Gamma^a_bd - d_d Gamma^a_bc + Gamma^e_bd Gamma^a_ec - Gamma^e_bc Gamma^a_ed, measuring non-commutation of covariant derivatives and the change of a vector transported around an infinitesimal loop; it vanishes exactly when the (symmetric) connection is flat.

**How introduced:** Appears algebraically from the commutator (6.39)-(6.40); tensor character by the quotient argument (6.41); geometric meaning given in §6.7; revisited with the metric connection in §6.12.

**Prerequisites:** commutator of covariant derivatives, affine connection

$$
R^a_{bcd} = \partial_c\Gamma^a_{bd} - \partial_d\Gamma^a_{bc} + \Gamma^e_{bd}\Gamma^a_{ec} - \Gamma^e_{bc}\Gamma^a_{ed}
$$

$$
\nabla_{[c}\nabla_{d]}X^a = \tfrac12 R^a_{bcd}X^b
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.5 p.94 (pdf 109); DIV §6.5 p.95 (pdf 110); DIV §6.7 p.98 (pdf 113); DIV §6.12 p.105 (pdf 120)

### quotient argument for tensor character

*technique · mention* · also: quotient theorem

If a quantity contracted with an arbitrary tensor always yields a tensor, the quantity itself is a tensor.

**How introduced:** Used to conclude that R^a_bcd is a (1,3) tensor since the left side of (6.41) is tensorial for arbitrary X.

**Prerequisites:** contraction

**Where:** DIV §6.5 p.95 (pdf 110)

### geodesic coordinates

*technique · core* · also: normal coordinates at a point (weak form), locally inertial-type coordinates

Coordinates about a point P in which the components of a symmetric connection vanish at P, obtained from any chart centred at P by adding a quadratic term with coefficients equal to Gamma(P); the first derivatives of Gamma are not removable in general.

**How introduced:** Proved constructively in §6.6 with (6.42) and the transformation law (6.23); advertised as the easiest way to establish tensor identities.

**Prerequisites:** inhomogeneous transformation law of the connection, symmetric connection

$$
x'^a = x^a + \tfrac12 Q^a_{bc}x^b x^c
$$

$$
[\Gamma'^a_{bc}]_P \stackrel{*}{=} 0
$$

**Notes:** Used later for (6.80), (6.83), the Palatini identity (DIV ch11) and as the mathematical form of the local inertial frame of the equivalence principle (DIV ch09).

**Where:** DIV §6.6 p.95 (pdf 110); DIV §6.6 p.96 (pdf 111)

### integrable connection

*definition · core* · also: path-independent parallel transport

A connection for which parallel transport of any vector from one point to another gives a result independent of the path, so a single vector generates a covariantly constant field over the manifold.

**How introduced:** Motivated by Fig. 6.8: in a general affine manifold two paths give different transported vectors, so the everyday idea of parallelism fails.

**Prerequisites:** parallel transport

**Where:** DIV §6.7 p.96 (pdf 111)

### integrability condition

*principle · developed*

A first-order PDE system d_c X^a = F^a_c(x, X) can have solutions only if the second mixed partial derivatives computed from it agree, d_d d_c X^a = d_c d_d X^a.

**How introduced:** Applied to the equation nabla_c X^a = 0 in the necessity half of Lemma 1.

**Prerequisites:** partial derivative

$$
\partial_d\partial_c X^a = \partial_c\partial_d X^a
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.7 p.97 (pdf 112)

### parallel transport around an infinitesimal loop

*phenomenon · core* · also: holonomy of a small parallelogram

Transporting X along the two routes of an infinitesimal coordinate parallelogram with sides dx and Dx gives vectors differing at second order by -R^a_bcd X^b dx^c Dx^d; equivalently a vector carried round the loop changes by a Riemann term.

**How introduced:** Explicit Taylor expansion to second order in the sufficiency half of Lemma 1, drawn in Fig. 6.9.

**Prerequisites:** parallel transport, Riemann tensor, Taylor expansion

$$
\Delta X^a = -R^a_{bcd}X^b\delta x^c\Delta x^d
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.7 p.97 (pdf 112); DIV §6.7 p.98 (pdf 113)

### simply connected manifold

*definition · mention* · also: manifold with no holes

A connected manifold in which every closed loop can be continuously shrunk to a point, equivalently any two curves with the same endpoints can be continuously deformed into each other.

**How introduced:** Invoked parenthetically to pass from infinitesimal loops to finite paths (Fig. 6.10).

**Where:** DIV §6.7 p.98 (pdf 113)

### parallel frame and dual frame

*technique · developed* · also: bold-index frame X_i^a, coframe X^i_b

A set of n linearly independent vector fields X_i^a, each parallel transported, together with the inverse matrix X^i_b satisfying X^i_b X_i^a = delta^a_b; the connection can be expressed through the frame.

**How introduced:** Built in the sufficiency half of Lemma 2 from integrability; symmetry of Gamma then forces the dual covectors to be curl-free and hence gradients of n scalar functions that serve as flat coordinates.

**Prerequisites:** integrable connection, matrix inverse

X^i_b X_i^a = \delta^a_b

$$
\Gamma^a_{cb} = -X^i_c\partial_b X_i^a

### affine flatness

*definition · core* · also: affine flat manifold, flat connection

A manifold with connection is affine flat if some coordinate system exists in which the connection components vanish everywhere.

**How introduced:** Defined at the end of §6.6 as the global counterpart of geodesic coordinates; characterized in §6.7.

### flatness theorem

*theorem · core* · also: affine flat iff Riemann tensor vanishes, Lemma: integrable iff R = 0, Lemma: flat iff symmetric and integrable

A manifold with symmetric connection is affine flat exactly when its Riemann tensor vanishes everywhere. This combines two lemmas: integrable iff R = 0, where the sufficiency half needs a simply connected manifold to pass from infinitesimal loops to finite paths; and affine flat iff symmetric and integrable.

**How introduced:** Assembled from two boxed lemmas with necessity and sufficiency proofs; necessity of Lemma 2 is Exercise 6.12.

### metric tensor

*mathematical-object · core* · also: metric, g_ab, metric form, first fundamental form

A symmetric (0,2) tensor field g_ab with non-vanishing determinant at every point, used to assign squared lengths, angles and a causal classification to vectors and to identify vectors with covectors.

### signature

*definition · developed* · also: signature of the metric

The number of positive minus the number of negative eigenvalues of the matrix g_ab at a point (equivalently of +1 minus -1 entries in a diagonalized form); there are no zero eigenvalues since the determinant is non-zero, and the signature is independent of coordinates and constant on a connected manifold for a continuous non-degenerate metric.

**How introduced:** Defined in §6.8 with two unanswered 'why?' prompts; invariance argued in §6.11 from pointwise diagonalization and continuity.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, eigenvalues

**Notes:** The invariance under coordinate change is Sylvester's law of inertia, which the book does not name.

**Where:** DIV §6.8 p.100 (pdf 115); DIV §6.11 p.104 (pdf 119)

### Riemannian metric

*definition · introduced* · also: positive definite metric, Riemannian manifold

A metric all of whose eigenvalues are positive, so that every non-zero vector has positive squared length; a manifold carrying one is a Riemannian manifold.

**How introduced:** Given as the case in which the metric defines genuine distances and lengths.

**Prerequisites:** signature

**Where:** DIV §6.8 p.100 (pdf 115)

### Lorentzian metric

*definition · core* · also: signature -2 metric, pseudo-Riemannian metric (one time dimension)

A metric with one positive and (in four dimensions) three negative eigenvalues, so signature -2 in the book's convention; other authors use the opposite overall sign with signature +2.

**How introduced:** Stated as the case relevant to relativity, with the alternative convention flagged in parentheses.

**Prerequisites:** signature

**Where:** DIV §6.8 p.100 (pdf 115)

### line element

*definition · core* · also: interval, ds^2

The quadratic expression ds^2 = g_ab dx^a dx^b giving the squared infinitesimal distance (or interval) between neighbouring points; ds^2 means the square of ds.

**How introduced:** Defined immediately after Riemannian metrics; used in the exercises to read off g_ab from line elements of flat 3-space and Minkowski space.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor

$$
ds^2 = g_{ab}(x)\,dx^a dx^b
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.8 p.100 (pdf 115)

### norm of a vector

*definition · introduced* · also: squared length, magnitude

The scalar X^2 = g_ab X^a X^b; for a Lorentzian metric it can be positive, negative or zero.

**How introduced:** Defined together with positive definiteness.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor

$$
X^2 := g_{ab}X^a X^b
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.8 p.100 (pdf 115)

### null vector and null cone

*definition · developed* · also: light cone in the tangent space, timelike, spacelike and null vectors

A non-zero vector with zero norm is null; the null vectors at P form a cone in the tangent space T_P M that separates timelike from spacelike vectors, exactly as in special relativity.

**How introduced:** Obtained from the indefiniteness of a Lorentzian metric and linked back to the special relativistic null cone.

**Prerequisites:** Lorentzian metric, tangent space, norm of a vector

$$
g_{ab}X^a X^b = 0
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.8 p.100 (pdf 115)

### angle between vectors

*definition · introduced* · also: cosine formula

For non-null X and Y, cos(X,Y) = g_ab X^a Y^b divided by the square roots of the absolute values of their norms.

**How introduced:** Given as a further use of the metric; needed later to show angles are conformally invariant.

**Prerequisites:** norm of a vector

$$
\cos(X,Y) = \frac{g_{ab}X^aY^b}{|g_{cd}X^cX^d|^{1/2}|g_{ef}Y^eY^f|^{1/2}}
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.8 p.101 (pdf 116)

### orthogonality

*definition · introduced*

Vectors are orthogonal when g_ab X^a Y^b = 0; in particular a null vector is orthogonal to itself.

**How introduced:** Stated after the angle formula with the self-orthogonality remark.

**Prerequisites:** angle between vectors, null vector and null cone

$$
g_{ab}X^aY^b = 0
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.8 p.101 (pdf 116)

### inverse metric

*mathematical-object · core* · also: contravariant metric, g^ab

The matrix inverse g^ab of g_ab, defined by g_ab g^bc = delta_a^c; it is a symmetric (2,0) tensor.

**How introduced:** Defined in components with the remark that tensor character is not obvious; Exercise 6.13 proves it.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, Kronecker delta

$$
g_{ab}g^{bc} = \delta_a^c
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.8 p.101 (pdf 116)

### raising and lowering indices

*operation · core* · also: index gymnastics, associated tensors

Contracting with g_ab lowers an index and with g^ab raises one; tensors related this way are treated as different component representations of one geometric object and share a kernel letter.

**How introduced:** Introduced with (6.58)-(6.59) once the inverse metric exists.

**Prerequisites:** inverse metric, contraction

$$
T_{\cdots a\cdots} = g_{ab}T^{\cdots b\cdots}
$$

$$
T^{\cdots a\cdots} = g^{ab}T_{\cdots b\cdots}
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.8 p.101 (pdf 116)

### order of mixed indices

*convention · introduced* · also: horizontal index position

When indices can be raised and lowered, their left-to-right order matters: X_a^b (first slot lowered) and X^b_a (second slot lowered) are in general different arrays.

**How introduced:** Warning at the end of §6.8.

**Prerequisites:** raising and lowering indices

**Where:** DIV §6.8 p.101 (pdf 116)

### interval along a curve

*definition · developed* · also: arc length, proper time (timelike case)

For a timelike curve x^a(u), the integral from P1 to P2 of the square root of g_ab (dx^a/du)(dx^b/du) du, independent of the parametrization.

**How introduced:** Obtained by dividing the line element by du^2 in §6.9.

**Prerequisites:** line element

$$
s = \int_{P_1}^{P_2}\left(g_{ab}\frac{dx^a}{du}\frac{dx^b}{du}\right)^{1/2}du
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.9 p.101 (pdf 116); DIV §6.9 p.102 (pdf 117)

### metric geodesic

*definition · core* · also: extremal curve, timelike metric geodesic

A curve between two points whose interval is stationary under small variations fixed at the endpoints; the stationary value may be a maximum, minimum or saddle.

**How introduced:** Defined variationally, with the Euler-Lagrange derivation postponed to Chapter 7 and the resulting equations quoted.

**Prerequisites:** interval along a curve, calculus of variations

$$
g_{ab}\frac{d^2x^b}{du^2} + \{bc,a\}\frac{dx^b}{du}\frac{dx^c}{du} = \left(\frac{d^2s}{du^2}\Big/\frac{ds}{du}\right)g_{ab}\frac{dx^b}{du}
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.9 p.102 (pdf 117)

### Christoffel symbols of the first kind

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: {ab,c}, Gamma_cab

The combinations {ab,c} = (1/2)(d_b g_ac + d_a g_bc - d_c g_ab) of first derivatives of the metric; symmetric in a and b and not a tensor.

**How introduced:** Appear in the quoted Euler-Lagrange form of the metric geodesic equation.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, partial derivative

$$
\{ab,c\} = \tfrac12(\partial_b g_{ac} + \partial_a g_{bc} - \partial_c g_{ab})
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.9 p.102 (pdf 117)

### Christoffel symbols of the second kind

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: {a bc}, Christoffel bracket

The first-kind symbols with the index raised, {a bc} = g^ad {bc,d}.

**How introduced:** Produced by multiplying the geodesic equation by g^ad; then identified with the metric connection in §6.10.

**Prerequisites:** Christoffel symbols of the first kind, inverse metric

$$
\left\{ {a \atop bc} \right\} = g^{ad}\{bc,d\}
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.9 p.102 (pdf 117)

### proper parametrization of metric geodesics

*technique · developed* · also: parameter linear in interval

If the parameter u is a linear function of the interval s, the right-hand side of the metric geodesic equation vanishes; with u = s the tangent has unit norm.

**How introduced:** Read off from the factor (d^2s/du^2)/(ds/du) in (6.64).

**Prerequisites:** metric geodesic

$$
u = \alpha s + \beta
$$

$$
\frac{d^2x^a}{ds^2} + \left\{ {a \atop bc} \right\}\frac{dx^b}{ds}\frac{dx^c}{ds} = 0
$$

$$
g_{ab}\frac{dx^a}{ds}\frac{dx^b}{ds} = 1
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.9 p.102 (pdf 117); DIV §6.9 p.103 (pdf 118)

### spacelike geodesic

*definition · mention*

The analogue of a timelike metric geodesic for curves with spacelike tangent, using the proper distance d sigma^2 = -g_ab dx^a dx^b.

**How introduced:** One-sentence remark after (6.68).

**Prerequisites:** metric geodesic

$$
d\sigma^2 = -g_{ab}dx^a dx^b
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.9 p.103 (pdf 118)

### null geodesic

*definition · developed*

A geodesic of an indefinite metric along which the interval between any two points is zero, i.e. with null tangent; with an affine parameter it satisfies the geodesic equation with no right-hand side, the parameter being fixed up to linear maps.

**How introduced:** Stated after timelike and spacelike geodesics, with the parametrization claim asserted rather than derived.

**Prerequisites:** metric geodesic, null vector and null cone, affine parameter

$$
\frac{d^2x^a}{du^2} + \left\{ {a \atop bc} \right\}\frac{dx^b}{du}\frac{dx^c}{du} = 0
$$

$$
g_{ab}\frac{dx^a}{du}\frac{dx^b}{du} = 0
$$

**Notes:** Conformally related metrics share null geodesics (§6.13, Exercise 6.29).

**Where:** DIV §6.9 p.103 (pdf 118)

### affine versus metric geodesics

*principle · core* · also: two notions of straight line

On a manifold with independent connection and metric there are two families of privileged curves: affine geodesics, which never change direction, and metric geodesics, which extremize length; in Euclidean space both are straight lines, but in general they differ.

**How introduced:** Opening of §6.10 with Fig. 6.11 showing two differently oriented hatchings of curves on a manifold.

**Prerequisites:** affine geodesic, metric geodesic

**Where:** DIV §6.10 p.103 (pdf 118)

### metric connection

*mathematical-object · core* · also: Levi-Civita connection, Christoffel connection, Riemannian connection

The unique symmetric connection built from the metric, Gamma^a_bc = (1/2) g^ad (d_b g_dc + d_c g_db - d_d g_bc), for which affine and metric geodesics coincide; the book uses only this connection from §6.10 on.

**How introduced:** Obtained by comparing the affine geodesic equation (6.37) with the metric one (6.67) and demanding they agree; symmetric by inspection, connection-like transformation left to Exercise 6.17.

**Prerequisites:** affine versus metric geodesics, Christoffel symbols of the second kind, affine connection

$$
\Gamma^a_{bc} = \tfrac12 g^{ad}(\partial_b g_{dc} + \partial_c g_{db} - \partial_d g_{bc})
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.10 p.103 (pdf 118); DIV §6.10 p.104 (pdf 119)

### metric compatibility

*identity · core* · also: covariantly constant metric, nabla g = 0

With the metric connection the covariant derivative of the metric vanishes identically, and so does that of the inverse metric. Raising and lowering indices therefore commutes with covariant differentiation. The Kronecker delta is covariantly constant for any connection (Exercise 6.4); the book simply lists it alongside.

**How introduced:** Stated as an identity with proof left to Exercise 6.20, which also deduces nabla_b X_a = g_ac nabla_b X^c.

**Prerequisites:** metric connection, covariant derivative of a general tensor

$$
\nabla_c g_{ab} \equiv 0
$$

$$
\nabla_c\delta^a_b \equiv 0
$$

$$
\nabla_c g^{ab} \equiv 0
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.10 p.104 (pdf 119)

### fundamental theorem for the metric connection

*theorem · core* · also: uniqueness of the Levi-Civita connection

For a symmetric connection, the covariant derivative of the metric vanishes if and only if the connection is the metric connection.

**How introduced:** Boxed theorem in §6.10; the converse is Exercise 6.21, solved by permuting indices in (6.77) and adding.

**Prerequisites:** metric compatibility, symmetric connection

**Where:** DIV §6.10 p.104 (pdf 119)

### derivative of the metric in terms of the connection

*identity · developed*

Metric compatibility rearranged: d_c g_ab = Gamma^d_ac g_db + Gamma^d_bc g_ad, so the first derivatives of the metric vanish wherever the connection components do.

**How introduced:** Derived inside the metric flatness proof and explicitly flagged as useful later.

**Prerequisites:** metric compatibility

$$
\partial_c g_{ab} = \Gamma^d_{ac}g_{db} + \Gamma^d_{bc}g_{ad}
$$

**Notes:** Used in DIV ch07 to derive the contracted Christoffel identity and in Exercise 6.21.

**Where:** DIV §6.11 p.105 (pdf 120)

### flat metric

*definition · core* · also: metric flatness

A metric for which some coordinate system makes g_ab diagonal with every diagonal entry +1 or -1 at every point (not just at one point).

**How introduced:** Contrasted in §6.11 with the always-available pointwise diagonalization.

**Prerequisites:** signature, metric tensor

**Where:** DIV §6.11 p.104 (pdf 119)

### metric flatness theorem

*theorem · core* · also: flat iff Riemann tensor vanishes (metric version)

A metric is flat if and only if the Riemann tensor of its metric connection vanishes; hence for the metric connection metric flatness and affine flatness coincide.

**How introduced:** Boxed theorem in §6.11 proved with the §6.7 theorem and (6.77).

**Prerequisites:** flat metric, flatness theorem, derivative of the metric in terms of the connection

**Where:** DIV §6.11 p.105 (pdf 120)

### algebraic symmetries of the Riemann tensor

*identity · core*

With the metric connection, R_abcd is antisymmetric in its last pair and in its first pair, symmetric under exchange of the two pairs, and satisfies the cyclic identity on its last three indices.

**How introduced:** (6.78) is immediate from the definition, (6.79) from symmetry of Gamma, (6.80) via geodesic coordinates, (6.81) by combination.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann tensor, geodesic coordinates, raising and lowering indices

$$
R_{abcd} = -R_{abdc} = -R_{bacd} = R_{cdab}
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.12 p.105 (pdf 120); DIV §6.12 p.106 (pdf 121)

### cyclic identity

*identity · developed* · also: first Bianchi identity, algebraic Bianchi identity

For a symmetric connection the sum of R^a_bcd over cyclic permutations of b, c, d vanishes, equivalently R^a_[bcd] = 0.

**How introduced:** Listed as (6.79) with proof in Exercise 6.24.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann tensor, symmetric connection

$$
R^a_{bcd} + R^a_{dbc} + R^a_{cdb} \equiv 0
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.12 p.106 (pdf 121)

### number of independent Riemann components

*theorem · developed*

The symmetries reduce the n^4 components of R_abcd to n^2(n^2 - 1)/12 independent ones: 0, 1, 6 and 20 for n = 1, 2, 3, 4.

**How introduced:** Stated after the symmetry summary (6.82) and tabulated case by case at the start of §6.13.

**Prerequisites:** algebraic symmetries of the Riemann tensor

$$
\tfrac{1}{12}n^2(n^2-1)
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.12 p.106 (pdf 121); DIV §6.13 p.107 (pdf 122)

### Bianchi identities

*identity · core* · also: differential Bianchi identities, second Bianchi identity

The cyclic sum over three indices of the covariant derivative of R_de.. vanishes: nabla_a R_debc + nabla_c R_deab + nabla_b R_deca = 0, equivalently R_de[ab;c] = 0.

**How introduced:** Quoted as most easily proved in geodesic coordinates; proof in Exercise 6.25.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann tensor, geodesic coordinates

$$
\nabla_a R_{debc} + \nabla_c R_{deab} + \nabla_b R_{deca} \equiv 0
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.12 p.106 (pdf 121)

### Ricci tensor

*mathematical-object · core* · also: R_ab

The contraction of the Riemann tensor on its first and third indices, R_ab = R^c_acb, symmetric by pair symmetry.

**How introduced:** Defined in §6.12 as the first of several tensors built from curvature.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann tensor, contraction

$$
R_{ab} = R^c_{acb} = g^{cd}R_{dacb}
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.12 p.106 (pdf 121)

### Ricci scalar

*mathematical-object · core* · also: curvature scalar, R

The trace of the Ricci tensor, R = g^ab R_ab.

**How introduced:** Final contraction after the Ricci tensor.

**Prerequisites:** Ricci tensor, inverse metric

$$
R = g^{ab}R_{ab}
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.12 p.106 (pdf 121)

### Einstein tensor

*mathematical-object · core* · also: G_ab

The symmetric tensor G_ab = R_ab - (1/2) g_ab R, whose covariant divergence vanishes identically.

**How introduced:** Built from Ricci tensor and scalar in §6.12 without physical motivation; it becomes the left side of the field equations in DIV ch10.

**Prerequisites:** Ricci tensor, Ricci scalar

$$
G_{ab} = R_{ab} - \tfrac12 g_{ab}R
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 6.26 shows G_ab = 0 iff R_ab = 0.

**Where:** DIV §6.12 p.106 (pdf 121)

### contracted Bianchi identities

*identity · core* · also: divergence-free Einstein tensor

Contracting the Bianchi identities twice gives nabla_b G_a^b = 0 identically.

**How introduced:** Stated as a consequence of (6.83); deduced in Exercise 6.25.

**Prerequisites:** Bianchi identities, Einstein tensor

$$
\nabla_b G_a^{\ b} \equiv 0
$$

**Notes:** Key later for compatibility of the field equations with conservation of energy-momentum.

**Where:** DIV §6.12 p.106 (pdf 121)

### curvature sign conventions

*convention · introduced*

Authors differ in the overall sign of the Riemann tensor, the choice of contraction for the Ricci tensor, and the metric signature, so formulas must be checked against a book's conventions.

**How introduced:** Closing remark of §6.12, echoing the signature remark of §6.8.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann tensor, Ricci tensor, signature

**Where:** DIV §6.12 p.106 (pdf 121)

### Weyl tensor

*mathematical-object · core* · also: conformal tensor, C_abcd

For n >= 4, the part of the Riemann tensor left after subtracting terms built from the Ricci tensor, Ricci scalar and metric so that every contraction vanishes; in four dimensions it carries the 10 curvature components not fixed by R_ab.

**How introduced:** Motivated by the component count in n = 4 (20 = 10 Ricci + 10 Weyl) and defined by an explicit formula (6.88).

**Prerequisites:** Riemann tensor, Ricci tensor, Ricci scalar, number of independent Riemann components

$$
C_{abcd} = R_{abcd} + \tfrac12(g_{ad}R_{cb} + g_{bc}R_{da} - g_{ac}R_{db} - g_{bd}R_{ca}) + \tfrac16(g_{ac}g_{db} - g_{ad}g_{cb})R
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.13 p.107 (pdf 122)

### trace-free property of the Weyl tensor

*identity · developed*

The Weyl tensor has all the algebraic symmetries of R_abcd and in addition C^a_bad = 0, which together imply that every contraction of any pair of its indices vanishes.

**How introduced:** Stated as (6.89)-(6.90), proof in Exercise 6.27.

**Prerequisites:** Weyl tensor, algebraic symmetries of the Riemann tensor

$$
C^a_{bad} \equiv 0
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.13 p.107 (pdf 122)

### conformally related metrics

*definition · core* · also: conformal rescaling, conformal transformation of the metric

Two metrics on the same manifold with g-bar_ab = Omega^2 g_ab for a non-vanishing differentiable function Omega; they agree on angles, ratios of lengths and null directions but not on lengths.

**How introduced:** Defined in §6.13; invariance of angles and ratios follows from (6.53) and (6.55) (Exercise 6.28).

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, angle between vectors

$$
\bar{g}_{ab} = \Omega^2 g_{ab}
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.13 p.107 (pdf 122); DIV §6.13 p.108 (pdf 123)

### conformal invariance

*definition · developed* · also: conformally invariant quantity

A quantity is conformally invariant if it is unchanged when the metric is replaced by a conformally related one; examples are angles, null geodesics and the Weyl tensor C^a_bcd with one index up, while g_ab, Gamma, R^a_bcd and the Weyl tensor in other index positions are not.

**How introduced:** Defined via (6.92) with an explicit warning about index position.

**Prerequisites:** conformally related metrics, Weyl tensor

$$
\bar{C}^a_{bcd} = C^a_{bcd}
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 6.29 (null geodesics) and 6.30 (transformation of Gamma and Riemann) supply the proofs.

**Where:** DIV §6.13 p.108 (pdf 123)

### conformally flat metric

*definition · developed*

A metric expressible as Omega^2 times a flat metric in some coordinate system.

**How introduced:** Defined at the end of §6.13 before two quoted theorems.

**Prerequisites:** conformally related metrics, flat metric

$$
g_{ab} = \Omega^2\eta_{ab}
$$

**Where:** DIV §6.13 p.108 (pdf 123)

### Weyl criterion for conformal flatness

*theorem · introduced*

A metric is conformally flat if and only if its Weyl tensor vanishes everywhere. The book's box gives no dimension restriction. The statement is correct for n >= 4, which is also the range where the book defines C_abcd; in n = 3 the Weyl tensor vanishes identically and the Cotton tensor decides instead.

**How introduced:** Quoted without proof as the first closing theorem of §6.13.

**Prerequisites:** Weyl tensor, conformally flat metric

**Notes:** The book does not restate the n >= 4 restriction in the theorem box; in three dimensions the Weyl tensor vanishes identically and a different (Cotton) tensor decides conformal flatness.

**Where:** DIV §6.13 p.108 (pdf 123)

Every two-dimensional Riemannian metric is (locally) conformally flat; Exercise 6.31 proves the Lorentzian analogue using null coordinates.

**How introduced:** Quoted without proof as the second closing theorem of §6.13.

## Key equations

### (6.1) Transformation of the partial derivative of a vector · central · DIV §6.1 p.85 (pdf 100)

$$
\partial'_c X'^a = \frac{\partial x'^a}{\partial x^b}\frac{\partial x^d}{\partial x'^c}\partial_d X^b + \frac{\partial^2 x'^a}{\partial x^b\partial x^d}\frac{\partial x^d}{\partial x'^c}X^b
$$

The first term is the (1,1) tensor law; the second, involving second derivatives of the coordinate change, spoils tensor character unless the transformation is linear.

**Symbols:** X^a vector components; x^a old, x'^a new coordinates

### Components at two points use different Jacobians · supporting · DIV §6.1 p.86 (pdf 101)

$$
X'^a_P = \left[\frac{\partial x'^a}{\partial x^b}\right]_P X^b_P, \qquad X'^a_Q = \left[\frac{\partial x'^a}{\partial x^b}\right]_Q X^b_Q
$$

Why a difference quotient of components is not tensorial: the transformation matrices are evaluated at different points.

**Symbols:** P, Q neighbouring points

### (6.2) Integral curves · supporting · DIV §6.2 p.86 (pdf 101)

$$
\frac{dx^a}{du} = X^a(x(u))
$$

ODE whose solutions are the curves of the congruence generated by a vector field.

**Symbols:** u curve parameter

### (6.3) Infinitesimal point transformation · derivation-step · DIV §6.2 p.87 (pdf 102)

$$
\tilde{x}^a = x^a + \delta u\,X^a(x)
$$

Actively maps P to a nearby point Q on the integral curve through P, in the same chart.

**Symbols:** delta u small parameter

### (6.4) · derivation-step · DIV §6.2 p.87 (pdf 102)

$$
\frac{\partial\tilde{x}^a}{\partial x^b} = \delta^a_b + \delta u\,\partial_b X^a
$$

Jacobian of the point map, identity plus a first-order correction.

### (6.5) Dragged-along tensor · derivation-step · DIV §6.2 p.88 (pdf 103)

$$
\tilde{T}^{ab}(\tilde{x}) = \frac{\partial\tilde{x}^a}{\partial x^c}\frac{\partial\tilde{x}^b}{\partial x^d}T^{cd}(x) = T^{ab}(x) + [\partial_c X^a T^{cb}(x) + \partial_d X^b T^{ad}(x)]\delta u + O(\delta u^2)
$$

Components at Q of the tensor carried from P by the point map.

**Symbols:** T^{ab} rank-2 contravariant tensor field

### (6.6) Tensor already at Q · derivation-step · DIV §6.2 p.88 (pdf 103)

$$
T^{ab}(\tilde{x}) = T^{ab}(x) + \delta u\,X^c\partial_c T^{ab}(x)
$$

First-order Taylor expansion of the field at the displaced point.

### (6.7) Definition of the Lie derivative · central · DIV §6.2 p.88 (pdf 103)

$$
\mathrm{L}_X T^{ab} = \lim_{\delta u\to 0}\frac{T^{ab}(\tilde{x}) - \tilde{T}^{ab}(\tilde{x})}{\delta u}
$$

Compare the field's own value at Q with the value dragged from P.

**Symbols:** L_X Lie derivative along X

### (6.8) Lie derivative of a (2,0) tensor · central · DIV §6.2 p.88 (pdf 103)

$$
\mathrm{L}_X T^{ab} = X^c\partial_c T^{ab} - T^{ac}\partial_c X^b - T^{cb}\partial_c X^a
$$

Directional derivative minus one correction per upper index from the flow's shear.

### (6.9)-(6.10) Adapted coordinates · supporting · DIV §6.2 p.88 (pdf 103)

$$
X^a \stackrel{*}{=} \delta^a_1, \quad X \stackrel{*}{=} \partial_1, \quad \mathrm{L}_X T^{ab} \stackrel{*}{=} \partial_1 T^{ab}
$$

In coordinates whose x^1 lines are the integral curves, Lie differentiation is plain partial differentiation.

**Symbols:** starred equals: holds only in special coordinates

### (6.11) Linearity · supporting · DIV §6.2 p.89 (pdf 104)

$$
\mathrm{L}_X(\lambda Y^a + \mu Z^a) = \lambda\mathrm{L}_X Y^a + \mu\mathrm{L}_X Z^a
$$

Linear over constants.

**Symbols:** lambda, mu constants

### (6.12) Leibniz rule · supporting · DIV §6.2 p.89 (pdf 104)

$$
\mathrm{L}_X(Y^a Z_{bc}) = Y^a(\mathrm{L}_X Z_{bc}) + (\mathrm{L}_X Y^a)Z_{bc}
$$

Product rule for tensor products.

### (6.13) Commutes with contraction · supporting · DIV §6.2 p.89 (pdf 104)

$$
\delta^b_a\,\mathrm{L}_X T^a_{\ b} = \mathrm{L}_X T^a_{\ a}
$$

Contracting before or after Lie differentiation gives the same result (proved via L_X delta = 0 in Exercise 6.1).

**Symbols:** Printed as delta^b_a L_X T^a_b = L_X T^a_a (checked on the rendered page); the reading copy misplaces the delta indices.

### (6.14) Lie derivative of a scalar · supporting · DIV §6.2 p.89 (pdf 104)

$$
\mathrm{L}_X\phi = X\phi = X^a\partial_a\phi
$$

Directional derivative along X.

### (6.15) Lie derivative of a vector · central · DIV §6.2 p.89 (pdf 104)

$$

## Worked examples

### Example · intro · DIV §6.1 p.85 (pdf 100)

**Problem:** Decide whether the partial derivatives of a vector field's components form a (1,1) tensor.

**Method:** Differentiate the contravariant transformation law with respect to the new coordinates, using the chain rule to convert to old-coordinate derivatives and the product rule on the Jacobian factor.

**Key insight:** The extra term carries second derivatives of the coordinate change, which vanish only for linear transformations; the root cause is comparing components at different points.

**Result:** Equation (6.1): tensor term plus inhomogeneous term, so d_b X^a is not a tensor.

**Concepts:** non-tensorial character of the partial derivative, comparison of tensors at different points

### Example · standard · DIV §6.2 p.87 (pdf 102)

**Problem:** Derive the Lie derivative of a rank-2 contravariant tensor field along X.

**Method:** Apply the infinitesimal point map (6.3), expand its Jacobian (6.4), push T^ab forward with the tensor law to first order (6.5), Taylor expand the field at the new point (6.6), subtract and take the limit (6.7).

**Key insight:** Using the flow to carry the tensor removes the two-point problem without any connection; each upper index produces a -T d X correction from the Jacobian.

**Result:** L_X T^ab = X^c d_c T^ab - T^ac d_c X^b - T^cb d_c X^a (6.8).

**Concepts:** Lie dragging, Lie derivative, active versus passive transformation

### Example · standard · DIV §6.3 p.90 (pdf 105)

**Problem:** Find how Gamma must transform if nabla_c X^a = d_c X^a + Gamma^a_bc X^b is to be a tensor, and extend the derivative to covectors and general tensors.

**Method:** Demand the (1,1) law for nabla_c X^a, use (6.1) for the partial derivative and isolate Gamma' (Exercise 6.3); then impose nabla phi = d phi and the Leibniz rule on X_a Y^a (Exercise 6.5).

**Key insight:** The connection's inhomogeneous term is built to cancel exactly the inhomogeneous term of the partial derivative; Leibniz then fixes the sign for lower indices.

**Result:** (6.23)/(6.24), (6.26), (6.27).

**Concepts:** affine connection, covariant derivative, covariant derivative of a covector

### Example · standard · DIV §6.4 p.93 (pdf 108)

**Problem:** Show that the affine geodesic property is independent of parametrization and that an affine parameter always exists.

**Method:** Change parameter u -> u-tilde, express first and second derivatives by the chain rule, substitute into the geodesic equation, and read off the new coefficient lambda-tilde (6.35).

**Key insight:** Reparametrization only adds a term along the tangent, so it can be tuned to cancel lambda; the residual freedom is affine.

**Result:** (6.36); choosing lambda-tilde = 0 gives (6.37) with s fixed up to alpha s + beta.

**Concepts:** reparametrization of geodesics, affine parameter

### Example · standard · DIV §6.5 p.94 (pdf 109)

**Problem:** Compute the commutator of two covariant derivatives acting on a vector.

**Method:** Treat nabla_c X^a as a (1,1) tensor, apply (6.27) for the second derivative in both orders, subtract, and assume mixed partials of X commute.

**Key insight:** All terms with first derivatives of X cancel, leaving a piece linear in X (curvature) and a piece proportional to the torsion.

**Result:** (6.39)-(6.41), defining R^a_bcd and proving it is a tensor.

**Concepts:** commutator of covariant derivatives, Riemann tensor

### Example · standard · DIV §6.6 p.95 (pdf 110)

**Problem:** Construct coordinates at a point P in which a symmetric connection vanishes.

**Method:** Put P at the origin, apply a quadratic change x' = x + (1/2) Q x x with constant symmetric Q, note the Jacobian is the identity at P, and substitute into (6.23).

**Key insight:** Only the symmetric part of Gamma can be cancelled by second derivatives of a coordinate change, and only at a point.

**Result:** Gamma'(P) = Gamma(P) - Q, zero for Q = Gamma(P) (6.43).

**Concepts:** geodesic coordinates, inhomogeneous transformation law of the connection

### Example · standard · DIV §6.7 p.96 (pdf 111)

**Problem:** Prove that an integrable connection has vanishing Riemann tensor.

**Method:** Integrability yields a covariantly constant field from any initial vector; its PDE (6.44) requires commuting mixed partials (6.45); rewrite the commutator identity to see this means R^a_bcd X^b = 0 for arbitrary X.

**Key insight:** Curvature is exactly the obstruction to solving nabla X = 0 for a full set of initial data.

**Result:** Necessity half of Lemma 1.

**Concepts:** integrable connection, integrability condition, Riemann tensor

### Example · challenging · DIV §6.7 p.97 (pdf 112)

**Problem:** Show that transport around an infinitesimal loop changes a vector by a Riemann term, and hence R = 0 implies integrability.

**Method:** Parallel displace X along delta x then Delta x, Taylor expanding Gamma at the intermediate point to second order; repeat in the opposite order; subtract, relabel dummy indices and compare with (6.40); extend to finite paths by deformation through thin loops (Fig. 6.10).

**Key insight:** The antisymmetric combination of second-order terms is precisely R^a_bdc; simple connectedness lets local path independence become global.

**Result:** Delta X^a = -R^a_bcd X^b delta x^c Delta x^d.

**Concepts:** parallel transport around an infinitesimal loop, simply connected manifold, Riemann tensor

### Example · challenging · DIV §6.7 p.98 (pdf 113)

**Problem:** Prove that a symmetric integrable connection admits coordinates in which it vanishes everywhere.

**Method:** Parallel propagate n independent vectors to get a frame, invert it to a dual frame, express Gamma through the frame (6.47)-(6.48), use symmetry to show each dual covector is curl-free and thus a gradient df^i, adopt f^i as coordinates and verify Gamma' = 0 via (6.23).

**Key insight:** Integrability gives a parallel frame; symmetry (no torsion) makes the frame holonomic, i.e. a coordinate basis.

**Result:** Sufficiency half of Lemma 2, hence affine flat iff R = 0.

**Concepts:** parallel frame and dual frame, affine flatness, flatness theorem

### Example · standard · DIV §6.10 p.103 (pdf 118)

**Problem:** Find the connection for which affine geodesics and metric geodesics coincide, and check its properties.

**Method:** Compare the affinely parametrized equation (6.37) with the interval-parametrized metric equation (6.67) and identify Gamma with the Christoffel symbols; verify symmetry, connection law (Exercise 6.17), metric compatibility (Exercise 6.20) and uniqueness (Exercise 6.21, by cyclically permuting (6.77) and adding).

**Key insight:** One structure (the metric) can fix the other (the connection), making 'never turning' and 'extremal length' the same notion.

**Result:** (6.72), (6.74) and the theorem of §6.10.

**Concepts:** metric connection, metric compatibility, fundamental theorem for the metric connection

### Example · standard · DIV §6.11 p.105 (pdf 120)

**Problem:** Prove a metric is flat iff its Riemann tensor vanishes.

**Method:** Necessity: constant +-1 metric gives zero Christoffel symbols everywhere, hence zero R. Sufficiency: R = 0 gives coordinates with Gamma = 0 everywhere (§6.7); then (6.77) forces d_c g_ab = 0, and a constant symmetric matrix can be diagonalized to +-1 by a linear change.

**Key insight:** Metric compatibility ties the constancy of g to the vanishing of Gamma, so affine and metric flatness coincide.

**Result:** Theorem of §6.11.

**Concepts:** metric flatness theorem, derivative of the metric in terms of the connection

### Example · intro · DIV §6.13 p.107 (pdf 122)

**Problem:** Count curvature components in dimensions 1 to 4 and split the four-dimensional count.

**Method:** Evaluate n^2(n^2-1)/12 and compare with the number of components of the symmetric Ricci tensor, n(n+1)/2.

**Key insight:** In 2D curvature is one scalar, in 3D Ricci determines everything, and in 4D half the 20 components are invisible to Ricci and form the Weyl tensor.

**Result:** 0, 1, 6, 20; in 4D 10 Ricci plus 10 Weyl.

**Concepts:** number of independent Riemann components, Weyl tensor


## Analogies and intuitions

### Integral curves of a vector field are like field lines of an electric field or streamlines of a fluid flow. → integral curves of a vector field · strong · DIV §6.2 p.86 (pdf 101)

Invoked to make the ODE construction of a congruence from a vector field feel familiar.

**Where it breaks down:** Field lines in electromagnetism are usually drawn without a parameter; here the parametrization u matters because the Lie derivative uses the flow distance du.

**App idea:** Overlay a fluid tracer animation on the vector field so the flow parameter becomes elapsed time.

### In coordinates adapted to X, Lie differentiation is just ordinary partial differentiation along x^1. → Lie derivative · useful · DIV §6.2 p.88 (pdf 103)

Offered after (6.8) to demystify the formula: the correction terms are artefacts of using coordinates not aligned with the flow.

**Where it breaks down:** Adapted coordinates exist only where X is non-zero and only locally; they cannot be used for two non-commuting fields at once.

**App idea:** Morph a Cartesian grid into flow-adapted coordinates and show the Lie derivative formula's correction terms disappearing.

### Two meanings of a straight line in Euclidean space: a line that never changes direction and the shortest route between two points. → affine versus metric geodesics · strong · DIV §6.10 p.103 (pdf 118)

Motivates why a manifold with both a connection and a metric has two geodesic families and why identifying them singles out the metric connection.

**Where it breaks down:** In Lorentzian geometry timelike geodesics locally maximize rather than minimize interval, and null geodesics have zero length, so 'shortest' must be read as 'stationary'.

**App idea:** Side-by-side ant experiments: one ant walks without turning, the other finds the shortest string between pins; on a sphere with the Levi-Civita connection they agree, with a twisted connection they do not.

### A derivative of something that is zero everywhere must be zero, because the difference quotient subtracts zeros. → metric flatness theorem · useful · DIV §6.11 p.105 (pdf 120)

Parenthetical reassurance in the necessity proof that vanishing Gamma everywhere implies vanishing derivatives of Gamma, contrasted implicitly with geodesic coordinates where Gamma vanishes only at a point.

**Where it breaks down:** Only valid for vanishing on an open set, not at a single point.

### The null cone of a Lorentzian metric in each tangent space is the same structure met in special relativity. → null vector and null cone · useful · DIV §6.8 p.100 (pdf 115)

Transfers spacetime-diagram intuition for timelike, spacelike and null vectors to a general curved metric, pointwise.

**Where it breaks down:** In curved spacetime cones tilt and change from point to point; there is no global Minkowski diagram.

**App idea:** Field of little light cones over a 2D spacetime chart that tilt as the learner edits metric functions.

### Deforming one path into another through a stack of neighbouring paths, each differing by a thin loop (Fig. 6.10). → integrable connection · useful · DIV §6.7 p.98 (pdf 113)

Turns the infinitesimal-loop result into path independence for finite paths.

**Where it breaks down:** Fails if the region has holes; the book mentions simple connectedness but gives no example of what goes wrong.

**App idea:** Cone surface with its tip removed: flat everywhere, yet vectors carried around the tip rotate, showing why the no-holes assumption is needed.


## Misconceptions addressed

### The partial derivative of a tensor's components is itself a tensor. · DIV §6.1 p.85 (pdf 100)

**Correction:** Under non-linear coordinate changes an extra second-derivative term appears (6.1); only the covariant or Lie derivative is tensorial.

**Why tempting:** In Cartesian coordinates of flat space and in special relativity with Lorentz transformations the Jacobian is constant, so partials do behave like tensors.

### The connection coefficients Gamma^a_bc form a tensor. · DIV §6.3 p.91 (pdf 106)

**Correction:** They transform inhomogeneously (6.23); they can be made zero at a point by a coordinate change, which no non-zero tensor allows. Differences of connections and the torsion are tensors, sums are not.

**Why tempting:** They carry three indices in tensor positions and appear in tensor equations.

### If the connection vanishes at a point in geodesic coordinates, spacetime is flat there, or its derivatives vanish too. · DIV §6.6 p.96 (pdf 111)

**Correction:** Geodesic coordinates remove Gamma only at P; its first derivatives, and hence the Riemann tensor, generally survive. Removing Gamma on a whole neighbourhood is possible only when R = 0.

**Why tempting:** Zero Christoffel symbols is the usual computational signature of flat Cartesian coordinates.

### A geodesic is always the shortest path between two points. · DIV §6.9 p.102 (pdf 117)

**Correction:** Metric geodesics make the interval stationary: a maximum, minimum or saddle; timelike geodesics in a Lorentzian metric locally maximize interval, null geodesics have zero interval.

**Why tempting:** Euclidean intuition and the common slogan 'shortest distance'.

### Affine parameter values measure the same length on any two geodesics. · DIV §6.4 p.94 (pdf 109)

**Correction:** Each geodesic's affine parameter is fixed only up to s -> alpha s + beta, so affine lengths compare segments on one geodesic only; comparing different geodesics needs a metric.

**Why tempting:** The word 'length' suggests a universal ruler.

### Geodesics and parallel transport are metric notions. · DIV §6.4 p.92 (pdf 107)

**Correction:** Affine geodesics and parallel transport need only a connection; the metric enters only in §6.8, and the Lie derivative needs neither.

**Why tempting:** Most GR courses introduce the metric first and derive Gamma from it.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The eigenvalues of the metric matrix are geometric invariants. · DIV §6.8 p.100 (pdf 115)

**Correction:** Eigenvalues change with coordinates; only the count of positive and negative ones (the signature) is invariant.

**Why tempting:** Eigenvalues of a linear operator are invariant, but g_ab is a bilinear form, which transforms by congruence rather than similarity.

### Once indices can be raised and lowered, their horizontal order does not matter. · DIV §6.8 p.101 (pdf 116)

**Correction:** X_a^b and X^b_a are generally different; order of slots must be preserved when moving indices.

**Why tempting:** Symmetric tensors like g_ab and R_ab hide the issue.

### Covariant derivatives commute like partial derivatives. · DIV §6.5 p.94 (pdf 109)

**Correction:** Their commutator is governed by the Riemann tensor (and torsion); commuting holds for all tensors only when R = 0 and the connection is symmetric.

**Why tempting:** Mixed partial derivatives commute and nabla looks like a partial derivative plus a correction.

### The Weyl tensor is conformally invariant in any index position. · DIV §6.13 p.108 (pdf 123)

**Correction:** Only C^a_bcd with one index up is unchanged by g -> Omega^2 g; lowering or raising uses the rescaled metric and introduces powers of Omega.

**Why tempting:** Raising and lowering is usually treated as mere notation.

### Conformally related metrics describe the same geometry. · DIV §6.13 p.107 (pdf 122)

**Correction:** They share angles, length ratios, null geodesics and the Weyl tensor, but not lengths, timelike geodesics, the connection or the Riemann tensor.

**Why tempting:** Light cones, and thus causal structure, look identical.

### All authors' Riemann and Ricci tensors agree in sign. · DIV §6.12 p.106 (pdf 121)

**Correction:** Sign conventions for Riemann, Ricci and the signature vary; formulas must be translated between books.

**Why tempting:** Symbols are identical across texts.

### Vanishing Riemann tensor alone guarantees affine flatness for any connection. · DIV §6.7 p.99 (pdf 114)

**Correction:** The book's theorem assumes a symmetric connection; with torsion, a connection can have zero curvature yet no coordinates make Gamma vanish, as Lemma 2 requires symmetry.

**Why tempting:** The final theorem box omits the standing symmetry assumption.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

## Applications and observations

- **Lorentzian signature chosen for relativity** (numerical-estimate, DIV §6.8 p.100 (pdf 115)): The metric of relativity has one positive and three negative eigenvalues, signature -2, carrying the special-relativistic null cone into each tangent space. Exercise 6.22 checks signature, non-singularity and flatness of the Minkowski line element. Key numbers: signature -2 = 1 - 3; the +2 convention flips all signs
- **Counting curvature degrees of freedom** (numerical-estimate, DIV §6.13 p.107 (pdf 122)): Riemann components drop from n^4 to n^2(n^2-1)/12. In spacetime 20 remain, of which 10 are fixed by the Ricci tensor and 10 are Weyl. Own framing (not in this chapter): the field equations will tie the Ricci part to matter locally, leaving the Weyl part for tidal fields and gravitational waves in vacuum. Key numbers: n=2: 1; n=3: 6; n=4: 20 = 10 + 10; unconstrained n^4 = 256 in 4D
- **Size of the connection and metric (own estimate)** (numerical-estimate, DIV §6.3 p.90 (pdf 105)): A general connection in n dimensions has n^3 components; a symmetric one n^2(n+1)/2; the metric has n(n+1)/2. In 4D these are 64, 40 and 10, which motivates exploiting symmetry (e.g. Exercise 6.32) when computing curvature by hand. These counts are our own arithmetic, not stated in the text beyond n^3. Key numbers: 64, 40, 10 for n = 4

## Notation and conventions

- **Metric signature:** Lorentzian metrics have signature -2, i.e. (+,-,-,-). — The book notes the opposite (+2) convention used elsewhere. (DIV §6.8 p.100 (pdf 115))
- **Partial derivative:** Written d_b X^a, dX^a/dx^b or X^a_{,b}; the book mixes the first three. Also called the ordinary derivative. — X^a_{|b} is listed as another notation in the literature. (DIV §6.1 p.85 (pdf 100))
- **Covariant derivative:** Written nabla_c X^a or X^a_{;c} (also X^a_{||c} in the literature); derivative index is the last lower index on Gamma. (DIV §6.3 p.90 (pdf 105))
- **Starred equality:** An equals sign with an asterisk marks an equation valid only in a particular coordinate system (e.g. adapted or geodesic coordinates). (DIV §6.2 p.88 (pdf 103))
- **Lie derivative symbol:** Roman L with subscript X: L_X T. (DIV §6.2 p.88 (pdf 103))
- **Absolute derivative:** D/Du along a curve with parameter u; nabla_X for contraction with X. (DIV §6.4 p.92 (pdf 107))
- **Identity sign:** Triple bar marks identities holding for all tensors/metrics, e.g. nabla_c g_ab identically 0, Bianchi identities. — Continues the identity-versus-equation distinction from Chapter 5. (DIV §6.10 p.104 (pdf 119))
- **Christoffel symbols:** First kind {ab,c}; second kind as a brace with a above bc. After §6.10 the metric connection is written Gamma^a_bc. (DIV §6.9 p.102 (pdf 117))
- **Riemann tensor index convention:** R^a_bcd = d_c Gamma^a_bd - d_d Gamma^a_bc + Gamma^e_bd Gamma^a_ec - Gamma^e_bc Gamma^a_ed, so [nabla_c, nabla_d] X^a = R^a_bcd X^b. — Same Riemann and Ricci index conventions as Misner-Thorne-Wheeler, though MTW use signature +2. (DIV §6.5 p.94 (pdf 109))
- **Ricci contraction:** R_ab = R^c_acb, contracting first and third indices. (DIV §6.12 p.106 (pdf 121))
- **Bold frame indices:** Bold i labels members of a set of n vector fields X_i^a and is summed over like a tensor index but is not a coordinate index. — Bold type is lost in the text export of §6.7. (DIV §6.7 p.98 (pdf 113))
- **Kernel letter under index moves:** The same letter denotes a tensor with any index positions, e.g. T_ab, T_a^b, T^a_b, T^ab. — Horizontal index order must be kept. (DIV §6.8 p.101 (pdf 116))
- **ds^2 and interval:** ds^2 means (ds)^2; ds is called distance in Riemannian and interval in relativistic contexts; d sigma is used for spacelike separations. (DIV §6.8 p.100 (pdf 115))
- **Anti-symmetrization brackets:** Square brackets on indices anti-symmetrize with a normalizing factor, as in nabla_[c nabla_d] X^a = (1/2) R^a_bcd X^b. — Defined in (5.33). (DIV §6.5 p.95 (pdf 110))

## Teaching gems

### Diagnose once, reuse thrice: every derivative fails because it subtracts objects at different points, and each new structure (flow, connection, metric) is just a rule for bringing them together. · DIV §6.1 p.86 (pdf 101)

**Why it works:** Gives learners a single organizing question for three constructions that otherwise look unrelated.

**App idea:** A 'how do I compare these arrows?' hub page with three tabs (drag by flow, transport by connection, transport by metric connection), each showing the same two-point comparison with a different rule.

### Lie derivative as dragging with the flow, then subtracting at one point; in flow-adapted coordinates it is just d/dx^1. · DIV §6.2 p.87 (pdf 102)

**Why it works:** Separates the geometric idea from the index formula and explains the correction terms as coordinate artefacts.

**App idea:** Lie dragging sandbox with a du slider showing the difference quotient converging to the bracket [X,Y].

### Define the connection through a triangle of arrows at Q: actual field, parallel copy, and their tensorial difference (Fig. 6.5). · DIV §6.3 p.90 (pdf 105)

**Why it works:** Makes nabla X = d X + Gamma X read as 'change minus the change you would have had anyway', and explains the minus sign.

**App idea:** Polar-coordinate plane where the learner sees component-copying fail and Christoffel-corrected transport succeed.

### Two notions of straight line (never turning, extremal length) that coincide exactly for the metric connection. · DIV §6.10 p.103 (pdf 118)

**Why it works:** Motivates the Christoffel formula as the answer to a geometric question rather than a memorized expression.

**App idea:** Perturb a connection away from Levi-Civita and watch straightest and shortest paths from one point separate.

### Curvature as the second-order failure of a vector to return unchanged around a tiny parallelogram, computed explicitly by Taylor expansion. · DIV §6.7 p.97 (pdf 112)

**Why it works:** Connects the algebraic commutator definition to a measurable holonomy with a clear area scaling.

**App idea:** Loop shrinker on a sphere plotting angle mismatch against area, recovering Gaussian curvature as the slope.

### Geodesic coordinates: a quadratic coordinate tweak kills Gamma at one point but cannot touch its derivatives. · DIV §6.6 p.95 (pdf 110)

**Why it works:** Short, concrete construction that pinpoints why curvature is coordinate-invariant and prefigures local inertial frames.

**App idea:** Coordinate grid warping around P in which the Christoffel readout at P goes to zero while the Riemann readout stays unchanged.

### Flatness from a parallel frame: path independence gives a frame, torsion-freeness makes its dual covectors gradients, and those gradients are Cartesian coordinates. · DIV §6.7 p.98 (pdf 113)

**Why it works:** Constructive proof showing exactly what role integrability and symmetry each play.

**App idea:** On a plane with curvilinear coordinates, transport a frame everywhere, then integrate the dual covectors to reconstruct Cartesian grid lines.

### Counting components in n = 1..4 to motivate the Weyl tensor as the curvature Ricci cannot see. · DIV §6.13 p.107 (pdf 122)

**Why it works:** A quick arithmetic table explains why 4D is the first dimension with independent 'free' curvature.

**App idea:** Dimension slider with a bar split into Ricci and Weyl components.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **No concrete example in the text: connection, parallel transport, geodesics and curvature are never computed for a sphere, a plane in polar coordinates or any explicit metric (only in exercises).** — Learners can manipulate indices without ever seeing a non-zero Christoffel symbol in flat space or a non-zero curvature. *Suggestion:* Pair §6.3-6.5 with polar coordinates on the plane and the 2-sphere, computing Gamma, a geodesic and R^theta_phi theta phi explicitly.
- **Printed slip in the loop argument: the second route is described as going via x + delta x, but it goes via x + Delta x.** — Confuses which two routes are being compared and the sign of the result. *Suggestion:* State clearly: route A through x + delta x, route B through x + Delta x, and ask learners to check the relabelling of dummy indices.
- **Printed slip in geodesic coordinates: the Jacobian of the quadratic transformation is printed with x^d where x^b is needed.** — A repeated index appearing three times breaks the summation convention and may stall a careful reader. *Suggestion:* Write d x'^a / d x^d = delta^a_d + Q^a_bd x^b.
- **Printed slips in the exercises, each checked on the rendered pages. Exercise 6.19 (pdf p.125) gives a positive definite line element but asks for null geodesics with l^2 + m^2 - n^2 = 0. Exercise 6.7(i) (pdf p.124) has nu where mu is meant. Exercise 6.30(iii) (pdf p.126) repeats a free index within a single term.** — Learners may waste time or doubt correct work. *Suggestion:* Use ds^2 = dx^2 + dy^2 - dz^2 in 6.19 and verify 6.30(iii) with computer algebra.
- **The final flatness theorem box omits the standing assumption that the connection is symmetric, and the global statement also needs simple connectedness.** — Readers may believe R = 0 alone implies flatness for any connection on any manifold. *Suggestion:* Restate: for a torsion-free connection on a simply connected region, flat iff R = 0; show a cone or a teleparallel connection as counterexamples.
- **The Weyl conformal flatness theorem is stated without its dimension restriction (n >= 4); in 3D the Weyl tensor vanishes identically though not every 3-metric is conformally flat (the Cotton tensor decides). The 2D result is local.** — Overgeneralization to three dimensions and to global statements. *Suggestion:* Add the n >= 4 caveat and mention the Cotton tensor in a side note.
- **Metric geodesics are defined as stationary-interval curves using a square-root Lagrangian, but null curves have zero interval, so the variational definition does not apply to them; the book simply asserts the null geodesic equation.** — Unclear why null geodesics satisfy the same equation or what 'affine parameter' means for them before Chapter 7. *Suggestion:* Preview the quadratic Lagrangian g(dx/du, dx/du) of Chapter 7, or define null geodesics as null affine geodesics of the metric connection after §6.10.
- **Christoffel symbols appear in §6.9 before being identified with the connection in §6.10, with two notations for the same object.** — Learners may think Christoffel symbols and Gamma are unrelated objects. *Suggestion:* Announce early that the brace symbols will turn out to be the connection coefficients of the metric.
- **The Lie derivative derivation uses active transformations that the text itself calls intricate, with no picture of the flow map acting on a whole field and no example calculation.** — Sign errors (dragging forward versus pulling back) and confusion over why the formula differs from the covariant derivative. *Suggestion:* Compute L_X Y for X a rotation field and Y a constant field in the plane, and animate the dragging.
- **Several 'why?' prompts on signature and many '(exercise)' steps are left without hints in the text, including the tensor character of g^ab and of R.** — Self-learners can miss key logical steps such as Sylvester's law of inertia. *Suggestion:* Provide short tutor-side answers: congruence transformations preserve inertia; quotient theorem for R; primed version of (6.57) for g^ab.
- **No physical interpretation of Riemann, Ricci or Einstein tensors (e.g. tidal forces, geodesic deviation, volume change) is offered here.** — Curvature feels purely formal until later chapters. *Suggestion:* Foreshadow geodesic deviation and tidal acceleration when introducing R, and note that G_ab will equal a source term.
- **The second closing theorem of §6.13 is stated only for two-dimensional Riemannian metrics. The Lorentzian version is left to Exercise 6.31, yet ch18 cites 'the second theorem in §6.13' for a Lorentzian 2-space.** — A learner may not see why a result stated for positive definite metrics justifies the double null coordinates behind Penrose diagrams. *Suggestion:* Treat Exercise 6.31, which builds null coordinates for a 2D Lorentzian metric, as the version actually needed later, and work it before ch18.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'In Cartesian coordinates the derivative of a vector field is obvious. What goes wrong in polar coordinates, even on a flat plane?' Let the learner discover that basis vectors change from point to point before showing (6.1).
- Keep one organizing question in view: how do I compare a tensor at P with one at Q? Present Lie dragging, parallel transport and the metric connection as three answers.
- Order of explanation: non-tensorial partial derivative -> Lie derivative idea (flow) with a planar example -> connection via Fig. 6.5 triangle -> covariant derivative formulas and sign rules -> parallel transport and geodesics -> commutator and Riemann -> geodesic coordinates -> loop holonomy and flatness -> metric -> metric connection -> identities -> Ricci, Einstein -> Weyl and conformal.
- Check for understanding: ask why Gamma can vanish at a point but R cannot be made to vanish by coordinates; a good answer mentions the inhomogeneous law and tensor character.
- Check sign rules with a quick drill: Lie derivative has minus per upper index with d X; covariant derivative has plus per upper index with Gamma. Ask the learner to write L_X and nabla_c for T^a_b.
- When a learner asks why we need a connection if we have a metric, explain that the book separates them deliberately: affine geodesics and parallel transport need only Gamma; the metric then selects a unique torsion-free compatible Gamma.
- Common question: 'Is a geodesic the shortest path?' Answer with stationary interval, the Lorentzian maximization of proper time, and zero length for null geodesics.
- Common question: 'Why does the Lie derivative not need a connection?' Answer: the flow of X itself provides the identification of nearby points and tensors; show (6.29) to see Gamma cancels anyway.
- Flag book-specific conventions early: signature -2, R^a_bcd with MTW-style indices, Ricci contraction on first and third index; warn when mixing with Schutz or Carroll (signature +2).
- Point out the printed slips (via Delta x in §6.7, x^b in §6.6, Exercise 6.19 sign) if the learner is working from the book, to prevent loss of confidence.
- For advanced learners, connect §6.7 to holonomy and teleparallel (torsionful, curvature-free) connections; for beginners, stay with the sphere and the plane.
- Use Exercise 6.32 (spherically symmetric metric) as a capstone, ideally with a computer-algebra demo so the learner sees the pipeline metric -> Gamma -> Riemann -> Ricci -> Einstein.
