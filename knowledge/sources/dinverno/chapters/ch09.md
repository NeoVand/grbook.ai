# DIV ch09 · The principles of general relativity

> Before writing any field equation, the chapter lays out the five heuristic principles behind Einstein's theory (Mach, equivalence, general covariance, minimal coupling, correspondence) and shows how the equivalence principle turns connection coefficients into gravitational forces and the metric into the gravitational potential.

**Pages:** printed 153–170 · pdf 168–185 · **Difficulty:** math 2/5, conceptual 4/5, novice-friendliness 4/5

Mathematically light (Newtonian mechanics and two lines of tensor calculus) but conceptually demanding: the reader must hold several contested, imprecise principles in mind, distinguish three masses, and follow the compressed step from Christoffel symbols to curved space-time. The text is clear and discursive; the main stumbling blocks are the terse geometric bridge at the end of §9.4, the reversed weak/strong naming, and the index structure of (9.12).

## Role in the book

Chapter 9 is the hinge of Part C. Parts A and B supplied special relativity (via the k-calculus) and the full tensor machinery (connections, geodesics, curvature, variational methods), and Chapter 8 recast special relativity as flat Minkowski geometry. This chapter supplies the physical motivation that justifies moving to a curved, non-flat metric: it argues from Newtonian inertial forces and the equality of the three Newtonian masses to the equivalence principle, and from there to the idea that the metric plays the role of gravitational potential and that the field equations should be second-order PDEs in g_ab. It sets up Chapter 10 (non-local lift experiments, geodesic deviation, vacuum field equations), Chapter 12 (minimal coupling of the energy-momentum conservation law), and Chapter 16 (Eotvos experiment, Einstein equivalence principle, Brans-Dicke).

## Learning objectives

- Reader can name the five guiding principles of general relativity and say which is regarded as the key one and which are controversial.
- Reader can transform Newton's second law into a uniformly accelerated frame and identify the resulting inertial force -ma, noting that it is proportional to the body's mass.
- Reader can describe the four phases of Newton's bucket experiment and contrast Newton's absolute-space explanation with Mach's explanation in terms of rotation relative to distant matter.
- Reader can state Mach's principle informally and in the three variants M1-M3, and list its main weaknesses (non-quantitative, does not explain why acceleration rather than velocity matters).
- Reader can explain why the agreement between the Earth's rotation period relative to local inertial frames and relative to the fixed stars is a Newtonian coincidence that Mach's view explains.
- Reader can distinguish inertial, passive gravitational, and active gravitational mass and derive m^I = m^P from universality of free fall and m^P = m^A from Newton's third law.
- Reader can state the four versions P1-P4 of the principle of equivalence and explain what 'local' means in them.
- Reader can analyse the four lift cases and say which pairs are indistinguishable and which version of the equivalence principle each pair illustrates.
- Reader can argue that the Christoffel terms in the geodesic equation play the role of inertial (and hence gravitational) forces, so that the metric acts as a potential and space-time must be curved.
- Reader can state the principles of general relativity and of general covariance, explain the 'emptiness' objection, and explain why preferred coordinates can still be useful.
- Reader can apply minimal gravitational coupling to generalize a special-relativistic equation (partial derivatives to covariant derivatives, no explicit curvature terms) and recognize its ambiguity.
- Reader can draw the correspondence diagram linking general relativity, special relativity, Newtonian gravity and Newtonian mechanics, naming the limits (no gravity; weak field and low speed) on each arrow.

## Teaching approach

A discursive, physics-first chapter with almost no new mathematics. It works through Newtonian mechanics to expose 'coincidences' (inertial forces proportional to mass; equal inertial and gravitational mass; inertial frames not rotating relative to the stars) and treats each coincidence as a clue to deeper physics. Principles are stated as labelled, numbered statements (M1-M3, P1-P4), often as statements of impossibility, and the chapter is candid that several of them are philosophically contested. It ends each principle with a pointer to how it is used mathematically later, and the tensor apparatus appears in just two short places: the geodesic equation rewritten in general coordinates (§9.4) and the conservation-law example used to illustrate minimal coupling (§9.6).

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, thought-experiment, historical-narrative, diagram-driven, survey-overview

**Narrative arc**

1. Justify spending a chapter on principles rather than starting from a Lagrangian: principles give insight and a route to repair if the theory fails.
2. List the five principles and flag which are controversial (Mach, covariance) and which is agreed to be central (equivalence).
3. Ask whether Newton's laws hold in non-inertial frames; derive the inertial force in a uniformly accelerated frame and note it scales with mass.
4. Ask how inertial frames are detected: Newton's absolute space and the rotating bucket.
5. Offer Mach's alternative (inertia from relative motion with respect to distant matter), test it with a rotating universe, the North Pole pendulum coincidence, one-particle and anisotropic universes, the Hughes-Drever null result, and a varying G.
6. Assess Mach's principle critically and distil three formal variants M1-M3 for later checking against the theory.
7. Separate the concept of mass into inertial, passive and active; use Galileo's free-fall result and Newton's third law to show all three coincide in Newtonian theory.
8. Promote the free-fall coincidence to a foundational postulate (P1), add universal coupling (P2), then local indistinguishability of free fall and inertial motion (P3) and of acceleration and gravity (P4).
9. Illustrate P3 and P4 with Einstein's four lift experiments.
10. Bridge to geometry: in general coordinates the geodesic equation acquires Christoffel terms that are inertial forces; by equivalence gravity must also live in these terms, so the metric must be non-flat and act as a potential obeying second-order field equations.
11. Generalize the principle of relativity to all observers and sharpen it into general covariance (tensorial equations), with caveats.
12. Add minimal coupling as an Occam's razor for porting special-relativistic laws, illustrated with energy-momentum conservation.
13. Close with the correspondence principle and a diagram of limits.

**Signature moves**

- Treats every Newtonian 'coincidence' as a signal that a deeper principle is at work, and shows how GR turns a Newtonian coincidence into a foundational hypothesis whose failure would sink the theory.
- Takes the stance that inertial forces are real because their effects are real (blackouts, shattered flywheels), rather than dismissing them as fictitious, which makes gravity-as-inertial-force natural.
- Uses the counterfactual 'hold the bucket fixed and rotate the universe' to separate Newton's and Mach's predictions sharply.
- Splits Newtonian mass into three operationally distinct masses and then re-derives their equality, so the reader sees the equality as an empirical input rather than a definition.
- States several principles as impossibility statements ('there are no local experiments which ...'), echoing how the principle of relativity is framed.
- Numbers competing formulations (M1-M3, P1-P4) explicitly so that they can later be checked against the finished theory.
- Reads the Christoffel symbols as force terms and hence the metric components as potentials, borrowing the Newtonian phi / grad phi structure to predict second-order field equations.
- Illustrates minimal coupling with a deliberately artificial alternative equation containing the Riemann tensor that also reduces correctly in flat space-time, making the ambiguity concrete.
- Frames the correspondence principle as part of scientific modelling: every theory has a domain of validity, drawn as a commutative-looking box diagram of specializations.

## Section by section

### §9.1 The role of physical principles — p.153 (pdf 168)

Explains why the authors discuss guiding principles instead of simply postulating a Lagrangian: they illuminate how GR's framework differs from Newtonian and special-relativistic ones and offer a physical basis for repair if the theory fails. Lists the five principles and notes the disputes over their status.

**Concepts:** Heuristic role of physical principles, Mach's principle, Principle of equivalence, Principle of general covariance, Principle of minimal gravitational coupling, Correspondence principle

**Key moves**
- Contrasts a Lagrangian-first presentation with a principles-first one and argues for the latter on grounds of insight and robustness.
- Cites Einstein's unsuccessful unified-field programme as a cautionary tale of mathematics detached from physical principle.
- Records the controversy: covariance called empty by some (Bondi, Fock) but sufficient by others (Anderson); doubt whether GR is Machian; broad agreement that equivalence is the key principle.

### §9.2 Mach's principle — p.154 (pdf 169)

Derives the inertial force in a uniformly accelerated frame, then asks what fixes inertial frames. Presents Newton's absolute space and the rotating bucket, Mach's relational alternative, supporting evidence (North Pole pendulum coincidence), consequences (anisotropic inertia, G varying with epoch), the Hughes-Drever null result, criticisms, and three formal versions M1-M3.

**Concepts:** Inertial frame, Uniformly accelerated reference frame, Inertial force, Absolute space, Newton's bucket experiment, Mach's principle, Inertial frames non-rotating relative to the distant stars, Anisotropy of inertia, Hughes-Drever experiment, Machian variation of the gravitational constant, Brans-Dicke theory

**Key moves**
- Transforms F = m xddot into frame S' with x = x' + s to get F - ma = m x'ddot, an extra mass-proportional force seen by S'.
- Defends inertial forces as physically real and asks for their origin, a question Newtonian theory leaves open.
- Walks through the four bucket phases B1-B4 to show concavity tracks the water's rotation, not the bucket's.
- Replaces 'rotation relative to absolute space' by 'rotation relative to the fixed stars' and tests the two views with a rotating universe around a fixed bucket.
- Uses the equality of the Foucault-type pendulum rotation period and the sidereal rotation period as evidence for Mach.
- Argues by reciprocity and a one-particle universe that inertia is set by the global mass distribution, implying anisotropic inertia in an anisotropic universe and a G that depends on the cosmic state.
- Weighs the null Hughes-Drever result and CMB isotropy, then lists weaknesses of Mach's idea and states M1-M3.

### §9.3 Mass in Newtonian theory — p.159 (pdf 174)

Distinguishes inertial, passive gravitational and active gravitational mass by the roles they play in Newton's second law, in the force on a body in a potential, and in the potential a body produces. Uses equal free-fall acceleration (Pisa, the Moon hammer-and-feather drop) to equate inertial and passive mass, and Newton's third law for two bodies to equate passive and active mass.

**Concepts:** Inertial mass, Passive gravitational mass, Active gravitational mass, Universality of free fall, Equality of inertial and passive gravitational mass, Equality of passive and active gravitational mass

**Key moves**
- Defines each mass through the equation it appears in: (9.5), (9.6), (9.7).
- Writes m_i^I a_i = -m_i^P grad phi for two dropped bodies; equal accelerations force the ratio m^I/m^P to be universal, set to 1 by choice of units.
- Notes the equality is tested to 1 part in 10^12 (forward to §16.3).
- Uses the fact that gravity cannot be shielded to consider two mutually attracting bodies; Newton's third law forces m^P/m^A to be universal, set to 1.

### §9.4 The principle of equivalence — p.162 (pdf 177)

Defines a gravitational test particle and states four versions of the equivalence principle (P1 universality of free fall, P2 universal coupling, P3 local equivalence of free fall and field-free inertial motion, P4 local equivalence of uniform acceleration and rest in a field). Illustrates P3/P4 with Einstein's four lift experiments and then argues that gravitational forces must reside in the connection of a non-flat metric that acts as a potential.

**Concepts:** Gravitational test particle, Universality of free fall, Universal coupling of gravity, Freely falling frame, Local inertial frame, Einstein equivalence principle (local form), Equivalence of acceleration and gravitation, Gravity as an inertial effect, Einstein lift thought experiment, Christoffel symbols as inertial forces, Metric as gravitational potential, Curved space-time from the equivalence principle, Second-order field equations for the metric

**Key moves**
- Contrasts the status of P1 in Newtonian theory (an accommodatable empirical coincidence) and in GR (a hypothesis whose failure refutes the theory).
- Extends gravitational coupling to energy via mass-energy equivalence (P2).
- Defines 'local' as a region where variations of the gravitational field are unobservable, so a non-rotating freely falling frame recovers special relativity (P3).
- Notes the second coincidence (gravity and inertial forces both proportional to mass) and compares mg with ma to motivate P4.
- Runs the four lift cases: 1 and 3 indistinguishable (P4); 2 and 4 indistinguishable (P3).
- Writes the free-particle equation in Minkowski coordinates and in general coordinates; the Christoffel terms are the inertial forces of a still-flat metric.
- Requires gravity also to appear in Gamma; for it to be distinguishable from pure coordinate effects the metric must be non-flat; hence g_ab are potentials and field equations should be second-order PDEs in g_ab.

### §9.5 The principle of general covariance — p.165 (pdf 180)

Extends the principle of special relativity to all observers, links observers to coordinate systems, notes that curved space-time has no canonical coordinates, and restates the idea as the requirement that physical equations be tensorial. Acknowledges the objection that any theory can be written tensorially and warns that symmetry-adapted coordinates remain useful.

**Concepts:** Principle of general relativity, Principle of general covariance, Canonical coordinate system, Tensor equation

**Key moves**
- Argues that Earth-bound, non-inertial observers must be able to discover physical laws, so all observers should be equivalent.
- Identifies observers with coordinate systems; flatness and integrability of the connection give Minkowski coordinates a canonical status that disappears in curved space-time.
- Refines 'any coordinates will do' into 'the theory is invariant under coordinate transformations', i.e. tensorial form.
- Notes the retrospective emptiness objection and justifies the Part B tensor digression.

### §9.6 The principle of minimal gravitational coupling — p.165 (pdf 180)

Introduces a simplicity rule for carrying special-relativistic equations over to curved space-time: replace partial derivatives by covariant ones and add no explicit curvature terms. Illustrates with energy-momentum conservation and an alternative curvature-containing equation that would equally reduce to the flat-space law.

**Concepts:** Principle of minimal gravitational coupling, Energy-momentum conservation law, Riemann curvature tensor, Covariant derivative

**Key moves**
- Starts from partial_b T^ab = 0 in Minkowski coordinates.
- Offers nabla_b T^ab = 0 as the simplest tensorial generalization.
- Exhibits a rival equation with an added Riemann-tensor term that also collapses to the flat law because the curvature vanishes there.
- Forbids explicit curvature terms, while admitting the rule was only implicit for Einstein and is vague.

### §9.7 The correspondence principle — p.166 (pdf 181)

Places GR within the book's modelling philosophy: a new theory must reproduce older successful theories within their domains. GR must reduce to special relativity without gravity and to Newtonian gravity for weak fields and slow motion, as summarized in a four-box diagram.

**Concepts:** Correspondence principle, Newtonian limit, Domain of validity of a physical model

**Key moves**
- Recalls that every model carries a range of validity.
- Names the two limits of GR: no gravitation gives special relativity; weak field plus low speed gives Newtonian gravity.
- Draws arrows of specialization converging on Newtonian mechanics without gravity.

### Exercises and further reading — p.167 (pdf 182)

Eleven exercises keyed to §9.2-§9.7 (accelerated-frame mechanics, rotating frames, mass equalities, lift analysis, tidal deformation, geodesics in cylindrical coordinates, light bending by equivalence, minimal coupling, and the c to infinity limit), followed by a short list of other introductory and advanced GR texts.

**Concepts:** Inertial force, Tidal effects, Christoffel symbols as inertial forces, Principle of minimal gravitational coupling, Correspondence principle

**Key moves**
- Exercises push the reader to compute pendulum and water-surface tilts in accelerated frames and to derive the general non-inertial equation of motion.
- Exercise 9.8 foreshadows gravitational light bending from the equivalence principle alone.
- Exercise 9.11 asks for the null cone to be flattened as c grows without bound, linking the correspondence principle to absolute simultaneity.

## Concepts

### Heuristic role of physical principles

*historical-idea · introduced* · also: guiding principles of general relativity

The view that a theory's construction is guided by a small set of physical principles which, even when imprecise, constrain its mathematical form and indicate where to look if the theory needs revision.

**How introduced:** Opens the chapter as a defence of the pedagogical choice: one could start from a Lagrangian, but examining Einstein's principles reveals how the framework differs and offers a physically grounded route to repair; Einstein's later fruitless unification attempts serve as the counterexample.

**Where:** DIV §9.1 p.153 (pdf 168)

### Inertial frame

*definition · revisited* · also: inertial observer, privileged frame

A reference frame in which a body free of forces stays at rest or moves uniformly in a straight line, so that Newton's laws hold in their standard form.

**How introduced:** Revisited from Chapter 2 through the question of whether Newton's laws hold in every frame and how the Universe singles out the privileged class in which they do.

**Where:** DIV §9.2 p.154 (pdf 169)

### Uniformly accelerated reference frame

*definition · introduced* · also: non-inertial frame, accelerated frame in standard configuration

A frame S' whose origin accelerates with constant acceleration a relative to an inertial frame S, related (Newtonianly, frames in standard configuration) by x = x' + s(t), y = y', z = z', t = t' with s double-dot = a.

**How introduced:** Set up with Fig. 9.1 using the familiar standard-configuration picture, but with the relative displacement s now accelerating rather than growing linearly.

**Prerequisites:** Inertial frame

$$
x = x' + s,\ y=y',\ z=z',\ t=t'
$$

$$
\ddot x = \ddot x' + a
$$

**Where:** DIV §9.2 p.154 (pdf 169)

### Inertial force

*phenomenon · core* · also: fictitious force, apparent force, pseudo-force

An extra force term, proportional to the mass of the body, that appears when Newton's second law is written in terms of accelerations measured in a non-inertial frame; for linear acceleration a it is -ma, and rotating frames give centrifugal, Coriolis and Euler terms.

**How introduced:** Derived in two lines by differentiating the accelerated-frame transformation twice and moving ma to the force side. The authors deliberately call these forces real by their effects (astronaut blackouts, bursting flywheels) and use them to pose the question of their physical origin, which Mach tries to answer.

**Prerequisites:** Uniformly accelerated reference frame, Newton's second law

$$
F - ma = m\ddot x'
$$

**Notes:** The key observation carried forward to §9.4 is that every inertial force carries the body's mass as a factor.

**Where:** DIV §9.2 p.155 (pdf 170)

### Centrifugal and Coriolis forces

*phenomenon · mention* · also: rotating-frame inertial forces

Inertial forces in a frame rotating with angular velocity omega: centrifugal -m omega x (omega x r'), Coriolis -2m omega x r'dot, plus the Euler term -m omegadot x r' when omega varies.

**How introduced:** Mentioned as further examples of inertial forces and left to Exercise 9.2, which derives the full non-inertial equation of motion.

**Prerequisites:** Inertial force

$$
\mathbf F - [m\mathbf a + 2m\boldsymbol\omega\times\dot{\mathbf r}' + m\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r') + m\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}\times\mathbf r'] = m\ddot{\mathbf r}'
$$

**Where:** DIV §9.2 p.155 (pdf 170); DIV p.167 (pdf 182)

### Absolute space

*historical-idea · introduced* · also: Newton's absolute space

Newton's postulated immovable background against which all motion is ultimately referred; inertial observers are those at rest or moving uniformly relative to it, and inertial forces arise from absolute acceleration.

**How introduced:** Introduced as Newton's answer to 'how do we detect inertial frames?', with a brief quotation of Newton's definition, and immediately operationalized through the bucket experiment.

**Prerequisites:** Inertial frame

**Where:** DIV §9.2 p.155 (pdf 170)

### Newton's bucket experiment

*experiment-or-observation · developed* · also: rotating bucket, bucket experiment

A suspended bucket of water is spun via a twisted rope; the water surface is flat when only the bucket rotates, becomes concave once the water rotates, stays concave after the bucket stops while the water still turns, and flattens when the water stops. Surface curvature thus tracks the water's rotation, not its rotation relative to the bucket.

**How introduced:** Presented as four labelled phases B1-B4 with Fig. 9.2, then extended to linear acceleration (tilted surface, Fig. 9.3), leading to the rule that an observer is inertial exactly when a carried bucket shows a flat surface. It is then reinterpreted in Machian terms.

**Prerequisites:** Absolute space, Inertial force

**Where:** DIV §9.2 p.155 (pdf 170); DIV §9.2 p.156 (pdf 171)

### Mach's principle

*principle · core* · also: matter there influences inertia here, Machian viewpoint, M1, M2, M3

The idea that only relative motion is meaningful and that inertia, inertial forces and the inertial frames themselves are determined by the distribution and motion of all the matter in the universe, dominated by distant matter.

**How introduced:** Motivated as the answer to the origin of inertial forces left unanswered by Newton. Built from Berkeley and Mach's relational view, tested against the bucket (rotate the universe instead), supported by the pendulum-at-the-pole coincidence, extended to anisotropic and one-particle universes, critiqued, and finally crystallized into three sharper statements: M1 matter determines the geometry (privileged particle and light paths); M2 no matter, no geometry; M3 a lone body has no inertia.

**Prerequisites:** Inertial force, Absolute space, Newton's bucket experiment

**Notes:** The book stresses that GR is at best partly Machian and that Bondi and Samuel catalogue ten versions of the principle; M1-M3 are promised to be checked against the finished theory.

**Where:** DIV §9.2 p.156 (pdf 171); DIV §9.2 p.157 (pdf 172); DIV §9.2 p.158 (pdf 173); DIV §9.2 p.159 (pdf 174)

### Inertial frames non-rotating relative to the distant stars

*experiment-or-observation · developed* · also: fixed stars coincidence, pendulum at the North Pole

The empirical fact that the frame in which a freely swinging pendulum's plane stays fixed (a local inertial frame) is, to experimental accuracy, non-rotating relative to the distant stars, so the Earth's rotation period measured both ways agrees.

**How introduced:** A pendulum at the North Pole (Fig. 9.4) measures the Earth's rotation relative to absolute space; astronomy measures it relative to the stars; the agreement is an unexplained coincidence for Newton but expected for Mach. Used to launch the methodological maxim that coincidences in a theory signal something fundamental.

**Prerequisites:** Inertial frame, Mach's principle

**Where:** DIV §9.2 p.156 (pdf 171); DIV §9.2 p.157 (pdf 172)

### Anisotropy of inertia

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: direction-dependent inertia, anisotropic inertial mass

A Machian expectation that if matter in the universe were unevenly distributed, a body's inertial response would depend on the direction in which it is accelerated.

**How introduced:** Reached by a reciprocity argument (inertial effects scale with the masses of the inducing bodies) and a one-particle thought experiment (a tiny second mass cannot restore full inertia), illustrated schematically in Fig. 9.5 with different effective masses for accelerations toward and across a concentration of matter.

**Prerequisites:** Mach's principle

**Where:** DIV §9.2 p.157 (pdf 172)

### Hughes-Drever experiment

*experiment-or-observation · introduced* · also: Hughes-Drever null experiment

Experiments around 1960 by Hughes and by Drever showing that inertial mass is isotropic to at least one part in 10^18, often described as the most precise null experiment ever performed.

**How introduced:** Used as the observational test of anisotropic inertia: its null result means either Mach's principle fails or the universe is highly isotropic, the latter supported by CMB uniformity.

**Prerequisites:** Anisotropy of inertia

**Where:** DIV §9.2 p.158 (pdf 173)

### Machian variation of the gravitational constant

*historical-idea · introduced* · also: G as a function of epoch, G(t)

The inference that, if a body's mass depends on the state of the universe while its potential depends only on the body, then G carries information about the universe and could vary with cosmic epoch or position; a fully Machian theory would let one compute G from cosmic structure.

**How introduced:** Argued from the Newtonian potential phi = -Gm/r by noting that Mach makes m depend on the rest of the universe; used later to motivate Brans-Dicke theory.

**Prerequisites:** Mach's principle, Active gravitational mass

$$
\phi = -Gm/r
$$

**Where:** DIV §9.2 p.158 (pdf 173)

### Brans-Dicke theory

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: scalar-tensor theory

An alternative relativistic theory of gravity from the 1960s, intended to be more fully Machian than GR, in which the effective gravitational coupling varies with epoch.

**How introduced:** Mentioned only in a parenthesis while assessing how far Einstein succeeded in incorporating Mach's principle.

**Prerequisites:** Mach's principle, Machian variation of the gravitational constant

**Where:** DIV §9.2 p.158 (pdf 173)

### Inertial mass

*physical-quantity · core* · also: inertia, m^I

The mass appearing in Newton's second law, measuring a body's resistance to changes in its motion; a priori it has nothing to do with gravity.

**How introduced:** Introduced first of three masses by the equation F = d(m^I v)/dt, with emphasis that nothing links it to gravitation in advance.

**Prerequisites:** Newton's second law

$$
\mathbf F = \frac{d(m^{I}\mathbf v)}{dt}
$$

$$
\mathbf F = m^{I}\mathbf a
$$

**Where:** DIV §9.3 p.159 (pdf 174)

### Passive gravitational mass

*physical-quantity · core* · also: m^P

The mass that measures how strongly a body responds to an externally imposed gravitational field, via F = -m^P grad phi.

**How introduced:** Second of the three masses, defined operationally through (9.6): place the body at a point of known potential phi and the force it feels is proportional to m^P times the potential gradient. The text stresses that this mass, unlike m^I, is tied to gravitation by definition.

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian gravitational potential

$$
\mathbf F = -m^{P}\nabla\phi
$$

**Where:** DIV §9.3 p.159 (pdf 174)

### Active gravitational mass

*physical-quantity · core* · also: m^A, source mass

The mass that measures how strong a gravitational field a body produces, via phi = -G m^A / r for a point source.

**How introduced:** Defined as the source strength in the potential of a point mass, completing the three-mass decomposition.

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian gravitational potential

$$
\phi = -\frac{Gm^{A}}{r}
$$

**Where:** DIV §9.3 p.160 (pdf 175)

### Universality of free fall

*principle · core* · also: Galileo's result, equal acceleration of falling bodies, principle of equivalence P1, strong principle of equivalence (d'Inverno's naming), weak equivalence principle (modern usage)

The empirical law, elevated to a principle, that a gravitational test particle's motion in a gravitational field does not depend on its mass or composition; in Newtonian language all bodies at the same place fall with the same acceleration (neglecting air resistance).

**How introduced:** First appears as the observational input of §9.3, credited to Galileo's Pisa drops (Fig. 9.6) and the Moon hammer-and-feather demonstration (Fig. 9.7); then restated in §9.4 as P1 and given a changed status: a mere coincidence in Newtonian theory but a pillar of GR.

**Prerequisites:** Inertial mass, Passive gravitational mass, Gravitational test particle

$$
\mathbf a_1 = \mathbf a_2
$$

**Notes:** Naming alert: this chapter calls P1 the 'strong' form and P2 the 'weak' form; §16.4 of the same book, like most modern literature, calls universality of free fall the weak equivalence principle.

**Where:** DIV §9.3 p.160 (pdf 175); DIV §9.4 p.162 (pdf 177)

### Equality of inertial and passive gravitational mass

*law · core* · also: m^I = m^P, equivalence of inertial and gravitational mass

In Newtonian gravity the ratio of inertial to passive gravitational mass is the same for all bodies and can be set to one by choice of units, so m^I = m^P.

**How introduced:** Derived by dropping two bodies, writing Newton's second law with the gravitational force for each, and using equal observed accelerations; tested to 1 part in 10^12 as described in Chapter 16.

**Prerequisites:** Inertial mass, Passive gravitational mass, Universality of free fall

$$
m^{I}_1/m^{P}_1 = m^{I}_2/m^{P}_2
$$

$$
m^{I} = m^{P}
$$

**Where:** DIV §9.3 p.160 (pdf 175); DIV §9.3 p.161 (pdf 176)

### Equality of passive and active gravitational mass

*law · developed* · also: m^P = m^A

In Newtonian gravity the ratio of passive to active gravitational mass is universal and can be set to one, so a body's response to gravity equals its strength as a source.

**How introduced:** Derived from the fact that nothing is shielded from gravity: two isolated bodies attract each other (Fig. 9.8), and Newton's third law applied to the two forces fixes the ratio.

**Prerequisites:** Passive gravitational mass, Active gravitational mass, Newton's third law

$$
m^{P}_1/m^{A}_1 = m^{P}_2/m^{A}_2
$$

$$
m = m^{I} = m^{P} = m^{A}
$$

**Where:** DIV §9.3 p.161 (pdf 176); DIV §9.3 p.162 (pdf 177)

### Gravitational test particle

*definition · introduced* · also: test particle

An idealized body that is acted on by gravity but whose own gravitational influence is negligible, so the field it moves in is fixed in advance.

**How introduced:** Defined at the start of §9.4 so that the free-fall result can be stated cleanly without back-reaction.

**Where:** DIV §9.4 p.162 (pdf 177)

### Universal coupling of gravity

*principle · developed* · also: principle of equivalence P2, weak principle of equivalence (d'Inverno's naming), no gravitational shielding

The statement that the gravitational field couples to everything: all matter and, by mass-energy equivalence, all energy both responds to and sources gravity, so nothing can be screened from it.

**How introduced:** Motivated by the no-shielding observation of §9.3 and extended to energy using special relativity's mass-energy equivalence; labelled P2.

**Prerequisites:** Active gravitational mass, Mass-energy equivalence

**Where:** DIV §9.4 p.162 (pdf 177); DIV §9.4 p.163 (pdf 178)

### Freely falling frame

*definition · core* · also: free fall, co-moving frame of a test particle

A reference frame moving with a gravitational test particle under gravity alone; if in addition it is non-rotating, it is locally an inertial frame.

**How introduced:** Introduced as the way to 'remove gravity locally' despite universal coupling: fall with the test particles and gravity disappears within a small region. Einstein's 'happiest thought' about a person falling from a roof supplies the image.

**Prerequisites:** Universality of free fall, Inertial frame

**Where:** DIV §9.4 p.163 (pdf 178)

### Local inertial frame

*definition · core* · also: locally inertial frame, locality

A non-rotating freely falling frame restricted to a region small enough that variations of the gravitational field across it cannot be detected; within it test particles move uniformly in straight lines and special relativity holds.

**How introduced:** The book defines 'locally' operationally, as confinement to a region where field variation is unobservably small, anticipating the non-local lift experiments and geodesic deviation of Chapter 10.

**Prerequisites:** Freely falling frame

**Notes:** Exercise 9.6 (a falling sphere of dust elongates) probes what goes wrong when the region is not small.

**Where:** DIV §9.4 p.163 (pdf 178)

### Einstein equivalence principle (local form)

*principle · core* · also: principle of equivalence P3, local indistinguishability of free fall and inertial motion

Within a small enough region, an observer who is freely falling without rotation cannot tell, by any experiment, that a gravitational field is present: the results match those of an unaccelerated observer far from all matter.

**How introduced:** Stated as P3 immediately after defining local inertial frames and phrased as a statement of impossibility; illustrated by lift Cases 2 and 4.

**Prerequisites:** Local inertial frame, Freely falling frame

**Notes:** The label 'Einstein equivalence principle' is the modern name; this chapter only calls it P3. The book's §16.4 gives a separate, more detailed Einstein equivalence principle. Chapter 10 §10.5 revisits freely falling frames.

**Where:** DIV §9.4 p.163 (pdf 178)

### Equivalence of acceleration and gravitation

*principle · core* · also: principle of equivalence P4, uniform acceleration equivalent to a uniform gravitational field

Within a small enough region, a reference frame undergoing linear acceleration with respect to a special-relativistic inertial frame cannot be told apart from a frame held stationary in a gravitational field.

**How introduced:** Obtained by comparing the inertial force ma of (9.3) with the weight mg, both proportional to the body's mass; stated as P4 and illustrated by lift Cases 1 and 3.

**Prerequisites:** Inertial force, Uniformly accelerated reference frame

**Where:** DIV §9.4 p.163 (pdf 178)

### Gravity as an inertial effect

*historical-idea · developed* · also: gravitation as a frame effect, coincidence of mass-proportional forces

Einstein's reinterpretation of gravity as a force of the same kind as inertial forces, arising because we describe motion from frames that are not freely falling.

**How introduced:** Motivated by the 'other coincidence': of all forces only inertial forces and gravity scale with the mass of the body acted on, suggesting a common origin.

**Prerequisites:** Inertial force, Universality of free fall

**Where:** DIV §9.4 p.163 (pdf 178)

### Einstein lift thought experiment

*experiment-or-observation · core* · also: lift experiments, Einstein elevator, Gedankenexperiment

An observer in a closed windowless room performs local mechanical experiments to determine its state of motion in four situations: accelerating rocket far from masses, coasting rocket, at rest on Earth, and falling freely down an evacuated shaft. A released body falls with g in the first and third, and floats in the second and fourth, so these pairs are indistinguishable.

**How introduced:** Presented after P3 and P4 as their vivid illustration with a matched set of cartoons (Figs. 9.9-9.12), then used as the springboard to curved space-time. Chapter 10 returns to the same four cases with two released bodies to expose tidal (non-local) differences.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein equivalence principle (local form), Equivalence of acceleration and gravitation

**Where:** DIV §9.4 p.163 (pdf 178); DIV §9.4 p.164 (pdf 179)

### Christoffel symbols as inertial forces

*technique · core* · also: connection terms as force terms, metric connection in non-inertial coordinates

Writing the free-particle equation d^2x^a/dtau^2 = 0 in non-Minkowski coordinates introduces connection terms Gamma^a_bc (dx^b/dtau)(dx^c/dtau); these are exactly the inertial forces of the non-inertial description, even though the metric remains flat.

**How introduced:** Brief bridging argument at the end of §9.4 which identifies 'using a non-inertial frame' with 'using general coordinates' and reads the extra geodesic terms as forces, recalling Chapter 6's metric connection and metric geodesics.

**Prerequisites:** Inertial force, Metric connection, Metric geodesic equation

$$
\frac{d^2x^a}{d\tau^2} + \Gamma^a_{bc}\frac{dx^b}{d\tau}\frac{dx^c}{d\tau} = 0
$$

**Where:** DIV §9.4 p.164 (pdf 179)

### Curved space-time from the equivalence principle

*principle · core* · also: non-flat metric, gravity as curvature

Because the equivalence principle puts gravitational forces in the same connection terms as inertial ones, and genuine gravity must be distinguishable from a mere change of coordinates in flat space-time, the connection must be the metric connection of a non-flat metric.

**How introduced:** Presented as the 'simplest generalization': keep Gamma as a metric connection but drop flatness; justified by the need to separate gravitational from purely coordinate effects.

**Prerequisites:** Christoffel symbols as inertial forces, Equivalence of acceleration and gravitation, Metric flatness

**Where:** DIV §9.4 p.164 (pdf 179)

### Metric as gravitational potential

*principle · core* · also: g_ab as potentials

If the connection coefficients are interpreted as gravitational force terms, then the metric components from which they are built by first derivatives play the role of gravitational potentials, generalizing the Newtonian phi.

**How introduced:** Direct structural analogy: Newtonian force is a first derivative of phi, and Gamma^a_bc is a first derivative of g_ab.

**Prerequisites:** Curved space-time from the equivalence principle, Newtonian gravitational potential

**Where:** DIV §9.4 p.164 (pdf 179)

### Second-order field equations for the metric

*principle · introduced* · also: field equations as second-order PDEs in g_ab

The expectation that the gravitational field equations of GR, like Poisson's equation for phi, are second-order partial differential equations for the potentials g_ab.

**How introduced:** Stated as the remaining task at the end of §9.4, pointing forward to Chapter 10.

**Prerequisites:** Metric as gravitational potential

**Where:** DIV §9.4 p.164 (pdf 179)

### Principle of general relativity

*principle · developed* · also: all observers are equivalent

All observers, inertial or not, are equivalent in their ability to discover the laws of physics.

**How introduced:** Framed as the logical completion of the special principle of relativity: since we live on a non-inertial Earth, the laws must be discoverable from non-inertial frames too.

**Prerequisites:** Principle of special relativity, Inertial frame

**Where:** DIV §9.5 p.165 (pdf 180)

### Principle of general covariance

*principle · core* · also: general covariance, principle of covariance

The equations of physics should be expressible in tensorial form, so that they hold in every coordinate system.

**How introduced:** Reached by identifying observers with coordinate systems: curved space-time has no canonical coordinates, so the content of 'all observers are equivalent' becomes invariance of the equations under coordinate transformations. Hedged with two caveats: symmetry-adapted coordinates are still sensible, and any theory can be written tensorially (a point recognized only after GR).

**Prerequisites:** Principle of general relativity, Tensor equation, Canonical coordinate system

**Notes:** The book attributes the 'empty' view to Bondi and Fock and the opposite strong view to Anderson.

**Where:** DIV §9.5 p.165 (pdf 180)

### Canonical coordinate system

*definition · introduced* · also: preferred coordinates, Minkowski coordinates

A coordinate system singled out by the geometry itself, such as Minkowski coordinates in flat space-time, which exist globally because the metric is flat and its connection integrable; a generic curved space-time has none.

**How introduced:** Used to explain why special relativity privileges inertial frames and why that privilege lapses in curved space-time, equated with the absence of global inertial observers.

**Prerequisites:** Metric flatness

**Where:** DIV §9.5 p.165 (pdf 180)

### Tensor equation

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: tensorial form

An equation between tensors of the same type, which, if true in one coordinate system, is true in all.

**How introduced:** Invoked as the mathematical content of general covariance and the reason for the Part B digression into tensors.

**Where:** DIV §9.5 p.165 (pdf 180)

### Principle of minimal gravitational coupling

*principle · core* · also: minimal coupling, comma-goes-to-semicolon rule, simplicity principle

When a special-relativistic law is carried over to curved space-time, add no terms that contain the curvature tensor explicitly. In practice this means writing the law in tensor form (partial derivatives become covariant derivatives, eta_ab becomes g_ab) and stopping there. The book's formal statement is only the no-curvature clause; the derivative-replacement recipe is implied by its example.

**How introduced:** Motivated by the gap left by the earlier principles (they do not tell you how to port a special-relativistic equation), presented as Occam's razor, and illustrated with the conservation law and a rival equation carrying a curvature term that vanishes in flat space-time. Candidly described as implicit in Einstein, vague, and ambiguous.

**Prerequisites:** Principle of general covariance, Riemann curvature tensor, Covariant derivative

$$
\partial_b T^{ab}=0 \;\to\; \nabla_b T^{ab}=0
$$

**Notes:** The 'comma-goes-to-semicolon' alias is standard elsewhere, not used in this chapter.

**Where:** DIV §9.6 p.165 (pdf 180); DIV §9.6 p.166 (pdf 181)

### Energy-momentum conservation law

*law · mention* · also: divergence-free energy-momentum tensor

The statement that the energy-momentum tensor has vanishing divergence: partial_b T^ab = 0 in Minkowski coordinates, nabla_b T^ab = 0 in curved space-time.

**How introduced:** Used only as a preview example for minimal coupling; developed properly in Chapter 12.

**Prerequisites:** Covariant derivative

$$
\partial_b T^{ab} = 0
$$

$$
\nabla_b T^{ab} = 0
$$

**Where:** DIV §9.6 p.166 (pdf 181)

### Riemann curvature tensor

*mathematical-object · mention* · also: curvature tensor, R^a_bcd

The tensor built from the connection and its derivatives whose vanishing characterizes a flat metric; any term containing it disappears in special relativity.

**How introduced:** Relied on (from Chapter 6) to show why curvature terms can be added invisibly to a flat-space law, which is what minimal coupling forbids.

**Prerequisites:** Metric connection

**Where:** DIV §9.6 p.166 (pdf 181)

### Correspondence principle

*principle · core* · also: reduction to earlier theories

A new theory must agree with established earlier theories within their domains of validity: GR must reduce to special relativity when gravity is absent and to Newtonian gravity for weak fields and speeds small compared with light.

**How introduced:** Set in the book's modelling philosophy (every model has a range of validity) and summarized by a box-and-arrow diagram (Fig. 9.13) whose arrows point toward greater specialization.

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian limit

**Where:** DIV §9.7 p.166 (pdf 181)

### Newtonian limit

*technique · introduced* · also: weak-field slow-motion limit, Newtonian correspondence

The regime of weak gravitational fields and speeds much less than c in which GR must reproduce Newtonian gravitation.

**How introduced:** Named here as one arm of the correspondence principle; carried out explicitly in Chapter 10 §10.6.

**Where:** DIV §9.7 p.166 (pdf 181)

### Tidal effects

*phenomenon · mention* · also: non-local gravitational effects, field inhomogeneity

Relative accelerations of nearby freely falling bodies due to spatial variation of the gravitational field, which cannot be transformed away by free fall and set the size limit of a local inertial frame.

**How introduced:** Not developed in the text; implicit in the definition of 'locally' and raised by Exercise 9.6 (a falling sphere of particles changes shape), with full treatment deferred to Chapter 10.

**Prerequisites:** Local inertial frame

**Where:** DIV §9.4 p.163 (pdf 178); DIV p.168 (pdf 183)

## Key equations

### (9.1) Transformation to a uniformly accelerated frame · supporting · DIV §9.2 p.154 (pdf 169)

$$
x = x' + s, \quad y = y', \quad z = z', \quad t = t'
$$

Newtonian relation between an inertial frame S and a frame S' displaced along x by s(t) with clocks synchronized at coincidence.

**Symbols:** s(t): position of the origin of S' in S, with \ddot s = a

### (9.2) Acceleration transformation · derivation-step · DIV §9.2 p.154 (pdf 169)

$$
\dot x = \dot x' + \dot s, \qquad \ddot x = \ddot x' + \ddot s = \ddot x' + a
$$

Velocities and accelerations measured in S and S' differ by those of the moving origin. (The first relation is garbled in the OCR as \dot x = \dot x + \dot s.)

**Symbols:** dots: time derivatives, t = t'

### Newton's second law viewed from S' · derivation-step · DIV §9.2 p.154 (pdf 169)

$$
F = m\ddot x = m\ddot x' + ma
$$

Substitution of (9.2) into F = m\ddot x.

### (9.3) Inertial force in a linearly accelerated frame · central · DIV §9.2 p.155 (pdf 170)

$$
F - ma = m\ddot x'
$$

An accelerated observer who insists on 'force equals mass times my acceleration' must include an extra force -ma, proportional to the body's mass.

**Symbols:** F: applied force; m: mass; a: acceleration of S' relative to S

### Newtonian point-mass potential (Machian argument) · supporting · DIV §9.2 p.158 (pdf 173)

$$
\phi = -\frac{Gm}{r}
$$

Used to argue that G relates a body's mass (Mach: set by the universe) to its potential, so G encodes cosmic information.

**Symbols:** G: Newton's constant

### (9.4) Newton's second law with inertial mass · supporting · DIV §9.3 p.159 (pdf 174)

$$
\mathbf F = \frac{d(m^{I}\mathbf v)}{dt}
$$

Defines inertial mass as the quantity whose momentum changes in response to force.

**Symbols:** m^I: inertial mass

### (9.5) · supporting · DIV §9.3 p.159 (pdf 174)

$$
\mathbf F = m^{I}\mathbf a
$$

Second law for constant inertial mass.

### (9.6) Force on a passive gravitational mass · central · DIV §9.3 p.159 (pdf 174)

$$
\mathbf F = -m^{P}\,\mathrm{grad}\,\phi
$$

Defines passive gravitational mass as the response coefficient to a gravitational potential gradient.

**Symbols:** m^P: passive gravitational mass; phi: gravitational potential

### (9.7) Potential of an active gravitational mass · central · DIV §9.3 p.160 (pdf 175)

$$
\phi = -\frac{G m^{A}}{r}
$$

Defines active gravitational mass as the source strength of the potential.

**Symbols:** m^A: active gravitational mass; r: distance from the source

### Two dropped bodies · derivation-step · DIV §9.3 p.160 (pdf 175)

$$
m_1^{I}\mathbf a_1 = -m_1^{P}\,\mathrm{grad}\,\phi, \qquad m_2^{I}\mathbf a_2 = -m_2^{P}\,\mathrm{grad}\,\phi
$$

Equations of motion for two bodies released at the same place.

### Universal ratio of inertial to passive mass · derivation-step · DIV §9.3 p.160 (pdf 175)

$$
\mathbf a_1 = \mathbf a_2 \;\Rightarrow\; m_1^{I}/m_1^{P} = m_2^{I}/m_2^{P} = \alpha
$$

Equal accelerations force the same ratio for every body; choosing units sets alpha = 1.

**Symbols:** alpha: universal constant

### (9.8) Equality of inertial and passive gravitational mass · central · DIV §9.3 p.161 (pdf 176)

$$
m^{I} = m^{P}
$$

Newtonian encoding of universality of free fall; tested to 1 part in 10^12.

### Mutual potentials and forces of two bodies · derivation-step · DIV §9.3 p.161 (pdf 176)

$$
\phi_1 = -\frac{Gm_1^{A}}{r},\ \phi_2 = -\frac{Gm_2^{A}}{r};\qquad \mathbf F_1 = -m_1^{P}\,\mathrm{grad}_Q\phi_2,\ \mathbf F_2 = -m_2^{P}\,\mathrm{grad}_R\phi_1
$$

Each body feels the potential produced by the other.

**Symbols:** Q, R: positions of bodies 1 and 2

### Explicit two-body forces · derivation-step · DIV §9.3 p.162 (pdf 177)

$$
\mathrm{grad}_R = \hat{\mathbf r}\,\partial_r = -\mathrm{grad}_Q, \qquad \mathbf F_1 = \frac{G m_1^{P} m_2^{A}}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf r}, \quad \mathbf F_2 = -\frac{G m_2^{P} m_1^{A}}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf r}
$$

With Q at the origin the two gradients are opposite, giving the attractive forces in terms of passive and active masses.

**Symbols:** \hat r: unit vector from Q to R

### (9.9) Equality of passive and active gravitational mass · central · DIV §9.3 p.162 (pdf 177)

$$
\mathbf F_1 = -\mathbf F_2 \;\Rightarrow\; m_1^{P}/m_1^{A} = m_2^{P}/m_2^{A}\;\Rightarrow\; m^{P} = m^{A}
$$

Newton's third law makes the passive-to-active ratio universal; units set it to one.

### Single Newtonian mass · supporting · DIV §9.3 p.162 (pdf 177)

$$
m = m^{I} = m^{P} = m^{A}
$$

Why Newtonian theory can speak of just 'the mass' of a body.

### Free test particle in Minkowski coordinates · supporting · DIV §9.4 p.164 (pdf 179)

$$
\frac{d^2 x^a}{d\tau^2} = 0
$$

Straight-line motion of a free particle in an inertial frame of special relativity.

**Symbols:** tau: proper time; x^a Minkowski coordinates

### Geodesic equation in general coordinates · central · DIV §9.4 p.164 (pdf 179)

$$
\frac{d^2 x^a}{d\tau^2} + \Gamma^a_{bc}\frac{dx^b}{d\tau}\frac{dx^c}{d\tau} = 0
$$

Same free motion described in non-inertial coordinates; the connection terms are the inertial forces. Promoted by the equivalence principle to include gravity when g_ab is non-flat.

**Symbols:** Gamma^a_bc: metric connection of g_ab

### (9.10) Special-relativistic conservation law · supporting · DIV §9.6 p.166 (pdf 181)

$$
\partial_b T^{ab} = 0
$$

Energy-momentum conservation in Minkowski coordinates (developed in Chapter 12).

**Symbols:** T^{ab}: energy-momentum tensor

### (9.11) Minimally coupled conservation law · central · DIV §9.6 p.166 (pdf 181)

$$
\nabla_b T^{ab} = 0
$$

Simplest tensorial generalization of (9.10) to curved space-time.

**Symbols:** nabla: covariant derivative

### (9.12) Non-minimal rival generalization · supporting · DIV §9.6 p.166 (pdf 181)

$$
\nabla_b T^{ab} + g^{bc} R^{a}{}_{bcd} \nabla_e T^{cd} = 0
$$

An alternative equation that also reduces to (9.10) in flat space-time because the Riemann tensor vanishes there; minimal coupling excludes it. As transcribed, index c appears three times and e is free, so the printed index structure appears defective; the point survives with any well-formed curvature term, e.g. \lambda R_{cd}\nabla^a T^{cd}.

**Symbols:** R^a_{bcd}: Riemann tensor

## Worked examples

### Example · intro · DIV §9.2 p.154 (pdf 169)

**Problem:** Find the form of Newton's second law for a particle moving along x as described by an observer in a frame accelerating uniformly along x relative to an inertial frame.

**Method:** Write the Galilean-style transformation with displacement s(t), differentiate twice using t = t', substitute into F = m xddot and move ma to the force side.

**Key insight:** The non-inertial observer sees a reduced effective force F - ma; the extra term is proportional to the particle's mass, just like gravity.

**Result:** F - ma = m\ddot x' (9.3)

**Concepts:** Inertial force, Uniformly accelerated reference frame

### Example · intro · DIV §9.3 p.160 (pdf 175)

**Problem:** Show that inertial mass and passive gravitational mass are proportional with the same constant for all bodies.

**Method:** Two bodies released at the same point obey m_i^I a_i = -m_i^P grad phi; equate the observed accelerations and take ratios; repeat for any pair; absorb the universal constant into units.

**Key insight:** Universality of free fall is precisely the statement that m^I/m^P is universal; the equality is empirical, not logically required.

**Result:** m^I = m^P (9.8)

**Concepts:** Equality of inertial and passive gravitational mass, Universality of free fall

### Example · intro · DIV §9.3 p.161 (pdf 176)

**Problem:** Show that passive and active gravitational masses are proportional with a universal constant.

**Method:** Write each body's potential with its active mass and the force on the other with its passive mass, evaluate opposite gradients with Q at the origin, and impose Newton's third law F_1 = -F_2.

**Key insight:** Because gravity cannot be shielded, each body is simultaneously source and receiver; action-reaction then ties the two roles together.

**Result:** m^P = m^A (9.9), hence m = m^I = m^P = m^A

**Concepts:** Equality of passive and active gravitational mass, Active gravitational mass, Passive gravitational mass

### Example · intro · DIV §9.4 p.164 (pdf 179)

**Problem:** Determine what an observer sealed in a lift can learn about its state of motion from releasing a body in four situations (accelerating rocket, coasting rocket, on Earth, freely falling).

**Method:** Describe the released body's motion relative to the lift in each case and group cases by identical observations.

**Key insight:** Cases 1 and 3 match (acceleration mimics gravity, P4); cases 2 and 4 match (free fall erases gravity, P3). Local mechanics cannot reveal the difference.

**Result:** Released body falls with g in Cases 1 and 3; stays at rest in Cases 2 and 4.

**Concepts:** Einstein lift thought experiment, Equivalence of acceleration and gravitation, Einstein equivalence principle (local form)

### Example · standard · DIV §9.4 p.164 (pdf 179)

**Problem:** Argue from the equivalence principle that gravity requires a non-flat metric that plays the role of potential.

**Method:** Start from d^2x^a/dtau^2 = 0 in Minkowski coordinates; change to general coordinates to obtain Christoffel terms that represent inertial forces; demand by equivalence that gravity also appear there; note that a flat metric would make gravity indistinguishable from a coordinate change; take Gamma to be the metric connection of a non-flat g_ab; interpret g_ab as potentials by analogy with phi and expect second-order field equations.

**Key insight:** Inertial forces are coordinate artefacts of a flat connection; genuine gravity must be the same kind of term but not removable globally, which is curvature.

**Result:** Space-time is curved; g_ab are the gravitational potentials; field equations should be second-order PDEs in g_ab.

**Concepts:** Christoffel symbols as inertial forces, Curved space-time from the equivalence principle, Metric as gravitational potential

### Example · standard · DIV §9.6 p.166 (pdf 181)

**Problem:** Generalize the special-relativistic energy-momentum conservation law to curved space-time.

**Method:** Replace the partial derivative by a covariant derivative to get a tensor equation; observe that adding a term containing the Riemann tensor gives another equation with the same flat-space limit; select the one with no explicit curvature.

**Key insight:** General covariance plus the correspondence limit does not fix a unique generalization; a simplicity principle is needed to exclude curvature couplings.

**Result:** \nabla_b T^{ab} = 0 (9.11)

**Concepts:** Principle of minimal gravitational coupling, Energy-momentum conservation law, Riemann curvature tensor


## Analogies and intuitions

### A carried bucket of water as an inertial-frame detector: flat surface means inertial, curved or tilted surface means rotating or accelerating. → Inertial frame · strong · DIV §9.2 p.156 (pdf 171)

Turns Newton's abstract absolute space into an operational test every observer can perform.

**Where it breaks down:** The test works only inside Newtonian mechanics, where gravity counts as an ordinary force. A bucket resting on the Earth's surface shows a flat surface, yet by P3/P4 that frame is not locally inertial. A freely falling bucket, which is locally inertial, has no 'level' surface at all: the water simply floats. After §9.4 the operational test for a local inertial frame is 'released test particles stay put', not 'water is flat'.

**App idea:** Give the learner a virtual bucket they can carry into any simulation (rotating platform, accelerating car, free-falling lift) to test frames.

### Christoffel symbols are to the metric what the Newtonian force is to the potential phi. → Metric as gravitational potential · strong · DIV §9.4 p.164 (pdf 179)

Because Gamma is built from first derivatives of g_ab, reading Gamma as force makes g_ab the potential, and suggests second-order field equations like Poisson's.

**Where it breaks down:** Gamma is not a tensor and can be set to zero at a point; it is not a local physical force field. The invariant gravitational content is curvature (second derivatives), which the analogy does not capture.

**App idea:** Side-by-side panels: phi(x) with -grad phi arrows, and g_00(x) for a weak field with the corresponding Gamma^i_00 arrows, showing they coincide in the Newtonian limit.

### Inertial forces are as real as their effects: they black out pilots and burst flywheels. → Inertial force · useful · DIV §9.2 p.155 (pdf 170)

Motivates asking for a physical origin of inertial forces, and prepares the reader to accept gravity as an inertial force.

**Where it breaks down:** Can mislead learners into thinking inertial forces are interactions with a source in the same sense as electromagnetism; in modern GR language they reflect the choice of frame relative to geodesic motion.

### Distant stars versus the bucket: a tiny nearby mass matters less than an enormous distant one. → Mach's principle · useful · DIV §9.2 p.156 (pdf 171)

Explains why the bucket walls barely influence the water's inertia while the far-away but massive fixed stars dominate.

**Where it breaks down:** Purely qualitative; Mach supplies no force law, so the relative weights of near and far matter are not computed.

### Coincidences in a theory are clues that something deeper is going on. → Gravity as an inertial effect · strong · DIV §9.2 p.157 (pdf 172)

Recurring methodological refrain tying together the stars-inertial-frame agreement, m^I = m^P, and the mass-proportionality of both inertial and gravitational forces.

**Where it breaks down:** A heuristic, not a proof; some coincidences are just coincidences.

**App idea:** A 'coincidence board' the tutor fills in as the chapter progresses, each card flipping to reveal how GR explains it.

### Minimal coupling as Occam's razor. → Principle of minimal gravitational coupling · useful · DIV §9.6 p.165 (pdf 180)

Frames the rule as a preference for the simplest law consistent with covariance and the flat-space limit.

**Where it breaks down:** The book itself warns the rule is vague and ambiguous; for example, ordering of covariant derivatives can matter for fields with second derivatives.

### Arrows of specialization in a box diagram of theories. → Correspondence principle · useful · DIV §9.7 p.166 (pdf 181)

Shows GR at the apex with two independent simplifications that commute down to Newtonian mechanics.

**Where it breaks down:** Limits are not always as clean as arrows suggest (e.g. the c to infinity limit changes the causal structure qualitatively).

**App idea:** Theory-space map with sliders for field strength and speed (see Fig. 9.13 redesign).


## Misconceptions addressed

### Inertial (fictitious) forces are mere bookkeeping with no physical significance. · DIV §9.2 p.155 (pdf 170)

**Correction:** They produce real effects and, crucially, share the defining feature of gravity (proportionality to mass), which is what lets Einstein treat gravity as an inertial effect.

**Why tempting:** Introductory mechanics teaches that they disappear when one returns to an inertial frame.

### Mass is a single property, so equality of inertial and gravitational mass is automatic. · DIV §9.3 p.159 (pdf 174)

**Correction:** Newtonian theory has three conceptually distinct masses; their equality follows only from experimental facts (universality of free fall) plus the third law.

**Why tempting:** Textbooks write a single m everywhere and the equality holds to extraordinary precision.

### Heavier objects fall faster (the feather falls slower than the hammer because it is lighter). · DIV §9.3 p.160 (pdf 175)

**Correction:** The difference on Earth is air resistance; without air, as on the Moon, all bodies share the same acceleration.

**Why tempting:** Everyday experience in air.

### Since the equality of inertial and gravitational mass is verified, it must be a theorem of Newtonian mechanics. · DIV §9.4 p.162 (pdf 177)

**Correction:** In Newtonian theory it is an unexplained coincidence that the theory could survive losing; in GR it is foundational and its failure would refute the theory.

**Why tempting:** Well-tested facts feel like logical necessities.

### The equivalence principle says gravity and acceleration are identical everywhere, so gravity can be globally transformed away. · DIV §9.4 p.163 (pdf 178)

**Correction:** The equivalence holds only locally, over regions where variations of the field are unobservable; tidal effects remain (Exercise 9.6, Chapter 10).

**Why tempting:** The lift story is usually told without emphasizing the lift's size.

### Nonzero Christoffel symbols mean a gravitational field is present. · DIV §9.4 p.164 (pdf 179)

**Correction:** Christoffel symbols of a flat metric in non-inertial coordinates are non-zero and represent pure inertial forces; gravity requires the metric itself to be non-flat.

**Why tempting:** The chapter identifies Gamma with gravitational force terms, which can be over-read.

### General covariance means all coordinate systems are equally convenient, so preferred coordinates have no place in GR. · DIV §9.5 p.165 (pdf 180)

**Correction:** The principle requires tensorial equations, not that every chart is equally useful; symmetry-adapted coordinates are routinely the best choice.

**Why tempting:** The slogan 'any coordinate system will do'.

### Writing equations in tensor form is by itself a strong physical constraint that singles out GR. · DIV §9.5 p.165 (pdf 180)

**Correction:** Almost any theory can be rewritten tensorially; covariance acquires content only alongside other assumptions (no absolute objects, minimal coupling, simplicity).

**Why tempting:** Einstein named his theory after the principle and it guided him historically.

### General relativity fully realizes Mach's principle. · DIV §9.2 p.158 (pdf 173)

**Correction:** The book says only that Einstein partly succeeded in building Mach's ideas into GR, and it mentions Brans-Dicke theory as a rival claiming to be more Machian. Supplementary, not stated in this chapter: GR has vacuum solutions with definite geometry (Minkowski, Schwarzschild, gravitational waves), which conflict with M2 and M3, while frame dragging by rotating masses is a partly Machian effect.

**Why tempting:** Mach was a major inspiration and the principle is often quoted in popular accounts of GR.

### Minimal coupling gives a unique, unambiguous curved-space version of every special-relativistic law. · DIV §9.6 p.166 (pdf 181)

**Correction:** Different tensorial generalizations can share the same flat limit; minimal coupling is a simplicity preference that the book calls vague and ambiguous.

**Why tempting:** It is often taught as a mechanical 'comma to semicolon' substitution.

### The 'strong' equivalence principle is the statement that free fall is independent of mass and composition. · DIV §9.4 p.162 (pdf 177)

**Correction:** That is this chapter's labelling of P1, but in the book's own §16.4 and in most modern literature universality of free fall is the weak equivalence principle, while the strong principle extends local equivalence to self-gravitating bodies and all laws of physics.

**Why tempting:** Reading this chapter in isolation, P1 is explicitly called strong and P2 weak.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Newton's rotating bucket · DIV §9.2 p.155 (pdf 170)

**Setup:** A bucket of water on a twisted rope is released and passes through four phases: bucket spinning with flat water, both spinning with concave water, bucket stopped while water still spins and stays concave, both at rest with flat water.

**Lesson:** Surface curvature is tied to rotation of the water itself, not relative motion with the bucket; Newton took this as evidence for absolute rotation.

**App idea:** Timeline-scrubbable 3D bucket with angular-velocity plots (see Fig. 9.2 redesign).

### Fixed bucket in a rotating universe · DIV §9.2 p.156 (pdf 171)

**Setup:** Hold the bucket and water still while the entire system of fixed stars rotates around it.

**Lesson:** Mach predicts a concave surface because only relative rotation matters; Newton predicts a flat one. The counterfactual isolates the difference between the two views.

**App idea:** Star-dome toggle in the bucket simulation asking the learner to commit to a prediction before seeing both theories' outcomes side by side.

### Lone particle in an empty universe · DIV §9.2 p.157 (pdf 172)

**Setup:** A single particle exists in an otherwise empty universe; then a second particle of tiny mass is added.

**Lesson:** On Machian grounds the lone particle has no inertia, and the tiny newcomer can restore only a tiny amount, so inertia must be fixed by the total mass and distribution of the universe.

### Anisotropic universe · DIV §9.2 p.157 (pdf 172)

**Setup:** Imagine most of the universe's matter concentrated in one direction and accelerate a body in different directions.

**Lesson:** Machian inertia would be direction dependent; the Hughes-Drever null result shows either Mach is wrong or the universe is extremely isotropic.

**App idea:** Drag-the-matter toy model (see Fig. 9.5 redesign).

### Einstein's falling observer ('happiest thought') · DIV §9.4 p.163 (pdf 178)

**Setup:** A person falls freely from the roof of a house.

**Lesson:** For the falling observer there is locally no gravitational field: objects released beside them float along.

**App idea:** First-person free-fall scene where dropped keys and a coin hover; switching to the ground camera shows both following identical parabolas.

### Einstein's lift experiments · DIV §9.4 p.164 (pdf 179)

**Setup:** A windowless room in four states: accelerating rocket in deep space, coasting rocket, at rest on Earth, falling freely in an evacuated shaft; the occupant releases a body.

**Lesson:** Cases 1 and 3 cannot be told apart locally (P4); Cases 2 and 4 cannot be told apart (P3). This leads to gravity as inertial force and then to curved space-time.

**App idea:** Guess-your-situation lift game with a lift-size slider that reveals tidal effects (see Fig. 9.9 redesign).

### Galileo's Pisa drop · DIV §9.3 p.160 (pdf 175)

**Setup:** Two bodies of different mass (and composition) are released together from the top of the Leaning Tower.

**Lesson:** Neglecting air resistance they land together, the empirical basis for universality of free fall and m^I = m^P.

**App idea:** Drop lab with per-object m^I/m^P dials (see Fig. 9.6 redesign).


## Applications and observations

- **Hughes-Drever test of isotropy of inertia** (experiment, DIV §9.2 p.158 (pdf 173)): Null result for direction dependence of inertial mass, interpreted as either refuting Mach's principle or showing the universe is highly isotropic. Key numbers: Isotropy to at least 1 part in 10^18; around 1960.
- **Cosmic microwave background isotropy** (observation, DIV §9.2 p.158 (pdf 173)): Uniformity of the CMB strongly limits large-scale anisotropy of the universe, supporting the second reading of the Hughes-Drever result.
- **Earth rotation relative to local inertial frames and to the stars** (observation, DIV §9.2 p.157 (pdf 172)): A pendulum at the North Pole turns relative to the ground once per sidereal rotation, the same period measured relative to the fixed stars, within experimental error. Key numbers: Two periods agree to experimental accuracy (about one sidereal day).
- **Tests of equality of inertial and passive gravitational mass** (experiment, DIV §9.3 p.161 (pdf 176)): Eotvos-type torsion balance experiments (detailed in §16.3) verify m^I = m^P. Key numbers: 1 part in 10^12 as quoted here.
- **Hammer and feather on the Moon** (experiment, DIV §9.3 p.160 (pdf 175)): Airless demonstration that bodies of very different mass and shape fall together.
- **Physiological and engineering effects of inertial forces** (observation, DIV §9.2 p.155 (pdf 170)): Large accelerations cause astronaut blackouts and centrifugal stresses can shatter flywheels, cited to argue inertial forces are physically real.

## Historical notes

- **Isaac Newton:** Postulated absolute space as an immovable background and proposed the rotating bucket to detect absolute rotation. — Provided the Newtonian answer to what defines inertial frames, the view Mach and Einstein reacted against. (DIV §9.2 p.155 (pdf 170))
- **George Berkeley, Ernst Mach (1893):** Mach, building on Berkeley's earlier relational ideas, argued that only relative motion is meaningful and that inertia arises from interaction with the rest of the matter in the universe. — Mach's principle strongly shaped Einstein's search for the field equations, although GR ends up only partly Machian. Date note: the book gives 1893, the year of the English edition of Mach's 'The Science of Mechanics'. His critique of absolute space was first published in German earlier (the 1883 'Die Mechanik'), so a learner may meet the earlier date elsewhere. (DIV §9.2 p.156 (pdf 171))
- **V. W. Hughes, R. W. P. Drever (c. 1960):** Independent experiments showed mass (inertia) is isotropic to at least one part in 10^18. — Among the most precise null experiments; constrains anisotropic-inertia versions of Mach's principle. (DIV §9.2 p.158 (pdf 173))
- **C. Brans, R. H. Dicke (1960s):** Proposed an alternative relativistic gravity theory claimed to be more fully Machian than GR. — Shows Mach's principle still motivating theory-building; revisited in Chapter 16 with PPN tests. (DIV §9.2 p.158 (pdf 173))
- **H. Bondi, J. Samuel:** Catalogued ten distinct formulations of Mach's principle. — Illustrates how imprecise the principle is and why the book restricts itself to three sample versions. (DIV §9.2 p.158 (pdf 173))
- **Galileo Galilei:** Credited with the Pisa demonstration that bodies of different weight fall together. — Empirical root of universality of free fall and the equivalence principle. (DIV §9.3 p.160 (pdf 175))
- **Apollo astronaut (unnamed in the book; David Scott, Apollo 15, 1971) (1971):** An astronaut on the Moon dropped a hammer and a feather together and they landed at the same time. — A vivid airless demonstration of universality of free fall. (DIV §9.3 p.160 (pdf 175))
- **Albert Einstein (1907):** Realized that a freely falling observer feels no gravitational field, calling it the happiest thought of his life. — Seed of the equivalence principle and hence of GR's geometric view of gravity. (DIV §9.4 p.163 (pdf 178))
- **Albert Einstein:** Spent much of his later career on unsuccessful mathematical attempts to unify gravitation and electromagnetism. — Used by the authors as a warning about relying on mathematical devices rather than physical principles. (DIV §9.1 p.153 (pdf 168))
- **H. Bondi, V. Fock, J. L. Anderson:** Disputed the content of the principle of covariance: Bondi and Fock regard it as empty, Anderson as nearly sufficient to derive GR. — Shows the principles are contested and motivates the book's attempt at precise formulations. (DIV §9.1 p.153 (pdf 168))

## Notation and conventions

- **m^I, m^P, m^A:** Superscripts I, P, A distinguish inertial, passive gravitational and active gravitational mass; subscripts label bodies. (DIV §9.3 p.159 (pdf 174))
- **Principle labels M1-M3, P1-P4, bucket phases B1-B4:** Competing formulations and experimental phases are enumerated with letter-number labels for later reference. — P1 is called the strong and P2 the weak form of the equivalence principle in this chapter; §16.4 uses 'weak equivalence principle' for universality of free fall, the modern convention. (DIV §9.4 p.162 (pdf 177))
- **Overdot:** Derivative with respect to Newtonian time t (equal to t' for the accelerated frame). (DIV §9.2 p.154 (pdf 169))
- **Standard configuration:** Frames S and S' with parallel axes, relative motion (here acceleration) along the common x-axis, clocks zeroed when origins coincide. — Carried over from Chapter 2. (DIV §9.2 p.154 (pdf 169))
- **Gamma^a_bc, g_ab, eta_ab:** Gamma^a_bc denotes the metric connection of g_ab; eta_ab the Minkowski metric in Minkowski coordinates; lower-case Latin indices run 0-3 and the metric has signature -2 (from Chapter 8). (DIV §9.4 p.164 (pdf 179))
- **tau:** Proper time along the particle worldline, used as the parameter in the geodesic equation. (DIV §9.4 p.164 (pdf 179))
- **partial_b vs nabla_b:** Partial derivative (valid as a tensor operation only in Minkowski coordinates) versus covariant derivative. — The comma/semicolon notation is not used in this chapter. (DIV §9.6 p.166 (pdf 181))
- **R^a_bcd:** Riemann curvature tensor with one upper and three lower indices. (DIV §9.6 p.166 (pdf 181))
- **grad_Q, r-hat:** Gradient taken with respect to the position of the indicated body; unit radial vector from Q to R. (DIV §9.3 p.162 (pdf 177))

## Teaching gems

### Collect Newtonian 'coincidences' (inertial forces scale with m; m^I = m^P; local inertial frames do not rotate relative to the stars) and treat each as a clue; GR promotes the free-fall coincidence into a principle that the theory cannot survive without. · DIV §9.4 p.162 (pdf 177)

**Why it works:** Gives learners a detective narrative and a methodological lesson about theory change, rather than presenting the equivalence principle as a bare postulate.

**App idea:** A 'coincidence board' of three cards that the tutor fills in during the lesson; each card flips at the end to show the GR explanation (geodesics, metric, local Lorentz frames).

### Split 'mass' into inertial, passive and active, then recover their equality from two experiments (free fall; action equals reaction). · DIV §9.3 p.159 (pdf 174)

**Why it works:** Makes the equality empirical and falsifiable, so learners appreciate why Eotvos-type precision matters and why GR must build it in.

**App idea:** Two-body mass ledger with six independent mass sliders; unequal ratios make objects fall at different rates or make an isolated pair self-accelerate.

### Hold the bucket still and spin the universe: a single counterfactual that separates absolute-space and Machian predictions. · DIV §9.2 p.156 (pdf 171)

**Why it works:** Converts a philosophical debate into a concrete prediction the learner can commit to.

**App idea:** Prediction poll inside the 3D bucket scene before revealing the Newtonian and Machian outcomes side by side, with a note on frame dragging as GR's partial Machian effect.

### Derive the inertial force -ma in two lines from x = x' + s and stress that, like gravity, it is proportional to the body's mass. · DIV §9.2 p.155 (pdf 170)

**Why it works:** The shared mass-proportionality is exactly what makes equivalence possible; seeing it emerge from simple algebra grounds P4.

**App idea:** Accelerating-cart split screen with mass slider: inertial-force arrow and weight arrow both scale with m while accelerations do not.

### The four lift cases arranged as two matched pairs (accelerating rocket vs Earth; coasting rocket vs free fall), each pair illustrating one equivalence statement. · DIV §9.4 p.164 (pdf 179)

**Why it works:** The pairing structure makes P3 and P4 memorable and separable, and the same set of scenes can be reused in Chapter 10 with two bodies to show where equivalence breaks.

**App idea:** Guess-your-situation lift game with a lift-size slider that turns on tidal convergence of two dropped balls.

### Read the extra Christoffel terms of the geodesic equation in curvilinear coordinates as inertial forces, then insist gravity lives in the same terms; flatness would make gravity a coordinate illusion, so the metric must curve and becomes the potential. · DIV §9.4 p.164 (pdf 179)

**Why it works:** A compact bridge from physics (forces) to geometry (connections, metrics) using a structure the learner already met in Chapter 6.

**App idea:** Polar-coordinate free particle in flat space: plot the straight path and overlay the Gamma-term 'centrifugal' accelerations in (r, phi) components; a toggle switches on a weak gravitational field whose Gamma terms cannot be removed by any coordinate change over an extended region.

### Illustrate minimal coupling by writing down a second, curvature-containing equation that has the same flat-space limit. · DIV §9.6 p.166 (pdf 181)

**Why it works:** Shows concretely why covariance plus correspondence underdetermine physics and why a simplicity rule is needed.

**App idea:** Equation builder: learner assembles candidate curved-space laws from tokens (nabla, T, R, g); a checker reports tensoriality and the flat limit, and flags which candidates minimal coupling allows.

### Correspondence principle drawn as a square of theories with specialization arrows. · DIV §9.7 p.166 (pdf 181)

**Why it works:** Gives a durable mental map of which approximations lead where, and invites the question of what each arrow means quantitatively.

**App idea:** Theory-space map with sliders for GM/rc^2 and v/c and preset physical systems placed on it.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **The chapter labels universality of free fall (P1) the 'strong' equivalence principle and universal coupling (P2) the 'weak' one, which is reversed relative to modern usage and to the same book's §16.4.** — Learners who read other sources or later chapters will be confused about what the weak and strong equivalence principles assert. *Suggestion:* In the app, avoid the book's strong/weak labels; use descriptive names (universality of free fall = WEP; Einstein EP = WEP + local Lorentz and position invariance; SEP extends to self-gravitating bodies) and add a glossary note mapping P1-P4.
- **The step from 'Christoffel terms are inertial forces' to 'the metric must be non-flat' is compressed into a few sentences, with no explicit criterion for distinguishing gravity from coordinate effects.** — Learners may conclude that any non-zero Gamma means gravity, or not see why curvature (not Gamma) is the invariant signal. *Suggestion:* Make explicit that Gamma can always be set to zero at a point (local inertial frame) but cannot be removed over a region when the Riemann tensor is non-zero; connect to tidal effects and preview Chapter 10.
- **In equation (9.12) as it appears in the reading copy, the index c appears three times and e is left free, so the added term is not a valid tensor expression. Even the partial contraction g^{bc}R^a_{bcd} reduces to a Ricci tensor, up to sign.** — Careful readers checking index balance may lose confidence or copy an ill-formed expression. *Suggestion:* Present a well-formed non-minimal example such as nabla_b T^{ab} + lambda R_{cd} nabla^a T^{cd} = 0, and verify the printed book before quoting (9.12).
- **Tidal effects and the finite size of 'local' regions are mentioned only through the definition of locality and Exercise 9.6.** — Learners may overstate the equivalence principle and think gravity is entirely a frame effect. *Suggestion:* Pair the lift experiments immediately with a large-lift demo showing converging dropped balls, stating that this residual is what curvature measures.
- **Mach's principle is discussed qualitatively with no connection to what GR actually does (e.g. frame dragging, Lense-Thirring, or matter-free solutions violating M2), and M1-M3 are left unchecked in this chapter.** — Learners may leave thinking Mach's principle is either fully built into GR or irrelevant. *Suggestion:* Add a short forward note: vacuum solutions (Minkowski, Schwarzschild, gravitational waves) contradict M2/M3, while frame dragging near rotating masses is a partially Machian effect (Kerr chapter, Gravity Probe B).
- **The equivalence principle arguments use Newtonian language (forces, g, potentials) without discussing that in relativity light and clocks are also affected; Exercise 9.8 hints at light bending but the text does not mention gravitational redshift.** — The equivalence principle may seem to be only about mechanics. *Suggestion:* Add an EP-based light-bending and clock-rate demonstration in the lift game (light pulse across an accelerating lift; clocks at floor and ceiling).
- **The OCR of (9.2) drops the prime in the velocity relation, and the Fig. 9.7 image export mislabels the two-body image; the concave water surface in Fig. 9.2 is barely visible at small size.** — Content pipelines built from the reading copy may propagate errors. *Suggestion:* Use the corrected forms recorded in this dossier; redraw the bucket with an exaggerated paraboloid.
- **The principle of general covariance is stated without discussing what extra condition (absence of prior geometry or absolute objects) gives it physical bite.** — Learners may be unable to answer the emptiness objection the text raises. *Suggestion:* Supplement with a contrast: special relativity written covariantly still contains a fixed flat metric (prior geometry), while GR makes g_ab dynamical.

## Tutor notes

- Open with a question the learner can answer from experience: 'You are in a windowless room and feel pressed to the floor. Can you tell whether you are on Earth or in an accelerating rocket?' Then ask how they would find out, and steer toward local versus non-local experiments.
- Alternative opener for mechanically minded learners: 'Why does the water in a spinning bucket curve, and spinning relative to what?'
- Recommended order: accelerated frames and inertial forces, then bucket and Mach, then the three masses, then P1-P4 and the lifts, then the Gamma-as-force bridge to curved space-time, then covariance, minimal coupling and correspondence. If time is short, the Mach material can be condensed; the mass and equivalence material cannot.
- Check for understanding: ask the learner to say what feature inertial forces and gravity share (proportionality to the body's mass) and why that matters.
- Check: have the learner match each lift case to its partner and name which equivalence statement the pair illustrates.
- Check: ask whether non-zero Christoffel symbols prove that gravity is present (answer: no; polar coordinates in flat space give non-zero Gamma).
- Check: ask for the curved-space version of partial_b T^ab = 0 and for a different equation with the same flat limit, then ask which minimal coupling chooses.
- Common learner question: 'If gravity is just an inertial force, why can't we get rid of it everywhere by falling?' Answer via tidal effects: different parts of a large lift fall in different directions and at different rates; that residual is curvature.
- Common learner question: 'Is GR Machian?' Answer: partly; it was inspired by Mach but admits empty curved solutions, while rotating masses do drag local inertial frames (frame dragging), a Machian flavour.
- Terminology warning to give proactively: this book's 'strong' and 'weak' equivalence principles are swapped relative to most other sources; always restate the content rather than relying on the label.
- For advanced learners, connect P3 to the existence of normal (geodesic) coordinates where g_ab = eta_ab and Gamma vanishes at a point (DIV §6.6), and to the Einstein equivalence principle of §16.4.
- For novices, keep numbers concrete: m^I = m^P tested to 10^-12 here (modern tests reach about 10^-15), and isotropy of inertia to 10^-18.
- Keep principles as stated ideas, not memorized sentences: the book deliberately gives multiple formulations, so ask learners to paraphrase and to say what experiment would falsify each.
