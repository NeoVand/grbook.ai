# SCH ch07 · Physics in a curved spacetime

> Turns the differential geometry of chapter 6 into physics: it states the postulates linking a curved Lorentzian manifold to measurement, the weak and Einstein equivalence principles and the comma-goes-to-semicolon rule for carrying special-relativistic laws into curved spacetime, recovers Newtonian energy and force laws from geodesics of an assumed weak-field metric, and shows that metric symmetries give conserved momentum components (energy and angular momentum).

**Pages:** printed 170–182 · pdf 188–200 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 4/5

Short and readable. Sections 7.1 and 7.3 are conceptual with little algebra but ask for a real shift in viewpoint (weight, tides, no preferred frames, no local gravitational energy). Section 7.2 is routine first-order expansion of Christoffel symbols, though order counting in the two small parameters is glossed and one sign typo (7.22) can confuse. Section 7.4's symmetric/antisymmetric cancellation is the only slick step. The exercises, especially 7.5, 7.8 and 7.10, are considerably harder than the text and carry important physics.

## Role in the book

A short bridge chapter. Chapters 5 and 6 argued physically for curvature and built the manifold toolkit (Christoffel symbols, covariant derivatives, geodesics, Riemann tensor); chapter 4 built perfect fluids and T^{mu nu}. Chapter 7 answers only half of the question of what a gravity theory needs: how a given metric acts on particles and fluids. The other half, how matter produces curvature, is deferred to chapter 8, which derives the weak-field metric assumed here (Eq. 8.50) and fixes the constant in Einstein's equations by matching the Newtonian limit found in this chapter. The conserved -p_0 and p_phi introduced in section 7.4 become the main tools for Schwarzschild orbits in chapter 11, the fluid laws feed stellar structure in chapter 10, and Exercise 7.7 previews the Schwarzschild, Kerr and Robertson-Walker metrics.

## Learning objectives

- State the postulates identifying spacetime with a four-dimensional manifold whose metric is measured by rods and clocks and is Minkowskian at any single event in suitable coordinates.
- Contrast Newtonian spacetime (absolute time slices, separate time and distance measures) with relativistic spacetime unified by the invariant speed of light.
- State the weak equivalence principle as a geodesic postulate and explain why it is experimentally testable and how well it has been tested.
- State the Einstein equivalence principle, explain what "local" excludes, and reinterpret weight as a contact force and tides as the physical content of gravity.
- Apply the comma-goes-to-semicolon rule to particle conservation, entropy conservation and energy-momentum conservation for a perfect fluid.
- Explain, using the (nU^alpha)_{;alpha} = qR^2 counterexample, why the equivalence principle has physical content beyond agreement with special relativity.
- Derive dp^0/dtau = -m dphi/dt and dp^i/dtau = -m dphi/dx^i from the geodesic equation of the weak-field metric, keeping track of the weak-field and slow-motion approximations.
- Explain why the geodesic equation can be written with the four-momentum as tangent vector and why that form also covers photons.
- Explain why four-momenta at different events cannot be summed in curved spacetime and why gravitational potential energy of a system is not well defined.
- Derive m dp_beta/dtau = (1/2) g_{nu alpha,beta} p^nu p^alpha and state the conservation law for p_beta when the metric is independent of x^beta.
- Identify -p_0 in a stationary weak field with rest mass plus Newtonian potential plus kinetic energy, and p_psi in an axisymmetric field with mr^2 Omega.
- Explain why a generic metric has no conserved energy, using the count of ten metric functions versus four coordinate functions.
- Read off conserved momentum components for the Schwarzschild, Kerr and Robertson-Walker line elements and relate them to Killing vectors (exercise level).

## Teaching approach

A compact, physically argued chapter that proceeds by numbered postulates. Schutz sets up an explicit ledger of what a gravity theory must contain, fills in the "how matter responds" entry through equivalence principles, then immediately tests the scheme two ways: a counterfactual law shows the principle is falsifiable, and a component calculation shows the assumed weak-field metric reproduces Newton. Everyday experience (getting out of bed, astronauts, ocean tides) is reinterpreted rather than merely illustrated. The chapter then turns to conceptual hazards of the new picture (no preferred coordinates, no global vector sums, no local gravitational energy) and ends with a fully general derivation that symmetries of the metric yield conserved momentum components, each specialized back to a familiar Newtonian quantity. Much substantive physics (Euler equation, relativistic hydrostatics, Killing vectors, famous metrics) is pushed into the exercises.

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, geometric-first, thought-experiment, derivation-heavy, spiral-revisit

**Narrative arc**

1. Ask which mathematical structures correspond to physical measurement and list postulates I-III (manifold with metric, metric read by rods and clocks, metric Lorentzian at a point).
2. Contrast with Newton's spacetime to show that relativity's single metric is structurally simpler; split the theory into two tasks and postpone the field equation to chapter 8.
3. Derive the weak equivalence principle (postulate IV) from universality of free fall plus local inertial frames, and cite its experimental precision.
4. Generalize to the Einstein equivalence principle (IV') for all local physics; reinterpret weight, orbiting astronauts, and tides.
5. Translate IV' into the comma-goes-to-semicolon rule and apply it to particle, entropy and energy-momentum conservation for fluids.
6. Show physical content by exhibiting a rival law with a curvature term that agrees with SR yet predicts particle creation.
7. Assume the weak-field metric and derive Newtonian energy change and force law from the time and space components of the geodesic equation; comment that it had to work and that it is essential that it does.
8. Discuss curved intuition: no preferred frames, coordinate versus physical acceleration, no invariant sum of momenta, gravitational self-energy not localizable.
9. Derive the lowered-index geodesic equation, extract the symmetry-conservation theorem, and interpret -p_0 and p_psi in the Newtonian limit; note via a counting footnote that generic spacetimes lack a conserved energy.
10. Exercises extend to fluids (Euler, hydrostatic equilibrium), tidal equation from geodesic deviation, conserved integrals for continuous matter, famous metrics and Killing vectors.

**Signature moves**

- Numbered postulates (I, II, III, IV, IV') that make the logical structure of the theory explicit and checkable.
- Counterfactual law (nU^alpha)_{;alpha} = qR^2 to prove that the equivalence principle is a physical assumption, not tensor bookkeeping.
- Reinterpretation of mundane experience: the ground pushing us is what we call weight; astronauts a few hundred km up still feel nearly full Newtonian pull yet are weightless.
- A hidden-Moon scenario on a cloud-covered Earth to argue that only tidal (nonlocal) effects reveal gravity.
- Deflating commentary after the Newtonian-limit derivation ("no magic here") that redirects credit to the equivalence principle and the chosen metric.
- General derivation first (Eq. 7.29 valid for any metric), then specialization to stationary and axisymmetric cases with a Newtonian check at each step.
- Symmetric-times-antisymmetric cancellation used as the key algebraic trick in the conservation derivation.
- Footnoted degree-of-freedom count (10 metric components vs 4 coordinate functions) to show that time symmetry is special.
- Offloading substantial results (Euler equation, relativistic hydrostatic equilibrium, Killing vectors, Schwarzschild/Kerr/RW conserved quantities) into guided exercises.

## Section by section

### §7.1 The transition from differential geometry to gravity — p.170 (pdf 188)

Sets out what must be identified between geometry and measurement: spacetime is a 4D manifold with a metric read by rods and clocks, Minkowskian at any one event. Newton's spacetime is described as Euclidean slices stacked in absolute time, with no way to combine time and distance; the invariance of c supplies that combination. A complete theory needs a law of motion in a given metric (this chapter) and a law for how matter curves spacetime (chapter 8). Universality of free fall gives the weak equivalence principle as a geodesic postulate, tested to parts in 10^13; the Einstein equivalence principle extends this to all local physics. Weight is recast as the ground pushing us, tides as the true signature of gravity. The EEP becomes the comma-goes-to-semicolon rule, applied to particle, entropy and energy-momentum conservation for a perfect fluid, and a curvature-coupled rival law shows the rule has testable content.

**Concepts:** spacetime as a Lorentzian manifold, metric measured by rods and clocks, local flatness, Newtonian spacetime, invariant speed of light as unifier of space and time, Newtonian gravity, weak equivalence principle, tests of the weak equivalence principle, Einstein equivalence principle, local inertial frame, weight as a non-gravitational contact force, tidal effects as the measurable signature of gravity, comma-goes-to-semicolon rule, curvature coupling, conservation of particle number in curved spacetime, momentarily comoving reference frame, adiabatic flow, local conservation of energy-momentum, perfect-fluid stress-energy tensor, Ricci scalar

**Key moves**
- Postulates I-III: manifold with metric; metric gives rod lengths and clock times; metric reducible to eta at any event.
- Newtonian spacetime R^3 x R has invariant time intervals and spatial distances but no invariant length of a general spacetime curve; c provides the missing conversion.
- Split the theory into "response of matter to a given metric" (ch7) and "generation of curvature" (ch8), mirroring F = -m grad phi and Poisson's equation.
- Free fall is mass independent, so a freely falling frame removes acceleration of nearby particles; locally straight lines there are geodesics, giving postulate IV.
- Gravity is singled out because no particle is immune to it, making WEP a sharp experimental claim (bounds of a few parts in 10^13 cited).
- Postulate IV': all local non-gravitational experiments in a freely falling frame agree with SR; locality excludes long-range fields.
- Weight is a contact force; astronauts at ~300 km are weightless despite nearly Earth-surface Newtonian pull; tides from the Moon are the nonlocal, measurable part.
- Mathematical form: an SR tensor law holds unchanged in a local inertial frame, where Gamma vanishes, so replacing commas by semicolons gives the law in any coordinates; no explicit curvature terms allowed.
- Worked translation (nU^alpha)_{,alpha}=0 -> (nU^alpha)_{;alpha}=0; U^alpha S_{,alpha}=0 unchanged for a scalar; T^{mu nu}_{,nu}=0 -> T^{mu nu}_{;nu}=0 with eta -> g in the perfect-fluid tensor.
- Rival law (nU^alpha)_{;alpha}=qR^2 agrees with SR yet makes curvature create or destroy particles; choosing between them is an experimental question, and GR assumes the minimal choice.

### §7.2 Physics in slightly curved spacetimes — p.174 (pdf 192)

Takes on trust the weak-field line element in which the Newtonian potential phi (with |phi| << 1) modifies both g_00 and the spatial part, to be derived in chapter 8. Free fall is written as the geodesic equation for the four-momentum, which also works for photons. For slow particles the time component gives an energy that changes only when phi depends on time, and the spatial components, after computing Gamma^0_00 and Gamma^i_00 to first order, give exactly Newton's dp/dtau = -m grad phi, so Kepler orbits follow (Mercury being the exception). The author stresses that the success was guaranteed by the equivalence principle, that fluids need p << rho as well, and that reproducing the old theory in its domain is a requirement for any new one.

**Concepts:** weak-field metric, weak-field condition, geodesic equation, affine parameter, geodesic equation in terms of four-momentum, Christoffel symbols of the weak-field metric, slow-motion approximation, Newtonian limit of geodesic motion, correspondence principle, Newtonian limit of a perfect fluid, perihelion precession of Mercury

**Key moves**
- Adopt ds^2 = -(1+2phi)dt^2 + (1-2phi)(dx^2+dy^2+dz^2) with phi = -GM/r far from a mass; work to first order in phi.
- Rescale the affine parameter tau -> tau/m so the tangent vector is p; then nabla_p p = 0 holds for massive and massless particles alike.
- Time component: drop spatial momenta against p^0, compute Gamma^0_00 = phi_{,0} from the diagonal metric, set (p^0)^2 ~ m^2, obtain dp^0/dtau = -m dphi/dt.
- Interpretation: particle energy in this frame is conserved if the field is static, as in Newton, but the energy is frame specific.
- Space components: Gamma^i_00 = phi_{,i} to first order (g_{j0}=0 kills the time-derivative term), giving dp^i/dtau = -m phi_{,i}, i.e. Newton's second law with gravity.
- Both results rest on |phi| << 1 and p^0 >> p^i, the regime where Newton is tested; recovery was inevitable given straight-line fall in freely falling frames.
- For fluids the Newtonian limit also needs nonrelativistic random motions, p << rho (Exercise 7.5).

### §7.3 Curved intuition — p.176 (pdf 194)

Warns that agreement with Newton in the limit hides a very different conceptual structure. With no coordinates that are inertial everywhere, coordinate acceleration no longer tracks physical acceleration, so all coordinates must be treated equally and Christoffel symbols convert coordinate-dependent derivatives into invariant ones. Because vectors at different events can only be compared by parallel transport, which depends on path, a system's total four-momentum is not the sum of its particles' momenta. A total energy-momentum can be defined for isolated systems (later), and the gap between it and any naive sum, Newtonian binding energy, lives in the geometry and is not itself well defined.

**Concepts:** general covariance, coordinate acceleration versus covariant acceleration, path dependence of parallel transport, total four-momentum of a system in curved spacetime, gravitational binding energy, non-localizability of gravitational energy

**Key moves**
- In Newton and SR, inertial frames make zero physical acceleration coincide with zero coordinate acceleration; in curved spacetime no frame does this for all free particles.
- Hence no preferred coordinates: correct coordinate derivatives with Gamma and avoid coordinate-dependent reasoning.
- Sum of four-momenta requires comparing vectors at different points; parallel transport is path dependent, so the sum is not invariant.
- Total energy-momentum is definable for spatially bounded systems; Newtonian self-energy shows the total should not equal the sum of particle energies, and that energy cannot be assigned to particles.

### §7.4 Conserved quantities — p.177 (pdf 195)

Since gravity no longer acts as a force, momentum components are generally not constant along free fall, with one important exception. Writing the geodesic equation for lowered components and contracting the Christoffel symbol, a symmetric/antisymmetric cancellation leaves m dp_beta/dtau = (1/2) g_{nu alpha,beta} p^nu p^alpha for any metric. So if the metric does not depend on x^beta, p_beta is conserved. In a stationary field -p_0 is the conserved energy; expanding with the weak-field metric shows it is rest mass plus Newtonian potential and kinetic energy. Time independence is visible only in adapted coordinates, and a counting footnote shows generic metrics cannot be made stationary. Axial symmetry likewise conserves p_psi, which reduces to mr^2 Omega. Conservation of total mass and momentum of isolated self-gravitating systems is postponed.

**Concepts:** geodesic equation for covariant momentum components, conservation of momentum conjugate to an ignorable coordinate, stationary spacetime, conserved energy in a stationary spacetime, mass-shell condition, conserved angular momentum in an axisymmetric spacetime, generic absence of time symmetry

**Key moves**
- p^alpha p_{beta;alpha} = 0 expanded: m dp_beta/dtau = Gamma^gamma_{beta alpha} p^alpha p_gamma.
- Insert Gamma in terms of metric derivatives and raise p_gamma; the first and third metric-derivative terms form an antisymmetric pair in (nu, alpha) contracted with symmetric p^nu p^alpha, and vanish.
- Result Eq. 7.29 valid in complete generality: dp_beta/dtau depends only on derivatives of the metric with respect to x^beta.
- Stationary field: in adapted coordinates p_0 is conserved, so -p_0 is "the energy" without frame qualification; freely falling coordinates typically see time-dependent metric components.
- Solve p.p = -m^2 for p^0 in the weak-field metric, lower the index with g_00 = -(1+2phi), expand: -p_0 = m + m phi + p^2/2m.
- Footnote: 10 metric functions versus 4 coordinate functions, so stationarity is exceptional and generic spacetimes have no conserved particle energy.
- Axial symmetry: p_psi = g_psipsi p^psi ~ m r^2 Omega, the Newtonian angular momentum.

### §7.5 Bibliography — p.180 (pdf 198)

Short reading guide: Geroch for how curvature and physics fit together, advanced texts for conserved quantities, then a pointer that fixed-background physics prepares for quantum fields in curved spacetime (Birrell & Davies; Fulling; Parker & Toms) and on toward quantum gravity approached from classical GR (Rovelli & Vidotto; Bojowald; Thiemann).

**Concepts:** quantum field theory in curved spacetime

**Key moves**
- Frames the chapter's fixed-metric viewpoint as the starting point for quantum fields on curved backgrounds and for loop-style quantum gravity.

### Exercises — p.180 (pdf 198)

Ten exercises. They interpret the curvature-coupled particle law, compute the inverse metric, Christoffel symbols and Riemann tensor of the weak-field metric, show only g_00 drives Newtonian motion, derive Euler's equation and relativistic hydrostatic equilibrium from T^{mu nu}_{;nu}=0, find conserved momenta for Minkowski, Schwarzschild, Kerr and Robertson-Walker metrics, build conserved integrals for continuous matter (including total angular momentum), obtain the Newtonian tidal equation from geodesic deviation, and introduce Killing vectors.

**Concepts:** Newtonian limit of a perfect fluid, relativistic hydrostatic equilibrium, static metric, Killing vector, conserved quantities for continuous matter, geodesic deviation, Schwarzschild metric, Kerr metric, Robertson-Walker metric

**Key moves**
- Exercise 7.5 turns the fluid conservation law into Euler's equation and then into p_{,i} + (rho+p)[ln(-g_00)/2]_{,i} = 0, suggesting g_00 ~ -exp(2 phi).
- Exercise 7.8 converts T^nu_{mu;nu}=0 into an ordinary divergence when the metric ignores x^mu, yielding conserved volume integrals.
- Exercise 7.10 recasts the conservation law coordinate-free via Killing's equation.

## Concepts

### spacetime as a Lorentzian manifold

*principle · core* · also: Postulate I, spacetime manifold with metric

Events form a smooth four-dimensional manifold carrying a metric tensor. Postulate I itself says only 'manifold with a metric'; the Lorentzian signature (-+++) is supplied by Postulate III, which demands that the metric look Minkowskian at any one event. Gravity then appears as curvature of this metric rather than as a force on a flat background.

**How introduced:** Opening move of the chapter: the author asks which mathematical objects correspond to what is measured, recalls that nonuniform gravity forbids global inertial frames while local ones exist, and concludes that a curved manifold with a metric is the natural arena.

**Prerequisites:** differentiable manifold, metric tensor, local inertial frame

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.170 (pdf 188)

### metric measured by rods and clocks

*principle · developed* · also: Postulate II, operational meaning of the metric

The metric is physically measurable: for a small spacelike displacement dx the proper length read by a rod is |dx.dx|^{1/2}, and for a small timelike displacement the proper time read by a clock carried between the two events is |-dx.dx|^{1/2}.

**How introduced:** Stated as the second identification between geometry and experiment, directly after Postulate I, carrying the SR interpretation of the interval into curved spacetime.

**Prerequisites:** spacetime as a Lorentzian manifold, proper time

$$
\Delta \ell = |d\vec x\cdot d\vec x|^{1/2}
$$

$$
\Delta \tau = |-d\vec x\cdot d\vec x|^{1/2}
$$

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.170 (pdf 188)

### local flatness

*principle · revisited* · also: Postulate III, local Lorentz form of the metric

At any chosen event there exist coordinates in which the metric components equal the Minkowski values eta_{alpha beta}; generally no coordinates achieve this over a finite region when the spacetime is curved.

**How introduced:** Adopted as a third postulate because global Minkowski coordinates fail in nonuniform gravity while local inertial frames exist, which is exactly the property of a curved manifold proved in ch6.

**Prerequisites:** spacetime as a Lorentzian manifold, local inertial frame

$$
g_{\alpha\beta}(\mathcal P)=\eta_{\alpha\beta}
$$

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.170 (pdf 188)

### Newtonian spacetime

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: Galilean spacetime, R^3 x R, absolute time and space

Newton's arena: a Euclidean three-space at each instant of a universal time (topologically R^3 x R) with Galilean relativity. All observers agree on time elapsed between events and on distances between simultaneous events, but no invariant spacetime length exists for a curve that moves in both space and time.

**How introduced:** Described in prose to contrast with Einstein's single metric; used to argue that relativistic spacetime, with c converting time to distance, is structurally simpler.

**Prerequisites:** Galilean relativity

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.170 (pdf 188); SCH §7.1 p.171 (pdf 189)

### invariant speed of light as unifier of space and time

*historical-idea · introduced* · also: c as conversion factor

Because the speed of light is the same for every inertial observer, it provides a universal exchange rate between time and length, allowing one invariant interval that combines them; Newtonian physics lacks such a rate and keeps separate time and distance measures.

**How introduced:** Brief remark closing the comparison with Newtonian spacetime.

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian spacetime, invariant interval

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.171 (pdf 189)

### Newtonian gravity

*law · revisited* · also: Newton's law of gravitation, Poisson equation for gravity

In Newton's theory a body of mass m in a potential phi experiences the force -m grad phi, and the potential is produced by mass density through the Poisson equation grad^2 phi = 4 pi G rho. A relativistic theory must provide analogues of both laws.

**How introduced:** Used to structure the chapter: the force law analogue is found here, the field-equation analogue in ch8.

$$
\mathbf F=-m\nabla\phi
$$

$$
\nabla^2\phi=4\pi G\rho
$$

**Notes:** The printed text mixes lower-case phi and capital Phi for the same potential on this page.

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.171 (pdf 189)

### universality of free fall

*principle · revisited* · also: equality of gravitational and inertial mass

All bodies at a given place in a gravitational field acquire the same acceleration regardless of mass or composition, so a single freely falling frame removes the acceleration of every nearby body at once.

**How introduced:** Recalled from ch5 as the reason a freely falling frame is locally inertial; it is the observational root of postulate IV.

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.171 (pdf 189)

### weak equivalence principle

*principle · core* · also: WEP, Postulate IV, geodesic postulate

Test particles subject to no non-gravitational forces travel on timelike geodesics of the spacetime metric. In its more common flat-language form: all test bodies fall with the same acceleration independent of mass and composition.

**How introduced:** Built as a postulate from a chain of reasoning: because free-fall acceleration does not depend on mass, one freely falling frame removes the acceleration of all nearby particles; in that frame they move on locally straight paths, and such paths are by definition geodesics of the curved manifold. The author then stresses testability: every other interaction has particles immune to it, gravity has none.

**Prerequisites:** universality of free fall, local inertial frame, geodesic equation

$$
\nabla_{\vec U}\vec U=0
$$

**Notes:** Footnote 1 notes that WEP is usually stated without reference to curvature, and that it is the EEP that is normally taken to imply a curved-spacetime description.

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.171 (pdf 189); SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190)

### tests of the weak equivalence principle

*experiment-or-observation · introduced* · also: Eotvos-type experiments, free-fall universality tests

Experiments comparing the fall rates of bodies made of different materials; the book quotes fractional acceleration differences bounded to a few parts in 10^13 and mentions satellite proposals aiming at parts in 10^18.

**How introduced:** Quoted to support the claim that WEP ranks among the best-verified statements in all of physics.

**Prerequisites:** weak equivalence principle

$$
\eta=\Delta a/a\lesssim\text{few}\times10^{-13}
$$

**Notes:** Dated: the MICROSCOPE satellite reported bounds near 10^-15 in 2022 (after the book's text was written).

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190)

### Einstein equivalence principle

*principle · core* · also: EEP, Postulate IV'

A local, non-gravitational experiment done in a freely falling local inertial frame must give the same outcome as it would in the flat spacetime of special relativity. 'Local' rules out set-ups whose fields reach beyond the patch where the frame stays inertial. Hence gravity has no locally detectable effect; its influence shows up only when one compares regions of spacetime of finite extent.

**How introduced:** Motivated by the question of how fluids and other non-particle systems respond to a curved metric, generalizing IV from particles to all of local physics; then used to reinterpret weight and tides.

**Prerequisites:** weak equivalence principle, local inertial frame

**Notes:** The book cites Will (2006) for experimental tests.

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190)

### local inertial frame

*definition · revisited* · also: freely falling frame, locally inertial frame, LIF

Coordinates attached to a freely falling observer in which, at one event, the metric equals eta_{alpha beta} and its first derivatives vanish, so Christoffel symbols vanish there and special-relativistic laws hold to first order in the neighbourhood.

**How introduced:** Invoked to derive WEP and to justify the comma-goes-to-semicolon rule (Gamma = 0 at the origin makes partial and covariant derivatives agree there).

**Prerequisites:** local flatness, Christoffel symbols

$$
g_{\alpha\beta}=\eta_{\alpha\beta},\ g_{\alpha\beta,\gamma}=0\ \text{at}\ \mathcal P
$$

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.171 (pdf 189); SCH §7.1 p.173 (pdf 191)

### weight as a non-gravitational contact force

*phenomenon · developed* · also: weight as being pushed by the ground

In geometric gravity the sensation of weight is not gravity acting but the solid ground (or floor, chair) pushing us off our free-fall geodesic; bodies left to fall freely, like astronauts in orbit, feel no weight even where the Newtonian pull is nearly the surface value.

**How introduced:** Introduced with humour (stairs, mountains, getting out of bed) and the example of astronauts at about 300 km altitude, as a consequence of the EEP that lets the Newtonian gravitational force be discarded.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein equivalence principle, weak equivalence principle

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190)

### tidal effects as the measurable signature of gravity

*phenomenon · core* · also: tides, tidal forces, nonlocal effects of gravity

The variation of gravitational acceleration across an extended region, i.e. the relative acceleration of neighbouring free-fall paths, cannot be removed by any choice of freely falling frame; it is the frame-independent physical content of a gravitational field. Earth's ocean tides raised by the Moon are the standard example.

**How introduced:** Argued with a permanently cloudy Earth that falls freely toward the Moon: its inhabitants cannot feel the Moon locally but could infer it from the tides; linked to geodesic deviation.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein equivalence principle, geodesic deviation

$$
\frac{d^2\xi^i}{dt^2}=-\phi_{,ij}\xi^j
$$

**Notes:** The book's claim that tides are the only measurable aspect of gravity is an overstatement if read literally; see gaps_and_pitfalls.

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190)

### geodesic deviation

*law · revisited* · also: equation of geodesic deviation, Jacobi equation

Two neighbouring geodesics with tangent V and small connecting vector xi accelerate relative to each other according to nabla_V nabla_V xi^alpha = R^alpha_{mu nu beta} V^mu V^nu xi^beta; in the weak-field, slow-motion limit this reduces to the Newtonian tidal equation built from second derivatives of phi.

**How introduced:** Named in section 7.1 as the geometric meaning of tides; worked out in Exercise 7.9 for the weak-field metric.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann curvature tensor, geodesic equation

$$
\nabla_V\nabla_V\xi^\alpha=R^\alpha{}_{\mu\nu\beta}V^\mu V^\nu\xi^\beta
$$

$$
\frac{d^2\xi^i}{dt^2}=-\phi_{,ij}\xi^j
$$

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190); SCH p.182 (pdf 200)

### comma-goes-to-semicolon rule

*technique · core* · also: minimal coupling, principle of minimal gravitational coupling, covariantization of special-relativistic laws

The mathematical implementation of the EEP: write a local physical law as a tensor equation in SR with partial derivatives (commas); because it holds unchanged in a local inertial frame, where Christoffel symbols vanish, replacing the partial derivatives by covariant derivatives (semicolons) gives a tensor equation that holds in every coordinate system of curved spacetime, with no explicit curvature terms added.

**How introduced:** Presented right after the EEP as the rough mathematical meaning of the principle, then demonstrated on the conservation laws of perfect fluids; its content is exposed by a rival law with a Ricci-scalar term.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein equivalence principle, covariant derivative, local inertial frame

$$
T^{\mu\nu}{}_{,\nu}=0\ \longrightarrow\ T^{\mu\nu}{}_{;\nu}=0
$$

**Notes:** Ambiguous when a law has second derivatives, because covariant derivatives do not commute; the book does not discuss this.

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190); SCH §7.1 p.173 (pdf 191)

### curvature coupling

*definition · developed* · also: non-minimal coupling, explicit curvature terms

Terms in a curved-spacetime field or conservation law that involve the Riemann tensor or its contractions; since they vanish in flat spacetime, agreement with SR cannot exclude them. The EEP as used in this book forbids them.

**How introduced:** Introduced by imagining thermodynamics picking up Riemann terms, then made concrete with (nU^alpha)_{;alpha} = q R^2.

**Prerequisites:** Ricci scalar, comma-goes-to-semicolon rule

$$
(nU^\alpha)_{;\alpha}=qR^2
$$

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.173 (pdf 191)

### conservation of particle number in curved spacetime

*law · developed* · also: number-flux conservation

For a fluid with rest-frame number density n and four-velocity U, the number-flux vector N = nU has vanishing covariant divergence, (nU^alpha)_{;alpha} = 0, meaning particles are neither created nor destroyed anywhere.

**How introduced:** The first worked application of the comma-goes-to-semicolon rule: n and U are defined in a local inertial MCRF exactly as in SR, the SR law holds there, and Gamma = 0 at that event lets the comma become a semicolon.

**Prerequisites:** comma-goes-to-semicolon rule, momentarily comoving reference frame

$$
(nU^\alpha)_{;\alpha}=0
$$

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.173 (pdf 191)

### momentarily comoving reference frame

*definition · revisited* · also: MCRF, local rest frame of a fluid element

The inertial frame (in curved spacetime, the local inertial frame) instantaneously at rest with a fluid element; the fluid's n, rho, p and S are defined there and its time basis vector is the fluid four-velocity U.

**How introduced:** Carried over from ch4 by choosing a local inertial frame comoving with the fluid at each event.

**Prerequisites:** local inertial frame

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.173 (pdf 191)

### adiabatic flow

*law · revisited* · also: entropy conservation along fluid worldlines, isentropic flow of a perfect fluid

In a perfect fluid the specific entropy S of each element stays constant along its worldline, U^alpha S_{,alpha} = 0. Because S is a scalar, its covariant and partial derivatives coincide, so the law is unchanged in curved spacetime.

**How introduced:** Second example of the rule, chosen to show a case where nothing changes.

**Prerequisites:** comma-goes-to-semicolon rule

$$
U^\alpha S_{,\alpha}=0
$$

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.173 (pdf 191)

### local conservation of energy-momentum

*law · core* · also: divergence-free stress-energy tensor

The stress-energy tensor has zero covariant divergence, T^{mu nu}_{;nu} = 0. It expresses energy and momentum conservation in each local inertial frame and contains the equations of motion of the matter; in curved spacetime it does not by itself give globally conserved integrals.

**How introduced:** Third example of the rule, applied to a perfect fluid; the Newtonian limit of its spatial components is Euler's equation (Exercise 7.5).

**Prerequisites:** comma-goes-to-semicolon rule, perfect-fluid stress-energy tensor

$$
T^{\mu\nu}{}_{;\nu}=0
$$

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.173 (pdf 191); SCH §7.1 p.174 (pdf 192)

### perfect-fluid stress-energy tensor

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: perfect fluid T^{mu nu}

Stress-energy of a fluid with no viscosity or heat flow: the sum of energy density plus pressure times the outer product of the four-velocity with itself, and pressure times the inverse metric. Energy density and pressure are measured in the MCRF; in curved spacetime the flat inverse metric is replaced by the curved one, whose components reduce to the Minkowski values in a local inertial frame.

**How introduced:** Restated from ch4 with a parenthetical justification of the eta -> g replacement.

**Prerequisites:** momentarily comoving reference frame

T^{\mu\nu}=(\rho+p)U^\mu U^\nu+pg^{\mu\nu}

**Where:** SCH §7.1 p.174 (pdf 192)

### Ricci scalar

*mathematical-object · mention* · also: curvature scalar, scalar curvature

The full contraction of the Riemann tensor, R = g^{mu nu} R_{mu nu}; a scalar that vanishes in flat spacetime.

**How introduced:** Mentioned only as the ingredient of the counterexample law (nU^alpha)_{;alpha} = qR^2, with a pointer back to Eq. 6.92.

### weak-field metric

*solution-or-model · core* · also: Newtonian-limit metric, metric of a weak static field

The line element ds^2 = -(1+2phi)dt^2 + (1-2phi)(dx^2+dy^2+dz^2), accurate to first order in the Newtonian potential phi (negative, equal to -GM/r far from a mass M), valid when |phi| << 1.

**How introduced:** Assumed rather than derived, with a promise of derivation in ch8, because this chapter only asks how a given metric acts on matter.

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian gravity, line element, weak-field condition

$$
ds^2=-(1+2\phi)dt^2+(1-2\phi)(dx^2+dy^2+dz^2)

**Notes:** Only g_00 matters for slow-particle motion (Exercise 7.4); the spatial (1-2phi) factor matters for light bending and is fixed by the field equations in ch8 (Eq. 8.50).

**Where:** SCH §7.2 p.174 (pdf 192)

### weak-field condition

*definition · developed* · also: |phi| << 1

The Newtonian gravitational potential energy of a particle is tiny compared with its rest-mass energy, |m phi| << m (with c = 1), so |phi| << 1 and terms of order phi^2 may be dropped.

**How introduced:** Stated when introducing the weak-field metric; paired with the slow-motion condition as the two approximations behind the Newtonian limit.

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian gravity

$$
|\phi|\ll1
$$

**Where:** SCH §7.2 p.174 (pdf 192)

### geodesic equation

*law · revisited* · also: free-fall equation of motion

A curve with tangent U is a geodesic when U is parallel-transported along itself, nabla_U U = 0; in components d^2x^alpha/dlambda^2 + Gamma^alpha_{mu nu} (dx^mu/dlambda)(dx^nu/dlambda) = 0 for an affine parameter lambda. By postulate IV freely falling particles obey it.

**How introduced:** Applied to a massive particle using proper time as affine parameter.

**Prerequisites:** parallel transport, Christoffel symbols, affine parameter

$$
\nabla_{\vec U}\vec U=0
$$

**Where:** SCH §7.2 p.174 (pdf 192)

### affine parameter

*definition · revisited*

A parameter along a geodesic for which the geodesic equation takes the form nabla_U U = 0 without an extra term; any linear function a lambda + b of an affine parameter is again affine. Proper time is affine for timelike geodesics.

**How introduced:** Used to justify rescaling proper time by 1/m so that the tangent vector becomes the four-momentum.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic equation

**Where:** SCH §7.2 p.174 (pdf 192)

### geodesic equation in terms of four-momentum

*law · developed* · also: nabla_p p = 0

Free fall written with the four-momentum as tangent vector, nabla_p p = 0, obtained by using tau/m as affine parameter so dx/d(tau/m) = p. It applies equally to massless particles such as photons, which have a well-defined p but no four-velocity.

**How introduced:** A short reparametrization remark made "for convenience later"; its payoff is a single equation of motion for massive and massless particles.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic equation, affine parameter, four-momentum

$$
\nabla_{\vec p}\vec p=0
$$

**Where:** SCH §7.2 p.174 (pdf 192)

### Christoffel symbols of the weak-field metric

*technique · developed* · also: Gamma^0_00 and Gamma^i_00 in the Newtonian limit

To first order in phi, the connection coefficients that govern slow particles in the weak-field metric are Gamma^0_00 = phi_{,0} and Gamma^i_00 = phi_{,i}, found from the metric formula for Gamma using the fact that a diagonal metric has a diagonal inverse with reciprocal entries.

**How introduced:** Computed inline as the core step of the Newtonian-limit derivation; the full set is left to Exercise 7.3.

**Prerequisites:** Christoffel symbols, weak-field metric

$$
\Gamma^0{}_{00}=\phi_{,0}+O(\phi^2)
$$

$$
\Gamma^i{}_{00}=\phi_{,i}+O(\phi^2)

**Where:** SCH §7.2 p.175 (pdf 193); SCH §7.2 p.176 (pdf 194)

### slow-motion approximation

*technique · developed* · also: nonrelativistic limit, p^0 >> p^i

For particles moving slowly in the chosen coordinates, spatial momentum components are negligible compared with p^0, so geodesic-equation terms involving p^i are dropped and (p^0)^2 is replaced by m^2 at leading order.

**How introduced:** Applied to both the time and space components of the geodesic equation, then named as one of the two assumptions (with |phi| << 1) under which Newtonian gravity is recovered.

**Prerequisites:** four-momentum

$$
p^0\gg|p^i|,\quad (p^0)^2\approx m^2
$$

**Notes:** For bound orbits v^2 and |phi| are of the same order (virial relation), a point the text leaves implicit.

**Where:** SCH §7.2 p.175 (pdf 193); SCH §7.2 p.176 (pdf 194)

### Newtonian limit of geodesic motion

*theorem · core* · also: recovery of Newton's second law from geodesics

For weak fields and slow motion, the geodesic equation of the weak-field metric reduces to dp^i/dtau = -m phi_{,i} (Newtonian gravitational force law) and dp^0/dtau = -m dphi/dt (energy in this frame is conserved when the field is static).

**How introduced:** Derived component by component in section 7.2 and immediately interpreted: GR yields Kepler's planetary orbits except where neglected higher-order effects are measurable (Mercury). The author adds that the result was guaranteed by straight-line fall in freely falling frames.

**Prerequisites:** weak-field metric, geodesic equation in terms of four-momentum, Christoffel symbols of the weak-field metric, slow-motion approximation

$$
\frac{dp^i}{d\tau}=-m\,\phi_{,j}\delta^{ij}
$$

$$
\frac{dp^0}{d\tau}=-m\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}
$$

**Where:** SCH §7.2 p.175 (pdf 193); SCH §7.2 p.176 (pdf 194)

### correspondence principle

*principle · introduced* · also: recovery of the old theory in its domain

A new theory must reproduce the successful predictions of the theory it replaces within the regime where the old theory was confirmed; for GR this means Newtonian gravity for weak fields, slow motion and, for matter, pressure small compared with energy density.

**How introduced:** Stated as a closing reflection on the Newtonian-limit calculation: reassuring, indeed essential, but not a triumph in itself.

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian limit of geodesic motion

**Where:** SCH §7.2 p.176 (pdf 194)

### Newtonian limit of a perfect fluid

*theorem · introduced* · also: Euler equation from T^{mu nu}_{;nu}=0

When bulk velocities, random particle velocities (so p << rho) and |phi| are small, the spatial components of T^{mu nu}_{;nu} = 0 in the weak-field metric reduce to Euler's equation with gravity, v_{,t} + (v.grad)v + grad p/rho + grad phi = 0, and a static fluid obeys grad p + rho grad phi = 0.

**How introduced:** Mentioned in section 7.2 as the fluid analogue of the particle calculation, stressing that nonrelativistic matter also needs p << rho; carried out in Exercise 7.5.

**Prerequisites:** local conservation of energy-momentum, weak-field metric, conservation of particle number in curved spacetime

$$
\mathbf v_{,t}+(\mathbf v\cdot\nabla)\mathbf v+\nabla p/\rho+\nabla\phi=0
$$

**Where:** SCH §7.2 p.176 (pdf 194); SCH p.180 (pdf 198)

### static metric

*definition · introduced* · also: static spacetime

A metric is static if coordinates exist in which the time basis vector e_0 is timelike, there are no time-space components (g_{i0} = 0), and no component depends on the time coordinate.

**How introduced:** Defined inside Exercise 7.5(c) to derive relativistic hydrostatic equilibrium.

**Prerequisites:** stationary spacetime

$$
g_{i0}=0,\quad g_{\alpha\beta,0}=0
$$

**Where:** SCH p.180 (pdf 198)

### relativistic hydrostatic equilibrium

*law · introduced* · also: relativistic hydrostatic equation

In a static metric, a fluid at rest satisfies p_{,i} + (rho + p)[ (1/2) ln(-g_00) ]_{,i} = 0: the pressure gradient balances the gravitational pull encoded in g_00, with rho + p (not rho) as the effective inertial density. With g_00 ~ -exp(2 phi) it reduces to grad p + rho grad phi = 0.

**How introduced:** Exercise 7.5(c)-(d): deduced from T^{mu nu}_{;nu} = 0, suggesting the correspondence g_00 ~ -e^{2 phi}.

**Prerequisites:** static metric, local conservation of energy-momentum, perfect-fluid stress-energy tensor

$$
p_{,i}+(\rho+p)\left[\tfrac12\ln(-g_{00})\right]_{,i}=0
$$

**Where:** SCH p.180 (pdf 198); SCH p.181 (pdf 199)

### general covariance

*principle · developed* · also: no preferred coordinates, equal footing of all coordinate systems

Because in curved spacetime no coordinate system is inertial everywhere, no class of coordinates is privileged; physics must be expressed in tensor equations valid in all coordinates, and coordinate-dependent ways of thinking should be avoided.

**How introduced:** Given as the first way the curved-spacetime picture differs in concept from Newton and SR, argued from the mismatch between coordinate and physical acceleration.

**Prerequisites:** local inertial frame, coordinate acceleration versus covariant acceleration

**Where:** SCH §7.3 p.176 (pdf 194); SCH §7.3 p.177 (pdf 195)

### coordinate acceleration versus covariant acceleration

*definition · developed* · also: physical acceleration

The coordinate acceleration d^2x^i/dt^2 depends on the coordinates; the physical acceleration of a particle is the covariant derivative of its four-velocity along itself, nabla_U U (dU/dtau), which differs from the coordinate expression by Christoffel-symbol terms. In SR or Newtonian physics inertial frames make both vanish together for free particles; in curved spacetime no coordinates achieve this for all free particles.

**How introduced:** Section 7.3 argument: velocity is not locally measurable but acceleration is, which is why flat theories single out inertial coordinates.

**Prerequisites:** Christoffel symbols, geodesic equation

$$
\frac{dU^\alpha}{d\tau}+\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\mu\nu}U^\mu U^\nu
$$

**Notes:** Printed text writes d^2 U/dtau for the covariant quantity; dU/dtau (i.e. nabla_U U) is intended.

**Where:** SCH §7.3 p.177 (pdf 195)

### path dependence of parallel transport

*phenomenon · revisited* · also: non-integrability of parallel transport

In curved spacetime a vector parallel-transported from one event to another arrives with a value that depends on the route taken, so there is no invariant way to decide whether vectors at separated events are parallel or to add them.

**How introduced:** Used in section 7.3 to show why the SR notion of total four-momentum as a sum over particles fails.

**Prerequisites:** parallel transport, Riemann curvature tensor

**Where:** SCH §7.3 p.177 (pdf 195)

### total four-momentum of a system in curved spacetime

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: total mass-energy of an isolated system

In SR a system's total four-momentum is the sum of its particles' four-momenta; in curved spacetime that sum is not invariantly defined, but for an isolated (spatially bounded) system a total energy and momentum can still be defined, from the far-field geometry, and is conserved.

**How introduced:** Raised in section 7.3 and 7.4 with explicit postponement; ch8 defines the total mass of a relativistic source from its far field.

**Prerequisites:** path dependence of parallel transport, four-momentum

$$
\vec p_{\rm tot}^{\,SR}=\sum_i\vec p_{(i)}
$$

**Where:** SCH §7.3 p.177 (pdf 195); SCH §7.4 p.179 (pdf 197)

### gravitational binding energy

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: gravitational self-energy, gravitational potential energy of a system

The negative Newtonian energy gained when a bound system is assembled from constituents initially dispersed to infinity; it makes a system's total mass-energy smaller than the sum of its parts' energies. In GR it cannot be assigned to individual particles and, because the sum of particle energies is itself undefined, it is not separately well defined.

**How introduced:** Offered as a Newtonian reason why total mass-energy should differ from the sum of particle energies.

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian gravity, total four-momentum of a system in curved spacetime

**Where:** SCH §7.3 p.177 (pdf 195)

### non-localizability of gravitational energy

*principle · introduced* · also: energy stored in the geometry

There is no general invariant local energy density for the gravitational field itself; the energy associated with gravity resides in the geometry, and only each particle's four-momentum and the total energy-momentum of an isolated system have invariant meaning.

**How introduced:** Concluding claim of section 7.3, reached from the failure to define a sum of particle energies.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational binding energy, Einstein equivalence principle

**Where:** SCH §7.3 p.177 (pdf 195)

### geodesic equation for covariant momentum components

*identity · developed* · also: lowered-index geodesic equation

Along any geodesic, the covariant momentum components obey m dp_beta/dtau = (1/2) g_{nu alpha, beta} p^nu p^alpha. It follows from p^alpha p_{beta;alpha} = 0 once the Christoffel contraction is expanded and two metric-derivative terms cancel by symmetry, so the change of p_beta depends only on how the metric varies with x^beta.

**How introduced:** Derived in full generality at the start of section 7.4 after noting that, without gravitational force, momentum is generally not conserved.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic equation in terms of four-momentum, Christoffel symbols, index lowering

$$
m\frac{dp_\beta}{d\tau}=\tfrac12 g_{\nu\alpha,\beta}p^\nu p^\alpha
$$

**Where:** SCH §7.4 p.178 (pdf 196)

### conservation of momentum conjugate to an ignorable coordinate

*theorem · core* · also: symmetry implies conservation, cyclic coordinate conservation law

If no metric component depends on some coordinate x^beta, the covariant component p_beta of every freely falling particle's four-momentum, massive or massless, is constant along its path.

**How introduced:** Read off from Eq. 7.29 and highlighted as the one important exception to non-conservation of momentum components; illustrated with time independence (energy) and axial symmetry (angular momentum).

**Prerequisites:** geodesic equation for covariant momentum components

$$
g_{\alpha\nu,\beta}=0\ \Rightarrow\ p_\beta=\text{const}
$$

**Notes:** Coordinate-free version via Killing vectors is Exercise 7.10.

**Where:** SCH §7.4 p.178 (pdf 196)

### stationary spacetime

*definition · developed* · also: stationary gravitational field, time-independent metric

A spacetime whose metric components can be made independent of a timelike coordinate by a suitable choice of coordinates (time-space cross terms are allowed, unlike the static case). The property is visible only in adapted coordinates; generic coordinates, including freely falling ones, show time-dependent components.

**How introduced:** Introduced as the first application of the conservation theorem; the book notes the laboratory frame on Earth is the adapted frame.

**Prerequisites:** conservation of momentum conjugate to an ignorable coordinate

$$
g_{\alpha\beta,0}=0
$$

**Notes:** The book in section 7.4 only says 'time-independent in suitable coordinates'; the stationary/static distinction (static additionally requires g_{i0}=0) is drawn in Exercise 7.5(c). The timelike requirement on the symmetry direction is our clarification.

**Where:** SCH §7.4 p.178 (pdf 196)

### conserved energy in a stationary spacetime

*physical-quantity · core* · also: -p_0, Killing energy, energy at infinity

In coordinates where the metric is time independent, E = -p_0 is constant along every geodesic. For the weak-field metric -p_0 = m + m phi + p^2/2m: rest mass plus Newtonian potential energy plus kinetic energy. Unlike p^0, which is energy in one particular frame, -p_0 is the conserved quantity tied to the time symmetry.

**How introduced:** Derived by solving the normalization p.p = -m^2 for p^0, expanding to first order, and lowering the index with g_00 = -(1+2phi).

**Prerequisites:** stationary spacetime, mass-shell condition, weak-field metric, index lowering

$$
-p_0\approx m+m\phi+\frac{\mathbf p^2}{2m}
$$

**Notes:** Becomes the specific energy E-tilde = -p_0/m for Schwarzschild orbits in ch11 (Eq. 11.5).

**Where:** SCH §7.4 p.178 (pdf 196); SCH §7.4 p.179 (pdf 197)

### mass-shell condition

*identity · revisited* · also: four-momentum normalization

The four-momentum of a particle of rest mass m satisfies g_{alpha beta} p^alpha p^beta = -m^2 (zero for photons), which lets p^0 be expressed in terms of spatial momentum and the metric.

**How introduced:** Used to express p^0 in the weak-field metric on the way to interpreting -p_0.

**Prerequisites:** four-momentum, metric tensor

\vec p\cdot\vec p=g_{\alpha\beta}p^\alpha p^\beta=-m^2

**Where:** SCH §7.4 p.178 (pdf 196)

### generic absence of time symmetry

*principle · introduced* · also: counting argument against stationarity

A general metric has ten independent components, while a change of coordinates supplies only four free functions, so in general no coordinates make all components time independent; conserved particle energy exists only in special spacetimes.

**How introduced:** Given in footnote 2 to back the statement that a general gravitational field has no stationary frame.

**Prerequisites:** stationary spacetime

**Notes:** A heuristic count of functions, not a proof.

### conserved angular momentum in an axisymmetric spacetime

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: p_psi, axial Killing momentum

If coordinates can be chosen so the metric does not depend on an azimuthal angle psi, then p_psi is conserved along geodesics; for a nearly flat metric with g_psipsi ~ r^2 and slow motion it equals m r^2 Omega, the Newtonian angular momentum.

### conserved quantities for continuous matter

*theorem · introduced* · also: conserved integrals of T^nu_mu

If the metric does not depend on x^mu, T^nu_{mu;nu} = 0 reduces to an ordinary divergence (sqrt(-g) T^nu_mu)_{,nu} = 0, so for matter confined to a bounded region the integral of sqrt(-g) T^nu_mu n_nu over a slice x^0 = const is independent of x^0. ## Worked examples

### Covariantizing particle conservation (Eqs. 7.1-7.3) · intro · SCH §7.1 p.173 (pdf 191)

**Problem:** Find the curved-spacetime form of the SR law that particles are conserved in a fluid, and decide whether the answer is forced.

**Method:** Define n and U in a local inertial MCRF at the event; there the SR law holds; Christoffel symbols vanish at that event so the partial divergence equals the covariant one; the covariant form is a tensor equation and hence holds in all coordinates. Then compare with a rival (nU)_{;alpha} = qR^2 that also reduces to the SR law.

**Key insight:** The rule gives a unique minimal generalization, but agreement with SR alone does not select it; the EEP is an extra physical assumption decidable by observation.

**Result:** (nU^alpha)_{;alpha} = 0; the alternative with q R^2 would make curvature create or destroy particles.

**Concepts:** comma-goes-to-semicolon rule, conservation of particle number in curved spacetime, curvature coupling

### Covariantizing entropy and energy-momentum conservation (Eqs. 7.4-7.7) · intro · SCH §7.1 p.173 (pdf 191)

**Problem:** Carry the perfect-fluid entropy law and energy-momentum conservation into curved spacetime.

**Method:** Observe that the directional derivative of a scalar has no Christoffel terms, so the entropy law is unchanged; replace the comma by a semicolon in T^{mu nu}_{,nu} = 0 and replace eta by g in the fluid stress-energy tensor, noting that g has eta components in a local inertial frame.

**Key insight:** Scalars pass through untouched; tensors pick up Christoffel terms only through the covariant derivative.

**Result:** U^alpha S_{,alpha} = 0; T^{mu nu}_{;nu} = 0 with T^{mu nu} = (rho+p)U^mu U^nu + p g^{mu nu}.

**Concepts:** adiabatic flow, local conservation of energy-momentum, perfect-fluid stress-energy tensor

### Energy equation of a slow particle in a weak field (Eqs. 7.11-7.15) · standard · SCH §7.2 p.175 (pdf 193)

**Problem:** From the time component of nabla_p p = 0 in the weak-field metric, find how the particle's energy p^0 changes.

**Method:** Write p^alpha partial_alpha as m d/dtau; drop terms with spatial momentum; compute Gamma^0_00 using the diagonal inverse metric, keeping first order in phi; replace (p^0)^2 by m^2.

**Key insight:** To this order, the energy p^0 in the weak-field coordinates stays constant precisely when phi has no time dependence, just as in Newtonian mechanics; but p^0 is the energy relative to this particular frame, which section 7.4 improves on with -p_0.

**Result:** dp^0/dtau = -m partial phi/partial t (printed with partial tau).

**Concepts:** slow-motion approximation, Christoffel symbols of the weak-field metric, Newtonian limit of geodesic motion

### Newton's second law from the geodesic equation (Eqs. 7.16-7.24) · standard · SCH §7.2 p.175 (pdf 193)

**Problem:** Show that the spatial components of the geodesic equation in the weak-field metric reproduce the Newtonian gravitational force law.

**Method:** Truncate to the Gamma^i_00 (p^0)^2 term; set (p^0)^2 = m^2; compute Gamma^i_00 from the metric using g^{i alpha} = (1-2phi)^{-1} delta^{i alpha} and g_{j0} = 0, so only the spatial gradient of g_00 = -(1+2phi) survives.

**Key insight:** Only g_00 enters at this order (Exercise 7.4); the result was bound to work because free particles move straight in freely falling frames and the metric was chosen to encode phi in g_00.

**Result:** dp^i/dtau = -m phi_{,i}, so GR predicts Keplerian orbits to this order.

**Concepts:** Newtonian limit of geodesic motion, Christoffel symbols of the weak-field metric, weak-field metric

### Geodesic equation for lowered momentum components (Eqs. 7.25-7.29) · standard · SCH §7.4 p.178 (pdf 196)

**Problem:** Find a general form of the geodesic equation that makes conserved momentum components manifest.

**Method:** Start from p^alpha p_{beta;alpha} = 0; expand the covariant derivative of the one-form; insert Gamma in terms of metric derivatives; raise p_gamma with g^{gamma nu}; split the bracket into parts symmetric and antisymmetric under nu <-> alpha and drop the antisymmetric part against p^nu p^alpha.

**Key insight:** The symmetric/antisymmetric cancellation leaves only the derivative of the metric with respect to x^beta, so a metric independent of x^beta forces p_beta to be conserved.

**Result:** m dp_beta/dtau = (1/2) g_{nu alpha,beta} p^nu p^alpha.

**Concepts:** geodesic equation for covariant momentum components, conservation of momentum conjugate to an ignorable coordinate

### Interpreting the conserved energy -p_0 (Eqs. 7.30-7.34) · standard · SCH §7.4 p.178 (pdf 196)

**Problem:** In a stationary weak field, identify the physical content of the conserved component -p_0.

**Method:** Write p.p = -m^2 in the weak-field metric; solve for (p^0)^2; expand to first order in phi and p^2/m^2 and take the square root; lower the index with g_00 = -(1+2phi) and expand again.

**Key insight:** The covariant component, not p^0, is the conserved energy, and it automatically includes gravitational potential energy.

**Result:** -p_0 = m + m phi + p^2/2m: rest mass + potential energy + kinetic energy.

**Concepts:** conserved energy in a stationary spacetime, mass-shell condition, stationary spacetime

### Conserved angular momentum in an axisymmetric field (Eqs. 7.35-7.37) · intro · SCH §7.4 p.179 (pdf 197)

**Problem:** Show that p_psi for a metric independent of the azimuthal angle is the familiar angular momentum.

**Method:** Lower the index with g_psipsi; in slow motion p^psi ~ m dpsi/dt = m Omega; in a nearly flat metric g_psipsi = e_psi.e_psi ~ r^2 in cylindrical coordinates.

**Key insight:** Each ignorable coordinate gives a conserved quantity whose Newtonian limit is a known conservation law.

**Result:** p_psi ~ m r^2 Omega.

**Concepts:** conserved angular momentum in an axisymmetric spacetime

### Counting argument against generic stationarity (footnote 2) · intro · SCH §7.4 p.179 (pdf 197)

**Problem:** Can every gravitational field be made time independent by a clever choice of coordinates?

**Method:** Count degrees of freedom: a symmetric 4x4 metric has ten component functions; a coordinate transformation offers four functions x^alpha'(x^mu); four functions cannot in general remove time dependence from ten.

**Key insight:** Conserved particle energy is a property of special spacetimes, not of gravity in general.

**Result:** No; generic spacetimes have no stationary coordinates and so no conserved p_0.

**Concepts:** generic absence of time symmetry, stationary spacetime


## Analogies and intuitions

### Weight is the floor pushing you, not gravity pulling you: without the ground you would fall weightlessly along your natural path. → weight as a non-gravitational contact force · strong · SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190)

Reframes the everyday sense that gravity makes climbing hard or getting out of bed tiring: those sensations are forces from the Earth and objects on it that keep us off geodesics.

**Where it breaks down:** Leaves unexplained why the ground must push (the geodesics through the ground diverge from staying on the surface), which requires the curved-time picture of ch5; also does not address tides felt by extended bodies.

**App idea:** Split-screen elevator: one person on a scale on Earth, one in a freely falling cabin; toggle the floor on and off and show the worldline bending away from the geodesic only while contact force acts.

### Astronauts a few hundred kilometres up are almost as close to Earth's centre as we are, yet weightless. → Einstein equivalence principle · strong · SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190)

Blocks the explanation that orbiting astronauts escape gravity; their weightlessness comes from free fall, not distance.

**Where it breaks down:** A satellite is not a pure local inertial frame; tidal accelerations across the station are small but real.

**App idea:** Altitude slider from 0 to 36,000 km showing the Newtonian g (about 91% of surface value at 300 km) next to the reading on an onboard scale (always zero in orbit).

### Newtonian spacetime as a stack of Euclidean pages indexed by a universal clock, with no exchange rate between page number and distance on a page. → Newtonian spacetime · useful · SCH §7.1 p.171 (pdf 189)

Explains why Newton has invariant time and invariant spatial distances but no invariant length for a curve moving through both, and why c makes Einstein's single metric simpler.

**Where it breaks down:** The stacking picture hides that Newtonian spatial positions at different times are observer dependent (no absolute rest); the book states this but the image does not show it.

**App idea:** 3D stack of translucent spatial slices; drag an observer velocity to shear how points on different slices are matched, while slice separations and in-slice distances stay fixed; then switch to Minkowski with light cones.

### Recovering Newton is no magic: it had to work because particles move straight in freely falling frames. → Newtonian limit of geodesic motion · useful · SCH §7.2 p.176 (pdf 194)

Deflates the calculation's apparent triumph and points credit at the equivalence principle plus the chosen g_00.

**Where it breaks down:** The spatial part of the metric (1-2phi) is not tested by this argument; light bending needs it.

### Gravitational self-energy as the work you gain by bringing scattered particles together from infinity. → gravitational binding energy · useful · SCH §7.3 p.177 (pdf 195)

Shows that a bound system's total mass-energy should not equal the sum of particle energies, and that the difference cannot be attributed to any single particle.

**Where it breaks down:** The Newtonian picture suggests a well-defined potential energy, which the book then says fails in GR.

**App idea:** Assemble a cluster by dragging particles in from far away; a meter shows the sum of rest masses and the total mass (from the far-field orbit of a test particle) diverging as the cluster tightens.

### If the metric does not care about a coordinate, the matching momentum component is conserved (like a cyclic coordinate in Lagrangian mechanics). → conservation of momentum conjugate to an ignorable coordinate · strong · SCH §7.4 p.178 (pdf 196)

The book states the result directly from Eq. 7.29; the Lagrangian parallel is our addition for learners with analytical mechanics.

**Where it breaks down:** Symmetry may exist without any coordinate making it manifest in all components; Killing vectors (Exercise 7.10) are the coordinate-free statement.

**App idea:** Metric editor: learner types metric components; the app flags coordinates absent from all components, integrates a geodesic, and plots every p_beta against tau so the flat lines match the flagged coordinates.

### Adding momenta at different places is like adding arrows drawn on different parts of a globe: you must first slide one to the other, and the result depends on the route. → path dependence of parallel transport · useful · SCH §7.3 p.177 (pdf 195)

The book argues it abstractly; the globe image is the ch6 picture reused here to explain why total four-momentum is not a sum.

**Where it breaks down:** For weakly curved, isolated systems the ambiguity is small and a total momentum can be defined asymptotically.

**App idea:** Two particles on a sphere with tangent momenta; the learner draws a transport path between them and sees the vector sum change as the path changes.


## Misconceptions addressed

### What we feel as weight is the force of gravity acting on us. · SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190)

**Correction:** Gravity produces no local sensation; the felt force is the ground pushing us off our free-fall geodesic.

**Why tempting:** Everyday language and Newtonian training attribute the effort of standing or climbing to gravity.

### Orbiting astronauts are weightless because they are too far away for gravity to act. · SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190)

**Correction:** At a few hundred km the Newtonian pull is about 90% of its surface value; weightlessness is due to free fall.

**Why tempting:** "Zero gravity" language in media and the intuition that gravity weakens with height.

### Replacing commas by semicolons is a purely formal game with no physical consequence. · SCH §7.1 p.173 (pdf 191)

**Correction:** Other curved-spacetime laws, e.g. one with a qR^2 source, agree with SR but predict different physics; the minimal choice is a physical assumption testable by observation.

**Why tempting:** The rule looks like notation, and both candidate laws reduce to the same flat-space law.

### Any equation that reduces to the correct special-relativistic law in flat spacetime is automatically correct in curved spacetime. · SCH §7.1 p.173 (pdf 191)

**Correction:** Terms built from curvature vanish in flat spacetime, so flat-space agreement cannot fix them; the EEP rules them out by assumption.

**Why tempting:** The correspondence principle is often overextended into a uniqueness claim.

### The energy p^0 computed in the weak-field coordinates is the invariant energy of the particle. · SCH §7.2 p.175 (pdf 193)

**Correction:** The book stresses that p^0 is the energy relative to this one coordinate frame. In a stationary field the quantity that stays fixed along the whole trajectory is -p_0 in the adapted (time-independent) coordinates, and it is this conserved number that earns the name 'energy' without a frame qualifier.

**Why tempting:** In SR p^0 is called "the energy" and the weak-field coordinates look almost inertial.

### Recovering Newton's laws is strong independent evidence for GR. · SCH §7.2 p.176 (pdf 194)

**Correction:** It is required of any viable theory and follows almost inevitably from the equivalence principle plus the assumed g_00; the novel tests lie beyond the Newtonian limit (e.g. Mercury).

**Why tempting:** The calculation takes several steps and ends with a satisfying familiar result.

### The Newtonian limit only requires slow bulk motion. · SCH §7.2 p.176 (pdf 194)

**Correction:** It also requires |phi| << 1, and for fluids the internal random motions must be nonrelativistic too, p << rho.

**Why tempting:** Nonrelativistic is usually equated with small velocity.

### The spatial part of the weak-field metric drives Newtonian gravity. · SCH p.180 (pdf 198)

**Correction:** For slow particles only g_00 matters; g_xx may be anything of the form 1 + O(phi) without changing the Newtonian equations (Exercise 7.4).

**Why tempting:** Curved-space imagery (rubber sheets) suggests spatial curvature causes orbits.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Coordinate acceleration d^2x^i/dt^2 tells you whether a particle is physically accelerated. · SCH §7.3 p.177 (pdf 195)

**Correction:** In curved spacetime no coordinates make coordinate acceleration vanish for all free particles; the invariant acceleration includes Christoffel terms.

**Why tempting:** In Newtonian physics and SR inertial frames make the two coincide.

### A system's total four-momentum in curved spacetime is the sum of its particles' four-momenta. · SCH §7.3 p.177 (pdf 195)

**Correction:** Vectors at different events cannot be added invariantly because parallel transport is path dependent; only isolated systems have a (differently defined) total energy-momentum.

**Why tempting:** It works in SR and in Newtonian mechanics.

### Gravitational potential energy is a well-defined quantity in GR, just as in Newtonian gravity. · SCH §7.3 p.177 (pdf 195)

**Correction:** It would be the difference between total mass and the sum of particle energies, but the latter is undefined, so gravitational potential energy of a system is not well defined; energy of gravity lives in the geometry.

**Why tempting:** Potential energy is basic Newtonian bookkeeping, and -p_0 even contains an m phi term for single particles.

### If a gravitational field is stationary, its metric components are time independent in every coordinate system. · SCH §7.4 p.178 (pdf 196)

**Correction:** Only adapted coordinates show time independence; freely falling coordinates typically see time-varying components.

**Why tempting:** Stationarity sounds like an intrinsic property, which it is, but its coordinate signature is not.

### Every gravitational field admits a conserved particle energy. · SCH §7.4 p.179 (pdf 197)

**Correction:** Only stationary spacetimes do; ten metric functions cannot generically be made time independent with four coordinate functions.

**Why tempting:** Energy conservation is treated as universal in Newtonian mechanics.

### Photons cannot obey the geodesic equation because they have no proper time or four-velocity. · SCH §7.2 p.174 (pdf 192)

**Correction:** Using the four-momentum as tangent vector (affine parameter tau/m for massive particles) gives nabla_p p = 0, which applies to photons directly.

**Why tempting:** The geodesic equation is first taught with U = dx/dtau.

### Weightless astronauts at 300 km · SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190)

**Setup:** Compare a person standing on Earth with astronauts in low orbit, who are only slightly farther from Earth's centre.

**Lesson:** Newtonian pull is nearly the same, yet the astronauts feel nothing; weight is the push of the ground, and free fall along geodesics is gravity-free locally.

**App idea:** Orbit animation with an onboard spring scale and a ground scale; slider for altitude and a toggle to "switch off" the orbital velocity so the capsule drops, showing the scale stays at zero in both cases.

### Discovering the Moon from a cloud-covered Earth · SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190)

**Setup:** An Earth permanently shrouded in cloud falls freely around the Earth-Moon centre of mass; its inhabitants cannot see the Moon.

**Lesson:** Uniform Moon-induced acceleration is undetectable because the whole Earth falls with it; only the difference across the Earth (tides, geodesic deviation) reveals the Moon.

**App idea:** Hidden-Moon game: learner sees only ocean bulges on an Earth whose frame falls freely; they drag a guessed Moon position and mass until the predicted tidal ellipsoid matches.

### A universe where curvature makes particles · SCH §7.1 p.173 (pdf 191)

**Setup:** Replace particle conservation by (nU^alpha)_{;alpha} = qR^2, which agrees with SR in flat spacetime.

**Lesson:** Curvature would create or annihilate particles according to the sign of q; since flat-space physics cannot distinguish the laws, the EEP is a real physical postulate to be checked against observation.

**App idea:** Fluid parcel moving through a region of adjustable curvature; slider for q shows the particle count in a comoving box growing or shrinking only while R is nonzero.

### Freely falling observer in a static field · SCH §7.4 p.178 (pdf 196)

**Setup:** Describe a time-independent gravitational field from coordinates that fall freely through it.

**Lesson:** The falling observer sees the field change as they move, so metric components depend on their time coordinate; time symmetry is manifest only in the laboratory-type adapted frame.

### Assembling a star from dispersed particles · SCH §7.3 p.177 (pdf 195)

**Setup:** Bring particles in from infinity to form a bound system and ask what the total mass-energy is.

**Lesson:** Work is gained, so total mass-energy is less than the sum of parts; the deficit cannot be assigned to individual particles and resides in the geometry.


## Applications and observations

- **Laboratory and satellite tests of the weak equivalence principle** (experiment, SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190)): Fall-rate comparisons of test bodies of different composition bound fractional acceleration differences; satellite experiments are proposed for far greater precision. Key numbers: Book: few parts in 10^13, proposals to 10^18. Our note: MICROSCOPE (2022) reported about 10^-15.
- **Weightlessness in low Earth orbit** (numerical-estimate, SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190)): Astronauts at about 300 km altitude experience nearly surface-strength Newtonian gravity yet feel weightless because they are in free fall. Key numbers: Our estimate: g(300 km)/g(surface) = (6371/6671)^2 ~ 0.91.
- **Ocean tides as evidence of the Moon** (astrophysical-system, SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190)): Earth falls freely toward the Moon, so the Moon's mean pull is not felt; the variation of that pull across Earth drives the tides. Key numbers: Our estimate: lunar pull on Earth ~3.3e-5 m/s^2; tidal difference at Earth's surface 2GM_moon R_E/d^3 ~ 1.1e-6 m/s^2 (~1e-7 g).
- **Keplerian planetary motion and Mercury** (observation, SCH §7.2 p.176 (pdf 194)): Newtonian orbits follow from GR at lowest order; neglected higher-order terms are too small for most planets but measurable for Mercury (treated later). Key numbers: Our note: GR excess perihelion advance of Mercury ~43 arcsec per century.
- **Size of the weak-field parameter phi in nature** (numerical-estimate, SCH §7.2 p.174 (pdf 192)): The weak-field metric is justified when |phi| = GM/(rc^2) is small; this shows where the Newtonian limit is safe and where it fails. Key numbers: Our estimates: Earth surface 7e-10; Sun's potential at Earth orbit 1e-8 (compare v^2/c^2 of Earth ~1e-8); solar surface 2e-6; white dwarf ~1e-4; neutron star ~0.2.
- **Laboratory frame on Earth as a stationary frame** (observation, SCH §7.4 p.178 (pdf 196)): The lab frame is the one in which Earth's field is time independent, so the conserved -p_0 there matches the lab notion of energy including gravitational potential energy.

## Historical notes

- **Isaac Newton:** Newtonian mechanics set in a Euclidean space repeated through a universal time, with Galilean relativity, a force law F = -m grad phi and Poisson's equation for phi. — Provides the contrast class: GR must replace both laws, and its spacetime structure is argued to be simpler than Newton's. (SCH §7.1 p.170 (pdf 188))
- **Albert Einstein:** Invariance of the speed of light enabled a unified measure of time and space in a four-dimensional spacetime. — Makes an invariant length of arbitrary spacetime curves possible, the prerequisite for a metric theory of gravity. (SCH §7.1 p.171 (pdf 189))
- **Albert Einstein:** The equivalence principle in its general (Einstein) form: local non-gravitational physics in free fall is special relativity. — Foundation of the comma-goes-to-semicolon rule and hence of how all matter couples to the metric in GR. (SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190))
- **Clifford M. Will (2006):** Review of experimental tests of the Einstein equivalence principle cited by the book. — Supports the claim that EEP and WEP are among the best-tested principles in physics. (SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190))

## Notation and conventions

- **Metric signature:** (-,+,+,+): timelike dx.dx < 0, so clock time uses |-dx.dx|^{1/2} and p.p = -m^2. (SCH §7.1 p.170 (pdf 188))
- **Units:** Geometrized units with c = G = 1; phi is dimensionless, phi = -GM/r far from mass M, and |phi| << 1 means GM/(rc^2) << 1. — Newtonian formulas on p.171 still show G explicitly. (SCH §7.2 p.174 (pdf 192))
- **Commas and semicolons:** A comma before an index denotes a partial derivative, a semicolon a covariant derivative; the comma-goes-to-semicolon rule is named for this notation. (SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190))
- **Index ranges:** Greek indices run 0-3; Latin indices i, j run over spatial values 1-3; repeated indices summed. (SCH §7.2 p.175 (pdf 193))
- **Vectors and three-vectors:** Four-vectors carry arrows (p, U); bold symbols denote spatial three-vectors, and bold p^2 abbreviates (p^x)^2+(p^y)^2+(p^z)^2. (SCH §7.4 p.179 (pdf 197))
- **Sign of the conserved energy:** The conserved energy is -p_0 (covariant component with a minus sign), not p^0; p^0 is energy in a given frame. — Used again as E = -p_0 in ch11. (SCH §7.4 p.178 (pdf 196))
- **Azimuthal angle symbol:** Cylindrical coordinates are written (r, psi, z) so that phi stays reserved for the Newtonian potential in section 7.4. — The exercises switch back to phi as the azimuthal angle in Schwarzschild, Kerr and RW metrics. (SCH §7.4 p.179 (pdf 197))
- **Postulate labels:** I-III identify spacetime and metric with measurement; IV is the weak equivalence principle; IV' the Einstein equivalence principle. (SCH §7.1 p.170 (pdf 188))
- **Fluid variables:** n number density, rho total energy density, p pressure and S specific entropy are all defined in the MCRF; U is the fluid four-velocity. — p is used for both pressure and momentum; context decides. (SCH §7.1 p.173 (pdf 191))
- **Potential symbol:** Newtonian potential is phi; on p.171 Poisson's equation is printed with capital Phi for the same quantity. (SCH §7.1 p.171 (pdf 189))

## Teaching gems

### Exhibit a rival law, (nU^alpha)_{;alpha} = qR^2, that agrees with SR yet predicts particle creation, to prove the equivalence principle is physics. · SCH §7.1 p.173 (pdf 191)

**Why it works:** Learners often see covariantization as notation; a concrete falsifiable alternative shows what the principle rules out and why observation must decide.

**App idea:** Curvature-coupling sandbox: a comoving fluid box travels through a spacetime with a curvature bump; a q slider and a live particle counter show creation or depletion only inside the curved region, with a "GR" preset at q = 0.

### Recast weight as the ground pushing you, anchored by astronauts at ~300 km who feel no weight despite nearly full Newtonian pull. · SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190)

**Why it works:** Uses universal bodily experience and a vivid counterexample to dislodge the "gravity is a felt force" intuition in one step.

**App idea:** Scale-reading explorer: altitude and orbital-speed sliders show Newtonian g versus spring-scale reading for a capsule, a tower and a free-falling elevator.

### A cloud-covered Earth could find the Moon only through tides, so tides are the true signature of gravity. · SCH §7.1 p.172 (pdf 190)

**Why it works:** Turns the abstract statement "uniform fields are locally undetectable" into a detective puzzle with a familiar observable.

**App idea:** Tidal detective: an Earth-centred freely falling view shows ocean bulges; learner infers hidden Moon direction and distance; a toggle subtracts the uniform part to show it carries no information.

### Derive Newton's law from the geodesic equation, then immediately say "no magic here" and credit the equivalence principle and g_00. · SCH §7.2 p.176 (pdf 194)

**Why it works:** Pairs a satisfying calculation with metacognition about what was and was not tested, preventing overclaiming.

**App idea:** Weak-field orbit lab: integrate geodesics of the weak-field metric beside Newtonian orbits; sliders for the strength of phi and for a factor multiplying the spatial metric perturbation show that orbits depend only on g_00 at low speed.

### Get the conservation law from symmetry in one move: a symmetric p^nu p^alpha kills an antisymmetric pair of metric-derivative terms, leaving dp_beta/dtau controlled by the metric's x^beta dependence. · SCH §7.4 p.178 (pdf 196)

**Why it works:** A general, short derivation that makes a deep principle (symmetry gives conservation) visible in index algebra.

**App idea:** Index-cancellation animator: the three Christoffel terms are drawn as colour-coded tiles; swapping nu and alpha flips the sign of two tiles, which cancel against the symmetric momentum product, leaving the middle term.

### Unpack -p_0 as rest mass plus potential plus kinetic energy by expanding p.p = -m^2 in the weak-field metric. · SCH §7.4 p.179 (pdf 197)

**Why it works:** Connects an abstract conserved component to the Newtonian energy the learner already trusts and shows why the lowered index is the right one.

**App idea:** Energy-bar tracker: as a particle moves in a potential well, bars for p^0, -p_0, m phi and p^2/2m update; -p_0 stays flat while p^0 and the individual terms change.

### Count ten metric functions against four coordinate functions to show stationarity (and hence energy conservation) is special. · SCH §7.4 p.179 (pdf 197)

**Why it works:** A one-line degree-of-freedom argument that feels like proof and generalizes to other symmetry questions.

**App idea:** Coordinate-freedom puzzle: learner applies up to four coordinate functions to a time-dependent metric; a counter shows how many of the ten components can be made static.

### Contrast Newtonian spacetime (separate invariant time and distance, no invariant curve length) with Einstein's unified metric to argue relativity is structurally simpler. · SCH §7.1 p.171 (pdf 189)

**Why it works:** Reverses the common belief that relativity complicates space and time, giving motivation for using a single metric.

**App idea:** Structure toggle: the same set of events shown as stacked Galilean slices with a clock and ruler, then as Minkowski spacetime with light cones; curve length is computable only in the second view.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Eq. 7.22 is printed with +1/2 g_{00,j} delta^{ij}; from Eq. 7.21 with g_{j0}=0 the sign must be negative, and Eq. 7.23 is written with the correct minus sign.** — Careful readers get a sign that contradicts the next line and may doubt the Newtonian force direction. *Suggestion:* Show Gamma^i_00 = -(1/2) g_{00,j} delta^{ij} = phi_{,i} explicitly and check the sign with an attractive potential phi = -GM/r.
- **Eq. 7.33 is printed with p^0 (upper index) on the left side where the lowered component p_0 is meant.** — Confuses the central distinction between p^0 (frame energy) and -p_0 (conserved energy). *Suggestion:* Write p_0 = g_00 p^0 and use colour or spacing to separate raised and lowered components throughout the energy discussion.
- **Eq. 7.15 writes partial phi/partial tau; the partial derivative with respect to coordinate time t is meant. Section 7.3 also writes d^2 U/dtau for what should be dU/dtau (nabla_U U).** — Partial derivatives of a field with respect to a worldline parameter are ill-defined, and d^2 U is meaningless; learners may think a new object is involved. *Suggestion:* State dp^0/dtau = -m partial_t phi (with dt/dtau ~ 1) and write the covariant acceleration as nabla_U U.
- **The comma-goes-to-semicolon rule is ambiguous when an SR law contains second derivatives, since covariant derivatives do not commute and different orderings differ by curvature terms (e.g. Maxwell's equations written with the vector potential).** — Learners may think the rule always gives a unique answer, contradicting the claim that curvature terms never appear. *Suggestion:* Add a short example showing an ordering ambiguity and explain that GR resolves it by writing laws in first-order form or by extra physical input.
- **Read literally, the claim that tides are the sole measurable effect of gravity overstates the case; what is meant is that only effects which cannot be transformed away by going to a local freely falling frame carry physical content.** — Learners may wonder whether gravitational redshift between separated clocks, or the push of the floor, count as measuring gravity. *Suggestion:* Phrase as "everything gravity does that survives transforming to free fall is tidal (curvature)"; note that redshift over height is also a nonlocal comparison.
- **The weak-field metric is postulated without derivation, and the text never explains why the spatial part carries (1-2phi) when slow-particle motion does not need it.** — The metric can feel arbitrary; learners may credit spatial curvature for Newtonian orbits. *Suggestion:* Point to Exercise 7.4 and ch8 §8.4, and preview that light bending depends on the spatial part (it doubles the Newtonian deflection).
- **The derivation treats |phi| << 1 and v << 1 as independent small parameters, but for bound orbits v^2 ~ |phi| (virial relation); the consistent order counting is left implicit.** — Advanced learners may worry that dropped p^i p^j Gamma terms are comparable to kept ones. *Suggestion:* Show that dropped terms are O(phi v^2) or O(phi v), one order below the kept O(phi) force, with Earth-orbit numbers (phi ~ v^2 ~ 1e-8).
- **The quoted WEP bound (parts in 10^13) is dated; the MICROSCOPE satellite reached about 10^-15 in 2022.** — Minor; learners may quote an out-of-date precision. *Suggestion:* Update the number in app content and cite the satellite result.
- **Postulate IV applies to test particles; the book does not mention the strong equivalence principle or the fact that self-gravitating bodies and spinning bodies deviate from simple geodesic motion.** — Learners may assume planets and neutron stars follow geodesics exactly by postulate rather than by a later result. *Suggestion:* Add a brief note separating test-particle WEP from the strong EP and from finite-size effects.
- **Conserved quantities are introduced only via coordinates adapted to a symmetry; the coordinate-free Killing-vector statement appears only in Exercise 7.10, and the counting argument in footnote 2 is heuristic.** — Learners may think symmetry is coordinate dependent, or miss symmetries not manifest in given coordinates (e.g. the extra rotational Killing vectors of Schwarzschild). *Suggestion:* Introduce Killing vectors alongside Eq. 7.29 in the app, with Minkowski's ten as the running example, and link to Exercise 7.7(b).
- **Exercise 7.7(a) prints rho_alpha where the momentum components p_alpha are meant (confirmed on the pdf 199 render). The reading-copy export additionally garbles dr^2 as dt^2 in line elements (ii), (iii) and (iv); the printed page has dr^2.** — Confusion with density, and wrong metrics if working from the export. *Suggestion:* Use the verified line elements (dr^2 terms) and p_alpha notation in any derived material.
- **The claim that Einstein's spacetime is structurally simpler than Newton's is asserted but not unpacked (Newtonian spacetime has a degenerate structure: absolute time plus a separate spatial metric plus a connection).** — Beginners may find the claim puzzling since relativity seems harder. *Suggestion:* Show explicitly the two separate Newtonian measures versus one Lorentzian metric; optionally mention Newton-Cartan geometry for advanced learners.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: "Standing on the floor, are you in an inertial frame? What would an accelerometer read?" Let the learner commit before introducing the idea that weight is the floor's push.
- Second hook: "Astronauts on the ISS are about 400 km up. How strong is Earth's gravity there?" Most learners guess near zero; the answer (~90%) motivates free fall as the key concept.
- Order of explanation: postulates I-III -> WEP as geodesic postulate -> EEP and locality -> weight and tides -> comma-to-semicolon with fluids -> qR^2 counterexample -> weak-field Newtonian limit -> curved intuition -> conserved quantities.
- When teaching the comma-goes-to-semicolon rule, ask the learner to propose a second law that also reduces to SR; if stuck, offer the Ricci-scalar source and ask what it would predict.
- For the Newtonian limit, have the learner name the two small parameters before computing; afterwards ask which metric component did all the work (answer: g_00), then point to Exercise 7.4.
- Check for understanding: "Why is p^0 not the conserved energy but -p_0 is?" A good answer mentions the lowered index, time-independence of the metric, and frame dependence of p^0.
- Check for understanding: "Give a coordinate system in which a static field looks time dependent." Expected: freely falling coordinates.
- If the learner knows Lagrangian mechanics, connect Eq. 7.29 to cyclic coordinates via the Lagrangian (1/2) g_{mu nu} xdot^mu xdot^nu; if not, use the direct symmetric/antisymmetric cancellation.
- Common learner question: "Where does gravitational potential energy go in GR?" Answer: for a single particle in a stationary field it appears inside -p_0; for a system it is not localizable and only total mass of an isolated system is defined (ch8).
- Common learner question: "Why can't I add momenta at different points?" Use parallel transport on a sphere from ch6 and show two paths giving different results.
- Common learner question: "Do photons follow geodesics if they have no proper time?" Point to nabla_p p = 0 and the freedom to rescale affine parameters.
- Flag printed typos proactively when the learner works from the book: Eq. 7.22 sign, Eq. 7.33 index position, partial phi/partial tau in 7.15, and d^2U/dtau in section 7.3.
- For advanced learners, use Exercise 7.10 to introduce Killing vectors and ask how many a Schwarzschild metric has (four), versus the one or two manifest as ignorable coordinates.
- Avoid rubber-sheet imagery here: this chapter shows slow-particle motion comes from g_00 (time curvature), not spatial curvature.
- Numbers to have ready: phi at Earth's surface ~7e-10, Sun's surface ~2e-6, neutron star ~0.2; lunar tidal acceleration ~1e-6 m/s^2.
