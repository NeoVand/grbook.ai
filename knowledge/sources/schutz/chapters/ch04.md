# SCH ch04 · Perfect fluids in special relativity

> Builds the special-relativistic description of continuous matter step by step: dust and its number-flux four-vector, one-forms as the natural representation of surfaces, the stress-energy tensor read as a table of momentum fluxes, its symmetry, the local conservation laws (T^{ab}_{,b}=0, N^a_{,a}=0), the perfect-fluid tensor (rho+p)U(x)U + p g^{-1}, and from these the adiabatic condition and the relativistic Euler equation, closing with why T must be the source of gravity and with the four-dimensional Gauss theorem.

**Pages:** printed 84–110 · pdf 102–128 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

The early sections (dust, flux, one-forms as surfaces) are gentle and picture based. Difficulty rises in §4.5 (integrating-factor definition of T and S, torque proof) and especially §4.7, the first genuine tensor-calculus manipulation, where learners must track product rules, contractions and MCRF simplifications. Several steps are stated tersely ('it can be shown', 'a little algebra'), there are two printed typos in formulas (Eqs. 4.21, 4.56) and one in the inertia scaling, and §4.8 is qualitative. Readers who skipped Chapter 3's one-form material will struggle with §4.3; readers weak in thermodynamics will find §4.5 abrupt.

## Role in the book

Chapter 3 supplied one-forms and general tensors as abstract machines; Chapter 4 gives them their first physical jobs before the book turns to curved manifolds in Chapters 5-6. It fixes the source side of GR: the stress-energy tensor, the perfect-fluid model, and the conservation law T^{ab}_{,b}=0 that Chapter 7 will rewrite with covariant derivatives and Chapter 8 will place on the right-hand side of Einstein's equations. It is also the first place the reader manipulates tensor-calculus expressions (derivatives of products, contraction with U, projection in the MCRF), and it previews the physics of relativistic stars (pressure as a source and as inertia, hence collapse; Chapters 10-11) and of cosmological fluids (Chapter 12). The MCRF-per-element idea introduced here is reused in Chapter 5 as the model for local inertial frames.

## Learning objectives

- Decide whether the continuum approximation applies to a system by asking whether elements exist that are large enough to average over yet small enough to be homogeneous.
- Explain why a fluid is characterized by weak shear (anti-slip) forces and why a perfect fluid has none, leaving only pressure.
- Derive the number density n/sqrt(1-v^2) and particle flux n v^i/sqrt(1-v^2) of dust in a frame where it moves, using Lorentz contraction of a box of particles.
- Assemble density and fluxes into the number-flux four-vector N = nU and recover the rest density as the scalar n = (-N.N)^{1/2}.
- Interpret a flux as world lines piercing a three-surface in spacetime, and see number density as the flux across a surface of constant t.
- Represent a hypersurface by its (unit) normal one-form and compute the flux across it as the contraction <n~, N>; describe an inertial frame by the one-form dt and write E = <dt, p>.
- Argue from counting one-forms (or gamma factors) that energy density must be a component of a (2 0) tensor, and state the component meaning of T^{ab} as the flux of a-momentum across a surface of constant x^b.
- Show that dust has T = rho U(x)U and write its components in any frame.
- Define the MCRF scalars of a fluid (n, rho, rho_0, Pi, T, p, S) and derive n dq = drho - (rho+p) dn/n = nT dS from the first law with particle conservation.
- Interpret each block of T in the MCRF (energy density, heat flux, momentum density, stress) and prove T^{ab} = T^{ba} with the shrinking-cube torque argument.
- Derive the local conservation laws T^{ab}_{,b} = 0 and N^a_{,a} = 0 by energy/particle bookkeeping on a small box.
- Deduce the perfect-fluid tensor diag(rho,p,p,p) in the MCRF from 'no heat conduction, no viscosity', and promote it to T^{ab} = (rho+p)U^aU^b + p eta^{ab}.
- Project the conservation law along U to obtain adiabatic flow (dS/dtau = 0) and perpendicular to U to obtain the relativistic Euler equation (rho+p)a_i + p_{,i} = 0, identifying rho+p as inertial mass density.
- Explain qualitatively why the full T, including pressure, must source gravity and why this makes pressure support self-defeating in very compact stars.
- State and apply the four-dimensional Gauss theorem with outward normal one-forms to turn the differential conservation laws into integral balance statements.

## Teaching approach

Schutz climbs a ladder of increasingly realistic matter models - dust, general single-component fluid, perfect fluid - and at every rung asks the same two questions: what is this quantity in the rest frame of the element, and what geometric object carries it into every other frame? Physical counting arguments (boxes of particles that Lorentz-contract, energy flowing through the faces of a small cube, torques on a shrinking element) come first; the one-form/vector/tensor language of Chapter 3 is then used to name what the counting found. Rank is diagnosed by how many one-forms a quantity needs (or how many gamma factors it picks up). A recurring device is to compute components in the MCRF, where everything is simple, and then write a frame-independent tensor equation that reduces to those components. The chapter ends by using the new tensor calculus on the perfect-fluid tensor, extracting thermodynamics and the Euler equation from one divergence, and by pointing forward to GR, where the whole of T must gravitate.

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, geometric-first, derivation-heavy, diagram-driven, spiral-revisit

**Narrative arc**

1. Motivation: astrophysical sources of gravity are often well modelled as perfect fluids; set up the continuum idea (fields defined through averaging over elements, each with its own MCRF) and characterize fluids by weak shear forces.
2. Simplest model, dust: count particles in the rest frame (n), then boost and let the box Lorentz-contract, giving density n gamma; count the particles in a slanted box that reach a surface in time dt to get the flux n gamma v^i.
3. Unification: the four numbers are the components of one vector N = nU; the Galilean split into an invariant density and a frame-dependent flux vector is an artefact of looking at three of four components; n = sqrt(-N.N) is a scalar like rest mass.
4. Geometric reinterpretation: a flux is the number of world lines crossing a unit three-volume of a hypersurface; choosing the hypersurface t = const shows density is a timelike flux; hypersurfaces are best described by normal one-forms, and the flux is <n~, N>. Bonus: a frame is naturally described by dt, with E = <dt, p>.
5. Energy and momentum of dust: energy density picks up two gamma factors, so it cannot be a vector component; count the one-forms needed and define T by its component meaning; build T = p(x)N = rho U(x)U from its MCRF values and check the moving-frame components against first principles.
6. General fluids: define all scalars in the element's MCRF (Table 4.1); use the first law and particle number conservation to reach n dq = drho - (rho+p)dn/n, and define T and S through an integrating factor.
7. Read T in the MCRF block by block (energy density, heat-conduction energy flux, associated momentum density, stress) and show stresses are the forces between neighbouring elements; prove symmetry of T^{ij} with a torque argument on a vanishing cube and equality of energy flux and momentum density from E = m.
8. Conservation: balance energy flowing through the six faces of a small cube against the rate of change inside and shrink the cube to reach T^{0b}_{,b} = 0, then T^{ab}_{,b} = 0; do the same for particles, N^a_{,a} = 0, justified by baryon conservation.
9. Perfect fluid: no heat conduction kills T^{0i}; no viscosity forces T^{ij} diagonal in every rotated frame, hence p delta^{ij}; write diag(rho,p,p,p), verify that (rho+p)U^aU^b + p eta^{ab} reproduces it, and promote to a tensor equation. Dust is the p = 0 case; pressure comes from random motion even without collisions.
10. First tensor-calculus workout: expand the divergence using particle conservation and U^a_{,b}U_a = 0; the component along U gives drho/dtau = (rho+p)/n dn/dtau, i.e. dS/dtau = 0 (adiabatic flow); the components orthogonal to U, evaluated in the MCRF, give (rho+p)a_i + p_{,i} = 0, the relativistic Euler equation with inertia rho+p.
11. Look ahead to GR: a relativistic source cannot be rest-mass density or T^{00} alone, so Einstein took all of T; pressure both gravitates and adds inertia, so in very compact stars raising pressure fails and collapse follows; p approaches rho only in neutron-star matter or ultra-hot gases.
12. Integral form: state the four-dimensional Gauss theorem, evaluate it on a coordinate box with outward normal one-forms (+-dt, +-dx), and read it as particle or energy bookkeeping; the exercises extend the machinery to collisionless gases, the virial theorem, radiation from a moving star and the Faraday tensor.

**Signature moves**

- Rest-frame first, then promote: find components in the MCRF where the physics is transparent, then exhibit a tensor expression that reproduces them, concluding it holds in all frames (dust T, perfect-fluid T, symmetry of T proven in one frame).
- Rank by counting: a number density needs one one-form (the surface) and picks up one gamma; energy density needs two one-forms (which component of momentum, which surface) and picks up two gammas, so it is a component of a (2 0) tensor.
- Spacetime flux picture: surfaces become world-tubes, particles become world lines, and 'density' is just the flux across the t = const slice, which unifies density and flux before any algebra.
- Explicit parallel with 3D electrostatics: the flux E.n of Gauss' law becomes the contraction of N with the unit normal one-form, keeping properties of the matter (N) separate from properties of the surface (n~).
- Shrinking-element limits used as reductio: unbalanced forces or asymmetric stresses would give a vanishing element infinite linear or angular acceleration.
- Newtonian checkpoints: Euler equation, continuity equation, advective derivative and Newton's second law are recovered as limits (in text and in Exercises 4.9, 4.10, 4.17, 4.18).
- Thermodynamics as a by-product of conservation: temperature and entropy enter only as an integrating factor of the first law, and entropy conservation later drops out of the U-component of T^{ab}_{,b}=0.
- Deliberate forward-looking physics payoff: the chapter closes its SR material by arguing, without equations, why pressure matters more in GR and leads to collapse.

## Section by section

### §4.1 Fluids — p.84 (pdf 102)

Motivates fluids as first-approximation sources in astrophysical GR, defines a continuum as matter described only by averaged fields, introduces the fluid element (big enough to average, small enough to be uniform) with its own MCRF, and separates fluids from solids by the strength of forces tangent to the interface between elements; a perfect fluid has no such forces, only pressure.

**Concepts:** continuum approximation, fluid element, momentarily comoving reference frame, fluid, perfect fluid, pressure

**Key moves**
- Gravity of a lake depends on bulk averages, not on individual molecules, motivating field variables.
- Element size is squeezed between graininess and inhomogeneity; if no such scale exists (very rarefied gas) the continuum picture fails.
- Rigidity is traced to shear forces that stop neighbouring elements sliding; weakness of these defines a fluid, their absence a perfect fluid.

### §4.2 Dust: the number-flux vector N — p.85 (pdf 103)

Defines dust as particles sharing a common rest frame, defines the scalar n as the rest-frame number density, derives the moving-frame density (one gamma factor from Lorentz contraction) and the flux across constant-x surfaces from a box of particles, and shows these are the components of N = nU; stresses that this unifies Galilean density and flux into a single frame-independent vector, with n = sqrt(-N.N).

**Concepts:** dust, number density, particle flux, number-flux four-vector, geometric object versus components, rest-frame scalar quantities

**Key moves**
- Lorentz contraction along the motion shrinks the volume by sqrt(1-v^2), raising the density to n/sqrt(1-v^2) (Eq. 4.2).
- Particles that cross area dA in time dt fill a box of length v dt; with a general velocity the box is a parallelepiped whose height is v^x dt, giving Eq. 4.3.
- N = nU has components (n gamma, n gamma v^i): time component = density, space components = fluxes (Eq. 4.5).
- Frame independence refers to the geometric object, not the components; the old 3-flux was frame dependent because it was only three of four components.
- N.N = -n^2 makes n a scalar, the analogue of rest mass (Eq. 4.6).

### §4.3 One-forms and surfaces — p.88 (pdf 106)

Redraws the flux calculation in a spacetime diagram, where a spatial surface sweeps out a three-dimensional world-tube and flux is the count of world lines through a unit three-volume of it; choosing a t = const slice shows density is a timelike flux. Surfaces phi = const are then represented by the gradient one-form or the unit normal one-form, and flux becomes <n~, N>. Finally an inertial frame is characterized by dt (its surfaces of simultaneity), giving E = <dt, p> without the minus sign of E = -p.U.

**Concepts:** number density as timelike flux, hypersurface, normal one-form, unit normal one-form, hypersurface volume element, flux as contraction of one-form with vector, frame as a one-form

**Key moves**
- Flux across a constant-x surface = world lines crossing a unit cube dt dy dz of the surface's world-tube (Fig. 4.4).
- Replacing the world-tube by a slice t = const, the same count is the number in a unit spatial volume: density (Fig. 4.5).
- Normal one-form d phi fixes the surface up to scale; normalize by |d phi| = |eta^{ab} phi_{,a} phi_{,b}|^{1/2} when the surface is not null (Eqs. 4.7-4.8).
- Check with phi = x: <dx, N> = N^x, the flux; phi = t gives N^0, the density.
- The frame one-form g(U, .) equals -dt, so dt (surfaces of simultaneity) specifies the frame and E = <dt, p> = p^0 (Eq. 4.10).

### §4.4 Dust again: the stress-energy tensor — p.91 (pdf 109)

Introduces rho as MCRF energy density (rho = mn for dust), shows that the moving-frame energy density rho/(1-v^2) has two gamma factors, and argues that energy density, energy flux, momentum density and momentum flux each need two one-forms, so they are components of a (2 0) tensor T. Defines T^{ab} as the flux of a-momentum across constant-x^b surfaces, interprets the four blocks, and identifies dust's T as p(x)N = rho U(x)U, whose boosted components match first-principles expectations and are symmetric.

**Concepts:** energy density, gamma-squared transformation of energy density, stress-energy tensor, dust stress-energy tensor, stress, symmetry of the stress-energy tensor

**Key moves**
- Energy per particle and number per volume each gain gamma: rho/(1-v^2) (Eq. 4.13), impossible for a vector component.
- One one-form selects the energy (or momentum) component, the other selects the surface: two slots, a (2 0) tensor.
- Component definition T(dx^a, dx^b) = T^{ab} (Eq. 4.14) with block meanings (Eqs. 4.15-4.18); linearity proof deferred to Exercise 4.5.
- In the MCRF dust has only T^{00} = rho; p(x)N has the same components, so T = rho U(x)U (Eq. 4.19) and T^{ab} = rho U^aU^b (Eq. 4.20).
- Moving-frame components (Eq. 4.21) are symmetric, foreshadowing a general result.

### §4.5 General fluids — p.93 (pdf 111)

Adds random particle motions and interparticle forces. All fluid scalars are defined in the (element- and time-dependent) MCRF, collected in Table 4.1. The first law with a fixed particle number gives n dq = drho - (rho+p)dn/n, whose integrating factor defines temperature and specific entropy. The MCRF blocks of T are interpreted (heat conduction appears in T^{0i}), T^{ij} is shown to be the force per area between adjacent elements, and T is proven symmetric: T^{ij} by a torque argument on a shrinking cube, T^{0i} = T^{i0} because moving energy is moving mass.

**Concepts:** momentarily comoving reference frame, rest-mass density, internal energy per particle, first law of thermodynamics for a fluid element, temperature and specific entropy via integrating factor, stress, heat conduction, symmetry of the stress-energy tensor, equality of energy flux and momentum density, two-parameter thermodynamic state

**Key moves**
- MCRF is local in space and time; every fluid scalar is its MCRF value (Table 4.1: U, n, N, rho, Pi, rho_0, T, p, S).
- dQ = dE + p dV with V = N/n and E = rho N/n gives n dq = drho - (rho+p) dn/n (Eqs. 4.22-4.24).
- A two-variable differential form always has an integrating factor: drho - (rho+p)dn/n = nT dS, so dq = T dS (Eqs. 4.25-4.26).
- Force F from element A on B across area A pours momentum at rate F: T^{ij} = F^i/A; forces normal to faces give a diagonal T^{ij}.
- Torque on a cube of side l is l^3(T^{xy} - T^{yx}) while inertia scales as l^5; angular acceleration would diverge as l -> 0 unless T^{xy} = T^{yx} (Eqs. 4.27-4.29).
- Energy flux = energy density x speed = mass density x speed = momentum density, so T^{0i} = T^{i0}.

### §4.6 Conservation of energy-momentum — p.98 (pdf 116)

Balances the energy flowing across the faces of a small cubical element against the rate of change of energy inside; dividing by the volume and shrinking the cube yields the four-divergence law T^{0b}_{,b} = 0, and by the same reasoning T^{ab}_{,b} = 0 for momentum, valid for any material in SR. The same argument for particles gives N^a_{,a} = (nU^a)_{,a} = 0, which the book assumes throughout by taking n to count baryons.

**Concepts:** local conservation of energy-momentum, four-divergence, conservation of particle number, baryon number conservation

**Key moves**
- Inflow through face at x = 0 minus outflow at x = l, for all three directions, equals d(l^3 T^{00})/dt (Eq. 4.30).
- Limit l -> 0 converts differences to derivatives (Eqs. 4.31-4.32), giving T^{0a}_{,a} = 0 (Eq. 4.33).
- Replacing the energy index by a momentum index gives T^{ab}_{,b} = 0 (Eq. 4.34).
- Particle bookkeeping gives N^a_{,a} = 0 (Eq. 4.35); baryon number makes this almost no restriction.

### §4.7 Perfect fluids — p.100 (pdf 118)

Defines a perfect fluid as having no viscosity and no heat conduction in the MCRF. These kill T^{0i} and force T^{ij} to be diagonal in every rotated MCRF, hence proportional to delta^{ij}, the proportionality constant being the pressure. The MCRF matrix diag(rho,p,p,p) is matched by (rho+p)U^aU^b + p eta^{ab} and promoted to T = (rho+p)U(x)U + p g^{-1}. Dust is the pressureless case and pressure is momentum flux from random motion. Expanding T^{ab}_{,b} = 0 with particle conservation and U^a_{,b}U_a = 0 gives, along U, conservation of specific entropy, and orthogonal to U, the relativistic Euler equation (rho+p)a_i + p_{,i} = 0.

**Concepts:** perfect fluid, viscosity, pressure, perfect-fluid stress-energy tensor, pressure as momentum flux from random motion, orthogonality of four-velocity and its gradient, derivative along a world line, adiabatic flow, four-acceleration, relativistic Euler equation, inertial mass density rho + p

**Key moves**
- No heat conduction: energy moves only with particles, so T^{0i} = T^{i0} = 0 in the MCRF.
- No viscosity: T^{ij} diagonal for all orientations of the spatial axes, so T^{ij} = p delta^{ij}; six functions reduce to one.
- Matrix (Eq. 4.36) reproduced by (rho+p)U^aU^b + p eta^{ab} (Eq. 4.37), hence the tensor Eq. 4.38.
- Pressure-free perfect fluid = dust; a collisionless gas still has pressure because pressure is momentum flux.
- Differentiate U^aU_a = -1 to get U^a_{,b}U_a = 0 (Eqs. 4.42-4.44).
- Contract Eq. 4.45 with U_a: U^b[rho_{,b} - (rho+p)n_{,b}/n] = 0, i.e. drho/dtau = (rho+p)/n dn/dtau, which with Eq. 4.25 means dS/dtau = 0 (Eqs. 4.46-4.50).
- In the MCRF U^i = 0 but its derivatives are not zero; the spatial components give (rho+p)U_{i,b}U^b + p_{,i} = 0, i.e. (rho+p)a_i + p_{,i} = 0 (Eqs. 4.51-4.54).
- Compare to the Newtonian rho a + grad p = 0: rho+p is the inertial mass density, and only a pressure difference across the element pushes it.

### §4.8 Importance for general relativity — p.103 (pdf 121)

Looks ahead: a relativistic theory cannot use rest-mass density as its gravitational source (mass and energy interconvert, and precision experiments rule it out), nor T^{00} alone (frame dependent), so Einstein took the whole tensor T. Pressure therefore both sources gravity and contributes inertia via rho+p. In a compact star the pressure gradient needed to resist gravity is larger than in Newtonian theory and itself adds to gravity; once p is comparable with rho, no pressure suffices and collapse is inevitable. p ~ rho only in neutron-star matter or relativistic gases.

**Concepts:** stress-energy tensor as source of gravity, pressure as a source of gravity, relativistic gravitational collapse, inertial mass density rho + p

**Key moves**
- Rule out rest-mass density (not relativistic, conflicts with experiment) and T^{00} alone (needs a preferred frame); only the full tensor is acceptable.
- Hydrostatic support: required pressure gradient p_{,i} = -(rho+p) a_i exceeds the Newtonian value.
- Feedback loop: extra pressure is extra source, requiring more pressure; for p comparable to rho this runs away into collapse.

### §4.9 Gauss' law — p.104 (pdf 122)

States the four-dimensional divergence theorem relating the four-volume integral of V^a_{,a} to the flux of V through the bounding three-surface with outward unit normal one-forms, and evaluates the surface term on a coordinate box: the t = const faces carry +-V^0, the x = const faces carry +-V^x. For N or T^{0a} this reads as 'change in the amount inside equals net flow across the walls', the integral version of particle and energy conservation.

**Concepts:** Gauss' law in four dimensions, outward normal orientation on spacetime boundaries, unit normal one-form, local conservation of energy-momentum, conservation of particle number

**Key moves**
- Proof taken over from 3D without detail (Eq. 4.57).
- Outward normals on a box: dt on the later face, -dt on the earlier face (outward = to the past), +-dx on the spatial faces (Fig. 4.9).
- Surface integral becomes differences of V^0 between times plus differences of V^x between walls (Eq. 4.58); with divergence zero, content change equals boundary flux.

### §4.10 Bibliography — p.106 (pdf 124)

Points readers weak in thermodynamics or fluid mechanics to Fermi and to Landau & Lifshitz, to Landau & Lifshitz and Jackson for the electromagnetic stress-energy tensor, to Israel & Stewart for dissipative relativistic fluids, and to Andreasson for collisionless (kinetic) matter in GR.

**Concepts:** dissipative relativistic fluids, collisionless gas

**Key moves**
- Signals that viscosity and heat flow in relativity are subtle and deferred to specialist literature.
- Offers kinetic theory as an alternative continuum model to fluids.

## Concepts

### continuum approximation

*technique · introduced* · also: continuum

Treating a system of enormously many particles through smooth fields (density, velocity, pressure, temperature, ...) defined at every event, obtained by averaging over elements; valid only when a scale exists that is large compared with interparticle spacing yet small compared with the scale on which averages vary.

**How introduced:** Opens the chapter: the gravity of a lake depends on averaged properties, not on which molecule is where; the need for an averaging scale is derived from the fact that averages vary with depth or altitude.

**Notes:** Exercise 4.1 asks the reader to test the approximation on planets, lava, traffic and plasmas.

**Where:** SCH §4.1 p.84 (pdf 102)

### fluid element

*definition · introduced* · also: element

A collection of particles large enough that individual particles are irrelevant but small enough that density, mean velocity, temperature etc. can be taken as single values; in the continuum limit it is idealized as a point.

**How introduced:** Defined as the answer to 'how big a region should one average over?'; admitted to be an imprecise but useful term.

**Prerequisites:** continuum approximation

**Where:** SCH §4.1 p.84 (pdf 102); SCH §4.5 p.93 (pdf 111)

### momentarily comoving reference frame

*definition · revisited* · also: MCRF, rest frame of a fluid element

For a given fluid element at a given event, the inertial frame in which the element's total spatial momentum is zero at that instant; it varies from element to element and from moment to moment because elements accelerate and move relative to each other.

**How introduced:** Recalled from particle kinematics (§2.3) and assigned to each fluid element; later stressed that it is truly momentary and local, and used as the frame in which all fluid scalars are defined and all component calculations are done.

**Prerequisites:** fluid element, four-velocity

**Notes:** Chapter 5 reuses this idea as the analogy for local inertial frames in curved spacetime.

**Where:** SCH §4.1 p.84 (pdf 102); SCH §4.5 p.93 (pdf 111); SCH §4.7 p.102 (pdf 120)

### fluid

*definition · introduced*

A continuum in which the forces between neighbouring elements that act parallel to their common interface (shear or anti-slip forces) are weak compared with the normal push-pull force (pressure), so the material flows.

**How introduced:** Contrasted with rigid solids by asking what makes a substance rigid; the answer is tangential forces that stop elements sliding. The boundary is acknowledged to be fuzzy since most solids flow under high pressure.

**Prerequisites:** continuum approximation, fluid element

**Where:** SCH §4.1 p.84 (pdf 102); SCH §4.1 p.85 (pdf 103)

### perfect fluid

*solution-or-model · core* · also: ideal fluid

A fluid with no viscosity and no heat conduction in the MCRF of each element, so that the only interaction between neighbouring elements is isotropic pressure; its stress-energy tensor is fully fixed by rho, p and U.

**How introduced:** Previewed in §4.1 as a fluid with zero anti-slip forces; defined precisely in §4.7 by the two MCRF conditions, and called a relativistic generalization of the ideal gas and, after dust, the simplest fluid.

**Prerequisites:** fluid, viscosity, heat conduction, stress-energy tensor

$$
T^{\alpha\beta}=(\rho+p)U^\alpha U^\beta+p\eta^{\alpha\beta}
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.1 p.85 (pdf 103); SCH §4.7 p.100 (pdf 118)

### dust

*solution-or-model · core* · also: pressureless perfect fluid, incoherent matter

A collection of particles with no random motions: at each event all nearby particles share one velocity, so in the MCRF every particle is at rest; it has zero pressure and T = rho U(x)U.

**How introduced:** Chosen as the simplest possible fluid to launch the relativistic description; the book jokes about the name. Later identified as the p = 0 case of the perfect fluid.

**Prerequisites:** fluid element, four-velocity

$$
\mathbf T=\rho\,\vec U\otimes\vec U
$$

**Notes:** The book's first definition says all particles are at rest in 'some Lorentz frame'; for a general flow this should be read element by element.

**Where:** SCH §4.2 p.85 (pdf 103); SCH §4.4 p.93 (pdf 111); SCH §4.7 p.101 (pdf 119)

### number density

*physical-quantity · core* · also: rest density, proper number density, n

The scalar n equal to the number of particles per unit volume measured in the element's MCRF; in a frame where the particles move with speed v the measured number per unit volume is larger, n/sqrt(1-v^2).

**How introduced:** Found by counting particles in a box in the rest frame, then noting that in a moving frame the same particles occupy a Lorentz-contracted box (Fig. 4.1).

**Prerequisites:** Lorentz contraction, momentarily comoving reference frame

$$
n/\sqrt{1-v^2}
$$

$$
n=(-\vec N\cdot\vec N)^{1/2}
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.2 p.85 (pdf 103); SCH §4.2 p.86 (pdf 104); SCH §4.2 p.88 (pdf 106)

### particle flux

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: flux across a surface, number flux

The number of particles crossing unit area of a given surface per unit time; it depends on both the frame (through area and time) and the orientation of the surface, and for dust across a constant-x surface equals n v^x/sqrt(1-v^2).

**How introduced:** Computed by drawing the box (or slanted parallelepiped) of particles that will reach the surface within a time interval (Figs. 4.2-4.3).

**Prerequisites:** number density

$$
(\text{flux})^{\bar x}=\frac{n v^{\bar x}}{\sqrt{1-v^2}}
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 4.2 criticizes the loose phrase 'flux in the x direction': the flux is across a surface of constant x.

**Where:** SCH §4.2 p.85 (pdf 103); SCH §4.2 p.86 (pdf 104); SCH §4.2 p.87 (pdf 105)

### number-flux four-vector

*physical-quantity · core* · also: number-flux vector, particle current, N

The four-vector N = nU whose component along the one-form dt in any frame is the particle density there and whose components along dx^i are the particle fluxes across surfaces of constant x^i.

**How introduced:** Obtained by noticing that the density and fluxes of dust computed separately are exactly n times the components of the four-velocity; celebrated as a unification of Galilean density and flux.

**Prerequisites:** four-velocity, number density, particle flux

$$
\vec N=n\vec U
$$

$$
\vec N\to(n\gamma,\,n\gamma v^x,\,n\gamma v^y,\,n\gamma v^z)
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.2 p.87 (pdf 105); SCH §4.2 p.88 (pdf 106); SCH §4.3 p.90 (pdf 108); SCH §4.6 p.99 (pdf 117)

### geometric object versus components

*principle · revisited* · also: frame independence of tensors

A vector or tensor exists independently of any frame and gives frame-independent numbers when fed frame-independent arguments, while its components, being its values on a particular basis, change from frame to frame.

**How introduced:** Re-emphasized from Chapter 3 using N: the prerelativistic 3-flux was frame dependent because it was only three of the four components of a single invariant object.

**Prerequisites:** one-form, number-flux four-vector

**Where:** SCH §4.2 p.88 (pdf 106)

### rest-frame scalar quantities

*convention · developed* · also: MCRF definition of fluid scalars

The rule that every scalar attribute of a fluid element (number density, energy density, pressure, temperature, specific entropy, ...) is defined as its value measured in that element's MCRF, which makes it a Lorentz scalar.

**How introduced:** Motivated by the analogy between n and rest mass, then stated as a blanket convention for general fluids and tabulated in Table 4.1.

**Prerequisites:** momentarily comoving reference frame

**Notes:** The book limits itself to single-component fluids (one species of particle), so interpenetrating flows with several four-velocities at one event are excluded.

**Where:** SCH §4.2 p.88 (pdf 106); SCH §4.5 p.93 (pdf 111); SCH §4.5 p.94 (pdf 112)

### number density as timelike flux

*principle · developed* · also: density as flux across t = const

Number density in a frame is the flux of particle world lines across a hypersurface of constant time in that frame, just as spatial fluxes are world-line crossings of hypersurfaces of constant x^i.

**How introduced:** Shown with a pair of spacetime diagrams: first world lines crossing the world-tube of a surface x = const in an interval dt, then the same world lines crossing a slice t = const across an interval dx.

**Prerequisites:** particle flux, hypersurface, world line

**Where:** SCH §4.3 p.88 (pdf 106); SCH §4.3 p.89 (pdf 107)

### hypersurface

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: three-surface, world-tube of a surface

A three-dimensional surface in four-dimensional spacetime, typically a level set phi(t,x,y,z) = const; a two-dimensional spatial surface at rest sweeps out such a three-surface over time, and a surface of simultaneity is another example.

**How introduced:** Arises when the spatial surface of the flux calculation is followed through time in a spacetime diagram; its 'unit volume' is dt dy dz.

**Prerequisites:** world line

$$
\phi(t,x,y,z)=\text{const}
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.3 p.88 (pdf 106); SCH §4.3 p.90 (pdf 108); SCH §4.9 p.104 (pdf 122)

### normal one-form

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: gradient one-form as normal

For a hypersurface phi = const, the gradient one-form d phi (components phi_{,a}); it vanishes on every vector tangent to the surface and so determines the surface, but only up to an overall factor.

**How introduced:** Introduced as a cleaner representation of surfaces than the 'clumsy' description via world-tubes.

**Prerequisites:** one-form, gradient one-form, hypersurface

$$
\tilde{\mathrm d}\phi
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.3 p.90 (pdf 108)

### unit normal one-form

*mathematical-object · core* · also: n tilde, unit-normal one-form

The normal one-form rescaled to unit magnitude using the inverse metric, n~ = d phi/|d phi| with |d phi| = |eta^{ab} phi_{,a} phi_{,b}|^{1/2}; defined only for non-null hypersurfaces.

**How introduced:** Presented in direct analogy with the unit normal of 3D vector calculus, and later used with an outward sense in the 4D Gauss theorem.

**Prerequisites:** normal one-form, metric tensor

$$
\tilde n=\tilde{\mathrm d}\phi/|\tilde{\mathrm d}\phi|
$$

$$
|\tilde{\mathrm d}\phi|=|\eta^{\alpha\beta}\phi_{,\alpha}\phi_{,\beta}|^{1/2}
$$

**Notes:** The book warns that n~ has nothing to do with the number density n despite the shared letter.

**Where:** SCH §4.3 p.90 (pdf 108); SCH §4.9 p.104 (pdf 122)

### hypersurface volume element

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: surface element one-form, d^3S

The directed three-volume element of a hypersurface, formed as the unit normal one-form times the coordinate three-volume dx^a dx^b dx^c spanning the surface; a unit three-volume is represented by n~ itself.

**How introduced:** Generalized from the vector area element (unit normal times area) of 3D Gauss' law.

**Prerequisites:** unit normal one-form

$$
\tilde n\,\mathrm dx^\alpha\mathrm dx^\beta\mathrm dx^\gamma
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.3 p.90 (pdf 108); SCH §4.9 p.104 (pdf 122)

### flux as contraction of one-form with vector

*operation · core* · also: <n~, N>

The flux of a conserved current N across a hypersurface is the number obtained by contracting N with the surface's unit normal one-form, <n~, N> = N^a n_a; the matter and the surface enter as separate objects.

**How introduced:** Compared with E.n in electrostatics; checked by choosing phi = x (giving N^x) and phi = t (giving N^0, the density). Presented as a physical instance of a vector being a function of one-forms.

**Prerequisites:** unit normal one-form, number-flux four-vector

$$
\langle\tilde n,\vec N\rangle=N^\alpha n_\alpha
$$

$$
\langle\tilde{\mathrm d}x,\vec N\rangle=N^x
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.3 p.90 (pdf 108)

### frame as a one-form

*principle · introduced* · also: dt as a family of simultaneity surfaces

An inertial frame can be specified by the one-form dt, pictured as its stack of surfaces of simultaneity, instead of by its four-velocity; the two are related by g(U, .) = -dt, and energy in the frame is E = <dt, p> = p^0.

**How introduced:** Offered as a 'useful fact' after the surface discussion: lowering the frame's U gives components (-1,0,0,0), identical to -dt.

**Prerequisites:** one-form, metric tensor, four-momentum

$$
U_\alpha=\eta_{\alpha\beta}U^\beta,\ U_0=-1,\ U_i=0
$$

$$
E=\langle\tilde{\mathrm d}t,\vec p\rangle=p^0
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.3 p.90 (pdf 108); SCH §4.3 p.91 (pdf 109)

### energy density

*physical-quantity · core* · also: rho, total mass-energy density

The scalar rho equal to the total energy per unit volume (rest mass plus random kinetic, chemical, interaction energies) measured in the MCRF; for dust rho = mn.

**How introduced:** Introduced for dust by multiplying particle energy m by number density n in the rest frame; broadened in Table 4.1 to all forms of energy.

**Prerequisites:** number density, rest-frame scalar quantities

$$
\rho=mn\ \text{(dust)}
$$

$$
\rho=n(m+\Pi)
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.4 p.91 (pdf 109); SCH §4.5 p.94 (pdf 112)

### gamma-squared transformation of energy density

*principle · developed*

Energy per unit volume of dust seen in a frame where it moves is rho/(1-v^2): one gamma from the increased energy per particle and one from Lorentz contraction of the volume, which shows energy density is not a component of a four-vector.

**How introduced:** Computed directly after the energy density is defined, then used as the key clue that a second-rank tensor is needed.

**Prerequisites:** energy density, number density, Lorentz contraction

$$
\rho/(1-v^2)
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.4 p.91 (pdf 109); SCH §4.4 p.92 (pdf 110)

### stress-energy tensor

*physical-quantity · core* · also: energy-momentum tensor, T

The symmetric (2 0) tensor T whose component T^{ab} = T(dx^a, dx^b) in any inertial frame is the flux of the a-component of four-momentum across a surface of constant x^b: T^{00} energy density, T^{0i} energy flux, T^{i0} momentum density, T^{ij} momentum flux (stress).

**How introduced:** Motivated by counting one-forms: selecting 'energy' (a component of p) needs one, selecting 'density' or 'flux across a surface' needs another. Defined via components in an arbitrary frame, with tensor character left to Exercise 4.5.

**Prerequisites:** one-form, four-momentum, particle flux, gamma-squared transformation of energy density

$$
T^{\alpha\beta}=\mathbf T(\tilde{\mathrm d}x^\alpha,\tilde{\mathrm d}x^\beta)
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.4 p.92 (pdf 110); SCH §4.5 p.95 (pdf 113); SCH §4.7 p.100 (pdf 118); SCH §4.8 p.103 (pdf 121)

### dust stress-energy tensor

*solution-or-model · core*

For dust of particle rest mass m, T = p(x)N = mn U(x)U = rho U(x)U, with components rho U^aU^b in any frame: rho gamma^2 energy density, rho gamma^2 v^i energy flux and momentum density, rho gamma^2 v^iv^j momentum flux.

**How introduced:** Only T^{00} = rho is non-zero in the MCRF; the outer product p(x)N has exactly that component pattern there, so the two tensors are equal.

**Prerequisites:** stress-energy tensor, dust, outer product of vectors

$$
T^{\alpha\beta}=\rho U^\alpha U^\beta
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.4 p.92 (pdf 110); SCH §4.4 p.93 (pdf 111)

### rest-mass density

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: rho_0, baryonic mass density

rho_0 = mn, the energy associated with the rest mass of the particles alone per unit MCRF volume; it differs from rho by the internal energy density n Pi.

**How introduced:** Listed in Table 4.1; used in §4.8 as the quantity closest to Newton's gravitational source, which a relativistic theory must replace.

**Prerequisites:** number density, energy density

$$
\rho_0=mn
$$

$$
\rho=\rho_0+n\Pi
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.5 p.94 (pdf 112); SCH §4.8 p.103 (pdf 121)

### internal energy per particle

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: Pi, specific internal energy

Pi = rho/n - m, the energy per particle over and above its rest mass (thermal kinetic, chemical, interaction), so that rho = n(m + Pi).

**How introduced:** Defined in Table 4.1 as a catch-all for non-rest-mass energies.

**Prerequisites:** energy density, number density

$$
\Pi:=\rho/n-m
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.5 p.94 (pdf 112)

### two-parameter thermodynamic state

*principle · mention* · also: equation of state (implicit)

For a simple single-component fluid in local equilibrium, all thermodynamic quantities (p, T, S, ...) are functions of any two independent ones, e.g. rho and n.

**How introduced:** Asserted without proof ('it can be shown') to justify treating drho - (rho+p)dn/n as a differential form in two variables.

**Prerequisites:** energy density, number density

**Where:** SCH §4.5 p.95 (pdf 113)

### first law of thermodynamics for a fluid element

*law · developed*

Energy conservation for a fluid element in its MCRF: heat absorbed equals increase in energy plus work p dV; with a fixed number of particles this becomes n dq = drho - (rho+p) dn/n, q being heat per particle.

**How introduced:** Derived using V = N/n and E = rho V to eliminate the volume, explicitly as a statement of energy conservation in the MCRF.

**Prerequisites:** energy density, number density, pressure, conservation of particle number

$$
\Delta Q=\Delta E+p\Delta V
$$

$$
n\Delta q=\Delta\rho-\frac{\rho+p}{n}\Delta n
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.5 p.94 (pdf 112); SCH §4.5 p.95 (pdf 113)

### temperature and specific entropy via integrating factor

*definition · introduced* · also: S, entropy per particle

Because drho - (rho+p)dn/n is a differential form in two variables it admits an integrating factor: it equals A dB, and one defines temperature T = A/n and specific entropy S = B, giving drho - (rho+p)dn/n = nT dS and dq = T dS.

**How introduced:** Introduced as a mathematical convenience flowing from the first law, with the assurance (not proved) that T and S coincide with thermodynamic temperature and the entropy of the second law.

**Prerequisites:** first law of thermodynamics for a fluid element, two-parameter thermodynamic state

$$
\mathrm d\rho-(\rho+p)\mathrm dn/n=nT\,\mathrm dS
$$

$$
\Delta q=T\Delta S
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 4.8 shows that the heat one-form is not exact: there is no function q whose gradient it is.

**Where:** SCH §4.5 p.95 (pdf 113)

### heat conduction

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: energy flux in the MCRF

Transport of energy without bulk motion of the fluid; in the MCRF it appears as non-zero T^{0i}, and because moving energy carries momentum, as non-zero T^{i0}.

**How introduced:** Read off from the block structure of T in the MCRF; its absence is one of the two defining conditions of a perfect fluid.

**Prerequisites:** stress-energy tensor, momentarily comoving reference frame

**Where:** SCH §4.5 p.95 (pdf 113); SCH §4.7 p.100 (pdf 118)

### stress

*physical-quantity · core* · also: momentum flux, T^{ij}, stress tensor

The spatial block T^{ij} of the stress-energy tensor: the flux of i-momentum across a surface of constant x^j, equivalently the i-component of force per unit area that the material on one side exerts on the other across that surface.

**How introduced:** Interpreted using two adjacent cubical elements (Fig. 4.6): a force F across the shared face transfers momentum at rate F, so the momentum flux is F/A. Forces normal to faces give diagonal stresses; tangential ones (viscosity, rigidity) give off-diagonal stresses. Newton's second law can be used here because in the MCRF the velocities vanish; the force from A pours momentum into B whether or not B ends up accelerating, since B's net motion depends on all its neighbours.

**Prerequisites:** stress-energy tensor, Newton's second law

$$
T^{ij}=F^i/A
$$

**Notes:** In Newtonian physics T^{ij} is the familiar engineering stress tensor, which gives T its name.

**Where:** SCH §4.4 p.92 (pdf 110); SCH §4.5 p.95 (pdf 113); SCH §4.5 p.96 (pdf 114); SCH §4.5 p.98 (pdf 116)

### symmetry of the stress-energy tensor

*theorem · core* · also: T^{ab} = T^{ba}

The stress-energy tensor is symmetric, T(q~, r~) = T(r~, q~); in the MCRF this splits into T^{ij} = T^{ji} (from finite angular acceleration of small elements) and T^{0i} = T^{i0} (energy flux equals momentum density).

**How introduced:** First noticed in the dust components (Eq. 4.21), then proved for any material by showing symmetry of components in one frame (the MCRF), which implies symmetry of the tensor and so of its components in every frame.

**Prerequisites:** stress, stress-energy tensor, torque and moment of inertia

$$
T^{ij}=T^{ji}
$$

$$
T^{0i}=T^{i0}
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.4 p.93 (pdf 111); SCH §4.5 p.96 (pdf 114); SCH §4.5 p.97 (pdf 115); SCH §4.5 p.98 (pdf 116)

### equality of energy flux and momentum density

*principle · introduced*

In relativity (c = 1) energy and inertial mass are the same thing, so the rate at which energy flows through unit area equals the density of momentum carried: T^{0i} = T^{i0}.

**How introduced:** A two-line physical argument following the torque proof.

**Prerequisites:** stress-energy tensor, mass-energy equivalence

$$
T^{0i}=T^{i0}
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.5 p.98 (pdf 116)

### local conservation of energy-momentum

*law · core* · also: divergence-free stress-energy tensor, T^{ab}_{,b} = 0

In special relativity the energy and momentum of any closed material system satisfy T^{ab}_{,b} = 0: in each frame the time rate of change of the density of a-momentum is balanced by the divergence of its flux.

**How introduced:** Derived by energy bookkeeping on a small cube (Fig. 4.8) with inflow and outflow through opposite faces, followed by the limit l -> 0; momentum components follow by relabelling the index.

**Prerequisites:** stress-energy tensor, partial derivative, four-divergence

$$
T^{0\alpha}{}_{,\alpha}=0
$$

$$
T^{\alpha\beta}{}_{,\beta}=0
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 4.20 shows how an external four-force appears as a source, and Exercise 4.9 recovers Newton's second law from the spatial components.

**Where:** SCH §4.6 p.98 (pdf 116); SCH §4.6 p.99 (pdf 117); SCH §4.7 p.101 (pdf 119); SCH §4.9 p.105 (pdf 123)

### four-divergence

*operation · developed*

The contraction V^a_{,a} = dV^0/dt + dV^x/dx + dV^y/dy + dV^z/dz of the gradient of a vector field with itself; a frame-independent scalar in SR.

**How introduced:** Recognized in the conservation laws ('just a four-dimensional divergence').

**Prerequisites:** partial derivative, tensor contraction

$$
V^\alpha{}_{,\alpha}
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.6 p.98 (pdf 116); SCH §4.6 p.99 (pdf 117); SCH §4.9 p.104 (pdf 122)

### conservation of particle number

*law · core* · also: continuity equation, N^a_{,a} = 0

If particles are neither created nor destroyed, the number-flux vector is divergence free, (nU^a)_{,a} = 0; in the Newtonian limit this is dn/dt + div(n v) = 0.

**How introduced:** Derived by the same box argument as energy conservation, then assumed for all fluids in the book.

**Prerequisites:** number-flux four-vector, four-divergence

$$
N^\alpha{}_{,\alpha}=(nU^\alpha)_{,\alpha}=0
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.6 p.99 (pdf 117); SCH §4.7 p.101 (pdf 119); SCH §4.9 p.105 (pdf 123)

### baryon number conservation

*principle · mention* · also: baryon count

The total number of baryons (protons, neutrons and heavier unstable relatives that decay into them) is conserved in all observed processes, even when rest mass and particle identity change, so n can always be taken as baryon density.

**How introduced:** A reassuring aside explaining why particle conservation is hardly a restriction; notes theoretical exceptions (grand unified proton decay, black-hole formation and evaporation) that are unobserved or irrelevant for most problems.

**Prerequisites:** conservation of particle number

**Where:** SCH §4.6 p.99 (pdf 117)

### viscosity

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: shear stress

Forces between neighbouring fluid elements acting parallel to their common interface; they appear as off-diagonal spatial stresses T^{ij} (i != j).

**How introduced:** Identified with the 'anti-slipping' forces of §4.1; its absence is one of the two perfect-fluid conditions.

**Prerequisites:** stress

**Where:** SCH §4.5 p.96 (pdf 114); SCH §4.7 p.100 (pdf 118)

### pressure

*physical-quantity · core* · also: p, isotropic pressure

The scalar p measured in the MCRF such that, when stresses are isotropic, the force per unit area across any surface is p along the surface normal: T^{ij} = p delta^{ij} in the MCRF.

**How introduced:** Named in §4.1 as the normal push-pull force; defined precisely in §4.7: absence of viscosity makes T^{ij} diagonal for every rotation of axes, and only multiples of the identity have that property.

**Prerequisites:** stress, viscosity, rest-frame scalar quantities

$$
T^{ij}=p\,\delta^{ij}
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 4.11 supplies the rotation-invariance lemma.

**Where:** SCH §4.1 p.85 (pdf 103); SCH §4.7 p.100 (pdf 118); SCH §4.7 p.101 (pdf 119)

### pressure as momentum flux from random motion

*principle · developed* · also: kinetic pressure

Pressure is the flux of momentum across a surface in the MCRF, whether carried by forces between particles or by particles themselves crossing the surface; hence any matter with random velocities has pressure, even a collisionless gas, and only matter with no random motion (dust) is pressureless.

**How introduced:** Brief 'aside on the meaning of pressure' drawn from comparing the perfect-fluid and dust tensors; made quantitative in Exercise 4.22.

**Prerequisites:** pressure, dust, stress

**Where:** SCH §4.7 p.101 (pdf 119)

### perfect-fluid stress-energy tensor

*solution-or-model · core*

T = (rho+p) U(x)U + p g^{-1}, with components (rho+p)U^aU^b + p eta^{ab}; in the MCRF it is diag(rho, p, p, p).

**How introduced:** The MCRF matrix is derived from the perfect-fluid conditions; a covariant expression is guessed and checked component by component (e.g. the 00 entry gives rho+p-p = rho) and then declared frame invariant.

**Prerequisites:** perfect fluid, pressure, energy density, four-velocity, metric tensor

$$
\mathbf T=(\rho+p)\vec U\otimes\vec U+p\,\mathbf g^{-1}
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.7 p.100 (pdf 118); SCH §4.7 p.101 (pdf 119)

### orthogonality of four-velocity and its gradient

*identity · developed* · also: U_a U^a_{,b} = 0

Because U^aU_a = -1 everywhere, differentiating gives U_a U^a_{,b} = 0: the change of the four-velocity in any direction is orthogonal to the four-velocity itself.

**How introduced:** Proved as a lemma needed for the perfect-fluid equations, using that eta is constant and symmetric (Eqs. 4.42-4.44).

**Prerequisites:** four-velocity, metric tensor, partial derivative

$$
U^\alpha{}_{,\beta}U_\alpha=0
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.7 p.101 (pdf 119); SCH §4.7 p.102 (pdf 120)

### derivative along a world line

*operation · revisited* · also: d/dtau, convective derivative

For a field f, the rate of change following a fluid element is df/dtau = U^b f_{,b}, the directional derivative along the four-velocity.

**How introduced:** Invoked as already known when the term p_{,b}U^b appears in the U-projection of the conservation law.

**Prerequisites:** four-velocity, gradient one-form

$$
\frac{\mathrm df}{\mathrm d\tau}=U^\beta f_{,\beta}
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.7 p.101 (pdf 119); SCH §4.7 p.102 (pdf 120)

### adiabatic flow

*phenomenon · core* · also: conservation of specific entropy, isentropic flow along world lines

In a particle-conserving perfect fluid each element keeps its specific entropy, U^aS_{,a} = dS/dtau = 0, because no heat is conducted.

**How introduced:** Emerges from contracting the divergence of the perfect-fluid tensor with U_a: the result drho/dtau = (rho+p)/n dn/dtau is compared with the first law nT dS = drho - (rho+p)dn/n.

**Prerequisites:** perfect-fluid stress-energy tensor, temperature and specific entropy via integrating factor, orthogonality of four-velocity and its gradient, conservation of particle number

$$
U^\alpha S_{,\alpha}=0
$$

**Notes:** The book stresses that thermodynamic energy conservation is the component of T^{ab}_{,b}=0 parallel to U.

**Where:** SCH §4.7 p.102 (pdf 120)

### four-acceleration

*physical-quantity · revisited* · also: a

a^m = U^m_{,b}U^b = dU^m/dtau, the rate of change of four-velocity along the world line; orthogonal to U, so in the MCRF it has only spatial components.

**How introduced:** Recalled as a definition when the spatial part of the fluid equation produces U_{i,b}U^b; Exercise 4.17 shows it reduces to the advective derivative Dv/Dt.

**Prerequisites:** four-velocity, derivative along a world line

$$
a^\mu=U^\mu{}_{,\beta}U^\beta
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.7 p.103 (pdf 121); SCH p.107 (pdf 125)

### relativistic Euler equation

*law · core* · also: relativistic equation of motion of a perfect fluid

The components of T^{ab}_{,b} = 0 orthogonal to U for a perfect fluid, which in the MCRF read (rho+p)a_i + p_{,i} = 0: the pressure gradient accelerates the element, resisted by the inertia rho+p.

**How introduced:** Obtained by evaluating the spatial components of the expanded conservation law in the MCRF, where U^i = 0 but its derivatives need not vanish, then compared with the Newtonian rho a + grad p = 0.

**Prerequisites:** perfect-fluid stress-energy tensor, four-acceleration, local conservation of energy-momentum, momentarily comoving reference frame

$$
(\rho+p)a_i+p_{,i}=0
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 4.18 asks for a sharper proof that -grad p is the net force per unit volume.

**Where:** SCH §4.7 p.102 (pdf 120); SCH §4.7 p.103 (pdf 121)

### inertial mass density rho + p

*principle · developed* · also: enthalpy density (standard name for rho+p)

In relativistic fluid dynamics the combination rho + p, not rho, measures resistance of a fluid element to acceleration by pressure gradients; pressure contributes to inertia.

**How introduced:** Read off from the relativistic Euler equation by analogy with F = ma, then used in §4.8 to argue that relativistic stars need larger pressure gradients.

**Prerequisites:** relativistic Euler equation

$$
(\rho+p)a_i=-p_{,i}
$$

**Notes:** Standard texts call rho+p the enthalpy density; Schutz does not use that name.

**Where:** SCH §4.7 p.103 (pdf 121); SCH §4.8 p.104 (pdf 122)

### stress-energy tensor as source of gravity

*principle · introduced* · also: T as gravitational source

In GR the gravitational field is sourced by the whole stress-energy tensor, not by rest-mass density or energy density alone: every component, including momenta and stresses, gravitates.

**How introduced:** Argued by elimination: rest mass is not invariantly separable from other energy and a rest-mass source conflicts with precision experiments; T^{00} alone would single out a frame; so Einstein chose T.

**Prerequisites:** stress-energy tensor, rest-mass density

**Where:** SCH §4.8 p.103 (pdf 121)

### pressure as a source of gravity

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: active gravitational effect of pressure

Because all of T sources gravity, pressure adds to the gravitational pull of matter; this, together with pressure's contribution to inertia, makes pressure more important in GR than in Newtonian theory.

**How introduced:** Stated in §4.8 as the second key lesson of fluids for GR.

**Prerequisites:** stress-energy tensor as source of gravity, pressure

**Where:** SCH §4.8 p.103 (pdf 121); SCH §4.8 p.104 (pdf 122)

### relativistic gravitational collapse

*phenomenon · mention* · also: failure of pressure support

In sufficiently compact stars the extra pressure needed to support the star itself strengthens gravity and raises the required pressure further; once p is comparable with rho no pressure gradient can hold the star up and it must collapse.

**How introduced:** Qualitative feedback argument using -a_i = p_{,i}/(rho+p) and pressure as a source; details deferred to later chapters.

**Prerequisites:** pressure as a source of gravity, inertial mass density rho + p

$$
-a_i=\frac{p_{,i}}{\rho+p}
$$

**Notes:** Treat the book's 'p comparable with rho' as a heuristic. The sharp statement in GR is a bound on compactness: for static stars 2M/R < 8/9 (Buchdahl), from the TOV equation of Chapter 10. It is not a local threshold on p/rho.

**Where:** SCH §4.8 p.104 (pdf 122)

### Gauss' law in four dimensions

*theorem · developed* · also: divergence theorem in spacetime, Gauss' theorem

For a vector field V on a four-volume with closed boundary, the integral of V^a_{,a} d^4x equals the integral over the boundary three-surface of V^a n_a d^3S, where n~ is the outward unit normal one-form; for a divergence-free V the net flux out of any closed hypersurface vanishes.

**How introduced:** Stated without proof by appeal to the 3D theorem, then made concrete on a coordinate box (Fig. 4.9), turning N^a_{,a} = 0 and T^{0a}_{,a} = 0 into integral balance laws.

**Prerequisites:** four-divergence, unit normal one-form, hypersurface volume element

$$
\int V^\alpha{}_{,\alpha}\,\mathrm d^4x=\oint V^\alpha n_\alpha\,\mathrm d^3S
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 4.19 asks for the proof via Eq. 4.58; Exercise 4.25(g) uses it to recover the electric Gauss law.

**Where:** SCH §4.9 p.104 (pdf 122); SCH §4.9 p.105 (pdf 123)

### outward normal orientation on spacetime boundaries

*convention · introduced*

On the boundary of a four-volume the unit normal one-form in Gauss' law is taken to point outward: dt on the later constant-time face, -dt on the earlier face (outward there means toward the past), dx on the larger-x face and -dx on the smaller-x face.

**How introduced:** Spelled out for the box of Fig. 4.9 while evaluating the surface integral.

**Prerequisites:** unit normal one-form, Gauss' law in four dimensions

**Where:** SCH §4.9 p.104 (pdf 122); SCH §4.9 p.105 (pdf 123)

### collisionless gas

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: noncolliding particle system, kinetic matter

A system of particles that do not collide (e.g. radiation, rarefied plasma, stars in a galaxy or cluster) treated statistically; if its velocity distribution is isotropic in the MCRF its stress-energy tensor has perfect-fluid form, with pressure from particle motions.

**How introduced:** Mentioned in the pressure aside and bibliography (Andreasson); worked out in Exercise 4.22, including p = rho/3 for photons.

**Prerequisites:** pressure as momentum flux from random motion, perfect-fluid stress-energy tensor

$$
p=\tfrac13\rho\ \text{(photon gas)}
$$

**Where:** SCH §4.7 p.101 (pdf 119); SCH §4.10 p.106 (pdf 124); SCH p.108 (pdf 126)

### dissipative relativistic fluids

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: viscous and heat-conducting relativistic fluids

Relativistic fluids with viscosity and heat conduction, whose consistent (causal, stable) description is subtle and requires theories beyond the perfect fluid.

**How introduced:** Only flagged in the bibliography, with a pointer to Israel & Stewart.

**Prerequisites:** perfect fluid, viscosity, heat conduction

**Where:** SCH §4.10 p.106 (pdf 124)

### Faraday tensor

*physical-quantity · mention* · also: electromagnetic field tensor, F

The antisymmetric (2 0) tensor F with F^{0i} = E^i and F^{xy} = B^z (cyclic); Maxwell's equations become F^{mn}_{,n} = 4 pi J^m and F_{[mn,l]} = 0, and E and B mix under boosts as components of F.

**How introduced:** Built step by step in the long Exercise 4.25, which also connects charge conservation and Gauss' law to the chapter's number-flux and integral-conservation ideas.

**Prerequisites:** stress-energy tensor, number-flux four-vector, Gauss' law in four dimensions

$$
F^{\mu\nu}{}_{,\nu}=4\pi J^\mu
$$

$$
F_{\mu\nu,\lambda}+F_{\nu\lambda,\mu}+F_{\lambda\mu,\nu}=0
$$

**Where:** SCH p.108 (pdf 126); SCH p.109 (pdf 127); SCH p.110 (pdf 128)

### tensor virial theorem

*theorem · mention*

For a bounded system obeying T^{mn}_{,n} = 0, the second time derivative of the second moment of the energy distribution equals twice the volume integral of the stresses.

**How introduced:** Appears only as Exercise 4.23(b), derived by repeatedly using the conservation law and integrating by parts.

**Prerequisites:** local conservation of energy-momentum

$$
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}\int T^{00}x^ix^j\,\mathrm d^3x=2\int T^{ij}\,\mathrm d^3x
$$

**Notes:** Reused later in the book's treatment of gravitational-wave generation (quadrupole formula).

**Where:** SCH p.108 (pdf 126)

### (4.1) Rest number density · supporting · SCH §4.2 p.85 (pdf 103)

### (4.2) Number density in a moving frame · supporting · SCH §4.2 p.85 (pdf 103)

\frac{n}{\sqrt{1-v^2}} = \text{number density where the particles move with speed } v

Lorentz contraction of the containing box along the motion raises the count per volume by gamma.

**Symbols:** v speed of the dust in the observing frame

### Flux across constant-x surface (dust moving along x) · derivation-step · SCH §4.2 p.86 (pdf 104)

$$
(\text{flux})^{\bar x} = \frac{n v}{\sqrt{1-v^2}}

Particles within v dt of the surface cross it in time dt; multiply by the moving-frame density.

**Symbols:** v speed along x-bar

### (4.3) Flux for general velocity · supporting · SCH §4.2 p.87 (pdf 105)

$$
(\text{flux})^{\bar x} = \frac{n v^{\bar x}}{\sqrt{1-v^2}}
$$

Only the component of velocity normal to the surface carries particles across it.

**Symbols:** v^x-bar x-component of the dust velocity; v its magnitude

### (4.4) Number-flux vector · central · SCH §4.2 p.87 (pdf 105)

$$
\vec N = n\vec U
$$

Defines the four-vector that unifies density and flux.

**Symbols:** U four-velocity of the dust

### Four-velocity components · derivation-step · SCH §4.2 p.87 (pdf 105)

$$
\vec U \underset{\bar{\mathcal O}}{\to} \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2}},\frac{v^x}{\sqrt{1-v^2}},\frac{v^y}{\sqrt{1-v^2}},\frac{v^z}{\sqrt{1-v^2}}\right)

Standard components of U in a frame where the particles have three-velocity v.

### (4.5) Components of N · central · SCH §4.2 p.87 (pdf 105)

$$
\vec N \underset{\bar{\mathcal O}}{\to} \left(\frac{n}{\sqrt{1-v^2}},\frac{n v^x}{\sqrt{1-v^2}},\frac{n v^y}{\sqrt{1-v^2}},\frac{n v^z}{\sqrt{1-v^2}}\right)
$$

Time component is the density, spatial components the fluxes, in any frame.

### (4.6) Rest density from N · central · SCH §4.2 p.88 (pdf 106)

$$
\vec N\cdot\vec N = -n^2,\qquad n = (-\vec N\cdot\vec N)^{1/2}
$$

The invariant length of N is the rest density, exactly as the length of four-momentum is rest mass.

### (4.7) Unit normal one-form · central · SCH §4.3 p.90 (pdf 108)

$$
\tilde n := \tilde{\mathrm d}\phi / |\tilde{\mathrm d}\phi|
$$

Normalizes the gradient of phi to represent the non-null surface phi = const uniquely (up to sign).

**Symbols:** phi function whose level set is the surface

### (4.8) Magnitude of a one-form · supporting · SCH §4.3 p.90 (pdf 108)

$$
|\tilde{\mathrm d}\phi| = |\eta^{\alpha\beta}\phi_{,\alpha}\phi_{,\beta}|^{1/2}
$$

Norm of a one-form uses the inverse metric; absolute value covers timelike and spacelike normals.

**Symbols:** eta^{ab} inverse Minkowski metric

### (4.9) Hypersurface volume element · supporting · SCH §4.3 p.90 (pdf 108)

$$
\tilde n\,\mathrm dx^\alpha\,\mathrm dx^\beta\,\mathrm dx^\gamma
$$

Directed three-volume element in a hypersurface spanned by three distinct coordinates; the dx here are integration measures, not one-forms.

### Flux as contraction · central · SCH §4.3 p.90 (pdf 108)

$$
\text{flux across }\phi=\text{const} = \langle\tilde n,\vec N\rangle,\qquad \langle\tilde{\mathrm d}\bar x,\vec N\rangle = N^{\alpha}(\tilde{\mathrm d}\bar x)_\alpha = N^{\bar x}
$$

The four-dimensional analogue of E.n: contract the matter's vector with the surface's normal one-form.

### Frame one-form · supporting · SCH §4.3 p.91 (pdf 109)

U_\alpha = \eta_{\alpha\beta}U^\beta,\quad U_0=-1,\ U_i=0 \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf g(\vec U,\cdot) = -\tilde{\mathrm d}t

The lowered four-velocity of an inertial observer is minus the gradient of its time coordinate.

### (4.10) Energy via dt · supporting · SCH §4.3 p.91 (pdf 109)

E = \langle\tilde{\mathrm d}t,\vec p\rangle = p^0\quad(\text{cf. } E=-\vec p\cdot\vec U,\ \text{Eq. 2.35})

Defines the scalar rho.

With no random motion, all energy is rest mass.

\frac{\rho}{1-v^2} = \text{energy density where the particles move with speed } v

### (4.14) Definition of the stress-energy tensor · central · SCH §4.4 p.92 (pdf 110)

$$
\mathbf T(\tilde{\mathrm d}x^\alpha,\tilde{\mathrm d}x^\beta) = T^{\alpha\beta} := \text{flux of }\alpha\text{-momentum across a surface of constant } x^\beta

### (4.15)-(4.18) Block meanings of T · central · SCH §4.4 p.92 (pdf 110)

T^{00}=\text{energy density},\quad T^{0i}=\text{energy flux across } x^i=\text{const},\quad T^{i0}=i\text{-momentum density},\quad T^{ij}=\text{flux of } i\text{-momentum across } x^j=\text{const}

Dictionary of the four blocks of T.

(T^{00})_{\rm MCRF}=\rho=mn,\qquad (T^{0i})_{\rm MCRF}=(T^{i0})_{\rm MCRF}=(T^{ij})_{\rm MCRF}=0

No motion means no momentum and no flux.

**Symbols:** p = mU particle four-momentum

### (4.20) Dust components · supporting · SCH §4.4 p.93 (pdf 111)

T^{\alpha\beta} = \mathbf T(\tilde\omega^\alpha,\tilde\omega^\beta) = \rho\,\vec U(\tilde\omega^\alpha)\vec U(\tilde\omega^\beta) = \rho U^\alpha U^\beta

**Symbols:** omega^alpha basis one-forms

### (4.21) Dust components in a moving frame · supporting · SCH §4.4 p.93 (pdf 111)

T^{\bar0\bar0}=\frac{\rho}{1-v^2},\quad T^{\bar0\bar i}=\frac{\rho v^i}{1-v^2},\quad T^{\bar i\bar0}=\frac{\rho v^i}{1-v^2},\quad T^{\bar i\bar j}=\frac{\rho v^iv^j}{1-v^2}

\vec N:=n\vec U,\quad \Pi:=\rho/n-m\ \Rightarrow\ \rho=n(m+\Pi),\quad \rho_0:=mn,\quad \rho=\rho_0+n\Pi

### (4.22) First law · supporting · SCH §4.5 p.94 (pdf 112)

$$
\Delta E = \Delta Q - p\,\Delta V\quad\text{or}\quad \Delta Q = \Delta E + p\,\Delta V

Energy balance of a fluid element in its MCRF: heat in, work out.

**Symbols:** Q heat absorbed; V element volume; E element energy

### (4.23) Volume from particle number · derivation-step · SCH §4.5 p.94 (pdf 112)

$$
V = \frac{N}{n},\qquad \Delta V = -\frac{N}{n^2}\Delta n

For a fixed number of particles N, volume changes are density changes.

**Symbols:** N number of particles in the element (not the vector)

### Heat in terms of rho and n · derivation-step · SCH §4.5 p.94 (pdf 112)

$$
E=\rho V=\rho N/n,\quad \Delta E=\rho\Delta V+V\Delta\rho\ \Rightarrow\ \Delta Q=\frac{N}{n}\Delta\rho - N(\rho+p)\frac{\Delta n}{n^2}

Intermediate step eliminating E and V.

### (4.24) First law per particle · central · SCH §4.5 p.94 (pdf 112)

$$
n\,\Delta q = \Delta\rho - \frac{\rho+p}{n}\Delta n

**Symbols:** q heat absorbed per particle

### (4.25) Temperature and entropy via integrating factor · central · SCH §4.5 p.95 (pdf 113)

$$
\mathrm d\rho - (\rho+p)\,\mathrm dn/n = nT\,\mathrm dS

The first-law form has an integrating factor that defines temperature T and specific entropy S.

**Symbols:** T temperature; S entropy per particle

### (4.26) Heat and entropy · supporting · SCH §4.5 p.95 (pdf 113)

$$
\Delta q = T\,\Delta S
$$

Heat absorbed per particle is temperature times increase in specific entropy.

## Worked examples

### Density of moving dust (Eq. 4.2) · intro · SCH §4.2 p.85 (pdf 103)

**Problem:** Find the number of dust particles per unit volume in a frame where they all move with speed v.

**Method:** Take a rest-frame box of volume dx dy dz holding the particles; in the moving frame the dimension along the motion contracts by sqrt(1-v^2) while transverse ones do not; the particle count is unchanged.

**Key insight:** Counting is invariant, volume is not, so density acquires exactly one gamma factor.

**Result:** n/sqrt(1-v^2)

**Concepts:** number density, dust

### Flux of dust across a surface (Eq. 4.3) · intro · SCH §4.2 p.86 (pdf 104)

**Problem:** Compute the particle flux across a surface of constant x in a frame where dust moves with velocity v.

**Method:** Particles that cross area dA in time dt are those in a prism adjacent to the surface; for motion along x its volume is v dt dA, for general motion it is a parallelepiped of height v^x dt. Multiply by the moving-frame density and divide by dt dA.

**Key insight:** Only the normal velocity component matters, and density brings its own gamma.

**Result:** flux^x = n v^x/sqrt(1-v^2)

**Concepts:** particle flux

### Components and norm of N (Eqs. 4.5-4.6) · intro · SCH §4.2 p.87 (pdf 105)

**Problem:** Show that density and fluxes form a four-vector and extract the rest density invariantly.

**Method:** Multiply the known components of U in the moving frame by n; compare term by term with Eqs. 4.2-4.3; compute N.N = n^2 U.U = -n^2.

**Key insight:** The Galilean split between an invariant density and a frame-dependent flux dissolves into components of one vector.

**Result:** N -> (n gamma, n gamma v^i); n = (-N.N)^{1/2}

**Concepts:** number-flux four-vector, geometric object versus components

### Flux via a normal one-form · intro · SCH §4.3 p.90 (pdf 108)

**Problem:** Verify that contracting N with a surface's unit normal one-form gives the flux across that surface.

**Method:** Take phi = x: dx has components (0,1,0,0) and unit norm, so <dx, N> = N^x, the flux already found. Take phi = t: <dt, N> = N^0, the density.

**Key insight:** The same contraction handles spatial fluxes and density; the surface and the matter are separate objects.

**Result:** <n~, N> = N^x or N^0

**Concepts:** flux as contraction of one-form with vector, unit normal one-form

### Energy density of moving dust (Eq. 4.13) · intro · SCH §4.4 p.91 (pdf 109)

**Problem:** Find the energy per unit volume of dust in a frame where it moves with speed v.

**Method:** Density is n gamma; energy per particle is m gamma; multiply.

**Key insight:** Two gamma factors mean energy density cannot be a four-vector component; it must come from a second-rank tensor.

**Result:** rho/(1-v^2)

**Concepts:** gamma-squared transformation of energy density, energy density

### Stress-energy tensor of dust (Eqs. 4.19-4.21) · standard · SCH §4.4 p.92 (pdf 110)

**Problem:** Construct T for dust and give its components in any frame.

**Method:** In the MCRF only T^{00} = mn is non-zero. The outer product p(x)N = mn U(x)U has exactly these MCRF components, so the tensors agree; evaluating on basis one-forms gives rho U^aU^b, and inserting boosted U gives each block. Exercise 4.21(a)-(c) extends this to beams and rotating rings.

**Key insight:** Match components in one convenient frame, then the tensor equation holds everywhere.

**Result:** T^{00} = rho gamma^2, T^{0i} = T^{i0} = rho gamma^2 v^i, T^{ij} = rho gamma^2 v^iv^j

**Concepts:** dust stress-energy tensor, stress-energy tensor

### First law in fluid variables (Eqs. 4.22-4.26) · standard · SCH §4.5 p.94 (pdf 112)

**Problem:** Express the heat absorbed by a fluid element with fixed particle number in terms of changes in rho and n, and introduce T and S.

**Method:** Start from dQ = dE + p dV; substitute V = N/n, dV = -N dn/n^2, E = rho N/n; divide by N to get n dq = drho - (rho+p)dn/n; note the right side is a form in two variables, so it equals A dB; name A/n temperature and B specific entropy.

**Key insight:** Thermodynamic temperature and entropy can be introduced purely as bookkeeping devices of energy conservation.

**Result:** drho - (rho+p) dn/n = nT dS; dq = T dS

**Concepts:** first law of thermodynamics for a fluid element, temperature and specific entropy via integrating factor

### Symmetry of stresses from torques (Eqs. 4.27-4.29) · standard · SCH §4.5 p.96 (pdf 114)

**Problem:** Prove that T^{ij} = T^{ji} for any material.

**Method:** Put the origin at the centre of a cube of side l. Forces on opposite faces nearly cancel (else infinite linear acceleration). Face forces act at face centres l/2 from the axis: faces normal to x contribute -(1/2)l^3 T^{yx} each, faces normal to y contribute +(1/2)l^3 T^{xy} each, so tau_z = l^3(T^{xy} - T^{yx}). Moment of inertia scales as mass times l^2, i.e. as l^5, so angular acceleration scales as (T^{xy} - T^{yx})/l^2 and diverges as l -> 0 unless the stresses are equal.

**Key insight:** Torque scales as l^3 but inertia as l^5; only symmetric stress avoids ever-faster spinning of ever-smaller elements.

**Result:** T^{ij} = T^{ji}

**Concepts:** symmetry of the stress-energy tensor, stress

### Energy conservation from a box (Eqs. 4.30-4.34) · intro · SCH §4.6 p.98 (pdf 116)

**Problem:** Derive a local conservation law for energy and momentum.

**Method:** Sum energy inflow minus outflow through the six faces of a cube (each face flux times l^2); equate to d(l^3 T^{00})/dt; divide by l^3 and let l -> 0 to turn differences into partial derivatives; repeat with a momentum index.

**Key insight:** A conservation law in differential form is a four-divergence: rate of change of density plus divergence of flux vanishes.

**Result:** T^{ab}_{,b} = 0; similarly N^a_{,a} = 0

**Concepts:** local conservation of energy-momentum, conservation of particle number

### Perfect-fluid tensor from physical conditions (Eqs. 4.36-4.38) · standard · SCH §4.7 p.100 (pdf 118)

**Problem:** Find the stress-energy tensor of a fluid with no viscosity and no heat conduction.

**Method:** No heat conduction sets T^{0i} = 0 in the MCRF. No viscosity makes T^{ij} diagonal for every orientation of axes; only multiples of delta^{ij} have this property (Exercise 4.11), so T^{ij} = p delta^{ij}. Guess (rho+p)U^aU^b + p eta^{ab} and check entries in the MCRF, e.g. 00: (rho+p)(1) + p(-1) = rho; ij: 0 + p delta^{ij}.

**Key insight:** Physical restrictions reduce ten functions to rho, p and U; the invariant form follows by matching in the MCRF.

**Result:** T = (rho+p)U(x)U + p g^{-1}

**Concepts:** perfect-fluid stress-energy tensor, pressure, perfect fluid

### Adiabatic flow from energy conservation (Eqs. 4.39-4.50) · challenging · SCH §4.7 p.101 (pdf 119)

**Problem:** Extract the component of T^{ab}_{,b} = 0 along the flow for a perfect fluid and interpret it.

**Method:** Use (nU^b)_{,b} = 0 to write the inertial term as nU^b((rho+p)U^a/n)_{,b}; prove U^a_{,b}U_a = 0 by differentiating U.U = -1; contract with U_a so that derivatives of U drop and U.U = -1 remains; the pressure term becomes dp/dtau; simplify to drho/dtau = ((rho+p)/n) dn/dtau and compare with nT dS.

**Key insight:** Energy conservation along the flow is the first law, and for a perfect fluid it says specific entropy is carried unchanged.

**Result:** dS/dtau = 0

**Concepts:** adiabatic flow, orthogonality of four-velocity and its gradient, derivative along a world line

### Relativistic Euler equation (Eqs. 4.51-4.54) · challenging · SCH §4.7 p.102 (pdf 120)

**Problem:** Extract the momentum components of the perfect-fluid conservation law.

**Method:** Work in the MCRF at the event: U^i = 0 but U^i_{,b} need not vanish. The derivative of (rho+p)/n multiplies U^i and drops; what remains is (rho+p)U^i_{,b}U^b + p_{,b}eta^{ib} = 0; lower i and recognize U_{i,b}U^b as the four-acceleration.

**Key insight:** Pressure contributes to inertia: rho + p replaces rho in F = ma.

**Result:** (rho+p)a_i + p_{,i} = 0

**Concepts:** relativistic Euler equation, four-acceleration, inertial mass density rho + p

### Pressure gradient needed to support a star (§4.8) · intro · SCH §4.8 p.103 (pdf 121)

**Problem:** Estimate how the pressure gradient required to hold up a dense star differs from the Newtonian case.

**Method:** Let a_i be the acceleration an element would have under gravity alone; support requires a pressure gradient producing -a_i, so p_{,i} = -(rho+p)a_i. Compare with Newtonian p_{,i} = -rho a_i and add the fact that pressure itself gravitates.

**Key insight:** Both the inertial and the gravitational roles of pressure push the requirement up, so when p approaches rho support fails.

**Result:** -a_i = p_{,i}/(rho+p)

**Concepts:** pressure as a source of gravity, relativistic gravitational collapse

### Gauss' law on a coordinate box (Eq. 4.58) · standard · SCH §4.9 p.104 (pdf 122)

**Problem:** Evaluate the boundary integral of the four-dimensional divergence theorem for a box bounded by t_1, t_2, x_1, x_2 (and y, z faces).

**Method:** Assign outward normals: dt at t_2, -dt at t_1, dx at x_2, -dx at x_1; contract with V to get +-V^0 and +-V^x; group into differences across the box.

**Key insight:** For a conserved current, the change of the amount inside between two times equals the net flux through the spatial walls.

**Result:** Integral of [V^0(t_2) - V^0(t_1)] dx dy dz + integral of [V^x(x_2) - V^x(x_1)] dt dy dz + ... = 0 when V^a_{,a} = 0

**Concepts:** Gauss' law in four dimensions, outward normal orientation on spacetime boundaries


## Analogies and intuitions

### The electric flux E.n through a surface in Gauss' law → flux as contraction of one-form with vector · strong · SCH §4.3 p.90 (pdf 108)

The particle flux across a hypersurface is presented as the exact spacetime counterpart: contract the matter's four-vector with the surface's unit normal one-form.

**Where it breaks down:** In spacetime the normal is naturally a one-form, not a vector, and for null surfaces no unit normal exists; sign and orientation subtleties of Lorentzian normals have no 3D analogue.

**App idea:** Split-screen: 3D E-field through a tilted patch beside a spacetime world-line bundle through a tilted hypersurface, with the same 'dot with the normal' readout in both.

### Rest density n versus measured density is like rest mass versus energy → number density · strong · SCH §4.2 p.88 (pdf 106)

Shows why n is a scalar even though every observer measures a different density: it is the invariant length of N, as rest mass is the length of p.

**Where it breaks down:** Mass has no analogue of 'flux across a surface'; the analogy covers invariance, not the geometry of surfaces.

### Unifying density and flux into N parallels unifying energy and momentum into the four-momentum → number-flux four-vector · useful · SCH §4.2 p.88 (pdf 106)

Frames the new vector as another instance of relativity merging quantities that were separate in Galilean physics.

**Where it breaks down:** Energy and momentum were both frame dependent before relativity, whereas Galilean density was invariant; the unification here also changes the invariance status of density.

### A lake's gravity depends on bulk properties, not on where each water molecule is → continuum approximation · useful · SCH §4.1 p.84 (pdf 102)

Motivates describing matter by averaged fields when computing its gravitational field.

**Where it breaks down:** Says nothing about the conditions under which averaging fails (rarefied gases, Exercise 4.1 cases).

### Rigidity as the ability of neighbouring blocks to stop each other sliding → fluid · useful · SCH §4.1 p.84 (pdf 102)

Reduces the solid/fluid distinction to the presence of tangential forces at interfaces, which later become the off-diagonal stresses.

**Where it breaks down:** Real fluids have viscosity and solids creep; the classification is a matter of degree and timescale.

**App idea:** Two stacked blocks with an adjustable friction-like shear coupling; push the top block and watch it slide (fluid) or drag the lower block (solid).

### The one-form dt as a stack of surfaces of simultaneity that defines a frame → frame as a one-form · useful · SCH §4.3 p.91 (pdf 109)

Gives a picture in which an observer's frame is literally its family of 'now' slices, making E = <dt, p> a count of how many slices p pierces.

**Where it breaks down:** Fixes the frame only up to spatial rotations, and in curved spacetime a global time function may not exist.

**App idea:** Stack of tilted simultaneity sheets for a chosen boost; drag a four-momentum arrow and count the sheets it crosses to read off E.

### The Newtonian engineering stress tensor → stress · useful · SCH §4.5 p.98 (pdf 116)

Grounds the spatial block of T in a quantity a materials engineer already uses; the relativistic tensor inherits its name.

**Where it breaks down:** Relativistic T also includes energy and momentum densities and requires T^{0i} = T^{i0}, which has no Newtonian counterpart.

### F = ma for a fluid element, with a pressure difference as the net force → relativistic Euler equation · strong · SCH §4.7 p.103 (pdf 121)

Reads (rho+p)a_i = -p_{,i} as Newton's law: an element pushed equally from both sides feels no force, so only the gradient of p accelerates it, and rho + p is the mass per volume.

**Where it breaks down:** The 'mass' rho+p is not a rest mass of anything; it includes pressure, and in GR pressure also gravitates.

**App idea:** Row of fluid parcels with pressure arrows on both faces; adjust a pressure profile and see the net force and resulting acceleration, with a toggle to add p to the inertia.

### Moving energy is moving mass → equality of energy flux and momentum density · useful · SCH §4.5 p.98 (pdf 116)

Justifies T^{0i} = T^{i0} in one step: energy flux = energy density x speed = mass density x speed = momentum density.

**Where it breaks down:** Heuristic; it relies on c = 1 and on identifying a single transport speed, and does not by itself handle stress contributions.


## Misconceptions addressed

### The number of particles per unit volume is the same for all observers. · SCH §4.2 p.85 (pdf 103)

**Correction:** Measured density is larger by gamma in a frame where the particles move, because the volume Lorentz contracts; only the MCRF value n is invariant.

**Why tempting:** In Galilean physics density is an invariant scalar and counting feels observer independent.

### 'Frame independent' means a quantity has the same components in all frames. · SCH §4.2 p.88 (pdf 106)

**Correction:** A vector or tensor is frame independent as a geometric object; its components change with the frame. Invariance of components is special to scalars.

**Why tempting:** Everyday language equates 'independent of frame' with 'same numbers'.

### Flux across a surface of constant x is 'flux in the x direction', so it is naturally a vector along x. · SCH p.106 (pdf 124)

**Correction:** A flux is defined relative to a surface, which is represented by a normal one-form; the flux is the contraction of N with that one-form (Exercise 4.2).

**Why tempting:** In Euclidean 3D with an orthonormal basis, normals and directions are interchangeable.

### Energy density is the time component of some four-vector, like number density. · SCH §4.4 p.91 (pdf 109)

**Correction:** Energy density of moving dust carries two gamma factors, so it is the 00 component of a second-rank tensor, not a vector component.

**Why tempting:** Energy is the time component of four-momentum and density is the time component of N, so their combination seems vector-like.

### A single MCRF can be used for the whole fluid at all times. · SCH §4.5 p.93 (pdf 111)

**Correction:** The MCRF belongs to one element at one moment; different elements move relative to each other and accelerate, so each has its own, changing MCRF.

**Why tempting:** For a single particle in uniform motion one rest frame suffices forever.

### Because U^i = 0 in the MCRF, the derivatives U^i_{,b} also vanish there. · SCH §4.7 p.102 (pdf 120)

**Correction:** U^i vanishes only at the chosen event in that frame; neighbouring events have different velocities, so its gradient (and the acceleration) can be non-zero.

**Why tempting:** Setting a quantity to zero in a convenient frame feels like setting it zero identically.

### Pressure requires collisions or forces between particles; a collisionless gas has no pressure. · SCH §4.7 p.101 (pdf 119)

**Correction:** Pressure is momentum flux in the MCRF; particles carrying momentum across a surface produce it just as forces do. Only matter with no random motion (dust) is pressureless.

**Why tempting:** School pictures of pressure emphasize molecules hitting walls or each other.

### Stress symmetry is a special property of fluids. · SCH §4.5 p.97 (pdf 115)

**Correction:** The torque argument uses no material properties, so T^{ij} = T^{ji} holds for solids as well, in Newtonian physics and relativity.

**Why tempting:** The proof appears in a chapter about fluids.

### The force on a fluid element is -p, the pressure itself. · SCH §4.7 p.103 (pdf 121)

**Correction:** Pressure from the neighbour on one side is balanced by the neighbour on the other; only the change of p across the element gives a net force, -grad p.

**Why tempting:** Pressure is described as a force per area, so it is natural to treat it as the force.

### Gravity in a relativistic theory is sourced by mass density (or by energy density T^{00}). · SCH §4.8 p.103 (pdf 121)

**Correction:** Rest mass is not separately conserved in relativity and a rest-mass source conflicts with experiment; T^{00} alone is frame dependent. The source must be the whole tensor T, so momenta, stresses and pressure gravitate.

**Why tempting:** Newtonian gravity uses mass density, and E = mc^2 suggests just swapping in energy density.

### More pressure always makes a star more stable against collapse. · SCH §4.8 p.104 (pdf 122)

**Correction:** In GR pressure adds both inertia and gravitational pull; when p is comparable with rho, increasing the pressure increases the gravity it must fight and no equilibrium exists.

**Why tempting:** In Newtonian stars pressure is purely supportive.

### The unit normal one-form n~ and the number density n are related. · SCH §4.3 p.90 (pdf 108)

**Correction:** They are unrelated; the shared letter is a historical accident.

**Why tempting:** Same symbol, same section.

### The outward normal on every face of a spacetime box points toward the future or toward larger coordinates. · SCH §4.9 p.104 (pdf 122)

**Correction:** Outward means away from the enclosed region: on the earlier constant-time face the outward normal one-form is -dt (toward the past), on the smaller-x wall it is -dx.

**Why tempting:** Time is usually pictured as flowing only forward, so a past-directed normal seems wrong.

### T^{ab}_{,b} = 0 means the energy density at each point is constant in time. · SCH §4.6 p.98 (pdf 116)

**Correction:** It says the local rate of change of energy density is exactly compensated by the divergence of energy flux; energy can move around but not appear or disappear.

**Why tempting:** The word 'conservation' is often heard as 'staying put'.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Every surface has a unit normal one-form. · SCH §4.3 p.90 (pdf 108)

**Correction:** For a null hypersurface the gradient has zero norm and cannot be normalized; the book restricts Eq. 4.7 to non-null surfaces.

**Why tempting:** In Euclidean geometry every non-degenerate surface has a unit normal.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Dust means slowly moving particles. · SCH §4.2 p.85 (pdf 103)

**Correction:** Dust has no random (thermal) motion relative to its local rest frame; its bulk flow can be arbitrarily fast.

**Why tempting:** The everyday meaning of dust evokes something at rest.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Whirling fluid elements · SCH §4.5 p.97 (pdf 115)

**Setup:** Imagine the stresses on a small cube were not symmetric and shrink the cube: torque falls as l^3 but moment of inertia as l^5.

**Lesson:** Ever smaller elements would spin ever faster, which is absurd, so stresses are symmetric in any material.

**App idea:** Fractal-zoom animation into a fluid showing nested cubes spinning faster at each zoom level when asymmetry is switched on, and all still when it is off.

### Unbalanced forces on a vanishing element · SCH §4.5 p.96 (pdf 114)

**Setup:** Suppose forces on opposite faces of a small element did not nearly cancel as its size goes to zero.

**Lesson:** A finite net force on a vanishing mass would produce infinite acceleration, so opposite face forces cancel to leading order.

### Counting world lines through a hypersurface · SCH §4.3 p.88 (pdf 106)

**Setup:** Draw the world lines of dust and a surface; count how many world lines pierce a unit piece of the surface's world-tube, then of a constant-time slice.

**Lesson:** Flux and density are the same kind of quantity: world-line crossings of a hypersurface.

**App idea:** Covered by the hypersurface-counting widget in the Figure 4.4 redesign.

### Alternative sources for relativistic gravity · SCH §4.8 p.103 (pdf 121)

**Setup:** Try to build a relativistic gravity theory sourced by rest-mass density, or by energy density T^{00} alone.

**Lesson:** The first ignores interconvertibility of mass and energy and fails precision tests; the second needs a preferred frame; only the whole T works.

### Holding up an ever-denser star · SCH §4.8 p.104 (pdf 122)

**Setup:** Increase the central density of a star and ask what pressure gradient keeps each element from falling, remembering that pressure gravitates and adds inertia.

**Lesson:** The needed pressure feeds back into gravity; once p nears rho the feedback runs away and collapse is unavoidable.

**App idea:** Feedback-loop toy: slider for compactness; iterative bars show pressure needed and the extra gravity it generates, converging for low compactness and diverging beyond a threshold.


## Applications and observations

- **Stars and other astrophysical gravity sources modelled as perfect fluids** (astrophysical-system, SCH §4.1 p.84 (pdf 102)): The chapter opens by noting that in many astrophysical GR problems a perfect fluid is a good first model of the source; §4.8 applies the relativistic Euler equation to hydrostatic support of dense stars.
- **Neutron stars and relativistic gases, where p is comparable with rho** (astrophysical-system, SCH §4.8 p.104 (pdf 122)): Pressure becomes a sizeable fraction of the energy density only in matter as dense as a neutron star's or so hot that its particles move near light speed; in those regimes relativistic pressure effects control stability. Note that even an ultra-relativistic gas reaches only p = rho/3, so 'p comparable with rho' means 'of the same order', not equal. Key numbers: Own estimate for context (SI, p/(rho c^2)): water at 1 atm ~ 1e-15; centre of the Sun ~ 1e-6 to 1e-5; neutron-star core ~ 0.1-0.5; photon gas exactly 1/3.
- **Baryon number conservation and proton decay searches** (observation, SCH §4.6 p.99 (pdf 117)): Particle number conservation is justified by baryon conservation; theoretical violations (grand unification, black-hole evaporation, Chapter 11) had not been observed.
- **Collisionless systems: black-body radiation, rarefied plasmas, galaxies, globular clusters** (astrophysical-system, SCH p.108 (pdf 126)): Exercise 4.22 shows an isotropic velocity distribution gives a perfect-fluid tensor; for particles of one speed p and rho follow from m, v, n, and a photon gas has p = rho/3. Key numbers: p = rho/3 for radiation
- **Apparent brightness of a moving star** (observation, SCH p.108 (pdf 126)): Exercise 4.24 builds the frame-invariant stress-energy tensor of starlight and asks how the measured energy flux T^{0x} depends on the observer's recession speed. Key numbers: Rest frame: T^{00} = T^{0x} = T^{xx} = L/(4 pi x^2)
- **Tests of the continuum approximation on everyday systems** (numerical-estimate, SCH p.106 (pdf 124)): Exercise 4.1 has the reader judge planetary motion, lava flow, rush-hour and stop-sign traffic, and plasma dynamics against the element criterion.

## Historical notes

- **Albert Einstein:** Einstein guessed that the source of gravity in a relativistic theory must be the entire stress-energy tensor, and combined this with curved-space geometry to reach GR. — Explains why the chapter's careful construction of T is a prerequisite for the field equations. (SCH §4.8 p.103 (pdf 121))
- **History:** Before relativity, particle flux was treated as a three-vector defined relative to a chosen inertial frame, separate from an invariant number density. — Sets up the conceptual gain of the four-vector N. (SCH §4.2 p.88 (pdf 106))
- **History:** Grand unified theories suggest the proton may decay and black-hole formation followed by evaporation would not conserve baryons, but no violation of baryon number had been observed. — Justifies assuming N^a_{,a} = 0 for astrophysical fluids while flagging its theoretical limits. (SCH §4.6 p.99 (pdf 117))

## Notation and conventions

- **Units:** c = 1 throughout; speeds are dimensionless and gamma is written (1-v^2)^{-1/2}, equal to the boost matrix entry Lambda^0-bar_0. — Compare with p and rho in SI by dividing p by rho c^2. (SCH §4.4 p.91 (pdf 109))
- **Metric signature:** Minkowski metric eta = diag(-1, 1, 1, 1): U.U = -1, N.N = -n^2, lowered four-velocity components (-1, 0, 0, 0) in the rest frame, eta^{00} = -1 used in checking Eq. 4.37. (SCH §4.3 p.91 (pdf 109))
- **Vectors, one-forms, tensors:** Vectors carry an arrow (N, U, p); one-forms a tilde (n~, dt, omega~^alpha); tensors are bold (T, g); g^{-1} is the inverse metric with components eta^{ab}; (2 0) tensors take two one-form arguments. (SCH §4.3 p.90 (pdf 108))
- **Comma derivative:** A comma denotes a partial derivative with respect to a coordinate: T^{ab}_{,b} = dT^{ab}/dx^b, phi_{,a} = d phi/dx^a. — Becomes a semicolon (covariant derivative) in curved spacetime later in the book. (SCH §4.6 p.98 (pdf 116))
- **Index order in T^{ab}:** First index labels the component of four-momentum, second labels the coordinate held constant on the surface crossed. — Harmless in practice because T is proven symmetric. (SCH §4.4 p.92 (pdf 110))
- **Barred frame:** O-bar denotes the frame in which the dust moves, and barred indices label components in that frame; the printed book uses a bar throughout. — The reading-copy export often turns the bar into a tilde or a vector arrow (e.g. O~, x~); these are OCR artifacts, not a different frame or a one-form. (SCH §4.2 p.85 (pdf 103))
- **rho means total energy density:** rho is MCRF energy density including rest mass, thermal and other energies; rho_0 = mn is rest-mass density and Pi internal energy per particle. — Differs from Newtonian usage where rho is mass density. (SCH §4.5 p.94 (pdf 112))
- **Overloaded symbols:** n: rest number density; n~: unit normal one-form. T: temperature (italic) and stress-energy tensor (bold). N: particle count in an element and the flux vector. S: specific entropy and a surface. A: an interface area and a fluid element label. rho: charge density in Exercise 4.25. — The book explicitly warns only about n versus n~. (SCH §4.3 p.90 (pdf 108))
- **Index ranges:** Greek indices run over 0-3, Latin indices over spatial 1-3; summation convention applies. (SCH §4.4 p.92 (pdf 110))
- **Integration measures:** d^4x is the coordinate four-volume; d^3S the three-volume of a bounding hypersurface; plain dx^a in Eq. 4.9 are integration infinitesimals, not basis one-forms. (SCH §4.9 p.104 (pdf 122))

- **4.4** (intro): Show that an arbitrary observer measures dust number density equal to minus the dot product of N with that observer's four-velocity. — *Gives the frame-free recipe for 'what an observer measures', reused throughout GR.* Skills: projection onto an observer, number-flux vector

- **4.21** (challenging): Compute stress-energy components for a uniform particle beam, a thin rotating ring of particles, and two counter-rotating rings. — *Counter-rotating rings show that zero net momentum does not mean zero stress, a precursor of pressure from random motion.* Skills: constructing T for concrete matter, continuum modelling
- **4.22** (standard): For a collisionless gas with isotropic velocities in the MCRF, prove the stress-energy tensor is a perfect fluid, express p and rho for particles of one speed, and show radiation has p = rho/3. — *Makes the 'pressure is momentum flux' aside quantitative and yields the radiation equation of state used in cosmology.* Skills: kinetic theory, averaging over directions

## Teaching gems

### Count gamma factors to find a tensor's rank: density of a boosted box gains one gamma (vector component), energy density gains two (component of a second-rank tensor). · SCH §4.4 p.91 (pdf 109)

**Why it works:** Turns an abstract rank question into a concrete Lorentz-contraction calculation learners can do in their heads.

**App idea:** Gamma counter: pick a quantity (count, number density, energy density, pressure), boost with a slider, and watch stacked gamma tokens appear, each labelled by its physical origin.

### Density is just a flux across a surface of constant time: world lines piercing a 'now' slice. · SCH §4.3 p.89 (pdf 107)

**Why it works:** Unifies density and flux pictorially before any algebra and makes N = nU feel inevitable.

**App idea:** Rotatable 3D spacetime with a world-line bundle and a movable hypersurface patch that counts piercings, switching between t = const and x = const sheets.

### To decide how many slots a physical quantity needs, ask how many choices you must make: which component of momentum, and which surface it crosses. · SCH §4.4 p.92 (pdf 110)

**Why it works:** Gives learners a reusable test for whether a quantity is a scalar, vector or (2 0) tensor, and explains the meaning of each T^{ab} at once.

**App idea:** Interactive 4x4 grid of T^{ab}: choose a momentum component (row) and a surface (column) and see an animation of that momentum crossing that surface, with the name of the block.

### Symmetry of stress proved by imagining ever-smaller elements spinning ever faster. · SCH §4.5 p.97 (pdf 115)

**Why it works:** A vivid reductio using only torque and moment-of-inertia scaling; memorable and general (solids too).

**App idea:** Shrinking-cube spin simulator with adjustable T^{xy} and T^{yx}, plotting angular acceleration versus size.

### Write the answer in the rest frame, then find the tensor expression that reproduces it: diag(rho,p,p,p) -> (rho+p)U^aU^b + p eta^{ab}. · SCH §4.7 p.100 (pdf 118)

**Why it works:** Models the standard GR workflow of 'compute where it is easy, promote by covariance', and gives a quick self-check (the 00 entry).

**App idea:** Matrix playground: learner types a candidate covariant expression; the app evaluates it in the MCRF and in boosted frames against the known components.

### Pressure is momentum flux, so even a collisionless gas has pressure; dust is the only pressureless fluid. · SCH §4.7 p.101 (pdf 119)

**Why it works:** Corrects the collision-based picture of pressure and links fluids to kinetic theory and radiation.

**App idea:** Particle gas in a box with collisions toggled off; a counting plane tallies momentum carried across per unit time and area, matching p.

### Split the single equation T^{ab}_{,b} = 0 along and perpendicular to U: the parallel part is the first law (entropy carried unchanged), the perpendicular part is Euler's F = ma with inertia rho + p. · SCH §4.7 p.102 (pdf 120)

**Why it works:** Shows how one tensor equation packages thermodynamics and dynamics, and makes rho + p memorable as inertia.

**App idea:** Projection visualizer: a four-vector equation drawn as an arrow in a (t, x) diagram with the element's U; drag U and see the parallel (energy) and orthogonal (momentum) projections update with their physical labels.

### Pressure feeds gravity in GR, so beyond some compactness adding pressure is self-defeating. · SCH §4.8 p.104 (pdf 122)

**Why it works:** Delivers a striking physical consequence of T as source using only the Euler equation, motivating later stellar-structure chapters.

**App idea:** Compactness slider with an iterative 'pressure needed -> extra gravity -> pressure needed' loop that converges or blows up.

### Conservation via a spacetime box: amount inside at t_2 minus at t_1 equals net inflow through the walls, with outward normals +-dt and +-dx. · SCH §4.9 p.105 (pdf 123)

**Why it works:** Makes the 4D Gauss theorem concrete and explains the sign of the past-face normal.

**App idea:** Draggable spacetime rectangle with world lines; boundary crossings tagged +1/-1 and summed.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Printed typo in Eq. 4.21: the momentum-density component is printed as rho v^i (1 - v^2) instead of rho v^i/(1 - v^2).** — Breaks the visible symmetry T^{0i} = T^{i0} that the text immediately points out, confusing careful readers. *Suggestion:* Show the corrected component and let learners verify symmetry from rho U^aU^b.
- **Printed typo in Eq. 4.56: the Newtonian acceleration is printed as v-dot + (a.grad)a; it should be v-dot + (v.grad)v, as Exercise 4.17 correctly states.** — Readers unfamiliar with fluid dynamics may learn a wrong advective derivative. *Suggestion:* Use the corrected expression and connect it to the four-acceleration U^mu_{,b}U^b in the slow-motion limit.
- **The moment of inertia in the torque argument is written I = alpha rho l^2 ('mass times l^2'), but the mass of the element is rho l^3, so I scales as rho l^5; Eq. 4.28 is correct only with the l^5 scaling.** — A reader checking dimensions finds the argument inconsistent and may doubt the conclusion. *Suggestion:* Present the scaling explicitly: torque ~ l^3, inertia ~ l^5, angular acceleration ~ 1/l^2.
- **T is defined through components in an arbitrary frame and its tensor character is deferred to Exercise 4.5; linearity in both arguments is not obvious from the verbal definition.** — Learners may feel the definition is circular or that 'flux of momentum' could fail to transform properly. *Suggestion:* Pair the definition with the dust construction p(x)N as a concrete tensor, then generalize by linearity over particle species and random velocities.
- **The integrating-factor step (existence of A, B with drho - (rho+p)dn/n = A dB) and the identification of A/n and B with thermodynamic temperature and entropy are asserted without proof.** — Students without thermodynamics background may feel T and S come from nowhere. *Suggestion:* Explain that any differential form in two variables has integrating factors (curves of B = const are integral curves), and illustrate with an ideal gas where T and S are known.
- **T^{0i} = T^{i0} is justified by the slogan that energy and mass are the same; the argument is heuristic and hides subtleties (e.g. canonical versus symmetrized tensors for fields with spin).** — Advanced learners may wonder why some field-theory stress tensors are not symmetric. *Suggestion:* Mention that angular-momentum conservation requires a symmetric T and that field theories symmetrize (Belinfante); for fluids the physical argument suffices.
- **Orientation subtleties of normals in Lorentzian signature are glossed: the one-form dt raised with eta gives a past-pointing vector, and the claim that the 4D Gauss proof is exactly as in 3D hides sign conventions for spacelike boundary pieces.** — Learners who raise indices to draw normal vectors can get signs wrong in flux integrals. *Suggestion:* Keep normals as one-forms (as the book does) and demonstrate on the coordinate box that +-dt and +-dx give the right bookkeeping; only then discuss vector normals.
- **§4.8 speaks of the acceleration an element 'would have in the absence of pressure' due to the gravitational field, although gravity has not been included in SR fluid dynamics.** — Readers may think gravity simply enters as an external a_i in the SR equations. *Suggestion:* Present §4.8 as a heuristic preview and point to the TOV equation derived later for the precise statement.
- **The claim that p << rho in ordinary situations ('we saw earlier') is given without numbers, and the c = 1 units hide how extreme the ratio is.** — Learners lack intuition for when relativistic pressure corrections matter. *Suggestion:* Give SI estimates of p/(rho c^2) for water, the Sun's core, white dwarfs and neutron stars.
- **The first definition of dust (all particles at rest in one Lorentz frame) is global, while later use is element by element with varying MCRFs.** — Learners may think dust must be uniformly moving and cannot flow or collapse. *Suggestion:* Redefine dust locally: zero velocity dispersion at each event, with a velocity field that can vary.
- **Heat conduction and viscosity in relativity are dismissed to the bibliography without noting that naive (Eckart-type) relativistic heat-conduction theories violate causality.** — Curious learners may try to add Newtonian-style dissipation terms and hit inconsistencies. *Suggestion:* Add a short note on why dissipative relativistic hydrodynamics needs second-order (Israel-Stewart) theories, with pointers to modern applications such as neutron-star mergers.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'If you run past a cloud of dust, does it look denser, thinner, or the same?' Then 'and how much more energy per cubic metre?' The two answers (one gamma, two gammas) set up N and T.
- Order of explanation that works: continuum and MCRF -> dust density and flux -> N = nU and n = sqrt(-N.N) -> world lines through hypersurfaces -> one-forms as surfaces and flux = <n~, N> -> energy density and the one-form counting argument -> T^{ab} dictionary -> dust T -> symmetry -> conservation -> perfect fluid -> projections -> GR preview -> Gauss.
- Check understanding of §4.3 by asking the learner to compute <dt, N> and <dx, N> for dust moving at 0.6c, and to explain physically why both are 'fluxes'.
- Before the T^{ab} dictionary, have the learner say in words what T^{0x}, T^{x0}, T^{xy} and T^{yy} mean for a concrete system (a beam of dust, a gas in a box).
- Common question: 'Why is T symmetric - isn't momentum flux in x across y different from y-momentum across x?' Walk through the shrinking-cube torque scaling (torque ~ l^3, inertia ~ l^5) and note the printed I = alpha rho l^2 slip.
- Common question: 'Where does gravity go in the Euler equation?' Explain that SR has none; §4.8 inserts the acceleration gravity would cause as a heuristic, made rigorous later in the book.
- When deriving the Euler equation, pause to ask why U^i = 0 in the MCRF does not imply U^i_{,b} = 0 (Exercise 4.16); many learners stall here.
- Emphasize rho + p as inertia with a numerical check: for radiation p = rho/3, so inertia per volume is 4rho/3; for water it is rho to 15 decimal places (SI estimate).
- When learners confuse flux direction with surface orientation, return to one-forms as stacks of surfaces and to Exercise 4.2.
- Use dust as the p = 0 limit whenever a perfect-fluid formula is introduced; ask the learner to reduce it and compare with rho U^aU^b.
- For the GR preview, ask: 'If all of T gravitates, what is the gravitational effect of a box of hot gas compared with the same box cold?' (Hotter means more rho and more p, so more gravity.)
- Flag the three printed slips (Eq. 4.21 T^{i0}, Eq. 4.56 advective term, inertia scaling) when a learner reads alongside the book, so they do not lose confidence in their own algebra.
- For learners with electromagnetism background, connect N^a_{,a} = 0 to charge conservation and preview Exercise 4.25; for others, skip EM and use Exercise 4.22 (photon gas) instead.
