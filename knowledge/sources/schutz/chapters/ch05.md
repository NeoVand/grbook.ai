# SCH ch05 · Preface to curvature

> Physical arguments (redshift, equivalence principle, tides) show gravity forbids global inertial frames and points to curved spacetime; the chapter then builds the whole toolkit of covariant differentiation (Christoffel symbols, metric compatibility, Gamma from the metric) inside the flat Euclidean plane using polar coordinates.

**Pages:** printed 111–140 · pdf 129–158 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 4/5

Section 5.1 is algebraically light but conceptually rich (equivalence principle, locality, curvature as parallelism). Sections 5.2-5.4 are a gentle but index-dense introduction to covariant differentiation; the hardest moves are the tensor-equation argument, the dummy-index relabelling, and the permutation trick for Eq. 5.75. Section 5.5 is terse and notation-heavy (hats, bars, primes). The flat-space setting with Cartesian checks makes the chapter unusually friendly for a first contact with connections.

## Role in the book

This is the hinge between the special-relativity half of the book (chapters 1-4: Lorentz frames, one-forms and tensors, fluids and MCRFs) and the geometry of curved manifolds in chapter 6. Section 5.1 supplies the physical reason curvature is needed: energy conservation forces a gravitational redshift, the redshift rules out a Lorentz frame at rest on Earth, universality of free fall promotes freely falling frames to inertial status, and tidal nonuniformity restricts those frames to be local, which matches the local flatness of Riemannian geometry. Sections 5.2-5.5 then deliberately retreat to a flat plane with curvilinear coordinates, where Cartesian coordinates provide ground truth, to develop coordinate transformations, bases that vary from point to point, the metric in non-Cartesian coordinates, Christoffel symbols, covariant derivatives of all tensor types, the identity that the metric is covariantly constant, the formula for Gamma in terms of metric derivatives, and the distinction between coordinate and noncoordinate bases. Chapter 6 carries these results over nearly unchanged to curved manifolds. What it adds is local flatness made precise, parallel transport and geodesics, and the Riemann tensor.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can derive the first-order gravitational redshift 1 - gh from a no-perpetual-motion argument with a tower, a falling mass and a returning photon.
- Reader can reproduce Schild's spacetime-diagram argument that a static laboratory on Earth cannot be a Lorentz frame, given the measured redshift.
- Reader can explain why universality of free fall makes freely falling frames the natural inertial frames, state the weak and Einstein equivalence principles, and show to first order that a freely falling observer sees no redshift.
- Reader can explain why tidal nonuniformity limits inertial frames to small regions of space and time and connect this to local flatness and to geodesics that fail to stay parallel.
- Reader can test whether a coordinate transformation is admissible using its Jacobian and write the transformation matrices for vectors and one-forms as partial derivatives, verifying that they are mutual inverses.
- Reader can define a curve as a parametrized path, compute its tangent vector, and interpret a vector as the operator that returns the derivative of a scalar along the curve.
- Reader can compute the polar coordinate basis vectors and basis one-forms, their magnitudes, the metric components diag(1, r^2), the inverse metric, and the line element, and distinguish the one-form gradient from the vector gradient.
- Reader can compute derivatives of the polar basis vectors, read off all Christoffel symbols of polar coordinates, and assemble the covariant derivative of a vector field as a (1 1) tensor.
- Reader can derive the divergence and the Laplacian in plane polar coordinates from contraction of the covariant derivative.
- Reader can write the covariant derivative of one-forms and arbitrary tensors (one Gamma term per index with sign fixed by index position) and justify the one-form rule via the product rule on a contraction.
- Reader can prove that the metric is covariantly constant, that Christoffel symbols are symmetric in a coordinate basis, and derive the formula for Gamma in terms of first derivatives of the metric, then apply it to polar coordinates.
- Reader can explain in what sense Christoffel symbols are and are not tensor components.
- Reader can construct the orthonormal polar basis, prove via mixed partial derivatives that it is not a coordinate basis, and convert between coordinate-basis and orthonormal-basis components to reconcile vector-calculus textbook formulas.

## Teaching approach

The chapter has two halves with different styles. The first is a chain of physical thought experiments, each forcing the next conclusion: energy conservation implies redshift; redshift plus a static field forbids a Lorentz frame on Earth; universality of free fall picks out freely falling frames; nonuniform fields make those frames only local; failure of free world lines to stay parallel is identified with curvature. The second half is a patient, component-level but geometrically interpreted construction of tensor calculus in the one setting where the learner can always check the answer: the Euclidean plane in polar coordinates. Every new object (basis vectors, one-forms, metric components, Christoffel symbols, covariant derivative, metric compatibility) is computed explicitly for polar coordinates and cross-checked against Cartesian results or familiar vector-calculus formulas, and general statements are promoted using the argument that an equation between tensors true in Cartesian coordinates holds in every coordinate system.

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, thought-experiment, geometric-first, derivation-heavy, example-driven, computational-recipe, diagram-driven

**Narrative arc**

1. Motivating question: SR's metric is defined by rods and clocks in a global inertial frame; can such a frame exist when gravity is present?
2. Energy-conservation thought experiment on a tower predicts that a climbing photon loses the fraction gh of its energy; Pound-Rebka-Snider and GPS confirm it.
3. Schild's argument: in a static field, successive wave crests follow congruent world lines, so a Minkowski lab frame would give equal crest spacings at top and bottom, contradicting the measured redshift; the Earth frame is not inertial.
4. Universality of free fall means no particle can mark an unaccelerated frame at rest on Earth; freely falling frames are the candidates; the accelerating rocket runs the argument backwards and yields the equivalence principle (weak and Einstein forms).
5. Check: in a frame that begins falling when the photon is emitted, the Doppler shift exactly cancels the gravitational shift to first order, so no redshift is seen.
6. Limitation: frames falling at different places around Earth move in different directions and at different rates, so only local inertial frames exist; the nonuniformity (tidal force) is the true signature of gravity.
7. Geometric reinterpretation: parallel free world lines stay parallel in flat Minkowski space but not in a nonuniform field; like the sphere, spacetime is locally flat but curved, and free-fall paths are geodesics.
8. Strategic retreat: learn the mathematics of curvature first in curvilinear coordinates on the flat Euclidean plane, focusing on parallelism rather than metrics.
9. Formalism I: coordinate transformations and the Jacobian, one-forms defined from gradients of scalars, vectors as tangents to parametrized curves, transformation matrices as partial derivatives.
10. Formalism II: polar basis vectors and one-forms vary in direction and length from point to point; metric components, line element, inverse metric, and vector versus one-form gradient.
11. Formalism III: differentiating a vector requires differentiating the basis; Christoffel symbols encode basis derivatives; covariant derivative components form a single (1 1) tensor.
12. Payoff check: divergence and Laplacian in polar coordinates fall out of contraction.
13. Extension: covariant derivatives of one-forms and higher tensors via the product rule on scalars; systematic one-Gamma-per-index rule.
14. Metric enters: metric compatibility from the Cartesian-then-tensor-equation argument, explicit check in polars, symmetry of Gamma, and inversion to Gamma from metric derivatives.
15. Clarification: Gamma are components of a family of tensors nabla e_alpha but not of one tensor, so they do not transform tensorially.
16. Noncoordinate bases: orthonormal polar basis fails integrability; which results survive; why vector-calculus books get different-looking formulas.
17. Look ahead: what is missing is a quantitative measure of the failure of parallelism, the Riemann tensor.

**Signature moves**

- Derives the gravitational redshift from forbidding perpetual motion before any relativistic gravity theory is written down.
- Draws the light crests on a deliberately arbitrary curved path so the conclusion depends only on time-independence of the field, not on how gravity bends light.
- Turns the equivalence principle around by describing gravity-free acceleration of a rocket, which learners find more familiar than free fall.
- Verifies the candidate inertial frame by computing that the free-fall Doppler shift cancels the redshift to first order.
- Defines flatness and curvature through the fate of initially parallel free-particle world lines (Euclid's axiom) rather than through the metric, and insists the chapter is about parallelism, not metrics.
- Builds all of tensor calculus in the flat plane with polar coordinates, where Cartesian coordinates are always available to check every result.
- Promotes results with the pattern: prove it trivially in Cartesian coordinates, observe it is a tensor equation, conclude it holds in all coordinates.
- Treats the one-form (gradient) as the foundational object and defines vectors as tangents to curves that produce d phi/ds, the modern view, in contrast to transforming-like-a-displacement definitions.
- Explains non-unit polar basis lengths physically: e_theta must span one radian, so it grows with r, while d-tilde-theta grows near the origin.
- Uses the constant vector e_x having non-constant polar components as the motivating paradox for Christoffel symbols.
- Re-derives the textbook polar Laplacian as a reward for the formalism.
- Gives a mnemonic for covariant-derivative index placement (derivative index last, one Gamma per index, sign by up or down) and a reason for the sign (contraction of basis one-forms and vectors is constant).
- Takes a nuanced stance on whether Christoffel symbols are tensors instead of the blunt textbook denial.
- Reconciles the reader's prior vector-calculus formulas by exposing that those books silently use a noncoordinate orthonormal basis.

## Section by section

### §5.1 On the relation of gravitation to curvature — p.111 (pdf 129)

Opens by recalling that SR rests on global inertial frames whose metric is physically defined by clocks and rods, then tests whether gravity allows such frames. Subsections: the gravitational redshift from a tower thought experiment and its confirmation (Pound-Rebka-Snider, GPS); Schild's proof that no Lorentz frame is at rest on Earth; the principle of equivalence (universality of free fall, rocket, weak vs Einstein versions, locality, many freely falling frames); the redshift re-examined in a freely falling frame (none to first order); local inertial frames limited by nonuniformity (Figure 5.3); tidal forces as the essence of gravity; and the role of curvature, where non-parallel free world lines, local flatness of Riemannian spaces, and Einstein's identification of free-fall paths with geodesics motivate a study of curvature, begun in flat space with curvilinear coordinates.

**Concepts:** gravitational redshift, Pound-Rebka-Snider experiment, nonexistence of a Lorentz frame at rest on Earth, universality of free fall, weak equivalence principle, Einstein equivalence principle, local inertial frame, tidal forces, flat versus curved geometry via parallelism, local flatness, geodesic

**Key moves**
- Mass m dropped from height h arrives with energy m + mgh; converted to a photon and sent up, it must arrive with energy m, otherwise a perpetual-motion engine results; hence E'/E = 1 - gh.
- In a static field the second crest's world line is a time translate of the first; Minkowski geometry would then give equal crest intervals at bottom and top, but frequency is higher at the bottom, so the lab frame is not Lorentz.
- Gravity affects all bodies identically, so no free particle can define rest in a non-falling frame; a freely falling frame keeps free particles at uniform velocity.
- Rocket in empty space: dropped objects fall together and held objects have weight proportional to mass, so uniform gravity is equivalent to uniform acceleration.
- Weak equivalence principle concerns motion under gravity alone; Einstein's version asserts all local laws take their SR form in freely falling frames (used in chapter 7).
- Free-fall frame starting at rest at emission acquires speed gh during the light travel time h; the Doppler factor (1+gh)/sqrt(1-g^2h^2) cancels the redshift to first order.
- Frames falling toward Earth at different points converge and fall at different rates with height, so a rigid global freely falling frame is impossible; inertial frames are local, like MCRFs.
- Tidal forces are defined as field nonuniformities and are taken as the fundamental manifestation of gravity.
- Minkowski space is flat (parallel free world lines remain parallel) but non-Euclidean (null lines have zero length); nonuniform gravity breaks parallelism, so spacetime is curved yet locally flat like a sphere.
- Plan: study curvilinear coordinates on the flat plane to develop the tools; emphasis on parallelism, not metrics.

### §5.2 Tensor algebra in polar coordinates — p.118 (pdf 136)

Introduces general coordinates on the Euclidean plane with polar coordinates as the running example. Shows how coordinate increments transform, requires a nonvanishing Jacobian, and contrasts the old definition of vectors (transform like displacements) with the modern one built on one-forms defined as gradients of scalar fields, whose transformation law follows from the chain rule and acts on row vectors. Distinguishes paths from curves (parametrized paths), defines tangent vectors and the interpretation of a vector as the thing that produces d phi/ds from d-tilde-phi. Computes the polar basis vectors and basis one-forms, observes that they change direction and magnitude from point to point, computes the metric components diag(1, r^2), the line element dr^2 + r^2 d theta^2 (warning against confusing it with the tensor d-tilde-r tensor d-tilde-r + r^2 d-tilde-theta tensor d-tilde-theta), the inverse metric, and the vector gradient, whose components differ from those of the one-form gradient except in Cartesian coordinates.

**Concepts:** curvilinear coordinate transformation, Jacobian of a coordinate transformation, transformation matrices for vectors and one-forms, gradient one-form, curve versus path, tangent vector, coordinate basis vectors and basis one-forms in polar coordinates, position-dependent basis, metric tensor in polar coordinates, line element, inverse metric and the vector gradient

**Key moves**
- Increments Delta r and Delta theta are linear in Delta x and Delta y to first order (Eq. 5.4); a general coordinate map needs a nonzero Jacobian to assign distinct labels to distinct points.
- One-form d-tilde-phi is defined by its components (partial derivatives of phi) in any coordinates; the chain rule gives its transformation with matrix Lambda^alpha_beta' = dx^alpha/dx^beta', summed on the first index.
- The vector and one-form transformation matrices multiply to the identity by the chain rule.
- A curve is a map from an interval to the plane; reparametrizing gives a new curve with the same path; the tangent V with components d xi/ds, d eta/ds satisfies d phi/ds = <d-tilde-phi, V>.
- e_r = cos theta e_x + sin theta e_y, e_theta = -r sin theta e_x + r cos theta e_y; d-tilde-r and d-tilde-theta follow analogously; transformation matrix entries are fixed by index placement.
- |e_theta| = r because e_theta represents a displacement of one radian; |d-tilde-theta| = 1/r.
- g_alpha'beta' = e_alpha' . e_beta' gives g_rr = 1, g_theta theta = r^2, g_r theta = 0; line element dl^2 = dr^2 + r^2 d theta^2.
- Inverse metric diag(1, r^-2) raises the gradient: vector gradient has components (phi_,r , phi_,theta / r^2).

### §5.3 Tensor calculus in polar coordinates — p.124 (pdf 142)

Starts from the paradox that the constant vector e_x has polar components (cos theta, -sin theta / r) that vary, so differentiating components alone cannot give the derivative of a vector. Computes derivatives of the polar basis vectors (with Figure 5.6 as a picture), checks that the product rule including basis derivatives yields d e_x / d theta = 0, and defines Christoffel symbols as the components of basis-vector derivatives. Tabulates all polar Christoffel symbols, assembles the covariant derivative V^alpha_;beta and interprets it as the components of a single (1 1) tensor nabla V computable either directly with Gamma or by transforming from Cartesian coordinates. Covariant derivative of a scalar is the gradient. Divergence follows by contraction, giving the polar divergence and Laplacian formulas. The covariant derivative of a one-form is derived from the product rule applied to the scalar p_alpha V^alpha, and the pattern is extended to tensors of any rank.

**Concepts:** derivatives of basis vectors, Christoffel symbols, covariant derivative of a vector, covariant derivative tensor nabla V, covariant derivative of a scalar, divergence in curvilinear coordinates, Laplacian in polar coordinates, covariant derivative of one-forms and higher-rank tensors, Leibniz rule for covariant derivative

**Key moves**
- Differentiate e_r and e_theta using constancy of e_x and e_y: d e_r/dr = 0, d e_r/d theta = e_theta / r, d e_theta/dr = e_theta / r, d e_theta/d theta = -r e_r.
- Expand e_x in polar basis and differentiate with the product rule; component-derivative terms and basis-derivative terms cancel to give zero.
- Write d V/dx^beta = (dV^alpha/dx^beta) e_alpha + V^alpha d e_alpha/dx^beta; define Gamma^mu_alpha beta by d e_alpha/dx^beta = Gamma^mu_alpha beta e_mu.
- Relabel dummy indices to factor out e_alpha: V^alpha_;beta = V^alpha_,beta + V^mu Gamma^alpha_mu beta.
- Regard beta as a slot: nabla V maps e_beta to d V/dx^beta, a (1 1) tensor; its components in any system can be computed directly or by tensor transformation from Cartesian.
- Contraction is frame invariant, so V^alpha_;alpha in polars equals the Cartesian divergence; with Gamma^alpha_r alpha = 1/r this is (1/r) d(r V^r)/dr + d V^theta/d theta.
- Raise the gradient then take divergence to get the polar Laplacian.
- Apply the product rule to phi = p_alpha V^alpha, substitute V^alpha_;beta, and identify the remaining bracket as a tensor component, which defines the one-form covariant derivative with a minus-sign Gamma term (Eq. 5.63).
- Generalize: add a +Gamma term for each upper index and a -Gamma term for each lower index.

### §5.4 Christoffel symbols and the metric — p.130 (pdf 148)

Brings the metric into the covariant derivative. Because in Cartesian coordinates a vector and its associated one-form have equal components and nabla just differentiates components, lowering an index commutes with covariant differentiation; being a tensor statement, this holds in all coordinates, and comparing with the product rule shows the metric is covariantly constant. The identity is checked explicitly for g_theta theta;r in polar coordinates. A perspective paragraph notes that metric compatibility will persist in curved spaces. Symmetry of Christoffel symbols in their lower indices is proven from the symmetry of the second covariant derivative of a scalar. Three permutations of the compatibility equation are combined to solve for Gamma in terms of metric derivatives, checked on Gamma^theta_r theta = 1/r. The section ends by explaining that Gamma^mu_alpha beta are components of the (1 1) tensors nabla e_alpha, one per basis vector, but not of one (1 2) tensor, which is why they do not obey the tensor transformation law.

**Concepts:** metric compatibility, Cartesian-then-tensor-equation argument, symmetry of Christoffel symbols, Christoffel symbols from the metric, non-tensorial nature of Christoffel symbols

**Key moves**
- In Cartesian coordinates, nabla_beta of the one-form associated with V equals g(nabla_beta V, .) because components are equal and Gamma = 0; as a tensor equation it holds everywhere: V_alpha;beta = g_alpha mu V^mu_;beta.
- Covariantly differentiate V_alpha = g_alpha mu V^mu with the product rule and compare, for arbitrary V, to conclude g_alpha mu;beta = 0.
- Explicit check in polar coordinates: g_theta theta;r = 2r - r - r = 0.
- Second covariant derivative of a scalar is symmetric in Cartesian coordinates, hence in all; with partial derivatives commuting, Gamma^mu_alpha beta phi_,mu is symmetric for arbitrary phi, so Gamma is symmetric.
- Write compatibility for three index permutations, add two and subtract one, use symmetry of Gamma and g, divide by 2 and contract with g^alpha gamma to obtain Gamma from metric derivatives.
- Recompute Gamma^theta_r theta from g_theta theta = r^2 to check.
- Changing coordinates changes which tensors nabla e_alpha are relevant, so Gamma does not transform as a single tensor; Gamma vanishes in Cartesian but not polar coordinates.

### §5.5 Noncoordinate bases — p.134 (pdf 152)

Shows that not every field of bases arises from coordinates. The polar coordinate basis is not unit; the normalized basis e_r-hat = e_r, e_theta-hat = e_theta / r with dual one-forms d-tilde-r and r d-tilde-theta is orthonormal. Asking whether coordinates (xi, eta) generate it reduces, via the one-forms, to requiring partial derivatives of eta equal to -sin theta and cos theta, whose mixed partials disagree, so the basis is noncoordinate. Vector-calculus textbooks use this orthonormal basis, which explains why their divergence formula differs from Eq. 5.56 by the factor relating components. In a noncoordinate basis the comma no longer means a partial derivative with respect to a coordinate, Christoffel symbols can still be defined but are not symmetric, and the metric formula for Gamma fails. The general criterion is an integrability condition on the transformation matrix. Coordinate bases are simpler and used throughout the book; orthonormal bases are popular because the metric is the identity, but the only orthonormal coordinate basis in flat space is Cartesian.

**Concepts:** orthonormal basis, noncoordinate basis, integrability conditions, physical versus coordinate components

**Key moves**
- Normalize the polar basis to get e_r-hat and e_theta-hat and dual one-forms; verify orthonormality.
- Assume the orthonormal one-forms are gradients d-tilde-xi, d-tilde-eta; express them in Cartesian one-forms to get d eta/dx = -sin theta, d eta/dy = cos theta.
- Require equality of mixed partials; the condition fails (the left-hand combination equals 1/r), so no such eta exists.
- Coordinate-basis components (a, b) become orthonormal components (a, r b); the divergence formula changes accordingly to Eq. 5.87, with no contradiction.
- Define phi_,mu as d-tilde-phi(e_mu); in the orthonormal basis phi_,theta-hat = (1/r) d phi/d theta, not a partial derivative.
- Symmetry of Gamma and Eq. 5.75 rely on commuting partial derivatives and so hold only in coordinate bases (generalization deferred to Exercise 5.20).
- A basis is a coordinate basis only if its transformation matrix satisfies the integrability conditions (curl-free rows).

## Concepts

### gravitational redshift

*phenomenon · core* · also: gravitational frequency shift, Einstein redshift

Light climbing a height h in a gravitational field of strength g arrives with frequency (and energy) reduced by the fractional amount gh/c^2 to first order; equivalently clocks higher in the field run faster as seen from below.

**How introduced:** Derived without any theory of gravity from an idealized tower: a mass falls, gains energy mgh, is converted into a photon sent upward and reconverted to mass at the top. Forbidding perpetual motion requires the photon to lose exactly the energy gained, giving E'/E = 1 - gh (units c = 1). Then confirmed by Pound-Rebka-Snider and GPS, and reinterpreted as a Doppler shift seen from a freely falling frame.

**Prerequisites:** mass-energy equivalence, photon energy E = h nu, Newtonian free fall

$$
E'/E = h\nu'/h\nu = 1 - gh + O(v^4)
$$

**Notes:** The O(v^4) notation reflects that gh is of order v^2 for the falling mass. The book states only the first-order result.

**Where:** SCH §5.1 p.111 (pdf 129); SCH §5.1 p.112 (pdf 130); SCH §5.1 p.115 (pdf 133)

### Pound-Rebka-Snider experiment

*experiment-or-observation · introduced* · also: Pound-Rebka experiment, Harvard tower experiment

Laboratory measurement (1960, improved 1965) of the gravitational frequency shift of gamma rays over a vertical height of 22.5 m, using the Mössbauer effect for extreme frequency resolution; agreed with the predicted gh shift to about 1%.

**How introduced:** Cited immediately after the tower thought experiment as direct confirmation, then reused as the physical input to Schild's argument (measured nu_bottom > nu_top).

**Prerequisites:** gravitational redshift

**Where:** SCH §5.1 p.112 (pdf 130); SCH §5.1 p.113 (pdf 131)

### nonexistence of a Lorentz frame at rest on Earth

*theorem · developed* · also: Schild's argument, Schild's redshift argument

Given a time-independent gravitational field and the observed redshift, a frame at rest relative to Earth cannot have Minkowski geometry, because congruent world lines of successive light crests would force equal crest intervals at bottom and top, contradicting the measured frequency difference.

**How introduced:** Spacetime diagram (Figure 5.2) with height on the horizontal axis; tower bottom and top are vertical lines; two crests are drawn on arbitrary wiggly curves so that no assumption about how gravity bends light is made. Static field implies the second path is a time translate of the first; in Minkowski geometry this gives equal Delta t, but Delta t = 1/nu differs.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational redshift, spacetime diagram, Minkowski metric, time-translation invariance of a static field

$$
\Delta t = 1/\nu,\quad \nu_{\rm bottom} > \nu_{\rm top} \Rightarrow \Delta t_{\rm top} > \Delta t_{\rm bottom}
$$

**Notes:** Attributed to Schild (1967). The argument shows the Earth frame is not inertial, not that inertial frames do not exist.

**Where:** SCH §5.1 p.113 (pdf 131); SCH §5.1 p.114 (pdf 132)

### universality of free fall

*principle · developed* · also: Galileo's principle, equality of inertial and gravitational mass

All test bodies started with the same position and velocity follow the same trajectory in a gravitational field regardless of mass or composition.

**How introduced:** Contrasted with electromagnetism (charge-to-mass ratio dependence) and the strong and weak interactions, which act selectively and so permit an unaffected particle to mark an inertial frame; gravity does not, so no particle can mark a non-falling inertial frame on Earth. Galileo's Pisa experiment is invoked; a footnote points to the Eötvös experiment.

**Prerequisites:** inertial frame

**Where:** SCH §5.1 p.114 (pdf 132)

### weak equivalence principle

*principle · core* · also: principle of equivalence between gravity and acceleration, WEP

Motion of bodies under gravity alone is locally indistinguishable from motion viewed from a uniformly accelerated frame: a uniform gravitational field is equivalent to uniform acceleration relative to inertial frames, and freely falling frames are locally inertial for free particles.

**How introduced:** First argued forward (freely falling frames keep free particles at uniform velocity), then turned around with a rocket accelerating in empty space: dropped objects fall together toward the rear and held objects have weight proportional to mass. Called weak because it concerns only gravitational motion; noted as already a foundation of Newtonian gravity.

**Prerequisites:** universality of free fall, inertial frame

**Where:** SCH §5.1 p.114 (pdf 132); SCH §5.1 p.115 (pdf 133)

### Einstein equivalence principle

*principle · introduced* · also: EEP, the book's 'stronger form' of the equivalence principle

In a freely falling local inertial frame, the differential equations expressing all nongravitational laws of physics take the same local form as in special relativity; this tells how every force behaves in a gravitational field.

**How introduced:** Stated briefly as Einstein's extension of the weak principle beyond particle motion, with a forward pointer to its use in chapter 7.

**Prerequisites:** weak equivalence principle, local inertial frame

**Notes:** Schutz calls this a stronger form of the principle than the weak one. It should not be confused with the modern 'strong equivalence principle', which also covers self-gravitating bodies and gravitational experiments.

**Where:** SCH §5.1 p.115 (pdf 133)

### local inertial frame

*definition · core* · also: freely falling frame, local Lorentz frame

A freely falling reference frame that behaves as an inertial frame of special relativity only within a region of space and time small enough that nonuniformities of the gravitational field are undetectable; mathematically, inertial at a point.

**How introduced:** Once freely falling frames are shown to see no redshift, the book asks how far such a frame can extend. Figure 5.3 draws a lattice falling toward Earth: only the row through B, aimed at Earth's centre, moves along a genuine free-fall path; rows A and C would have to fall obliquely toward the centre, so a rigid lattice cannot follow them. Because g also changes with distance from Earth, parts of the lattice nearer and farther from Earth would need different accelerations, and both problems worsen as the fall proceeds. The limitation is likened to an MCRF of a fluid (valid only locally and momentarily); how small the region must be depends on the field's nonuniformity and the sensitivity of the experiment, and any theory of gravity must admit frames that are inertial at a point.

**Prerequisites:** weak equivalence principle, tidal forces, momentarily comoving reference frame

**Notes:** There are infinitely many local inertial frames at each event, differing by velocity and spatial orientation.

**Where:** SCH §5.1 p.115 (pdf 133); SCH §5.1 p.116 (pdf 134)

### tidal forces

*phenomenon · developed* · also: gravitational field nonuniformity, tidal acceleration

The differences in gravitational acceleration between nearby points; they cannot be removed by going to a freely falling frame and are regarded in GR as the fundamental, frame-independent manifestation of gravity.

**How introduced:** Named after ocean tides: a uniform field would make Earth fall freely with no tides, and tidal bulges come from the difference in lunar and solar pull across Earth's diameter. Identified as exactly what prevents global inertial frames.

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian gravity, local inertial frame

**Notes:** Made quantitative later via geodesic deviation and the Riemann tensor.

**Where:** SCH §5.1 p.117 (pdf 135)

### flat versus curved geometry via parallelism

*definition · developed* · also: Euclid's parallel postulate as flatness criterion, flat non-Euclidean geometry of Minkowski space

A space is flat if locally straight lines (geodesics) that start parallel remain parallel everywhere (Euclid's axiom holds); it is curved if they do not. Flatness is distinct from being Euclidean: Minkowski space is flat but has an indefinite metric with null straight lines.

**How introduced:** Free-particle world lines are the probe: in SR initially parallel ones stay parallel, so Minkowski space is flat; in a nonuniform field nearby free world lines diverge or converge, so spacetime is curved. The sphere, where great circles always meet, is the Euclidean-signature example.

**Prerequisites:** tidal forces, Minkowski metric

**Notes:** Sets up the chapter's stated emphasis on parallelism rather than metrics.

**Where:** SCH §5.1 p.117 (pdf 135)

### local flatness

*principle · introduced* · also: locally Euclidean, local flatness of Riemannian spaces

In any Riemannian space, a sufficiently small neighbourhood of any point can be treated as flat to good accuracy even though the space is globally curved.

**How introduced:** Map analogy: a town map on flat paper is accurate, a flat map of the whole globe is badly distorted. Presented as the geometric counterpart of the existence of local inertial frames.

**Prerequisites:** flat versus curved geometry via parallelism

**Notes:** Riemann (1826-1866) credited in a footnote. Made precise in chapter 6.

**Where:** SCH §5.1 p.117 (pdf 135)

### geodesic

*definition · mention* · also: locally straight line, free-fall world line

A curve that is locally straight in a (possibly curved) geometry; in GR the world lines of freely falling particles are identified with the geodesics of curved spacetime.

**How introduced:** Introduced informally as the generalization of straight lines on a sphere (great circles), then Einstein's key identification: free-fall paths are straight in local inertial frames but fail to stay parallel globally, just like geodesics.

**Prerequisites:** local flatness, local inertial frame

**Notes:** Formal definition via parallel transport comes in chapter 6.

**Where:** SCH §5.1 p.117 (pdf 135)

### curvilinear coordinate transformation

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: general coordinates (xi, eta), change of coordinates

A smooth invertible relabelling of points of the plane, (x, y) -> (xi(x,y), eta(x,y)), whose increments are related to Cartesian increments linearly to first order by the matrix of partial derivatives.

**How introduced:** Polar coordinates are the concrete case (Eqs. 5.3-5.4), then generalized to arbitrary (xi, eta), with the bad example xi = x, eta = 1 showing distinct points can collapse to one label.

**Prerequisites:** partial derivatives, chain rule

$$
x = r\cos\theta,\ y = r\sin\theta
$$

$$
\Delta\xi = (\partial\xi/\partial x)\Delta x + (\partial\xi/\partial y)\Delta y
$$

**Where:** SCH §5.2 p.118 (pdf 136)

### Jacobian of a coordinate transformation

*definition · introduced* · also: Jacobian determinant, singular transformation

The determinant of the matrix of partial derivatives of new coordinates with respect to old; a transformation is locally one-to-one where it is nonzero and singular where it vanishes.

**How introduced:** Motivated by requiring that zero coordinate increments imply zero displacement, i.e. the linear system (5.5) has only the trivial null solution.

**Prerequisites:** curvilinear coordinate transformation, determinant

$$
\det(\partial\xi^{\alpha'}/\partial x^{\beta}) \neq 0

**Notes:** Polar coordinates are singular at r = 0 (Exercise 5.3).

**Where:** SCH §5.2 p.118 (pdf 136)

### transformation matrices for vectors and one-forms

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: Lambda matrices, Lambda^alpha'_beta and Lambda^alpha_beta'

In general coordinates the vector transformation matrix is Lambda^alpha'_beta = dx^alpha'/dx^beta and the one-form matrix is Lambda^alpha_beta' = dx^alpha/dx^beta'; they are position-dependent inverses of one another, and index placement alone tells which derivative to use.

**How introduced:** Vector law from displacement transformation (Eq. 5.7); one-form law from the chain rule on gradients written as a row vector multiplying a matrix (Eq. 5.12), so summation is on the first index. Inverse relation verified by multiplying matrices and using the chain rule. Noted that Lorentz matrices were symmetric, hiding the row/column distinction in SR.

**Prerequisites:** curvilinear coordinate transformation, chain rule, matrix inverse

$$
V^{\alpha'} = \Lambda^{\alpha'}{}_{\beta}V^{\beta}
$$

$$
p_{\beta'} = \Lambda^{\alpha}{}_{\beta'}p_{\alpha}
$$

$$
\Lambda^{\alpha'}{}_{\beta}\Lambda^{\beta}{}_{\gamma'} = \delta^{\alpha'}{}_{\gamma'}

### gradient one-form

*mathematical-object · core* · also: d-tilde-phi, one-form as foundation of tensor algebra

For a scalar field phi, the one-form whose components in any coordinates are the partial derivatives of phi; its transformation law follows automatically from the chain rule.

**How introduced:** Presented as the modern foundation: instead of defining one vector (the displacement) and others by analogy, a whole class of one-forms is defined from derivatives, and vectors are then linear maps of one-forms to numbers. Basis one-forms d-tilde-r, d-tilde-theta are drawn as families of constant-r and constant-theta curves.

**Prerequisites:** one-form, partial derivatives

$$
\tilde{d}\phi \to (\partial\phi/\partial\xi,\ \partial\phi/\partial\eta)

**Where:** SCH §5.2 p.119 (pdf 137); SCH §5.2 p.120 (pdf 138)

### curve versus path

*definition · developed* · also: parametrized curve, reparametrization

A path is a set of points; a curve is a map from a real interval into the space, i.e. a path with a parameter value attached to each point. Reparametrizing gives a different curve with the same path.

**How introduced:** Adopts modern mathematical terminology; Figure 5.4 shows parameter tick marks s = 1..8 along a path. Emphasizes that infinitely many curves share a path.

$$
\{\xi = f(s),\ \eta = g(s),\ a \le s \le b\}
$$

**Where:** SCH §5.2 p.120 (pdf 138); SCH §5.2 p.121 (pdf 139)

### tangent vector

*mathematical-object · core* · also: d/ds, vector as directional-derivative operator

The vector with components (d xi/ds, d eta/ds) associated with a parametrized curve; contracted with d-tilde-phi it gives the rate of change d phi/ds of any scalar along the curve, which is why it can be identified with the operator d/ds.

**How introduced:** Derived from d phi/ds = <d-tilde-phi, V>: the one-form depends only on phi, the vector only on the curve. Components transform like displacements under coordinate changes since s is coordinate-independent. A path has many parallel tangents of different lengths (one per parametrization); V(s) reaches roughly from s to s+1.

**Prerequisites:** curve versus path, gradient one-form

$$
d\phi/ds = \langle\tilde{d}\phi, \vec{V}\rangle

$$
\vec{V} \to (d\xi/ds,\ d\eta/ds)

**Notes:** Book mentions but does not adopt the d/ds notation.

**Where:** SCH §5.2 p.120 (pdf 138); SCH §5.2 p.121 (pdf 139)

### coordinate basis vectors and basis one-forms in polar coordinates

*mathematical-object · core* · also: e_r, e_theta, d-tilde-r, d-tilde-theta, polar coordinate basis

e_r = cos theta e_x + sin theta e_y and e_theta = -r sin theta e_x + r cos theta e_y point along increasing r and theta; the dual one-forms are d-tilde-r = cos theta d-tilde-x + sin theta d-tilde-y and d-tilde-theta = (-sin theta d-tilde-x + cos theta d-tilde-y)/r.

**How introduced:** Computed from e_alpha' = Lambda^beta_alpha' e_beta with Lambda entries as partial derivatives; one-forms computed analogously with a nod to the ordinary calculus increments (Eq. 5.4). Figure 5.5 draws the vectors at three points and the one-forms as short segments of constant-r and constant-theta curves.

**Prerequisites:** transformation matrices for vectors and one-forms, gradient one-form

$$
\vec{e}_r = \cos\theta\,\vec{e}_x + \sin\theta\,\vec{e}_y
$$

$$
\vec{e}_\theta = -r\sin\theta\,\vec{e}_x + r\cos\theta\,\vec{e}_y
$$

$$
\tilde{d}\theta = -r^{-1}\sin\theta\,\tilde{d}x + r^{-1}\cos\theta\,\tilde{d}y

**Where:** SCH §5.2 p.121 (pdf 139); SCH §5.2 p.122 (pdf 140)

### position-dependent basis

*phenomenon · core* · also: non-constant basis, non-unit coordinate basis

In curvilinear coordinates the basis vectors and one-forms change direction and magnitude from point to point; for polar coordinates |e_r| = 1, |e_theta| = r, |d-tilde-r| = 1, |d-tilde-theta| = 1/r.

**How introduced:** Flagged as a point of great importance using Figure 5.5 (bases at A and C not parallel). Lengths explained physically: e_theta represents a one-radian displacement, which is longer far from the origin; d-tilde-theta is larger near the origin because a given vector sweeps more angle there.

**Prerequisites:** coordinate basis vectors and basis one-forms in polar coordinates

$$
|\vec{e}_\theta|^2 = r^2
$$

$$
|\vec{e}_r| = 1,\ |\tilde{d}r| = 1,\ |\tilde{d}\theta| = r^{-1}

**Notes:** This variation, not curvature, is what forces Christoffel symbols in flat space.

### metric tensor in polar coordinates

*mathematical-object · core* · also: g_alpha beta polar, metric components diag(1, r^2)

The Euclidean metric tensor, whose Cartesian components are delta_alpha beta, has polar components g_alpha'beta' = e_alpha' . e_beta', namely g_rr = 1, g_theta theta = r^2, g_r theta = 0.

**How introduced:** Computed directly as dot products of the polar basis vectors using the Cartesian metric; the same tensor, different components.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, coordinate basis vectors and basis one-forms in polar coordinates

g_{\alpha'\beta'} = \vec{e}_{\alpha'}\cdot\vec{e}_{\beta'}

(g_{\alpha\beta})_{\rm polar} = {\rm diag}(1, r^2)

### line element

*convention · developed* · also: dl^2, ds^2 as display of metric components

The squared length of an infinitesimal displacement written in terms of coordinate differentials, e.g. dl^2 = dr^2 + r^2 d theta^2; a compact display of metric components together with the coordinates used.

**How introduced:** Obtained as |dr e_r + d theta e_theta|^2, then contrasted with the tensor g = d-tilde-r (x) d-tilde-r + r^2 d-tilde-theta (x) d-tilde-theta, which is an operator that yields dl^2 when fed the displacement twice; the book admits the notations look confusingly alike but keeps the traditional form.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor in polar coordinates, tensor product of one-forms

$$
dl^2 = dr^2 + r^2\,d\theta^2
$$

$$
\mathbf{g} = \tilde{d}r\otimes\tilde{d}r + r^2\,\tilde{d}\theta\otimes\tilde{d}\theta

**Notes:** Here d means an infinitesimal coordinate increment, not the basis one-form.

**Where:** SCH §5.2 p.123 (pdf 141); SCH §5.2 p.124 (pdf 142)

### inverse metric and the vector gradient

*operation · developed* · also: index raising in polar coordinates, g^alpha beta

The inverse metric g^alpha beta (polar: g^rr = 1, g^theta theta = 1/r^2) maps one-forms to vectors; the vector gradient of phi has polar components (d phi/dr, r^-2 d phi/d theta), different from the one-form components (d phi/dr, d phi/d theta).

**How introduced:** Invert diag(1, r^2) and apply to the gradient one-form; conclusion drawn that even in Euclidean space vectors and their one-forms have different components except in Cartesian coordinates.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor in polar coordinates, gradient one-form

$$
(\vec{d}\phi)^\alpha = g^{\alpha\beta}\phi_{,\beta}
$$

$$
(\vec{d}\phi)^\theta = r^{-2}\,\partial\phi/\partial\theta
$$

**Notes:** Needed later for the polar Laplacian.

**Where:** SCH §5.2 p.124 (pdf 142)

### derivatives of basis vectors

*operation · core* · also: change of e_r with theta, basis-vector derivatives in polar coordinates

Since polar basis vectors vary with position, their partial derivatives with respect to the coordinates are nonzero vectors: d e_r/dr = 0, d e_r/d theta = e_theta/r, d e_theta/dr = e_theta/r, d e_theta/d theta = -r e_r.

**How introduced:** Motivated by the paradox that the constant field e_x has non-constant polar components (cos theta, -sin theta/r), so component derivatives alone cannot give the vector derivative. Computed by differentiating Cartesian expansions with constant e_x, e_y, and pictured in Figure 5.6 as the small difference between nearby radial unit vectors, which points along e_theta.

**Prerequisites:** coordinate basis vectors and basis one-forms in polar coordinates, position-dependent basis

$$
\partial\vec{e}_r/\partial\theta = r^{-1}\vec{e}_\theta
$$

$$
\partial\vec{e}_\theta/\partial\theta = -r\,\vec{e}_r
$$

$$
\partial\vec{V}/\partial x^\beta = (\partial V^\alpha/\partial x^\beta)\vec{e}_\alpha + V^\alpha\,\partial\vec{e}_\alpha/\partial x^\beta

### Christoffel symbols

*mathematical-object · core* · also: connection coefficients, Gamma^mu_alpha beta

The coefficients Gamma^mu_alpha beta defined by d e_alpha/dx^beta = Gamma^mu_alpha beta e_mu: the mu-th component of the rate of change of basis vector e_alpha along coordinate x^beta. For polar coordinates the only nonzero ones are Gamma^theta_r theta = Gamma^theta_theta r = 1/r and Gamma^r_theta theta = -r.

**How introduced:** Named after noticing that the basis-derivative term in the vector derivative is a vector and so can be expanded in the basis. Index roles spelled out (which basis vector, which coordinate, which component). Polar values read off from the basis derivatives; emphasized that once computed they let one work entirely in polar coordinates without reference to Cartesian vectors.

**Prerequisites:** derivatives of basis vectors

$$
\partial\vec{e}_\alpha/\partial x^\beta = \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\vec{e}_\mu
$$

$$
\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta} = \Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta r} = 1/r,\quad \Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta} = -r
$$

**Notes:** Tensor status deliberately postponed to §5.4.

**Where:** SCH §5.3 p.126 (pdf 144); SCH §5.3 p.127 (pdf 145)

### covariant derivative of a vector

*operation · core* · also: semicolon derivative, V^alpha_;beta

The components of the derivative of a vector field along coordinate x^beta, including the change of basis: V^alpha_;beta = V^alpha_,beta + V^mu Gamma^alpha_mu beta.

**How introduced:** Product rule on V = V^alpha e_alpha, substitute the Christoffel definition, relabel dummy indices to factor out e_alpha, and name the bracket with a semicolon.

**Prerequisites:** Christoffel symbols, Einstein summation convention

$$
V^\alpha{}_{;\beta} = V^\alpha{}_{,\beta} + V^\mu\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\mu\beta}
$$

$$
\partial\vec{V}/\partial x^\beta = V^\alpha{}_{;\beta}\,\vec{e}_\alpha
$$

**Where:** SCH §5.3 p.126 (pdf 144); SCH §5.3 p.127 (pdf 145)

### covariant derivative tensor nabla V

*mathematical-object · core* · also: nabla V as a (1 1) tensor, gradient of a vector field

The (1 1) tensor field that maps a basis vector e_beta to the vector d V/dx^beta; its components in any coordinates are V^alpha_;beta, which equal plain partial derivatives in Cartesian coordinates and can be computed either with that system's Christoffel symbols or by tensor transformation from Cartesian components.

**How introduced:** Obtained by treating the free derivative index beta as an input slot. Stressed as the foundation of all later work: one geometric object, many component representations.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative of a vector, tensor as linear map

$$
(\nabla\vec{V})^\alpha{}_\beta = (\nabla_\beta\vec{V})^\alpha = V^\alpha{}_{;\beta}
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 5.10 asks for a proof that it is a (1 1) tensor.

**Where:** SCH §5.3 p.128 (pdf 146)

### covariant derivative of a scalar

*identity · introduced* · also: nabla f = d-tilde-f

Because a scalar does not refer to any basis, its covariant derivative is just its gradient one-form, with components equal to partial derivatives.

**How introduced:** Short argument: covariant and partial derivatives differ only through basis changes, which a scalar lacks.

**Prerequisites:** gradient one-form, covariant derivative of a vector

$$
\nabla_\alpha f = \partial f/\partial x^\alpha
$$

$$
\nabla f = \tilde{d}f

**Where:** SCH §5.3 p.128 (pdf 146)

### divergence in curvilinear coordinates

*operation · developed* · also: V^alpha_;alpha, contraction of nabla V

The scalar obtained by contracting nabla V on its two indices; since contraction is basis independent it equals the Cartesian divergence, and in polar coordinates it is (1/r) d(r V^r)/dr + d V^theta/d theta.

**How introduced:** Link-up with familiar vector calculus before more theory: contract, use Gamma^alpha_r alpha = 1/r and Gamma^alpha_theta alpha = 0, and recognize the known formula (in coordinate-basis components).

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative tensor nabla V, contraction, Christoffel symbols

$$
V^\alpha{}_{;\alpha} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(rV^r) + \frac{\partial V^\theta}{\partial\theta}

**Notes:** Differs in appearance from textbook formula 5.87 only because of basis choice.

**Where:** SCH §5.3 p.128 (pdf 146); SCH §5.3 p.129 (pdf 147)

### Laplacian in polar coordinates

*identity · developed* · also: nabla^2 in polar coordinates, divergence of the gradient

nabla^2 phi = (1/r) d/dr (r d phi/dr) + (1/r^2) d^2 phi/d theta^2, the divergence of the vector gradient, identically equal to the Cartesian sum of second derivatives.

**How introduced:** Advertised at the end of §5.1 as a bonus that demystifies a memorized formula; obtained by first raising the gradient one-form to a vector (since divergence needs a vector) and inserting into the polar divergence.

**Prerequisites:** divergence in curvilinear coordinates, inverse metric and the vector gradient

$$
\nabla^2\phi = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial\theta^2}
$$

**Where:** SCH §5.3 p.129 (pdf 147)

### covariant derivative of one-forms and higher-rank tensors

*operation · core* · also: p_alpha;beta, one Gamma per index rule

For a one-form, the partial derivative of the components minus the one-form contracted with the Christoffel symbols (Eq. 5.63); for any tensor, add to the partial derivative one Christoffel term per index, with plus sign for upper indices (treated like vectors) and minus sign for lower indices (treated like one-forms), the derivative index always last on Gamma.

**How introduced:** Uses the scalar p_alpha V^alpha: expand its gradient with the ordinary product rule, replace V^alpha_,beta by V^alpha_;beta minus the Gamma term, and argue that the leftover bracket multiplying an arbitrary vector must be a tensor component. Sign explained: basis one-forms change oppositely to basis vectors because their contraction is a constant Kronecker delta. Then general formulas for (0 2), (2 0), (1 1) tensors are displayed as a systematic pattern.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative of a vector, contraction, quotient-style tensor argument

$$
p_{\alpha;\beta} = p_{\alpha,\beta} - p_\mu\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}
$$

$$
\nabla_\beta T_{\mu\nu} = T_{\mu\nu,\beta} - T_{\alpha\nu}\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\mu\beta} - T_{\mu\alpha}\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\nu\beta}
$$

**Where:** SCH §5.3 p.129 (pdf 147); SCH §5.3 p.130 (pdf 148)

### Leibniz rule for covariant derivative

*identity · developed* · also: product rule for nabla

Covariant differentiation of products and contractions obeys the ordinary product rule, e.g. the derivative of a one-form contracted with a vector equals the derivative of the one-form contracted with the vector plus the one-form contracted with the derivative of the vector.

**How introduced:** Falls out of the one-form derivation; justified because in Cartesian coordinates nabla reduces to partial differentiation of components, where the ordinary product rule holds.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative of one-forms and higher-rank tensors

$$
\nabla_\beta(p_\alpha V^\alpha) = p_{\alpha;\beta}V^\alpha + p_\alpha V^\alpha{}_{;\beta}
$$

**Where:** SCH §5.3 p.130 (pdf 148)

### Cartesian-then-tensor-equation argument

*technique · core* · also: prove it in a convenient frame, then generalize, tensor equation valid in one basis is valid in all

If an equation relating components of tensors holds in one coordinate system (typically Cartesian, where Gamma = 0 and g = delta), then because both sides transform identically it holds in every coordinate system.

**How introduced:** Used for the equality of divergences, symmetry of the second derivative of a scalar, and especially metric compatibility, where the verbal argument is repeated explicitly in primed and unprimed index equations for readers who need it.

**Prerequisites:** tensor transformation law

**Notes:** In curved spaces the role of Cartesian coordinates is taken by local inertial (locally flat) coordinates.

**Where:** SCH §5.3 p.128 (pdf 146); SCH §5.4 p.131 (pdf 149); SCH §5.4 p.133 (pdf 151)

### metric compatibility

*theorem · core* · also: covariant constancy of the metric, nabla g = 0, g_alpha beta;mu = 0

The covariant derivative of the metric tensor vanishes in every coordinate system; equivalently, lowering an index commutes with covariant differentiation.

**How introduced:** Argued from Cartesian coordinates, where a vector and its one-form have equal components and nabla is partial differentiation, so nabla of the one-form equals g applied to nabla V; this tensor equation holds everywhere; comparing with the product rule on V_alpha = g_alpha mu V^mu for arbitrary V gives nabla g = 0. Then checked in polar coordinates on g_theta theta;r, which vanishes 'almost magically' but, the book insists, necessarily. A perspective paragraph promises the result persists in curved spaces.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative of one-forms and higher-rank tensors, Cartesian-then-tensor-equation argument, metric tensor in polar coordinates

$$
V_{\alpha;\beta} = g_{\alpha\mu}V^\mu{}_{;\beta}
$$

$$
g_{\alpha\beta;\mu} = g_{\alpha\beta,\mu} - \Gamma^\nu{}_{\alpha\mu}g_{\nu\beta} - \Gamma^\nu{}_{\beta\mu}g_{\alpha\nu} = 0
$$

**Where:** SCH §5.4 p.130 (pdf 148); SCH §5.4 p.131 (pdf 149); SCH §5.4 p.132 (pdf 150)

### symmetry of Christoffel symbols

*theorem · core* · also: torsion-free connection in a coordinate basis, Gamma^mu_alpha beta = Gamma^mu_beta alpha

In any coordinate basis the Christoffel symbols are symmetric in their two lower indices.

**How introduced:** Proof via an arbitrary scalar: nabla nabla phi has components phi_,beta;alpha that are symmetric in Cartesian coordinates, hence in all; expanding with the one-form rule and using commuting partial derivatives leaves (Gamma^mu_alpha beta - Gamma^mu_beta alpha) phi_,mu = 0 for all phi.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative of one-forms and higher-rank tensors, Cartesian-then-tensor-equation argument, equality of mixed partial derivatives

$$
\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta} = \Gamma^\mu{}_{\beta\alpha}
$$

**Notes:** Fails in noncoordinate bases (§5.5); see commutation coefficients in Exercise 5.20.

**Where:** SCH §5.4 p.132 (pdf 150); SCH §5.4 p.133 (pdf 151); SCH §5.5 p.137 (pdf 155)

### Christoffel symbols from the metric

*identity · core* · also: Levi-Civita connection formula, Eq. 5.75

In a coordinate basis, each Christoffel symbol is one half of the inverse metric contracted with a combination of first partial derivatives of the metric: two index-permuted derivatives added and a third subtracted (Eq. 5.75).

**How introduced:** Described as some advanced index gymnastics: write metric compatibility with three cyclic index permutations, add two and subtract the third, cancel antisymmetric combinations using symmetry of Gamma, divide by 2 and contract with the inverse metric. Checked on Gamma^theta_r theta = 1/r in polars; reader urged to memorize it because it carries over unchanged to curved spaces.

**Prerequisites:** metric compatibility, symmetry of Christoffel symbols, inverse metric and the vector gradient

$$
\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\beta\mu} = \tfrac{1}{2}g^{\alpha\gamma}(g_{\alpha\beta,\mu} + g_{\alpha\mu,\beta} - g_{\beta\mu,\alpha})
$$

**Notes:** Valid only in coordinate bases.

**Where:** SCH §5.4 p.132 (pdf 150); SCH §5.4 p.133 (pdf 151); SCH §5.4 p.134 (pdf 152)

### non-tensorial nature of Christoffel symbols

*principle · developed* · also: Gamma is not a tensor, transformation of Gamma

For fixed alpha, Gamma^mu_alpha beta are the components of the (1 1) tensor nabla e_alpha, but there is no single (1 2) tensor with components Gamma^mu_alpha beta, so they do not transform by the tensor law (they vanish in Cartesian coordinates yet not in polar); only the full combination V^beta_,alpha + V^mu Gamma^beta_mu alpha is tensorial.

**How introduced:** Refines the common textbook slogan: a coordinate change replaces the basis vectors, so the relevant tensors nabla e_beta' are different tensors, not new components of the old ones.

**Prerequisites:** Christoffel symbols, covariant derivative tensor nabla V

**Notes:** Exercise 5.17 works out the inhomogeneous transformation explicitly.

**Where:** SCH §5.4 p.134 (pdf 152)

### noncoordinate basis

*definition · developed* · also: anholonomic basis, non-holonomic frame

A field of basis vectors that is not the coordinate basis of any coordinate system; in such a basis phi_,mu = d-tilde-phi(e_mu) is not a partial derivative with respect to a coordinate, Christoffel symbols need not be symmetric, and the metric formula for Gamma fails.

**How introduced:** Existence question posed for the orthonormal polar basis and answered through the one-forms: if they were gradients of some eta, mixed partials of eta would have to agree, and they do not. Then general remarks on which results survive, with the promise that the book rarely uses such bases.

**Prerequisites:** orthonormal basis, gradient one-form, equality of mixed partial derivatives

$$
\phi_{,\hat\theta} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta}
$$

$$
\nabla_{\hat\beta} = \Lambda^{\alpha}{}_{\hat\beta}\,\partial/\partial x^\alpha
$$

**Notes:** Orthonormal frames (tetrads) become important in later chapters for observers, but the book mostly avoids them.

**Where:** SCH §5.5 p.134 (pdf 152); SCH §5.5 p.136 (pdf 154); SCH §5.5 p.137 (pdf 155)

### integrability conditions

*theorem · introduced* · also: condition for a coordinate basis, curl-free condition on Lambda

A set of one-forms omega^alpha-bar = Lambda^alpha-bar_beta d-tilde-x^beta is a coordinate basis only if each Lambda^alpha-bar_beta is a partial derivative of some function, which requires d Lambda^alpha-bar_beta / dx^gamma = d Lambda^alpha-bar_gamma / dx^beta.

**How introduced:** Generalizes the mixed-partials failure found for the orthonormal polar basis; notes that an arbitrary position-dependent transformation matrix typically violates it.

**Prerequisites:** noncoordinate basis, transformation matrices for vectors and one-forms

$$
\partial_\gamma\Lambda^{\bar\alpha}{}_{\beta} = \partial_\beta\Lambda^{\bar\alpha}{}_{\gamma}
$$

**Where:** SCH §5.5 p.137 (pdf 155)

### physical versus coordinate components

*convention · introduced* · also: coordinate-basis components versus orthonormal components

A vector with polar coordinate-basis components (a, b) has orthonormal-basis components (a, r b); formulas like the divergence look different in the two bases while describing the same scalar.

**How introduced:** Used to reconcile Eq. 5.56 with the divergence formula in vector-calculus books (Eq. 5.87), removing an apparent contradiction.

**Prerequisites:** orthonormal basis, divergence in curvilinear coordinates

$$
V^{\hat\alpha} = (V^r,\ rV^\theta)
$$

$$
\nabla\cdot V = \frac{1}{r}\partial_r(rV^{\hat r}) + \frac{1}{r}\partial_\theta V^{\hat\theta}
$$

**Notes:** The term 'physical components' is not used by Schutz; it is the common engineering name.

**Where:** SCH §5.5 p.136 (pdf 154)

### Riemann curvature tensor

*mathematical-object · mention* · also: Riemann tensor

The tensor that quantifies how much parallelism fails in a curved space, i.e. the departure from Euclid's parallel axiom.

**How introduced:** Only named in the look-ahead as the missing measure to be developed next chapter.

**Prerequisites:** flat versus curved geometry via parallelism, covariant derivative of a vector

**Where:** SCH §5.6 p.138 (pdf 156)

### Rindler coordinates

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: Rindler space, coordinates of a uniformly accelerated observer

Coordinates (lambda, a) on one quadrant of the Minkowski t-x plane defined by t = a sinh lambda, x = a cosh lambda, adapted to uniformly accelerated observers; orthogonal, singular on the light lines |x| = |t|.

**How introduced:** Only in Exercise 5.21, as an application of curvilinear-coordinate machinery to SR, with a remark that the boundary lines resemble black-hole horizons.

**Prerequisites:** curvilinear coordinate transformation, Christoffel symbols from the metric, Jacobian of a coordinate transformation

$$
t = a\sinh\lambda,\ x = a\cosh\lambda
$$

**Where:** SCH p.140 (pdf 158)

## Key equations

### (5.1) Gravitational redshift from energy conservation · central · SCH §5.1 p.112 (pdf 130)

$$
\frac{E'}{E} = \frac{h\nu'}{h\nu} = \frac{m}{m + mgh + O(v^4)} = 1 - gh + O(v^4)
$$

A photon rising a height h loses the fraction gh of its energy and frequency, as required to prevent a perpetual-motion cycle.

**Symbols:** E, E': photon energy at bottom and top; nu, nu': frequencies; m: rest mass dropped; g: gravitational acceleration; h: tower height (units c = 1).

### (5.2) No redshift in a freely falling frame · supporting · SCH §5.1 p.115 (pdf 133)

$$
\frac{\nu(\text{freely falling})}{\nu'(\text{apparatus at top})} = \frac{1+gh}{\sqrt{1-g^2h^2}} = 1 + gh + O(v^4)
$$

The Doppler blueshift seen by a frame that has fallen for the light-travel time h exactly compensates the gravitational redshift to first order, so the falling observer measures the emitted frequency.

**Symbols:** gh: speed acquired by the falling frame during travel time h; nu: frequency in the freely falling frame; nu': frequency at top of apparatus.

### (5.3)-(5.4) Polar coordinates and their increments · supporting · SCH §5.2 p.118 (pdf 136)

$$
x = r\cos\theta,\ y = r\sin\theta;\qquad \Delta r = \cos\theta\,\Delta x + \sin\theta\,\Delta y,\quad \Delta\theta = -\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\Delta x + \frac{\cos\theta}{r}\Delta y
$$

Definition of polar coordinates and the first-order linear relation between polar and Cartesian displacements.

**Symbols:** r, theta: polar coordinates; x, y: Cartesian coordinates.

### (5.6) Nonvanishing Jacobian · supporting · SCH §5.2 p.118 (pdf 136)

$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}\partial\xi/\partial x & \partial\xi/\partial y\\ \partial\eta/\partial x & \partial\eta/\partial y\end{pmatrix} \neq 0
$$

Condition for (xi, eta) to label points uniquely near a point; where it fails the transformation is singular.

**Symbols:** xi, eta: new coordinates as functions of x, y.

### (5.8)-(5.9) Vector transformation law in general coordinates · central · SCH §5.2 p.119 (pdf 137)

$$
\Lambda^{\alpha'}{}_{\beta} = \frac{\partial x^{\alpha'}}{\partial x^{\beta}},\qquad V^{\alpha'} = \Lambda^{\alpha'}{}_{\beta}V^{\beta}
$$

Vector components transform like coordinate displacements, with a position-dependent matrix of partial derivatives.

**Symbols:** Primed indices: new coordinates (xi, eta); unprimed: Cartesian; indices take values 1, 2.

### (5.10) Gradient one-form · central · SCH §5.2 p.119 (pdf 137)

$$
\tilde{d}\phi \to \left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\xi},\ \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\eta}\right)
$$

Defines a one-form from any scalar field by its partial derivatives in whatever coordinates are used.

**Symbols:** phi: scalar field.

### (5.13)-(5.14) One-form transformation law · central · SCH §5.2 p.119 (pdf 137)

$$
\Lambda^{\alpha}{}_{\beta'} = \frac{\partial x^{\alpha}}{\partial x^{\beta'}},\qquad (\tilde{d}\phi)_{\beta'} = \Lambda^{\alpha}{}_{\beta'}(\tilde{d}\phi)_{\alpha}
$$

One-form components transform with the inverse matrix (derivatives of old coordinates by new), summed on the first index as for a row vector; follows from the chain rule.

**Symbols:** Lambda^alpha_beta': one-form transformation matrix.

### (5.15)-(5.16) Transformation matrices are inverses · derivation-step · SCH §5.2 p.120 (pdf 138)

$$
\Lambda^{\alpha'}{}_{\beta}\,\Lambda^{\beta}{}_{\gamma'} = \frac{\partial x^{\alpha'}}{\partial x^{\beta}}\frac{\partial x^{\beta}}{\partial x^{\gamma'}} = \delta^{\alpha'}{}_{\gamma'}
$$

By the chain rule the vector and one-form transformation matrices multiply to the identity.

### (5.19) Tangent vector as derivative operator · central · SCH §5.2 p.120 (pdf 138)

$$
\frac{d\phi}{ds} = \langle\tilde{d}\phi, \vec{V}\rangle,\qquad \vec{V} \to \left(\frac{d\xi}{ds},\ \frac{d\eta}{ds}\right)
$$

The rate of change of a scalar along a parametrized curve splits into a one-form depending only on the field and a vector depending only on the curve.

**Symbols:** s: curve parameter; V: tangent vector.

### (5.22)-(5.23) Polar basis vectors · central · SCH §5.2 p.121 (pdf 139)

$$
\vec{e}_r = \cos\theta\,\vec{e}_x + \sin\theta\,\vec{e}_y,\qquad \vec{e}_\theta = -r\sin\theta\,\vec{e}_x + r\cos\theta\,\vec{e}_y
$$

Coordinate basis vectors of polar coordinates expressed in the constant Cartesian basis; e_theta scales with r.

**Symbols:** e_x, e_y: Cartesian basis vectors.

### (5.26)-(5.27) Polar basis one-forms · supporting · SCH §5.2 p.122 (pdf 140)

$$
\tilde{d}r = \cos\theta\,\tilde{d}x + \sin\theta\,\tilde{d}y,\qquad \tilde{d}\theta = -\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\tilde{d}x + \frac{\cos\theta}{r}\tilde{d}y
$$

Basis one-forms of polar coordinates in the Cartesian one-form basis; they mirror ordinary calculus increments.

### (5.28a)-(5.28b) Magnitudes of polar bases · supporting · SCH §5.2 p.122 (pdf 140)

$$
|\vec{e}_\theta|^2 = r^2,\qquad |\vec{e}_r| = 1,\quad |\tilde{d}r| = 1,\quad |\tilde{d}\theta| = r^{-1}
$$

The polar coordinate basis is not a unit basis; the theta basis vector grows with radius and the theta one-form shrinks.

### (5.30)-(5.32) Polar metric components · central · SCH §5.2 p.123 (pdf 141)

$$
g_{\alpha'\beta'} = \mathbf{g}(\vec{e}_{\alpha'},\vec{e}_{\beta'}) = \vec{e}_{\alpha'}\cdot\vec{e}_{\beta'},\qquad (g_{\alpha\beta})_{\rm polar} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & r^2\end{pmatrix}
$$

Metric components in any basis are dot products of basis vectors; for polar coordinates they are diag(1, r^2).

**Symbols:** g: Euclidean metric tensor, delta_alpha beta in Cartesian coordinates (Eq. 5.29).

### (5.33) Polar line element · central · SCH §5.2 p.123 (pdf 141)

$$
d\vec{l}\cdot d\vec{l} = dl^2 = dr^2 + r^2\,d\theta^2
$$

Squared length of an infinitesimal displacement in polar coordinates, a compact display of metric components.

**Symbols:** dr, d theta: infinitesimal coordinate increments (not basis one-forms).

### Metric as a sum of basis tensor products · supporting · SCH §5.2 p.123 (pdf 141)

$$
\mathbf{g} = g_{\alpha\beta}\,\tilde{d}x^\alpha\otimes\tilde{d}x^\beta = \tilde{d}r\otimes\tilde{d}r + r^2\,\tilde{d}\theta\otimes\tilde{d}\theta
$$

The metric as a (0 2) tensor operator; fed the displacement vector in both slots it returns the line element.

### (5.34)-(5.36) Inverse metric and vector gradient in polars · supporting · SCH §5.2 p.124 (pdf 142)

$$
(g^{\alpha\beta}) = {\rm diag}(1, r^{-2}),\quad (\vec{d}\phi)^\alpha = g^{\alpha\beta}\phi_{,\beta},\quad (\vec{d}\phi)^r = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r},\quad (\vec{d}\phi)^\theta = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta}
$$

Raising the index of the gradient changes its theta component by 1/r^2: vector and one-form components differ outside Cartesian coordinates.

**Symbols:** vec d phi: vector gradient; phi_,beta: one-form gradient components.

### (5.37a)-(5.37b) Derivatives of e_r · central · SCH §5.3 p.124 (pdf 142)

$$
\frac{\partial\vec{e}_r}{\partial r} = 0,\qquad \frac{\partial\vec{e}_r}{\partial\theta} = -\sin\theta\,\vec{e}_x + \cos\theta\,\vec{e}_y = \frac{1}{r}\vec{e}_\theta
$$

The radial basis vector does not change along r but rotates with theta, its change pointing along e_theta.

### (5.38a)-(5.38b) Derivatives of e_theta · central · SCH §5.3 p.125 (pdf 143)

$$
\frac{\partial\vec{e}_\theta}{\partial r} = \frac{1}{r}\vec{e}_\theta,\qquad \frac{\partial\vec{e}_\theta}{\partial\theta} = -r\,\vec{e}_r
$$

e_theta grows linearly with r and turns inward as theta increases.

### (5.39)-(5.42) Consistency check on constant e_x · derivation-step · SCH §5.3 p.125 (pdf 143)

$$
\vec{e}_x = \cos\theta\,\vec{e}_r - \frac{\sin\theta}{r}\vec{e}_\theta \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{\partial\vec{e}_x}{\partial\theta} = -\sin\theta\,\vec{e}_r + \frac{\cos\theta}{r}\vec{e}_\theta - \frac{\cos\theta}{r}\vec{e}_\theta + \sin\theta\,\vec{e}_r = 0
$$

Derivatives of polar components are cancelled by derivatives of the polar basis, reproducing the constancy of e_x.

### (5.43) Derivative of a vector field in a varying basis · derivation-step · SCH §5.3 p.126 (pdf 144)

$$
\frac{\partial\vec{V}}{\partial x^\beta} = \frac{\partial V^\alpha}{\partial x^\beta}\vec{e}_\alpha + V^\alpha\frac{\partial\vec{e}_\alpha}{\partial x^\beta}
$$

Product rule: the derivative of a vector includes both component changes and basis changes.

### (5.44) Definition of Christoffel symbols · central · SCH §5.3 p.126 (pdf 144)

$$
\frac{\partial\vec{e}_\alpha}{\partial x^\beta} = \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\,\vec{e}_\mu
$$

Christoffel symbols are the basis components of the derivative of each basis vector along each coordinate.

**Symbols:** alpha: basis vector differentiated; beta: coordinate of differentiation; mu: component.

### (5.45) Christoffel symbols of plane polar coordinates · central · SCH §5.3 p.127 (pdf 145)

$$
\Gamma^\mu{}_{rr} = 0,\quad \Gamma^r{}_{r\theta} = \Gamma^r{}_{\theta r} = 0,\quad \Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta} = \Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta r} = \frac{1}{r},\quad \Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta} = -r,\quad \Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta\theta} = 0
$$

Complete table of connection coefficients for polar coordinates, allowing all derivative computations without Cartesian vectors.

### (5.46)-(5.48) Factoring out the basis · derivation-step · SCH §5.3 p.127 (pdf 145)

$$
\frac{\partial\vec{V}}{\partial x^\beta} = \frac{\partial V^\alpha}{\partial x^\beta}\vec{e}_\alpha + V^\alpha\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\vec{e}_\mu = \left(\frac{\partial V^\alpha}{\partial x^\beta} + V^\mu\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\mu\beta}\right)\vec{e}_\alpha
$$

Substituting the Christoffel definition and relabelling dummy indices gives the components of the vector derivative.

### (5.50)-(5.51) Covariant derivative of a vector · central · SCH §5.3 p.127 (pdf 145)

$$
V^\alpha{}_{;\beta} := V^\alpha{}_{,\beta} + V^\mu\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\mu\beta},\qquad \frac{\partial\vec{V}}{\partial x^\beta} = V^\alpha{}_{;\beta}\,\vec{e}_\alpha
$$

Semicolon notation for the full components of the derivative of a vector field.

**Symbols:** comma: partial derivative; semicolon: covariant derivative.

### (5.52) nabla V as a (1 1) tensor · central · SCH §5.3 p.128 (pdf 146)

$$
(\nabla\vec{V})^\alpha{}_\beta = (\nabla_\beta\vec{V})^\alpha = V^\alpha{}_{;\beta}
$$

The covariant derivative components are the components of one tensor field, so they transform tensorially.

### (5.53) Covariant derivative of a scalar · supporting · SCH §5.3 p.128 (pdf 146)

$$
\nabla_\alpha f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x^\alpha},\qquad \nabla f = \tilde{d}f
$$

For scalars the covariant derivative is the ordinary gradient.

### (5.54) Invariance of the divergence · supporting · SCH §5.3 p.128 (pdf 146)

$$
V^\alpha{}_{;\alpha} \equiv V^{\beta'}{}_{;\beta'}
$$

Contraction of nabla V gives the same scalar in Cartesian and in arbitrary coordinates.

**Symbols:** Unprimed: Cartesian; primed: arbitrary coordinates.

### (5.55) Contracted Christoffel symbols in polars · derivation-step · SCH §5.3 p.128 (pdf 146)

$$
\Gamma^\alpha{}_{r\alpha} = \Gamma^r{}_{rr} + \Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta} = \frac{1}{r},\qquad \Gamma^\alpha{}_{\theta\alpha} = 0
$$

Only the r contraction survives, producing the 1/r term in the divergence.

### (5.56) Divergence in polar coordinates (coordinate basis) · central · SCH §5.3 p.129 (pdf 147)

$$
V^\alpha{}_{;\alpha} = \frac{\partial V^r}{\partial r} + \frac{\partial V^\theta}{\partial\theta} + \frac{1}{r}V^r = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(rV^r) + \frac{\partial V^\theta}{\partial\theta}
$$

Divergence expressed with coordinate-basis polar components.

### (5.57)-(5.58) Laplacian in polar coordinates · central · SCH §5.3 p.129 (pdf 147)

$$
\nabla^2\phi = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial\theta^2} = \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2}
$$

Divergence of the vector gradient; derived rather than memorized, and identical to the Cartesian Laplacian.

### (5.59)-(5.62) Product-rule route to the one-form derivative · derivation-step · SCH §5.3 p.129 (pdf 147)

$$
\phi = p_\alpha V^\alpha,\quad \phi_{,\beta} = p_{\alpha,\beta}V^\alpha + p_\alpha V^\alpha{}_{,\beta} = \left(p_{\alpha,\beta} - p_\mu\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\right)V^\alpha + p_\alpha V^\alpha{}_{;\beta}
$$

Since every other term is tensorial for arbitrary V, the bracket must be a tensor component.

**Symbols:** p: one-form; V: arbitrary vector.

### (5.63) Covariant derivative of a one-form · central · SCH §5.3 p.130 (pdf 148)

$$
(\nabla_\beta\tilde{p})_\alpha = (\nabla\tilde{p})_{\alpha\beta} = p_{\alpha;\beta} = p_{\alpha,\beta} - p_\mu\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}
$$

One-form components acquire a minus-sign Christoffel correction, reflecting that basis one-forms change oppositely to basis vectors.

### (5.64)-(5.66) Covariant derivatives of rank-2 tensors · central · SCH §5.3 p.130 (pdf 148)

$$
\nabla_\beta T_{\mu\nu} = T_{\mu\nu,\beta} - T_{\alpha\nu}\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\mu\beta} - T_{\mu\alpha}\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\nu\beta};\quad \nabla_\beta A^{\mu\nu} = A^{\mu\nu}{}_{,\beta} + A^{\alpha\nu}\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta} + A^{\mu\alpha}\Gamma^\nu{}_{\alpha\beta};\quad \nabla_\beta B^\mu{}_\nu = B^\mu{}_{\nu,\beta} + B^\alpha{}_\nu\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta} - B^\mu{}_\alpha\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\nu\beta}
$$

Systematic rule: one Gamma term per index, plus for upper and minus for lower.

**Symbols:** T: (0 2) tensor; A: (2 0) tensor; B: (1 1) tensor.

### (5.67)-(5.68) Lowering an index commutes with nabla · supporting · SCH §5.4 p.131 (pdf 149)

$$
\nabla_\beta\tilde{V} = \mathbf{g}(\nabla_\beta\vec{V},\ \cdot\ ),\qquad V_{\alpha;\beta} = g_{\alpha\mu}V^\mu{}_{;\beta}
$$

True trivially in Cartesian coordinates and, being tensorial, true in all coordinates.

**Symbols:** tilde V = g(V, .): one-form associated with V.

### (5.71) Metric compatibility · central · SCH §5.4 p.132 (pdf 150)

$$
g_{\alpha\beta;\mu} \equiv 0
$$

The metric is covariantly constant in every coordinate system, a result that will remain true in curved spaces.

### (5.72) Covariant derivative of the metric · derivation-step · SCH §5.4 p.132 (pdf 150)

$$
g_{\alpha\beta;\mu} = g_{\alpha\beta,\mu} - \Gamma^\nu{}_{\alpha\mu}g_{\nu\beta} - \Gamma^\nu{}_{\beta\mu}g_{\alpha\nu}
$$

Setting it to zero links metric derivatives to Christoffel symbols; checked in polars: (r^2)_{,r} - r - r = 0.

### (5.73)-(5.74) Symmetry of Christoffel symbols · central · SCH §5.4 p.133 (pdf 151)

$$
\phi_{,\beta;\alpha} = \phi_{,\alpha;\beta}\;\Rightarrow\;\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta} = \Gamma^\mu{}_{\beta\alpha}
$$

Symmetric second covariant derivative of a scalar plus commuting partials forces symmetric Gamma in any coordinate basis.

### Permutation sum step · derivation-step · SCH §5.4 p.133 (pdf 151)

$$
g_{\alpha\beta,\mu} + g_{\alpha\mu,\beta} - g_{\beta\mu,\alpha} = 2\,g_{\alpha\nu}\Gamma^\nu{}_{\beta\mu}
$$

Adding two permutations of the compatibility condition and subtracting a third isolates Gamma.

### (5.75) Christoffel symbols from the metric · central · SCH §5.4 p.133 (pdf 151)

$$
\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\beta\mu} = \frac{1}{2}g^{\alpha\gamma}\left(g_{\alpha\beta,\mu} + g_{\alpha\mu,\beta} - g_{\beta\mu,\alpha}\right)
$$

Christoffel symbols of a coordinate basis follow from first derivatives of the metric; the workhorse formula for all of GR.

**Symbols:** g^alpha gamma: inverse metric.

### Check: Gamma^theta_r theta from the metric · derivation-step · SCH §5.4 p.134 (pdf 152)

$$
\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta} = \frac{1}{2r^2}\,g_{\theta\theta,r} = \frac{1}{2r^2}(r^2)_{,r} = \frac{1}{r}
$$

Formula 5.75 reproduces the value found from basis-vector derivatives.

### (5.76)-(5.78) Orthonormal polar basis · supporting · SCH §5.5 p.135 (pdf 153)

$$
\vec{e}_{\hat r} = \vec{e}_r,\ \vec{e}_{\hat\theta} = \frac{1}{r}\vec{e}_\theta;\quad \tilde\omega^{\hat r} = \tilde{d}r,\ \tilde\omega^{\hat\theta} = r\,\tilde{d}\theta;\quad g_{\hat\alpha\hat\beta} = \delta_{\hat\alpha\hat\beta},\ g^{\hat\alpha\hat\beta} = \delta^{\hat\alpha\hat\beta}
$$

Normalized polar basis vectors and one-forms are orthonormal.

**Symbols:** Hats on indices denote orthonormal-basis components.

### (5.80)-(5.84) Orthonormal polar basis fails integrability · supporting · SCH §5.5 p.136 (pdf 154)

$$
\tilde\omega^{\hat\theta} = -\sin\theta\,\tilde{d}x + \cos\theta\,\tilde{d}y \stackrel{?}{=} \tilde{d}\eta \Rightarrow \frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x} = -\sin\theta,\ \frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y} = \cos\theta \Rightarrow \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(-\sin\theta) = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\cos\theta)\ \text{(false)}
$$

No function eta has these partial derivatives, since the mixed partials disagree, so the orthonormal polar basis is noncoordinate.

**Symbols:** eta: hypothetical coordinate.

### (5.85)-(5.87) Divergence with orthonormal components · supporting · SCH §5.5 p.136 (pdf 154)

$$
V^{\alpha'} = (a, b) \Rightarrow V^{\hat\alpha} = (a, rb);\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{V} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(rV^{\hat r}) + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial V^{\hat\theta}}{\partial\theta}
$$

Vector-calculus textbook divergence uses orthonormal components; it agrees with Eq. 5.56 after the component conversion.

### (5.88)-(5.91) Comma derivative in a general basis · supporting · SCH §5.5 p.136 (pdf 154)

$$
\tilde{d}\phi(\vec{e}_\mu) = \phi_{,\mu};\qquad \phi_{,\mu} = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x^\mu}\ \text{(coordinate basis only)};\qquad \phi_{,\hat\theta} = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta};\qquad \nabla_{\hat\alpha}\phi \equiv \phi_{,\hat\alpha} = \Lambda^{\beta}{}_{\hat\alpha}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x^{\beta}}
$$

The comma derivative is defined by feeding a basis vector to the gradient one-form. It is a partial derivative only in a coordinate basis; in a general basis it is a matrix combination of coordinate partials.

### (5.92)-(5.93) Christoffel symbols in a general basis · supporting · SCH §5.5 p.137 (pdf 155)

$$
\nabla_{\hat\beta}\vec{e}_{\hat\alpha} = \Gamma^{\hat\mu}{}_{\hat\alpha\hat\beta}\,\vec{e}_{\hat\mu},\qquad \nabla_{\hat\beta} = \Lambda^{\alpha}{}_{\hat\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\alpha}}
$$

Gamma can be defined in any basis, but in a noncoordinate basis it need not be symmetric and Eq. 5.75 fails.

**Symbols:** x^alpha: any coordinate system; e_beta-hat: any basis.

### (5.94)-(5.95) Integrability conditions for a coordinate basis · supporting · SCH §5.5 p.137 (pdf 155)

$$
\tilde\omega^{\bar\alpha} = \Lambda^{\bar\alpha}{}_{\beta}\,\tilde{d}x^{\beta} = \frac{\partial x^{\bar\alpha}}{\partial x^{\beta}}\tilde{d}x^{\beta},\qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\gamma}\Lambda^{\bar\alpha}{}_{\beta} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\beta}\Lambda^{\bar\alpha}{}_{\gamma}
$$

A basis comes from coordinates only if its transformation matrix consists of partial derivatives, requiring these mixed-derivative equalities.

### (5.96) Uniformly accelerated observer (Rindler) coordinates · supporting · SCH p.140 (pdf 158)

$$
t(\lambda) = a\sinh\lambda,\qquad x(\lambda) = a\cosh\lambda
$$

World line of a uniformly accelerated observer. Letting a vary as well defines orthogonal coordinates (lambda, a), which fail on |x| = |t| and cover two opposite quadrants; the right-hand one is Rindler space (Exercise 5.21).

**Symbols:** a: constant (inverse acceleration); a lambda: proper time.

## Worked examples

### Tower redshift derivation (Eq. 5.1) · intro · SCH §5.1 p.112 (pdf 130)

**Problem:** Predict the frequency change of a photon climbing height h in a uniform field using only energy conservation.

**Method:** Drop rest mass m; to lowest order its energy on arrival is m + mgh. Convert it entirely into one upward photon (Earth takes up momentum but not energy). At the top convert back to mass m'; require m' = m to forbid a perpetual-motion gain, so the photon energy ratio is m/(m + mgh).

**Key insight:** The redshift is forced by conservation of energy plus mass-energy equivalence, independent of any detailed gravity theory.

**Result:** E'/E = nu'/nu = 1 - gh + O(v^4)

**Concepts:** gravitational redshift

### Schild's argument (Figure 5.2) · standard · SCH §5.1 p.113 (pdf 131)

**Problem:** Show that the redshift is incompatible with the Earth laboratory being a Lorentz frame.

**Method:** Draw tower ends as vertical world lines and two crests on arbitrary paths. Static field implies the second crest path is a time translate of the first, so in Minkowski geometry the crest separations at bottom and top are equal. But period is the reciprocal of frequency and frequency is lower at the top, so the period there is longer.

**Key insight:** Contradiction arises without knowing how gravity deflects light; only time independence and the measured redshift are used.

**Result:** Delta t_top > Delta t_bottom contradicts congruence; lab frame is not inertial.

**Concepts:** nonexistence of a Lorentz frame at rest on Earth

### Redshift viewed from a freely falling frame (Eq. 5.2) · standard · SCH §5.1 p.115 (pdf 133)

**Problem:** Determine the frequency of the photon measured in a frame released from rest at emission, when the photon reaches the top.

**Method:** Light travel time is h (c = 1); the frame gains downward speed gh relative to the apparatus. Apply the SR Doppler formula between the falling frame and the top apparatus, expand to first order, and compare with Eq. 5.1.

**Key insight:** The Doppler blueshift of the falling observer exactly cancels the gravitational redshift, supporting freely falling frames as inertial.

**Result:** nu(freely falling) = nu'(1 + gh) = nu(emitted) to first order

**Concepts:** weak equivalence principle, local inertial frame

### Vector and one-form transformation matrices are inverse (Eqs. 5.15-5.16) · intro · SCH §5.2 p.120 (pdf 138)

**Problem:** Verify directly that the vector matrix (derivatives of new coordinates by old) and the one-form matrix (derivatives of old coordinates by new) multiply to the identity.

**Method:** Multiply the two 2x2 matrices entry by entry. Each entry becomes a sum of two products of partials, which the chain rule collapses to a derivative of xi or eta with respect to xi or eta.

**Key insight:** Being inverse matrices is just the chain rule, so vector and one-form transformation laws automatically fit together.

**Result:** Lambda^alpha'_beta Lambda^beta_gamma' = delta^alpha'_gamma' (identity matrix)

**Concepts:** transformation matrices for vectors and one-forms

### Polar basis vectors, one-forms and their lengths (Eqs. 5.21-5.28) · intro · SCH §5.2 p.121 (pdf 139)

**Problem:** Construct the polar coordinate basis vectors and basis one-forms in terms of Cartesian ones and find their magnitudes.

**Method:** Use e_alpha' = Lambda^beta_alpha' e_beta with Lambda^x_r = dx/dr etc.; for one-forms use d-tilde-theta = (d theta/dx) d-tilde-x + (d theta/dy) d-tilde-y. Compute dot products with the Cartesian metric.

**Key insight:** Index positions dictate which partial derivative appears; e_theta has length r because it represents a one-radian step.

**Result:** |e_r| = 1, |e_theta| = r, |d r| = 1, |d theta| = 1/r

**Concepts:** coordinate basis vectors and basis one-forms in polar coordinates, position-dependent basis

### Polar metric, line element, inverse metric and vector gradient (Eqs. 5.29-5.36) · intro · SCH §5.2 p.123 (pdf 141)

**Problem:** Find the metric components, line element and inverse metric in polar coordinates and use them to raise the gradient of a scalar.

**Method:** g_alpha'beta' = e_alpha' . e_beta'; line element from |dr e_r + d theta e_theta|^2; invert the diagonal matrix; apply g^alpha beta to phi_,beta.

**Key insight:** Vector and one-form versions of the gradient differ in components outside Cartesian coordinates.

**Result:** g = diag(1, r^2); dl^2 = dr^2 + r^2 d theta^2; vector gradient (phi_,r , phi_,theta / r^2)

**Concepts:** metric tensor in polar coordinates, line element, inverse metric and the vector gradient

### Derivatives of polar basis vectors and the e_x check (Eqs. 5.37-5.42) · intro · SCH §5.3 p.124 (pdf 142)

**Problem:** Differentiate e_r and e_theta with respect to r and theta, then verify the constant vector e_x has zero theta derivative when expanded in the polar basis.

**Method:** Differentiate the Cartesian expansions using constant e_x, e_y and re-express results in the polar basis. Expand e_x = cos theta e_r - (sin theta / r) e_theta, apply the product rule, and insert the basis derivatives.

**Key insight:** Component-derivative terms and basis-derivative terms cancel; ignoring basis derivatives would wrongly give a nonzero result.

**Result:** d e_r/dr = 0, d e_r/d theta = e_theta/r, d e_theta/dr = e_theta/r, d e_theta/d theta = -r e_r; d e_x/d theta = 0

**Concepts:** derivatives of basis vectors

### Christoffel symbols of polar coordinates (Eq. 5.45) · intro · SCH §5.3 p.127 (pdf 145)

**Problem:** Read off every Gamma^mu_alpha beta for polar coordinates.

**Method:** Match each basis derivative to Gamma^mu_alpha beta e_mu component by component.

**Key insight:** Once tabulated, Cartesian vectors disappear from all later calculations.

**Result:** Nonzero: Gamma^theta_r theta = Gamma^theta_theta r = 1/r, Gamma^r_theta theta = -r

**Concepts:** Christoffel symbols

### Divergence and Laplacian in polar coordinates (Eqs. 5.54-5.57) · standard · SCH §5.3 p.128 (pdf 146)

**Problem:** Derive the divergence of a vector field and the Laplacian of a scalar in polar coordinates from the covariant derivative.

**Method:** Contract V^alpha_;beta, compute contracted Gammas (1/r and 0), write the result as a total derivative; then raise the gradient with the inverse metric and substitute into the divergence.

**Key insight:** The 1/r factors of the familiar formulas come from Christoffel symbols and the inverse metric, not from memorized rules.

**Result:** div V = (1/r) d(rV^r)/dr + dV^theta/d theta; Laplacian = (1/r) d/dr(r d phi/dr) + (1/r^2) d^2 phi/d theta^2

**Concepts:** divergence in curvilinear coordinates, Laplacian in polar coordinates

### Covariant derivative of a one-form from the product rule (Eqs. 5.59-5.63) · standard · SCH §5.3 p.129 (pdf 147)

**Problem:** Find the formula for the covariant derivative of a one-form.

**Method:** Form the scalar p_alpha V^alpha, differentiate with the ordinary product rule, replace V^alpha_,beta by V^alpha_;beta minus the Gamma term, relabel dummies, and argue that the coefficient of arbitrary V^alpha must be a tensor.

**Key insight:** Requiring the product rule and tensoriality fixes the minus sign without new geometric input.

**Result:** p_alpha;beta = p_alpha,beta - p_mu Gamma^mu_alpha beta

**Concepts:** covariant derivative of one-forms and higher-rank tensors, Leibniz rule for covariant derivative

### Metric compatibility argument and polar check (Eqs. 5.67-5.72) · standard · SCH §5.4 p.131 (pdf 149)

**Problem:** Show nabla g = 0 in all coordinates and verify one nontrivial component in polars.

**Method:** In Cartesian coordinates V_alpha;beta = V^alpha_;beta and g = delta, giving the tensor equation V_alpha;beta = g_alpha mu V^mu_;beta; differentiate V_alpha = g_alpha mu V^mu covariantly and compare. For the check take alpha = beta = theta, mu = r in Eq. 5.72.

**Key insight:** The apparent cancellation is guaranteed because all components belong to one tensor that vanishes in Cartesian coordinates.

**Result:** g_theta theta;r = 2r - r - r = 0

**Concepts:** metric compatibility, Cartesian-then-tensor-equation argument

### Symmetry of Gamma and Gamma from the metric (Eqs. 5.73-5.75) · challenging · SCH §5.4 p.132 (pdf 150)

**Problem:** Prove Gamma is symmetric in a coordinate basis and invert the compatibility condition to express Gamma through metric derivatives; test on Gamma^theta_r theta.

**Method:** Symmetry of nabla nabla phi in Cartesian coordinates carries to all; expand and use commuting partials. Then permute indices of g_alpha beta,mu = Gamma g + Gamma g three ways, add two, subtract one, cancel antisymmetric pairs, divide by 2, contract with g^alpha gamma. Evaluate with g_theta theta = r^2, g^theta theta = 1/r^2.

**Key insight:** Metric compatibility plus symmetry determine the connection uniquely from the metric.

**Result:** Gamma^gamma_beta mu = (1/2) g^alpha gamma (g_alpha beta,mu + g_alpha mu,beta - g_beta mu,alpha); Gamma^theta_r theta = 1/r

**Concepts:** symmetry of Christoffel symbols, Christoffel symbols from the metric

### Orthonormal polar basis is noncoordinate (Eqs. 5.80-5.84) · standard · SCH §5.5 p.135 (pdf 153)

**Problem:** Decide whether coordinates exist whose coordinate basis is the orthonormal polar basis.

**Method:** Work with one-forms: require omega^theta-hat = r d theta = d eta, so d eta/dx = -sin theta and d eta/dy = cos theta; test equality of mixed second derivatives using sin theta = y/r and cos theta = x/r.

**Key insight:** Checking integrability of the dual one-forms is easier than searching for coordinates; the mixed-partials condition fails (the sum of the two derivatives is 1/r, not 0).

**Result:** No such eta exists; the orthonormal basis is noncoordinate.

**Concepts:** noncoordinate basis, integrability conditions, orthonormal basis

### Reconciling the textbook divergence formula (Eqs. 5.85-5.87) · intro · SCH §5.5 p.136 (pdf 154)

**Problem:** Explain why vector-calculus books write the polar divergence differently from Eq. 5.56.

**Method:** Convert coordinate-basis components (a, b) to orthonormal components (a, rb) and substitute into Eq. 5.56.

**Key insight:** Different-looking formulas describe the same scalar in different bases.

**Result:** div V = (1/r) d(r V^r-hat)/dr + (1/r) d V^theta-hat/d theta

**Concepts:** physical versus coordinate components, divergence in curvilinear coordinates


## Analogies and intuitions

### Flat town map versus flat world map → local flatness · strong · SCH §5.1 p.117 (pdf 135)

A town fits on flat paper with negligible distortion while the whole globe cannot, illustrating that curved spaces look flat in small regions.

**Where it breaks down:** Suggests curvature is about embedding and distortion of maps; does not convey that curvature is intrinsic or how it is measured (parallel transport), and uses a positive-definite surface rather than spacetime.

**App idea:** Zoomable globe with a flat tangent-plane map overlay whose distortion readout shrinks as the mapped region shrinks.

### Accelerating rocket in empty space → weak equivalence principle · strong · SCH §5.1 p.114 (pdf 132)

Reverses the free-fall argument: inside a uniformly accelerating ship, dropped objects fall together and supported ones have weight proportional to mass, exactly as in uniform gravity.

**Where it breaks down:** Only reproduces uniform fields; cannot mimic tidal effects, which the chapter immediately stresses.

**App idea:** Split-screen rocket versus Earth-surface lab with identical dropping objects; a tidal-probe button reveals differences only on the Earth side for large labs.

### Local inertial frames are like MCRFs of a fluid → local inertial frame · useful · SCH §5.1 p.116 (pdf 134)

Just as a comoving frame is only valid momentarily for one fluid element, a freely falling frame is inertial only in a small region for a short time.

**Where it breaks down:** MCRF limitation comes from fluid motion, not from spacetime geometry; the parallel is structural only.

### Earth absorbing the photon's momentum like a bouncing rubber ball → gravitational redshift · useful · SCH §5.1 p.112 (pdf 130)

Reassures that converting the falling mass into a single upward photon does not violate momentum conservation, since a very massive Earth takes momentum but negligible energy.

**Where it breaks down:** Only defends one idealized step; the conversion itself remains magical.

### Great circles on a sphere as locally straight lines that meet → flat versus curved geometry via parallelism · strong · SCH §5.1 p.117 (pdf 135)

Dropping Euclid's parallel axiom yields curved geometry; initially parallel great circles cross, as initially parallel free-fall world lines in a nonuniform field do not stay parallel.

**Where it breaks down:** Sphere has positive curvature everywhere; tidal geometry of spacetime can converge in some directions and diverge in others.

**App idea:** Two ants walking north from the equator on a 3D globe along meridians, with a distance readout that shrinks, next to two free-falling particles above Earth converging.

### e_theta must stretch to span one radian → position-dependent basis · strong · SCH §5.2 p.122 (pdf 140)

Explains |e_theta| = r: a unit change in theta is a longer arc at large radius, and correspondingly d-tilde-theta is large near the origin where a small vector sweeps a big angle.

**Where it breaks down:** Tied to polar geometry; the general statement is simply g_theta theta = r^2.

**App idea:** Radial slider showing a one-radian arc and e_theta arrow growing together.

### A vector is a machine that produces d phi/ds when handed d phi → tangent vector · useful · SCH §5.2 p.121 (pdf 139)

Separates the curve-dependent part (vector) from the field-dependent part (one-form) of a directional derivative, justifying the modern identification of vectors with d/ds.

**Where it breaks down:** Abstract for novices; the identification with a derivative operator is only mentioned, not used.

### Basis one-forms change opposite to basis vectors because their pairing is constant → covariant derivative of one-forms and higher-rank tensors · useful · SCH §5.3 p.130 (pdf 148)

Rationalizes the minus sign in the one-form covariant derivative: the pairing of dual bases is a fixed Kronecker delta, so its derivative vanishes and changes must cancel.

**Where it breaks down:** A mnemonic rather than a derivation; the rigorous argument is the product rule.

### Flat but not Euclidean → flat versus curved geometry via parallelism · useful · SCH §5.1 p.117 (pdf 135)

Minkowski space satisfies the parallel axiom (flat) yet has a metric in which light rays are straight lines of zero length (not Euclidean), separating curvature from signature.

**Where it breaks down:** Brief; relies on prior comfort with null intervals.


## Misconceptions addressed

### A laboratory at rest on Earth's surface is an inertial (Lorentz) frame with gravity acting as an ordinary force. · SCH §5.1 p.113 (pdf 131)

**Correction:** Schild's argument with the measured redshift shows such a frame cannot have Minkowski geometry; freely falling frames are the inertial ones.

**Why tempting:** Newtonian physics treats the ground as a good inertial frame with gravity as a force.

### Showing the Earth frame is not inertial means there are no inertial frames at all in gravity. · SCH §5.1 p.114 (pdf 132)

**Correction:** Freely falling frames remain inertial in a restricted local sense.

**Why tempting:** The redshift argument appears to demolish SR altogether.

### One could build a single rigid freely falling frame covering Earth's whole neighbourhood. · SCH §5.1 p.116 (pdf 134)

**Correction:** Free-fall directions converge and rates vary with height and time, so freely falling frames are inertial only locally.

**Why tempting:** Near Earth's surface g seems uniform.

### Flat means Euclidean. · SCH §5.1 p.117 (pdf 135)

**Correction:** Minkowski spacetime is flat (parallel lines stay parallel) but not Euclidean (indefinite metric, null lines).

**Why tempting:** In everyday geometry flatness and Euclidean distance come together.

### The derivative of a vector field is obtained by differentiating its components. · SCH §5.3 p.124 (pdf 142)

**Correction:** In a position-dependent basis the basis vectors must be differentiated too; the constant field e_x has varying polar components.

**Why tempting:** True in Cartesian coordinates, which is all most learners have used.

### Christoffel symbols appearing means the space is curved. · SCH §5.3 p.127 (pdf 145)

**Correction:** Nonzero Gamma arise in flat space from curvilinear coordinates; they vanish in Cartesian coordinates. Curvature is a separate tensor (Riemann) to come.

**Why tempting:** Christoffel symbols are introduced in a chapter about curvature.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Christoffel symbols have nothing to do with tensors. · SCH §5.4 p.134 (pdf 152)

**Correction:** For fixed alpha they are components of the tensor nabla e_alpha, but they are not components of a single (1 2) tensor, so they do not transform tensorially.

**Why tempting:** Many texts state bluntly that Gamma is not a tensor.

### The line element dr^2 + r^2 d theta^2 is the same object as the metric tensor d-tilde-r (x) d-tilde-r + r^2 d-tilde-theta (x) d-tilde-theta. · SCH §5.2 p.123 (pdf 141)

**Correction:** The tensor is a machine taking two vectors; the line element is its value on an infinitesimal displacement, with d meaning a small increment.

**Why tempting:** The notations are nearly identical and texts use them interchangeably.

### In Euclidean space, vectors and their associated one-forms have the same components. · SCH §5.2 p.124 (pdf 142)

**Correction:** Only in Cartesian coordinates; in polar coordinates the theta components differ by r^2.

**Why tempting:** Euclidean experience with Cartesian components hides the distinction.

### The vanishing of g_theta theta;r is a lucky cancellation. · SCH §5.4 p.132 (pdf 150)

**Correction:** It follows necessarily because nabla g is a tensor whose Cartesian components are zero.

**Why tempting:** The explicit computation looks like terms cancelling by coincidence.

### The polar divergence formula from vector calculus contradicts Eq. 5.56, so one of them is wrong. · SCH §5.5 p.136 (pdf 154)

**Correction:** Textbooks use orthonormal (noncoordinate) components (V^r, r V^theta); both formulas agree.

**Why tempting:** Learners remember the 1/r in front of the theta term.

### Any smooth field of basis vectors, such as unit polar vectors, is the coordinate basis of some coordinate system. · SCH §5.5 p.136 (pdf 154)

**Correction:** Integrability conditions must hold; the orthonormal polar basis fails them.

**Why tempting:** Unit vectors r-hat and theta-hat are routinely associated with polar coordinates.

### A path has a unique tangent vector at each point. · SCH §5.2 p.121 (pdf 139)

**Correction:** Each parametrization gives a different curve with a tangent of different length; only a curve has a unique tangent.

**Why tempting:** Geometric tangent lines are unique.

### Curvature is primarily a property of the metric formula, so a non-constant metric like diag(1, r^2) means curved space. · SCH §5.1 p.117 (pdf 135)

**Correction:** The plane in polar coordinates has position-dependent metric components but is flat; the chapter emphasizes parallelism, measured later by the Riemann tensor.

**Why tempting:** Metric components vary in both curved spaces and curvilinear coordinates.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Einstein's tower perpetual-motion machine · SCH §5.1 p.111 (pdf 129)

**Setup:** Drop a mass from a tower, convert its entire energy at the base into a photon aimed upward, and reconvert the photon to mass at the top.

**Lesson:** Energy conservation requires a photon to lose energy gh when climbing: gravitational redshift.

**App idea:** Cycle animation with energy ledger and a no-redshift toggle producing runaway energy.

### Schild's congruent crests · SCH §5.1 p.113 (pdf 131)

**Setup:** Spacetime diagram of the tower in the Earth frame with two successive light crests travelling upward along identical but arbitrary paths.

**Lesson:** In a static field Minkowski geometry would give equal crest spacing at both heights; the measured redshift contradicts this, so the Earth frame is not Lorentz.

**App idea:** Draggable crest paths with automatic time translation and interval comparison.

### Uniformly accelerating rocket · SCH §5.1 p.114 (pdf 132)

**Setup:** Observer inside a rocket accelerating in empty space drops objects of different composition and weighs objects.

**Lesson:** Uniform gravity is indistinguishable from acceleration; the truly inertial frame is the one falling freely toward the rear.

**App idea:** Toggle between rocket and Earth views with identical particle motion.

### Redshift seen from a freely falling elevator · SCH §5.1 p.115 (pdf 133)

**Setup:** A frame released from rest when the photon leaves the bottom falls while the photon climbs; compare the photon's frequency in this frame with the emitted value.

**Lesson:** No redshift in the freely falling frame to first order, strengthening its claim to be inertial.

**App idea:** Photon and falling box animation with Doppler and gravitational shifts shown as opposing bars that cancel.

### Rigid lattice falling around Earth · SCH §5.1 p.116 (pdf 134)

**Setup:** Extend a freely falling reference lattice horizontally and vertically around the Earth (Figure 5.3).

**Lesson:** A rigid lattice can match free fall only along the line aimed at Earth's centre. Off-axis rows would have to converge, and rows at different distances would need different accelerations, so inertial frames are confined to small regions and short times.

**App idea:** N-body test-particle lattice with deviation readout versus lattice size.

### Initially parallel free world lines · SCH §5.1 p.117 (pdf 135)

**Setup:** Two nearby free particles start with parallel world lines in SR and in a nonuniform gravitational field.

**Lesson:** They stay parallel in SR (flat) but not under tidal gravity (curved), linking gravity to geometry.

**App idea:** Spacetime diagram of two radial free-fall world lines converging, paired with meridians on a sphere.


## Applications and observations

- **Pound-Rebka-Snider tower experiment** (experiment, SCH §5.1 p.112 (pdf 130)): Gamma-ray photons sent up (and down) a tower; Mössbauer resonance used to detect the tiny frequency difference predicted by Eq. 5.1. Key numbers: h = 22.5 m; agreement to about 1% (Pound and Snider 1965). Fractional shift gh/c^2 is roughly 2.5e-15 (our estimate, not stated in text).
- **GPS clock corrections** (technology, SCH §5.1 p.113 (pdf 131)): Orbiting atomic clocks run faster than ground clocks because of the gravitational redshift; without correction receivers would read inconsistent time stamps from the five or more satellites used and navigation would fail within minutes. Its successful operation counts as a precise redshift test. Key numbers: Errors would accumulate beyond usefulness in a few minutes; signals from 5 or more satellites.
- **Ocean tides** (observation, SCH §5.1 p.117 (pdf 135)): Tides arise from the difference of lunar and solar gravitational pull across Earth's diameter; a uniform field would produce none (Exercise 5.2).
- **Eötvös experiment** (experiment, SCH §5.1 p.114 (pdf 132)): Tests that all materials fall with the same acceleration, underpinning the equivalence principle (footnote and bibliography).
- **Accuracy of local inertial frames near Earth** (numerical-estimate, SCH §5.1 p.116 (pdf 134)): Freely falling frames near Earth's surface are inertial to high accuracy for current technology, but in principle any nonuniformity is detectable, so mathematically the frame is inertial only at a point; quantitative treatment deferred.

## Historical notes

- **Albert Einstein:** Proposed the idealized tower (energy conservation) argument for the gravitational redshift, and later extended the equivalence principle to all laws of physics. — Gravitational redshift became a foundational prediction; Einstein's principle guides how physics is placed in curved spacetime. (SCH §5.1 p.111 (pdf 129))
- **R. V. Pound, G. A. Rebka, J. L. Snider (1960, 1965):** Measured the gravitational frequency shift over 22.5 m using the Mössbauer effect, to about 1%. — First precise laboratory confirmation of gravitational redshift. (SCH §5.1 p.112 (pdf 130))
- **Alfred Schild (1967):** Gave the spacetime-diagram argument that a frame at rest on Earth cannot be a Lorentz frame. — Turns an energy-conservation effect into a proof that gravity is incompatible with global SR. (SCH §5.1 p.113 (pdf 131))
- **Galileo Galilei:** Leaning Tower of Pisa demonstration that bodies fall identically regardless of composition. — Universality of free fall singles gravity out among forces. (SCH §5.1 p.114 (pdf 132))
- **Loránd Eötvös, Robert Dicke (1964 (Dicke review)):** Torsion-balance tests of composition independence of free fall, cited in a footnote. — High-precision experimental basis for the weak equivalence principle. (SCH §5.1 p.114 (pdf 132))
- **Bernhard Riemann (1826-1866 (lifespan)):** Footnote 2 credits Riemann as the first to publish a detailed study of what happens when Euclid's parallel axiom is dropped. — Riemannian geometry became the mathematical language Einstein adopted. Tutor caution: the priority claim is the book's own. Lobachevsky (1829) and Bolyai (1832) published hyperbolic geometry earlier; Riemann's 1854 lecture is the source of general curved (Riemannian) geometry. (SCH §5.1 p.117 (pdf 135))
- **Neil Ashby (2003):** Review of relativistic effects in the Global Positioning System. — Shows the redshift is an engineering necessity. (SCH §5.1 p.113 (pdf 131))

## Notation and conventions

- **Units:** c = 1 throughout; gh in Eqs. 5.1-5.2 is dimensionless and equals gh/c^2 in ordinary units; travel time over height h is h. — G is not set to 1 here but does not appear. (SCH §5.1 p.112 (pdf 130))
- **Indices in the Euclidean plane:** Greek indices take the values 1, 2 (e.g. r, theta) in §§5.2-5.5; summation convention applies. Euclidean vectors carry arrows, one-forms tildes. — Stated in footnote 3. (SCH §5.2 p.118 (pdf 136))
- **Primed indices:** Unprimed indices refer to Cartesian (x, y), primed to the other system; Lambda^alpha'_beta = dx^alpha'/dx^beta for vectors and Lambda^alpha_beta' = dx^alpha/dx^beta' for one-forms. — Index placement alone selects the correct derivative. (SCH §5.2 p.119 (pdf 137))
- **Comma and semicolon:** V^alpha_,beta is a partial derivative of components; V^alpha_;beta the covariant derivative; derivative index is written last and nabla_beta denotes differentiation along e_beta. — In a noncoordinate basis the comma is defined as d-tilde-phi(e_mu) and is not a coordinate partial derivative. (SCH §5.3 p.127 (pdf 145))
- **Christoffel symbol index order:** Gamma^mu_alpha beta: upper index is the component, first lower index the basis vector differentiated, second lower index the differentiation direction. (SCH §5.3 p.126 (pdf 144))
- **Gradient notation:** d-tilde-phi (tilde) is the one-form gradient; d-arrow-phi (arrow) is the vector gradient obtained with the inverse metric; nabla phi is preferred for the one-form later. (SCH §5.2 p.124 (pdf 142))
- **Line element:** Metric components displayed as dl^2 = dr^2 + r^2 d theta^2, where d denotes an infinitesimal increment, following common practice despite the similarity to basis one-form notation. (SCH §5.2 p.123 (pdf 141))
- **Hats on indices:** A caret over an index (e_r-hat, V^theta-hat) marks components in an orthonormal basis; omega-tilde^alpha-hat for orthonormal basis one-forms. (SCH §5.5 p.135 (pdf 153))
- **Curve versus path:** Path denotes a set of points; curve denotes a parametrized path. (SCH §5.2 p.120 (pdf 138))
- **Tensor type notation:** (M N) tensors written as binomial-style column symbols; nabla V is (1 1), nabla T of a (0 2) tensor is (0 3). (SCH §5.3 p.130 (pdf 148))

## Teaching gems

### Derive gravitational redshift from forbidding perpetual motion on a tower. · SCH §5.1 p.112 (pdf 130)

**Why it works:** Needs only E = mc^2 and E = h nu, yields a quantitative prediction, and makes a relativistic effect feel inevitable.

**App idea:** Energy-ledger tower animation with a switch that disables redshift and shows energy piling up each cycle.

### Draw light crests on deliberately arbitrary paths so that congruence, not a model of light bending, drives the contradiction. · SCH §5.1 p.113 (pdf 131)

**Why it works:** Pre-empts the objection that gravity might bend light, isolating the essential assumption (static field).

**App idea:** Freehand crest drawer that auto-generates the translated second crest and compares intervals.

### Check the equivalence principle by computing that the free-fall Doppler shift cancels the redshift. · SCH §5.1 p.115 (pdf 133)

**Why it works:** Turns a principle into a verifiable first-order calculation.

**App idea:** Opposing bar meters for gravitational and Doppler shifts that sum to zero in the falling frame.

### Define curvature by whether initially parallel free world lines stay parallel, and note Minkowski space is flat but non-Euclidean. · SCH §5.1 p.117 (pdf 135)

**Why it works:** Connects tidal physics directly to geometry and separates curvature from metric signature.

**App idea:** Side-by-side: free particles in uniform versus radial field, and meridians on a globe, with separation plots.

### Learn connections in the flat plane with polar coordinates, where Cartesian coordinates can check everything. · SCH §5.1 p.117 (pdf 135)

**Why it works:** Separates the coordinate-induced part of calculus from genuine curvature, and every result can be verified by the learner.

**App idea:** Dual-pane calculator: compute any derivative in polars with Gamma and in Cartesian then transform; the two panes must agree.

### The constant vector e_x has changing polar components. · SCH §5.3 p.124 (pdf 142)

**Why it works:** A simple paradox that makes the need for basis derivatives, hence Christoffel symbols, obvious.

**App idea:** Drag a probe around a uniform arrow field and watch its polar components change while the arrow stays fixed.

### Picture d e_r / d theta as the small difference of neighbouring radial unit vectors (Figure 5.6). · SCH §5.3 p.125 (pdf 143)

**Why it works:** Gives a visual origin for a Christoffel symbol and invites learners to draw the other cases.

**App idea:** Difference-quotient animator that converges onto Gamma^mu_alpha beta e_mu as the increment shrinks.

### Rederive the polar Laplacian from contraction of the covariant derivative and index raising. · SCH §5.3 p.129 (pdf 147)

**Why it works:** Pays off the formalism with a formula learners once memorized, building trust.

**App idea:** Step-by-step symbolic derivation with each 1/r factor color-linked to the Gamma or g^theta theta that produced it.

### Prove it in Cartesian coordinates, observe it is a tensor equation, conclude it everywhere (metric compatibility, symmetry of Gamma). · SCH §5.4 p.131 (pdf 149)

**Why it works:** A reusable proof strategy that becomes the local-inertial-frame argument throughout GR.

**App idea:** Proof-pattern card game: learner chooses a statement, checks it in Cartesian coordinates, and the app confirms whether it is tensorial enough to generalize.

### Reconcile the vector-calculus divergence formula via orthonormal noncoordinate components. · SCH §5.5 p.136 (pdf 154)

**Why it works:** Resolves a conflict with prior knowledge instead of ignoring it, and motivates the coordinate/noncoordinate distinction.

**App idea:** Toggle between coordinate and orthonormal components of the same field and see both divergence formulas return the same heat-map.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Units with c = 1 are used silently in Eqs. 5.1-5.2 (gh dimensionless, travel time equals h).** — Learners may think gh is an energy or be unable to plug in numbers. *Suggestion:* Restore c explicitly: fractional shift gh/c^2, and compute it for 22.5 m (about 2.5e-15).
- **Figure 5.2 as printed labels both crest intervals Delta t_bot, while the text refers to Delta t_top at the top.** — Can confuse which interval is longer and undermine the argument. *Suggestion:* Relabel the top interval Delta t_top in any redesign and state explicitly that it is the longer one.
- **Why congruent crest paths imply equal coordinate time intervals relies on time-translation invariance of both the field and Minkowski geometry; this is stated quickly.** — The core of Schild's argument may feel like a sleight of hand. *Suggestion:* Show the second crest literally as the first translated upward in t, and measure intervals with a flat-geometry ruler.
- **Eq. 5.2 rests on two first-order shortcuts the book leaves implicit. The light travel time is taken to be h, even though the frame moves during the flight (a correction of order gh^2). And the Doppler factor uses the frame's speed gh at the moment the photon arrives, which is the only velocity that matters for that measurement. Signs are not discussed.** — Careful students may think the argument ignores the frame's acceleration during the flight, or may get the sign of the Doppler shift backwards. *Suggestion:* Point out that the frame falls toward the incoming photon, so it measures a blueshift relative to the apparatus at the top. Only its velocity at the reception event enters the Doppler formula, and every neglected term is second order, just like the terms dropped in Eq. 5.1.
- **The step from tidal forces to curved spacetime is heuristic; geodesic deviation is not quantified here.** — Learners may accept 'gravity is curvature' as a slogan without seeing how to measure it. *Suggestion:* Preview a simple Newtonian tidal tensor calculation and promise its geometric counterpart in the Riemann tensor.
- **Summation on the first versus second index of Lambda (row versus column vectors) is subtle and easily reversed.** — Sign and index errors in transforming one-forms. *Suggestion:* Drill with the rule that indices must match diagonally (upper with lower of same type) and verify against the chain rule.
- **Christoffel symbols are defined here using derivatives of basis vectors relative to a background Cartesian basis, which does not exist in curved space; metric compatibility is claimed to persist without proof.** — Learners may not see how the construction generalizes or why nabla g = 0 should hold in curved spacetime. *Suggestion:* Flag early that chapter 6 replaces Cartesian coordinates by local inertial coordinates at each point.
- **The mixed-partials test in Eq. 5.84 is declared false without computing the value.** — Learners may not see why it fails. *Suggestion:* Show the computation: d(y/r)/dy + d(x/r)/dx = x^2/r^3 + y^2/r^3 = 1/r, which is nonzero.
- **Section 5.5 notation mixes hats, bars, tildes and primes on indices, and the reading copy's OCR of Eqs. 5.92-5.94 is garbled.** — Hard to parse which basis each index refers to. *Suggestion:* Use one marking (hats for orthonormal) and color-code index types in app displays.
- **The one-form derivative argument that the bracket must be tensorial because it multiplies an arbitrary vector relies on a quotient-theorem idea not stated explicitly.** — Students may not see why tensoriality follows. *Suggestion:* State the quotient rule explicitly: if X_alpha V^alpha is a scalar for every vector V, then X is a one-form.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'If gravity were just a force in special relativity, could a lab on Earth be an inertial frame?' Let the learner commit, then run the tower and crest arguments.
- Before any formalism, ask the learner to predict what happens to a photon climbing out of a gravitational well and why energy conservation cares.
- Order for §5.1: redshift from energy, Schild contradiction, universality of free fall, rocket reversal, no redshift in free fall, locality and tides, curvature as failure of parallelism.
- Check for understanding: 'Why would a perfectly uniform field produce no tides?' and 'Why can't a single freely falling frame cover the whole Earth?'
- Stress repeatedly that §§5.2-5.5 are in flat space: Christoffel symbols here come from curvilinear coordinates, not curvature. Ask: 'Is the plane curved in polar coordinates?'
- Use the e_x paradox (constant vector, changing polar components) as the entry point to Christoffel symbols; have the learner compute d e_x / d theta both naively and correctly.
- When introducing the covariant derivative, have the learner compute one Christoffel symbol three ways: from basis derivatives, from Figure 5.6 geometry, and from Eq. 5.75.
- Mnemonic to teach: derivative index last on Gamma; one Gamma term per index; plus for upper, minus for lower. Test on nabla_beta B^mu_nu.
- Check for understanding: 'Why does the divergence need the vector gradient rather than the one-form gradient to build a Laplacian?'
- Common learner question: 'Is Gamma a tensor?' Answer with the book's nuance: it is a set of components of nabla e_alpha for each alpha, but not a single tensor; it vanishes in Cartesian but not polar coordinates.
- Common learner question: 'Why does my calculus book have (1/r) d V_theta/d theta in the divergence?' Explain orthonormal versus coordinate components.
- Emphasize the reusable proof strategy: verify in Cartesian (later, local inertial) coordinates, confirm the equation is tensorial, generalize. Ask the learner to apply it to show Gamma is symmetric.
- For novices, restore c in redshift estimates; for advanced learners, connect Exercise 5.21 (Rindler) to horizons and the equivalence principle.
- Close by asking what is still missing to describe genuine curvature, steering toward parallel transport and the Riemann tensor of chapter 6.
