# SCH ch12 · Gravitational wave astronomy

> Turns the gravitational-wave theory of Chapter 9 into observational practice: a tour of the source classes (compact binaries, spinning neutron stars, bursts, stochastic backgrounds) with solar-mass-normalised scaling laws, a discrete-sample introduction to how interferometer data are turned into detections (detector tensor and antenna patterns, noise spectra, matched filtering, false-alarm statistics, coincidence and time slides, null streams, pulsar and stochastic searches), and a narrative of the first LIGO-Virgo events (GW150914, GW170814, GW170817, GW190425, GW190521, GW190814) and what each taught.

**Pages:** printed 360–414 · pdf 378–432 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

The astrophysics survey (12.2) and detection stories (12.4) are accessible to anyone who has read Chapter 9. Section 12.3 needs comfort with probability, variances of sums, 2x2 linear algebra and some Fourier ideas; the discrete-sample treatment keeps it elementary but several printed equations contain slips that can confuse careful readers. Conceptually the hardest ideas are the difference between coherent and stochastic integration, trials factors, and the null stream. Very little differential geometry is required.

## Role in the book

New in the third edition, this chapter sits between the black-hole chapter (11) and cosmology (13) and is the payoff of Chapter 9: the chirp mass, time-to-merger, polarization-versus-inclination and standard-siren results derived there are now used on real data, and the Kerr ringdown and intermediate-mass black holes of Chapter 11 reappear as observed phenomena. It is the book's only sustained treatment of signal analysis and statistics, and it ends by pointing to Chapter 13 through the Hubble-Lemaitre constant measured with GW170817 and through the cosmological gravitational-wave background. Almost no new geometry is introduced; the tensor language is used once, elegantly, for the detector response.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can contrast gravitational and electromagnetic radiation as information carriers (what emits them, typical frequencies, coherence over the source, absorption, focusing, localization) and explain why they are complementary.
- Reader can estimate the gravitational-wave frequency at which a compact binary effectively merges, the remaining time to merger from a given frequency, and the ringdown frequency of the remnant, all scaled to solar masses.
- Reader can explain the formation puzzles for merging black-hole binaries (natal kicks, common envelopes, low metallicity, dynamical and triple channels) and how spin alignment could discriminate among them.
- Reader can estimate the strain from a slightly non-axisymmetric spinning neutron star and explain why long coherent integration and the spin-down limit matter for continuous-wave searches.
- Reader can write the detector response as the double contraction of a detector tensor with the wave tensor and decompose it into antenna pattern functions F+ and Fx.
- Reader can read an amplitude spectral density plot and attribute its low-, mid- and high-frequency walls to seismic/Newtonian, thermal and shot noise, and distinguish Gaussian noise from glitches.
- Reader can derive, for white noise, the matched-filter threshold A_min = sigma/||w|| and show that it improves on simple threshold crossing by roughly the square root of the number of signal cycles.
- Reader can compute Gaussian false-alarm probabilities with the tail bound, include the trials factor from many samples and templates, and justify signal-to-noise thresholds near 8.
- Reader can explain coincidence analysis, time slides, and why a network of three or more detectors enables coherent analysis, null streams, glitch vetoes, direction finding and calibration checks.
- Reader can reconstruct the two polarizations from two detectors with known sky position and infer the orbital inclination from the amplitude ratio, and explain the distance-inclination degeneracy for nearly face-on systems.
- Reader can explain why Doppler modulation from Earth's motion makes optimal all-sky pulsar searches computationally impossible, and estimate the resulting angular resolution and number of sky patches.
- Reader can derive the cross-correlation signal-to-noise ratio for a stochastic background and explain its slow fourth-root growth in amplitude.
- Reader can summarize what GW150914, GW170814, GW170817, GW190425, GW190521 and GW190814 each established (black-hole binaries exist, tensor polarization, multimessenger kilonova and r-process, standard-siren H0, mass-gap objects, higher multipoles).
- Reader can explain how a standard-siren distance plus a host-galaxy redshift yields the Hubble-Lemaitre constant, and why inclination uncertainty dominates the error.

## Teaching approach

An observational survey chapter written by a practitioner of gravitational-wave data analysis. Instead of geometry it teaches order-of-magnitude scaling, statistical reasoning and detection strategy. Every data-analysis idea is built from the simplest discrete model (sampled white Gaussian noise plus a known template) so that results like the square-root-of-N gain, the Gaussian false-alarm tail and the N^(1/4) stochastic scaling can be derived in a few lines, then corrected qualitatively for real coloured noise, glitches and huge parameter spaces. The detection stories at the end are used as case studies for the analysis concepts introduced earlier (glitch removal, antenna-pattern nulls, inclination errors, higher harmonics).

**Style:** observational-astrophysics, survey-overview, physical-motivation-first, computational-recipe, example-driven, historical-narrative

**Narrative arc**

1. Open with the Figure 12.1 time series of GW150914 in both LIGO detectors as proof that observational gravitational-wave astronomy exists.
2. Contrast gravitational and electromagnetic radiation (coherent global emission versus microscopic emission; low versus high frequencies; unfocusable versus imaging) to motivate multimessenger astronomy and to justify why theory of sources had to precede detection.
3. Survey source classes: binaries (merger frequency, time to merger, LISA-to-ground handoff, formation channels, ringdown, EMRIs), spinning neutron stars (strain estimate, spin-down limits), unmodelled bursts (supernovae, cosmic strings), stochastic backgrounds (astrophysical and cosmological, Omega_gw), and unknown sources.
4. Pose the question 'what counts as a detection?' and set up the detector response with a detector tensor, antenna patterns and network patterns.
5. Describe detector noise: amplitude spectral density, Gaussianity from the central limit theorem, shot/thermal/seismic walls, lines, Newtonian noise, glitches.
6. Build matched filtering from a toy model of sampled data, compare its threshold with naive threshold crossing, and extract the square-root-of-cycles gain.
7. Move from detection to measurement: template banks, intrinsic versus extrinsic parameters, Bayesian posteriors, polarization reconstruction with two detectors and inclination from the polarization ratio.
8. Quantify significance with Gaussian tail probabilities, real sample counts and trials factors; then confront glitches with coincidence and time slides.
9. Use a counting argument (two polarizations, N detectors) to motivate coherent analysis and derive the three-detector null stream and its uses.
10. Apply the principles class by class: Doppler-modulated pulsar searches and their impossible computing cost, cross-correlation searches for backgrounds, minimal-assumption burst searches.
11. Tell the story of the first detections as case studies, closing with catalogs, rising detection rates and the expanding network.

**Signature moves**

- Normalises every astrophysical formula to one solar mass and a benchmark frequency so readers can plug in real systems instantly (Eqs. 12.1-12.4).
- Teaches signal processing with discrete samples and Kronecker deltas instead of continuous power spectral densities, deferring the frequency domain to an exercise.
- Derives a scaling law from the simplest possible case (a pure sinusoid, co-located aligned detectors) and then argues it is robust.
- Converts abstract probabilities into countable events using actual sampling rates and run lengths (140,000 five-sigma fluctuations per year).
- Uses counting arguments (unknown functions versus data streams) to reveal structure such as the null stream before doing any algebra.
- Estimates computing cost in floating-point operations to show that an optimal search is out of reach, making the case for suboptimal methods quantitatively.
- Treats the detection narratives as worked illustrations of earlier analysis ideas (glitch in GW170817, Virgo's antenna-pattern null, higher multipoles in GW190521).
- Keeps flagging dates ('mid-2021') and open problems, presenting the field as live rather than finished.

## Section by section

### §12.1 Overview — p.360 (pdf 378)

Announces the first direct detection and uses the two-detector strain traces as the chapter's emblem. Sets up a systematic comparison of gravitational with electromagnetic waves: gravitational waves come from bulk mass-energy motions and encode global properties at nHz-kHz frequencies, pass through matter but cannot be focused, so detectors are nearly omnidirectional and only energetic relativistic sources are seen; light comes from microscopic charges at far higher frequencies, is absorbed and scattered but can be imaged. Both travel at c, are transverse, and can be lensed. The complementarity motivates multimessenger astronomy, while the differences warn against expecting counterparts. Explains that source theory was needed both to justify the investment and because predicted waveforms raise effective sensitivity.

**Concepts:** gravitational wave astronomy, multimessenger astronomy, complementarity of gravitational and electromagnetic observations, gravitational lensing of gravitational waves

**Key moves**
- The sign flip and time shift between Hanford and Livingston traces encode detector geometry and sky position.
- Emission from coherent bulk motion versus incoherent microscopic charges explains the frequency and information contrast.
- Weak coupling to matter is both a virtue (transparency) and a limitation (no focusing, only high-flux sources).
- Waveform prediction is part of detector sensitivity, which is why theory was essential before detection.

### §12.2 Astrophysical sources of gravitational waves — p.362 (pdf 380)

Groups sources into binaries, spinning neutron stars, bursts and the Big Bang. For binaries, notes that only mass ratio, eccentricity and separation matter once total mass sets the scale; slowly evolving binaries fill the LISA and pulsar-timing bands (white-dwarf confusion noise, supermassive black-hole background), whereas ground-based detectors see mergers. Rearranges the Chapter 9 phase-to-merger relation into a merger-frequency estimate (half an orbit left), checks it against GW150914 and binary neutron stars, derives the upper mass limit set by LIGO's 20 Hz floor, and rescales the time-to-merger formula to show that a 30 solar-mass chirp-mass system seen by LISA at 30 mHz merges in the ground band within a year. Discusses why merging compact binaries are hard to form (supernova kicks, common envelopes) and the low-metallicity, cluster, triple and primordial channels, the X-ray binary and binary-pulsar evidence, the short gamma-ray burst connection, LISA supermassive mergers, ringdown frequencies and EMRIs.

**Concepts:** mass ratio of a binary, chirp mass, merger frequency estimate, time to merger, multiband gravitational wave astronomy, confusion noise, natal kick, common envelope evolution, binary black hole formation channels, X-ray binary, short gamma-ray bursts from neutron star mergers, ringdown, extreme mass-ratio inspiral, merger remnant outcomes

**Key moves**
- Invert psi = (1/32)(M omega)^(-5/3) for f_gw = omega/pi and set psi = pi to get f_final = 4.0 kHz (1 solar mass / chirp mass).
- Check: GW150914 predicts 130 Hz against an observed peak near 250 Hz, a factor of two accepted for such a crude estimate.
- A 20 Hz low-frequency cutoff means chirp masses above about 200 solar masses merge below the ground-based band.
- Rescaled time-to-merger: 2.9 s at 100 Hz for one solar mass, steeply dependent on mass (-5/3) and frequency (-8/3).
- Ringdown at omega about 0.5/M gives 16 kHz per solar mass, 230 Hz for GW150914's remnant.
- Spin alignment as a fossil record of isolated-binary versus dynamical formation.

### Spinning neutron stars, bursts, stochastic backgrounds and unexpected sources (within 12.2) — p.367 (pdf 385)

Continuous-wave sources: a rotating neutron star with fractional asymmetry epsilon radiates h ~ 2 epsilon Omega^2 I / r, about 1e-26 for a millisecond-scale pulsar at 1 kpc with epsilon = 1e-6; reaching such amplitudes needs about 1e8 coherent cycles. Searches have found nothing; limits reach epsilon ~ 1e-7, and for the Crab the gravitational share of spin-down energy loss is tiny. Bursts: core-collapse supernovae cannot be predicted in detail, so they are sought as excess power, ideally in coincidence with neutrinos; cosmic strings are a speculative burst source. Stochastic backgrounds: random superpositions measured by cross-correlating detectors, characterised by mean square amplitude and Omega_gw; the LIGO pair correlates below about 100 Hz; Figure 12.2 shows the predicted binary black hole background and LIGO sensitivity; a cosmological background would probe the earliest Universe but is probably out of reach except via CMB polarization. Ends by hoping for entirely unanticipated sources, possibly linked to dark matter.

**Concepts:** continuous gravitational waves from spinning neutron stars, neutron star ellipticity, spin-down limit, r-mode instability, burst search, core-collapse supernova gravitational waves, cosmic strings, stochastic gravitational wave background, energy density parameter of gravitational waves, cross-correlation search, pulsar timing array

**Key moves**
- Amplitude estimate from the Chapter 9 rotating-quadrupole formula with realistic moment of inertia.
- Narrowband integration concentrates signal power into one frequency bin while noise spreads, so sensitivity grows as the square root of observing time.
- Spin-down sets an energy-based upper bound; amplitude limits below it translate quadratically into energy fractions.
- Correlation between two sites persists for wavelengths longer than the separation, fixing the usable band.
- The CMB is the electromagnetic analogue of a cosmological gravitational background, but gravity decoupled essentially at the beginning.

### §12.3 Finding weak signals in noise: what is a detection? — p.373 (pdf 391)

Frames every detection as a probabilistic statement with a false-alarm estimate. Sets up two frames: detector-based spherical coordinates and a sky-plane basis attached to the wave. Builds plus and cross basis tensors, writes the wave as a TT tensor, defines the detector tensor L(e_x e_x - e_y e_y), and obtains the arm-length difference as a double contraction; dividing by L gives the response F+ h+ + Fx hx with geometry-only antenna functions. Plots the antenna power pattern for one interferometer and the LIGO-Virgo network. Then describes intrinsic noise via the amplitude spectral density: mostly Gaussian by the central limit theorem, with shot noise at high frequency, thermal noise in the bucket, seismic noise at low frequency, narrow suspension and mains lines, design trade-offs (laser power, cryogenics), Newtonian noise that motivates underground and space detectors, and non-Gaussian glitches that limit burst searches.

**Concepts:** polarization basis tensors, detector tensor, antenna pattern functions, antenna power pattern, amplitude spectral density of detector noise, Gaussian detector noise, shot noise, thermal noise, seismic noise, Newtonian noise, glitches

**Key moves**
- Keep the wave's sky-plane basis independent of any detector so the same wave can be projected onto many detectors.
- Response = d : h, a coordinate-free double contraction (the tensor-as-machine idea applied to hardware).
- Separate geometry (F+, Fx depending on theta, phi) from waveform (h+, hx).
- Network power pattern is the sum of individual patterns for identical detectors; holes remain.
- Shot noise rises with frequency because fewer photons arrive per shorter sample interval.

### Matched filtering, template banks and parameter extraction (within 12.3) — p.378 (pdf 396)

GW170817 never exceeded 1e-22 yet had the highest significance, because its waveform was known. With sampled data x_j = n_j + A w_(j-D) in white Gaussian noise, naive threshold crossing requires A above sigma / w_max, whereas correlating with the template gives a Gaussian statistic whose mean peaks at the arrival index with value A||w||^2 and whose standard deviation is sigma||w||, so the threshold becomes sigma/||w||. For a sinusoid this is lower by sqrt(N/2), better read as the square root of the number of cycles because resampling changes sigma and N together; range grows as that factor and volume as its cube. Realistic searches use banks of thousands of post-Newtonian plus numerical-relativity templates, computed with FFTs in the frequency domain, currently for quasi-circular orbits; LISA adds orbital modulation and the EMRI template problem. The best-matching template gives intrinsic parameters (chirp mass best, then total mass, component masses and spins) with Bayesian posteriors; extrinsic parameters need a network. Two detectors with a known sky position invert a 2x2 matrix to recover h+ and hx; rotating the sky basis until they are in quadrature gives the inclination from their amplitude ratio, which is poorly constrained near face-on, feeding a distance-inclination degeneracy that higher harmonics can break.

**Concepts:** matched filtering, template bank, white noise, signal-to-noise ratio, square-root-of-cycles gain of matched filtering, detection range and volume, intrinsic and extrinsic parameters, Bayesian parameter estimation, polarization reconstruction with two detectors, inclination from polarization ratio, distance-inclination degeneracy, higher harmonics in binary waveforms

**Key moves**
- Model data as noise plus a shifted, scaled template; compute mean and variance of the correlation.
- Replace the template's peak value by its norm: the norm integrates over the signal's duration.
- Sinusoid example: squared norm is B^2 N/2 because the cos(2 omega t) term averages out.
- Resampling argument shows the gain counts cycles, not samples.
- Matrix inversion of the two-detector response (Eqs. 12.23-12.25).
- Quadrature criterion fixes the sky-basis rotation, giving |h+|/|hx| = (1 + cos^2 iota)/(2 cos iota).

### What is a detection? Significance, coincidence and time slides (within 12.3) — p.386 (pdf 404)

Uses the two-sided Gaussian tail probability and its bounds to show that a 5 sigma fluctuation, rare per sample, occurs about 140,000 times in a year of 8 kHz data; per independent filter sample (about 5 ms) the threshold must rise to about 6.5 for one false alarm every two years, and with 1e4 independent templates to about 8. Glitches make single-detector thresholds unreliable, so LIGO works in coincidence mode with a light-travel-time window; residual coincident glitches are measured by time slides that destroy true coincidences but preserve the glitch rate. Mentions that a Bayesian likelihood-ratio approach would be deeper than this frequentist treatment.

**Concepts:** false alarm probability, trials factor, coincidence analysis, time slides, false alarm rate, likelihood ratio

**Key moves**
- Upper tail bound phi(a)/a becomes accurate for large a (proved in Exercise 12.5).
- Count samples in a year to turn tiny probabilities into expected false alarms.
- Each template is an independent trial, multiplying the false-alarm rate.
- Shifting one detector's data by more than the coincidence window yields a pure-background sample.

### Coherent detection by a network and the null stream (within 12.3) — p.388 (pdf 406)

Coincidence uses only arrival times and amplitudes; with three or more detectors one can combine outputs before thresholding. Counting: a wave is two functions of time, so two non-aligned detectors can solve for them given the direction, three can find the direction with redundancy, and redundancy produces signal-free combinations. For three detectors, eliminating h+ and hx gives D12 delta3 + D23 delta1 + D31 delta2 = 0, where D_ab are 2x2 determinants of antenna factors; applied to the noisy outputs this null stream contains only noise and glitches. Uses: veto coincident glitches (bringing thresholds down to the Gaussian floor), measure and subtract a glitch overlapping a signal, find the sky direction as the one that nulls the signal, and test calibration consistency. Credits the aperture-synthesis analogy.

**Concepts:** coherent network analysis, null stream, glitch veto, detector calibration, aperture synthesis analogy

**Key moves**
- Count unknown waveforms against data streams to predict the number of null streams (N - 2).
- Substitute the two-detector solution into the third response to eliminate the signal.
- A wrong assumed direction leaves signal in the null stream, so minimizing null power locates the source.
- The null stream vanishes only if relative calibrations are right.

### Searching for pulsars, stochastic backgrounds and unmodelled bursts (within 12.3) — p.390 (pdf 408)

Pulsars: Earth's rotation and orbit Doppler-modulate a continuous signal; equating the accumulated frequency shift to the frequency resolution gives a coherent time of about 70 minutes (rotation) or a few hours (orbit) at 1 kHz, far shorter than the months needed. Demodulation depends on direction, so the orbit acts as a synthesized aperture with resolution about lambda/2R, some 0.4 arcsec at 1 kHz, requiring about 1e12 sky patches, multiplied by thousands to millions of spin-down values: optimal all-sky searches exceed world computing power (Exercise 12.9), so searches are suboptimal; targeted searches of known pulsars and Sco X-1 are easier. Stochastic: for co-located aligned detectors the correlation has mean N|h|^2 and standard deviation sqrt(N) sigma1 sigma2, so power SNR grows as N^(1/2) and amplitude SNR as N^(1/4); a background 1e4 below noise would need 1e16 samples, but a year at an effective 100 Hz reaches about 100 times below noise. LISA uses its Sagnac null stream to measure its own noise; pulsar timing correlates pulsars. Bursts: require strong signals, neutrino coincidence for supernovae, cosmic-string spectral templates, and sine-Gaussian banks; GW150914 was first flagged by such a burst search.

**Concepts:** Doppler modulation of continuous-wave signals, barycentric demodulation, angular resolution by orbital aperture synthesis, computational cost of all-sky continuous-wave searches, cross-correlation search, Sagnac null stream, sine-Gaussian burst templates, pulsar timing array, targeted continuous-wave search

**Key moves**
- Velocity change Omega^2 R T gives frequency drift f Omega^2 R T / c; equate to resolution 2/T.
- Diffraction-limit resolution lambda/(2R) with R the orbital radius.
- Number of patches 4 pi / (Delta theta)^2 times number of spin-down bins shows the combinatorial explosion.
- Correlation of two streams: signal term N|h|^2, dominant variance N sigma1^2 sigma2^2.
- Effective sample rate for correlation is set by the band where the detectors are correlated (about 100 Hz).
- Doppler-modulated pulsar signals are too intricate to be mimicked by instrumental artefacts, so single-detector searches with Gaussian false-alarm statistics are acceptable, with other detectors used for follow-up.

### §12.4 The first LIGO and Virgo detections — p.396 (pdf 414)

Opens by recalling what was expected before 2015: at design sensitivity Advanced LIGO should see roughly one event a year, most likely a binary neutron star near 100 Mpc, and with O1 running two to three times below design the first detection seemed years off. Then narrates GW150914: detected during an engineering run, a century after the field equations, first spotted by collaboration members in Hanover, recognisable as a binary black hole because its chirp was too fast and too strong for neutron stars; its parameters, graviton-mass bound, why the objects are called black holes, the unexpected size of its component masses and support for low-metallicity formation; O1's two further events. O2 added four LIGO-only black-hole events (GW170729 the farthest at 2.8 Gpc). GW170814 was the first three-detector event, shrinking the sky area and testing tensor versus pure scalar or vector polarization. GW170817 is treated in depth: 100 s in band, a gamma-ray burst 1.7 s after merger, a Livingston glitch removed by hand, Virgo's weak response used as directional information, 31 square degrees and 40 Mpc, the kilonova found in NGC4993 within 11 hours, masses, spin assumptions, Love-number limits, an off-axis jet, the first standard-siren H0 and its later radio-improved value, r-process element production, and how lucky its closeness was.

**Concepts:** GW150914, event naming convention, graviton mass bound from dispersion, polarization test of general relativity, GW170817, kilonova, standard siren, Hubble-Lemaitre constant from gravitational waves, tidal deformability, r-process nucleosynthesis in neutron star mergers, relativistic jet and viewing angle

**Key moves**
- Identification of a binary black hole from chirp rate and amplitude alone.
- Dispersion from a graviton mass accumulates over the propagation distance, so distant sources give strong bounds.
- An antenna-pattern null in one detector still carries directional information.
- H0 = recession velocity / standard-siren distance; inclination is the main error source.
- Tidal effects scale as 1/r^3 and so appear only in the last orbits.

### Further observations and catalogs (within 12.4) — p.403 (pdf 421)

GW190425: likely binary neutron star with total mass about 3.4 solar masses, seen significantly only by Livingston, poorly localized, no counterpart. GW190521: about 150 solar masses total, only four cycles (two orbits) in band, formed an intermediate-mass black hole; its 85 solar-mass primary is heavier than stellar collapse is expected to produce (the book does not name the pair-instability gap), suggesting an earlier merger; a possible AGN flare counterpart; and higher multipoles that moved the inferred distance from 4.1 to 5.3 Gpc. GW190814: 23 plus 2.6 solar masses, a mass-gap companion, 9:1 mass ratio whose higher harmonics sharpened the inclination and enabled a statistical H0 without a host. Catalogs GWTC-1 and GWTC-2, the rising detection rate driven by cubic volume scaling and lower thresholds, the trade-off between false positives and missed events, and the prospect of a five-detector network.

**Concepts:** GW190425, GW190521, GW190814, intermediate-mass black hole, lower mass gap, hierarchical black hole mergers, gravitational recoil, statistical standard-siren method, gravitational wave transient catalog

**Key moves**
- Higher multipoles have different angular patterns, so they break the distance-inclination degeneracy.
- Halving amplitude noise doubles range and multiplies searched volume by eight.
- A false-alarm threshold of two per year accepts about one spurious event in 39 in exchange for not discarding real ones.

## Concepts

### gravitational wave astronomy

*definition · core* · also: observational gravitational wave astronomy

The study of astrophysical and cosmological systems through the gravitational radiation they emit, using detectors that measure the tidal strain of passing waves (ground interferometers, space interferometers such as LISA, pulsar timing arrays).

**How introduced:** Opened with the Figure 12.1 strain traces of GW150914 as the moment the field became observational, then justified as a genuinely new window by comparing it point by point with electromagnetic astronomy.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational waves, interferometric detector

**Where:** SCH §12.1 p.360 (pdf 378); SCH §12.4 p.396 (pdf 414)

### complementarity of gravitational and electromagnetic observations

*principle · developed*

Gravitational waves are generated coherently by bulk motions of mass-energy, carry global source properties at low frequencies (nHz to kHz), penetrate matter and cannot be focused; electromagnetic waves come from microscopic charges, carry local physical information at high frequencies, are absorbed and scattered but can be imaged. Each reveals what the other hides.

**How introduced:** Laid out as a structured contrast (source of emission, frequency range, interaction with matter, focusing and localization, source requirements) followed by shared properties (speed c, transverse polarization, lensing).

**Prerequisites:** gravitational wave astronomy

**Notes:** Also used to explain why EM observations cannot reliably predict the gravitational-wave source population.

**Where:** SCH §12.1 p.360 (pdf 378); SCH §12.1 p.361 (pdf 379)

### multimessenger astronomy

*definition · developed* · also: multi-messenger astronomy

Coordinated observation of the same astrophysical event through different carriers (gravitational waves, photons across the spectrum, neutrinos, cosmic rays) so that each channel constrains what the others cannot.

**How introduced:** Motivated by the complementarity argument in the overview and realized spectacularly by GW170817 with its gamma-ray burst and kilonova; supernova searches with neutrino coincidence are a second example.

**Prerequisites:** complementarity of gravitational and electromagnetic observations

**Where:** SCH §12.1 p.361 (pdf 379); SCH §12.4 p.399 (pdf 417)

### gravitational lensing of gravitational waves

*phenomenon · mention*

Deflection and magnification of gravitational waves by intervening masses, exactly as for light; apart from lensing, a freely propagating gravitational wave's amplitude is not brightened or dimmed along its path.

**How introduced:** Listed briefly among the similarities between the two kinds of radiation.

**Prerequisites:** null geodesics

**Where:** SCH §12.1 p.361 (pdf 379)

### chirp mass

*physical-quantity · revisited* · also: script M

The mass combination (m1 m2)^(3/5)/(m1 + m2)^(1/5) that alone controls the leading-order (quadrupole, Newtonian-orbit) frequency evolution of an inspiralling binary; it is therefore the best-measured binary parameter.

**How introduced:** Revisited from Chapter 9 as the normalizing mass in the merger-frequency and time-to-merger scalings, then used to explain parameter-estimation hierarchies (chirp mass to better than 1 percent, other masses worse) and in Exercise 12.11.

**Prerequisites:** quadrupole formula, binary inspiral

$$
\mathcal M = (m_1 m_2)^{3/5}/(m_1+m_2)^{1/5}
$$

**Notes:** The text quotes 30.8 solar masses for GW150914 while Table 12.1 gives 28; the table lists redshift-corrected source-frame values, and the larger number is presumably closer to the detector-frame value (the book does not say which frame it used).

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.363 (pdf 381); SCH §12.3 p.383 (pdf 401); SCH p.413 (pdf 431)

### mass ratio of a binary

*convention · introduced* · also: epsilon = m2/m1

The ratio of the smaller to the larger component mass, taken here in the range 0 < m2/m1 <= 0.5 by the stated convention, which together with eccentricity and semi-major axis (in units of total mass) specifies a point-mass binary.

**How introduced:** Introduced to argue that binaries are the simplest and most reliably modelled radiating systems in astronomy because so few parameters are needed.

**Prerequisites:** binary inspiral

$$
\epsilon = m_2/m_1
$$

**Notes:** The stated upper bound 0.5 is odd: equal masses give 1, and the chapter later discusses near-equal-mass systems; the usual convention is 0 < q <= 1. The same symbol epsilon is reused for neutron-star asymmetry and in Exercise 12.7.

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.362 (pdf 380)

### merger frequency estimate

*technique · developed* · also: f_final

An order-of-magnitude estimate of the highest gravitational-wave frequency a point-mass inspiral reaches, obtained from the leading-order phase-to-merger relation by evaluating the frequency when half an orbit (orbital phase pi) remains: f_final is about 4.0 kHz times (one solar mass divided by the chirp mass).

**How introduced:** Motivated by the formal divergence of frequency for point particles; checked against GW150914 (130 Hz predicted, about 250 Hz observed) and binary neutron stars (a few kHz predicted, but tidal disruption near 1 kHz); used to derive the upper mass limit of ground-based detectors.

**Prerequisites:** chirp mass, orbital phase remaining before merger

$$
f_{\rm final}=4.0\,(1\,M_\odot/\mathcal M)\ \mathrm{kHz}
$$

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.363 (pdf 381)

### orbital phase remaining before merger

*physical-quantity · revisited* · also: psi

The orbital phase (in radians) a Newtonian inspiral still has to accumulate before the formal coalescence; at leading order psi = (1/32)(M omega)^(-5/3) with M the chirp mass and omega the orbital angular frequency.

**How introduced:** Taken from Eq. 9.159 and inverted to express frequency as a function of phase left.

**Prerequisites:** chirp mass

$$
\psi=\tfrac{1}{32}(\mathcal M\omega)^{-5/3}
$$

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.363 (pdf 381)

### time to merger

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: tau_merger, inspiral time

The remaining time until coalescence of a quasi-circular binary emitting at a given gravitational-wave frequency, at leading order proportional to chirp mass to the -5/3 power and frequency to the -8/3 power; 2.9 s for a one-solar-mass chirp mass at 100 Hz.

**How introduced:** Rescaled from Eq. 9.158 to quantify how long a LISA-band binary takes to reach the ground band and, in exercises, how long signals stay in band.

**Prerequisites:** chirp mass

$$
\tau=2.9\,(\mathcal M/M_\odot)^{-5/3}(f/100\,\mathrm{Hz})^{-8/3}\,\mathrm{s}
$$

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.364 (pdf 382); SCH p.407 (pdf 425)

### multiband gravitational wave astronomy

*technique · introduced* · also: LISA early warning

Observing the same binary at different evolutionary stages in different detector bands, e.g. a stellar-mass black-hole binary seen by LISA at tens of mHz that merges in the ground-based band months later, allowing advance prediction of merger time and sky position.

**How introduced:** Presented via Sesana's suggestion and a quick time-to-merger calculation for a 30 solar-mass chirp mass at 30 mHz giving nine to ten months.

**Prerequisites:** time to merger, LISA

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.363 (pdf 381); SCH §12.2 p.364 (pdf 382)

### confusion noise

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: confusion background, foreground from unresolved binaries

An effective noise created when so many overlapping signals share a frequency band that they cannot be individually resolved; for LISA the Galactic white-dwarf binaries below about 1 mHz exceed instrumental noise, and for pulsar timing unresolved supermassive black-hole binaries form a background.

**How introduced:** Mentioned while explaining which binaries are non-evolving in the low-frequency bands.

**Prerequisites:** stochastic gravitational wave background

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.362 (pdf 380); SCH §12.2 p.363 (pdf 381); SCH §12.2 p.370 (pdf 388)

### natal kick

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: supernova kick

The recoil velocity a neutron star or black hole acquires at birth from an asymmetric supernova explosion; it can unbind a binary or, if directed against the orbital motion, leave an eccentric orbit with a closer periastron.

**How introduced:** Part of the explanation of why compact binaries that merge within a Hubble time are rare: the system must survive two such kicks.

**Prerequisites:** binary star evolution

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.364 (pdf 382)

### common envelope evolution

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: common envelope

A binary evolution phase in which an expanding giant engulfs its compact companion so that gas drag shrinks the orbit; if drag is too strong the companion spirals into the core (forming a Thorne-Zytkow object) instead of leaving a close compact binary.

**How introduced:** Used to explain why many astronomers doubted before 2015 that binary black holes could form, and why low metallicity (smaller giants, weaker drag) offers a loophole.

**Prerequisites:** X-ray binary

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.364 (pdf 382); SCH §12.2 p.365 (pdf 383)

### binary black hole formation channels

*solution-or-model · developed*

Proposed routes to merging black-hole binaries: isolated evolution of low-metallicity massive binaries, dynamical capture in globular or nuclear clusters, secular evolution in hierarchical triples, and (speculatively) primordial black holes forming part of dark matter. Isolated binaries should have spins roughly aligned with the orbit; dynamical captures should have random spin orientations.

**How introduced:** Listed after the common-envelope problem, with spin alignment proposed as the observational discriminator; revisited when GW150914's high masses favoured low metallicity and GW190521's 85 solar-mass primary suggested hierarchical mergers.

**Prerequisites:** common envelope evolution, natal kick

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.365 (pdf 383); SCH §12.4 p.398 (pdf 416); SCH §12.4 p.404 (pdf 422)

### X-ray binary

*solution-or-model · introduced*

A close binary in which a neutron star or black hole accretes gas stripped from a normal or giant companion through a disk whose frictional heating makes it a bright X-ray source; evidence that systems can survive the first supernova.

**How introduced:** Invoked as astronomical evidence along the formation path of compact binaries; historical note on rocket discovery and Giacconi's Nobel Prize.

**Prerequisites:** accretion disk

**Notes:** Before 2015 the heaviest known stellar black holes, about 20 solar masses, were in X-ray binaries.

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.364 (pdf 382)

### short gamma-ray bursts from neutron star mergers

*phenomenon · developed* · also: short GRB

Brief bursts of gamma rays (the short-duration population) produced by narrow relativistic jets launched in binary neutron star (or neutron star-black hole) mergers, accompanied by a kilonova; confirmed by the GW170817/GRB170817A association.

**How introduced:** History from military satellites in the 1960s through the short/long classification and the 1989 merger proposal, leading to the GW170817 confirmation.

**Prerequisites:** kilonova

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.365 (pdf 383); SCH §12.4 p.401 (pdf 419)

### kilonova

*phenomenon · developed* · also: macronova, AT 2017gfo

An optical/infrared transient lasting days, powered by radioactive decay of heavy neutron-rich nuclei synthesized in material ejected during a neutron star merger.

**How introduced:** Predicted counterpart of short bursts; observed after GW170817 by six teams within about 11 hours, then followed until it faded.

**Prerequisites:** r-process nucleosynthesis in neutron star mergers

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.366 (pdf 384); SCH §12.4 p.400 (pdf 418); SCH §12.4 p.402 (pdf 420)

### ringdown

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: quasi-normal mode ringing, ringdown radiation

The exponentially damped oscillation with which a newly formed, distorted black hole sheds its deformations and settles to a Kerr black hole; its frequencies and damping times depend only on the final mass and spin, so they fingerprint a black hole.

**How introduced:** Estimated with omega about 0.5/M, giving 16 kHz per solar mass and about 230 Hz for the GW150914 remnant, close to the observed peak frequency; later cited as the direct test of a black-hole remnant not yet claimed by LIGO-Virgo.

**Prerequisites:** Kerr black hole, quasi-normal modes

$$
f_{\rm ringdown}\sim 16\,(1\,M_\odot/M)\,\mathrm{kHz}
$$

**Notes:** The estimate uses f = omega/2 pi, whereas the chirp formulas use f_gw = omega/pi with omega the orbital frequency; learners may confuse the two.

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.366 (pdf 384); SCH §12.4 p.397 (pdf 415)

### extreme mass-ratio inspiral

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: EMRI

The slow inspiral of a stellar-mass compact object (about 10-50 solar masses) into a supermassive black hole (about a million solar masses) after capture from the surrounding cluster, radiating in the LISA band for 1e5-1e6 orbits and mapping the strong-field spacetime of the large hole.

**How introduced:** Described as a capture-then-circularisation process, with the promise of precision strong-field tests and the theoretical challenge of building templates faithful over so many orbits.

**Prerequisites:** Kerr black hole, matched filtering

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.366 (pdf 384); SCH §12.2 p.367 (pdf 385); SCH §12.3 p.383 (pdf 401)

### continuous gravitational waves from spinning neutron stars

*phenomenon · developed* · also: continuous-wave sources, gravitational wave pulsars

Nearly monochromatic, long-lived gravitational radiation from a rotating neutron star that is not symmetric about its spin axis (crustal or core deformations, r-modes, accretion-driven asymmetries), at twice the spin frequency for a rigid deformation.

**How introduced:** Pulsars spin in the ground-based band; the amplitude is estimated from the rotating-quadrupole formula; discussion of search sensitivity, spin-down limits and non-detections.

**Prerequisites:** quadrupole formula, neutron star ellipticity

$$
h\sim 2\varepsilon\Omega^2 I_{\rm NS}/r
$$

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.367 (pdf 385); SCH §12.2 p.368 (pdf 386); SCH §12.3 p.390 (pdf 408)

### neutron star ellipticity

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: asymmetry parameter epsilon, fractional asymmetry

A dimensionless measure of how far a neutron star's mass distribution departs from axisymmetry about its rotation axis, roughly the fractional difference of equatorial moments of inertia; gravitational-wave strain is proportional to it.

**How introduced:** Enters the strain estimate with the illustrative value 1e-6; observational upper limits of about 1e-7 for some pulsars and a few times 1e-5 for the Crab show neutron stars are remarkably axisymmetric.

**Prerequisites:** moment of inertia tensor

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.367 (pdf 385); SCH §12.2 p.368 (pdf 386)

### spin-down limit

*technique · introduced*

The maximum gravitational-wave amplitude a pulsar could emit if all its observed rotational energy loss went into gravitational waves; measured upper limits below this bound constrain the fraction of spin-down that is gravitational, which scales as the square of the amplitude ratio.

**How introduced:** Explained for the Crab pulsar, whose amplitude limit is about 4 percent of the spin-down value.

**Prerequisites:** continuous gravitational waves from spinning neutron stars, gravitational wave energy flux

**Notes:** 4 percent in amplitude corresponds to about 0.16 percent of the energy, slightly above the text's 'less than 0.1%'.

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.368 (pdf 386)

### r-mode instability

*phenomenon · mention*

A gravitational-radiation-driven instability of rotational (Rossby-type) oscillation modes in rapidly spinning neutron stars, in which emission of gravitational waves makes the mode grow (a CFS-type instability).

**How introduced:** Named in a single sentence as a possible strong continuous-wave mechanism and, later, as a possible mechanism in accreting X-ray pulsars.

**Prerequisites:** continuous gravitational waves from spinning neutron stars

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.367 (pdf 385); SCH §12.3 p.392 (pdf 410)

### burst search

*technique · developed* · also: unmodelled burst search, excess-power search

A search for short gravitational-wave transients that makes minimal assumptions about waveform shape, requiring the signal to stand above the noise (or to match generic wavelets) and relying on coincidence, coherence or external triggers for confidence.

**How introduced:** Motivated by supernova core collapse, whose chaotic dynamics prevent waveform prediction; generalized to any unanticipated source; implemented with sine-Gaussian template banks.

**Prerequisites:** matched filtering, coincidence analysis

**Notes:** GW150914 was first flagged by an online burst search.

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.369 (pdf 387); SCH §12.3 p.395 (pdf 413)

### core-collapse supernova gravitational waves

*phenomenon · introduced*

Gravitational radiation from the non-spherical dynamics of a collapsing stellar core and proto-neutron star; together with neutrinos it is the only direct probe of the core, but predicted amplitudes are small and waveforms are stochastic, so only Galactic or nearby events are likely detectable.

**How introduced:** Presented as Weber's original goal; simulations suggest small amplitudes with possible spectral features; neutrino timing would anchor a search since the optical display appears hours later.

**Prerequisites:** burst search

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.368 (pdf 386); SCH §12.2 p.369 (pdf 387); SCH §12.3 p.395 (pdf 413)

### cosmic strings

*solution-or-model · mention*

Hypothetical one-dimensional topological defects formed in early-Universe phase transitions in some field theories; their oscillations, cusps and reconnections would emit gravitational-wave bursts with a characteristic spectral shape.

**How introduced:** Offered as a speculative burst source and a possible exotic dark component; searched for routinely with a spectral-shape filter.

**Prerequisites:** burst search

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.369 (pdf 387); SCH §12.3 p.395 (pdf 413)

### stochastic gravitational wave background

*phenomenon · developed* · also: stochastic background, astrophysical background, cosmological background

A random superposition of gravitational waves from very many unresolved sources arriving from all directions, statistically described by its mean square amplitude or spectral energy density rather than a waveform. The astrophysical background comes from recent populations such as binary black holes and white dwarfs; a cosmological background would be a relic of the very early Universe.

**How introduced:** Introduced by analogy with the cosmic microwave background, emphasising that the Universe has been transparent to gravitational waves since the beginning; Figure 12.2 shows the predicted binary black hole background against LIGO sensitivity; later its detection method is derived.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational wave energy flux, cosmic microwave background

**Notes:** Pulsar timing arrays reported evidence for a nHz background in 2023, after this edition was written.

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.369 (pdf 387); SCH §12.2 p.371 (pdf 389); SCH §12.3 p.392 (pdf 410)

### energy density parameter of gravitational waves

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: Omega_gw, Omega_GW

The energy density of a gravitational-wave background (usually per logarithmic frequency interval) divided by the critical energy density of the Universe; used because a random field has no characteristic amplitude but does have a mean energy density proportional to f^2 times the mean square strain.

**How introduced:** Justified from randomness (no single h) and homogeneity (same everywhere); quoted limits Omega_gw < 1.7e-7 from O1, about 1e-9 at LIGO design, and LISA reach near 1e-10.

**Prerequisites:** stochastic gravitational wave background

$$
\rho_{\rm gw}\propto f^2\langle h^2\rangle
$$

**Notes:** The book does not define Omega_gw per logarithmic frequency explicitly.

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.370 (pdf 388); SCH §12.2 p.371 (pdf 389)

### cross-correlation search

*technique · core* · also: correlation search for backgrounds

Detecting a stochastic background by multiplying the outputs of two detectors whose instrumental noises are independent but which see the same gravitational-wave field, and summing over time; the noise products average to zero while the common signal accumulates.

**How introduced:** Previewed in 12.2 with the fourth-root sensitivity scaling, then derived in 12.3 for two co-located aligned detectors with white noise.

**Prerequisites:** white noise, stochastic gravitational wave background, signal-to-noise ratio

$$
\mathrm{SNR}_{\rm amp}=N^{1/4}|h|/\sqrt{\sigma_1\sigma_2}
$$

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.370 (pdf 388); SCH §12.3 p.393 (pdf 411); SCH §12.3 p.394 (pdf 412)

### pulsar timing array

*experiment-or-observation · mention* · also: pulsar timing searches

A set of millisecond pulsars whose pulse arrival times are monitored for years; a nHz gravitational wave perturbs the arrival times, and correlations between pulsars reveal a background or individual supermassive black-hole binaries.

**How introduced:** Mentioned as the nHz band detector, expected to see the supermassive binary background first, and likened to a correlation search where each pulsar is a detector.

**Prerequisites:** cross-correlation search

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.363 (pdf 381); SCH §12.3 p.395 (pdf 413)

### polarization basis tensors

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: e_plus and e_cross, plus and cross basis tensors

Symmetric traceless tensors in the plane transverse to the propagation direction, built from an orthonormal sky-plane basis: e+ = eX eX - eY eY and ex = eX eY + eY eX; any TT wave's spatial metric perturbation is h+ e+ + hx ex.

**How introduced:** Constructed with outer products so that the wave can be described in a frame independent of any particular detector.

**Prerequisites:** outer product, transverse-traceless gauge

$$
\mathbf e_+=\hat e^R_x\otimes\hat e^R_x-\hat e^R_y\otimes\hat e^R_y
$$

$$
\mathbf e_\times=\hat e^R_x\otimes\hat e^R_y+\hat e^R_y\otimes\hat e^R_x
$$

**Notes:** Rotating the sky-plane basis by an angle psi mixes h+ and hx with angle 2 psi.

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.374 (pdf 392)

### detector tensor

*mathematical-object · core* · also: d

For an L-shaped interferometer with unit arm vectors e_x and e_y, the tensor d = L(e_x e_x - e_y e_y), whose full contraction with the wave tensor gives the difference in arm-length changes.

**How introduced:** Built in parallel with the wave's basis tensors so that the response becomes a single coordinate-free contraction; tested for special directions in Exercise 12.3.

**Prerequisites:** polarization basis tensors, tensor contraction

$$
\mathbf d=L(\hat e_x\otimes\hat e_x-\hat e_y\otimes\hat e_y)
$$

$$
\Delta_{\rm int}=d^{jk}h_{jk}
$$

**Notes:** As printed, d contains L and no factor 1/2, so d:h equals twice the physical arm-length difference (each arm changes by h L/2); conventional definitions include the 1/2. The dimensionless response is d:h/L in the book's notation.

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.374 (pdf 392); SCH §12.3 p.375 (pdf 393)

### antenna pattern functions

*mathematical-object · core* · also: F_plus, F_cross, beam pattern functions

The geometric coefficients F+ = d:e+/L and Fx = d:ex/L giving the detector strain delta = F+ h+ + Fx hx; they depend on the source direction relative to the detector's zenith and arms and on the chosen orientation of the sky-plane basis.

**How introduced:** Obtained by splitting the double contraction into polarization parts; emphasised that a single source has different angles, hence different F values, at each detector.

**Prerequisites:** detector tensor, polarization basis tensors

$$
\delta_{\rm int}=F_+(\theta,\phi)h_++F_\times(\theta,\phi)h_\times
$$

**Notes:** The book says F depends only on theta and phi; it also depends on the polarization angle of the sky basis, as the text itself uses when rotating that basis. Standard results: F+ = (1/2)(1+cos^2 theta)cos 2phi cos 2psi - cos theta sin 2phi sin 2psi, Fx = (1/2)(1+cos^2 theta)cos 2phi sin 2psi + cos theta sin 2phi cos 2psi.

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.375 (pdf 393); SCH §12.3 p.384 (pdf 402)

### antenna power pattern

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: sky sensitivity pattern, network response pattern

The polarization-averaged directional sensitivity F+^2 + Fx^2 of a detector, largest for waves arriving from the zenith or nadir and zero for waves arriving in the detector plane along a bisector of the arms (in the plane along an arm it is a quarter of the zenith value); for a network of identical detectors the patterns add. With the conventional detector-tensor normalization the zenith value is 1; the book's literal Eq. 12.8 would make it 4, and Figure 12.4 is drawn normalized.

**How introduced:** Plotted as 3D surfaces for a single interferometer and for the two LIGOs plus Virgo in Figure 12.4, with the caution that long signals see a rotating pattern.

**Prerequisites:** antenna pattern functions

$$
P(\theta,\phi)=F_+^2+F_\times^2
$$

**Notes:** GW170817 arrived near a null of Virgo's pattern, which itself helped localization.

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.375 (pdf 393); SCH §12.3 p.376 (pdf 394)

### amplitude spectral density of detector noise

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: [S_h(f)]^(1/2), strain noise curve, sensitivity curve

The square root of the noise power spectral density of the strain output, in units of 1/sqrt(Hz), giving the r.m.s. noise amplitude per unit bandwidth as a function of frequency; comparing it with signal spectra shows which sources a detector can see.

**How introduced:** Presented through Figure 12.5's LIGO and Virgo curves for successive observing runs, with the caption explaining why the square root is plotted.

**Prerequisites:** Fourier transform, Gaussian detector noise

$$
[S_h(f)]^{1/2}
$$

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.375 (pdf 393); SCH §12.3 p.377 (pdf 395)

### Gaussian detector noise

*definition · core* · also: stationary Gaussian noise

Noise whose value in each frequency bin (or sample) is drawn from a zero-mean normal distribution with a time-independent variance; expected when many independent noise mechanisms add, by the central limit theorem.

**How introduced:** Justified by the central limit theorem applied to thermal vibrations of atoms, photon arrivals and seismic jostling; contrasted with glitches.

**Prerequisites:** central limit theorem

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.375 (pdf 393); SCH §12.3 p.376 (pdf 394)

### shot noise

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: photon shot noise

Noise from the random (Poisson) arrival of photons at the photodetector; its relative size falls as the inverse square root of the number of photons, so it dominates interferometer sensitivity at high frequencies and is reduced by more laser power, at the cost of radiation-pressure and heating noise.

**How introduced:** Explained heuristically: higher frequencies need shorter sampling intervals with fewer photons each, giving a rise with frequency; then used in design trade-offs.

**Prerequisites:** Gaussian detector noise

$$
\text{noise}\propto N_\gamma^{-1/2}\propto f^{1/2}
$$

**Notes:** In real Fabry-Perot interferometers the high-frequency shot-noise rise in strain spectral density is roughly proportional to f, set by the arm-cavity response, not the f^(1/2) of this sampling heuristic.

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.376 (pdf 394); SCH §12.3 p.377 (pdf 395)

### thermal noise

*phenomenon · mention*

Brownian motion of mirror surfaces, coatings and suspension fibres driven by their temperature; it limits sensitivity in the most sensitive mid band (tens to hundreds of Hz) and produces narrow resonance lines from suspension modes.

**How introduced:** Named as the mid-band floor in Figure 12.5; motivates cryogenic mirrors in KAGRA.

**Prerequisites:** Gaussian detector noise

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.377 (pdf 395)

### seismic noise

*phenomenon · mention*

Ground vibrations transmitted to the test masses through their suspensions, increasingly hard to isolate at low frequencies and responsible for the steep low-frequency wall of ground-based sensitivity curves.

**How introduced:** Named as the reason noise rises below a few tens of Hz.

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.377 (pdf 395)

### Newtonian noise

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: gravity-gradient noise

Fluctuating tidal forces on the test masses from time-varying local mass distributions (passing seismic waves, atmospheric changes, moving people or vehicles); because it couples exactly like a gravitational wave it cannot be shielded, only reduced by going underground or into space.

**How introduced:** Presented as the limit for third-generation ground detectors and the principal reason LISA must fly: below 1 Hz the Earth is gravitationally too noisy.

**Prerequisites:** tidal acceleration, seismic noise

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.378 (pdf 396)

### glitches

*phenomenon · developed* · also: non-Gaussian transient noise, non-stationary noise

Short, loud, non-Gaussian noise transients of varied morphology, arising from malfunctioning electronics and control systems, which come and go in time and can mimic astrophysical bursts; they dominate the background of short-duration searches.

**How introduced:** Contrasted with stationary Gaussian noise; later drives the need for coincidence, time slides and null-stream vetoes; a real glitch overlapped GW170817 in Livingston.

**Prerequisites:** Gaussian detector noise

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.378 (pdf 396); SCH §12.3 p.387 (pdf 405); SCH §12.4 p.400 (pdf 418)

### matched filtering

*technique · core* · also: optimal linear filtering, template correlation

Correlating detector data with a predicted waveform (template) at all time offsets; for known signal shape in Gaussian noise this linear statistic maximizes signal-to-noise ratio, peaks at the arrival time with height proportional to amplitude times the template's squared norm, and allows detection of signals far below the raw noise level.

**How introduced:** Motivated by GW170817 being invisible in raw data yet highly significant; derived in a toy model of sampled white Gaussian noise plus a scaled, shifted template, compared with naive threshold crossing; historical origin in radar and in Kafka's 1977 bar-detector proposal.

**Prerequisites:** Gaussian detector noise, white noise, convolution

$$
(x*w)_k=\sum_j x_j w_{j-k}
$$

$$
A_{\min}=\sigma/\|w\|
$$

**Notes:** With coloured noise the correlation must be weighted by the inverse noise spectrum, which the book only mentions (frequency domain, Exercise 12.4).

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.378 (pdf 396); SCH §12.3 p.379 (pdf 397); SCH §12.3 p.380 (pdf 398); SCH §12.3 p.381 (pdf 399)

### template bank

*technique · developed* · also: bank of templates

A discrete set of model waveforms spanning the source parameter space (masses, spins) densely enough that any real signal matches some template closely; the template with the largest filter output estimates the parameters.

**How introduced:** Needed because binary masses range widely; templates use post-Newtonian inspirals joined to numerical-relativity mergers; computing cost handled with FFTs.

**Prerequisites:** matched filtering, post-Newtonian approximation

**Notes:** Current banks assume quasi-circular orbits because inspiral radiation circularizes most binaries; the book notes that searches for eccentric systems (e.g. from triples) are planned.

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.382 (pdf 400)

### white noise

*definition · introduced*

Noise with no statistical correlation between different times, so that the covariance of samples is sigma^2 times a Kronecker delta and the power spectrum is flat.

**How introduced:** Adopted as a simplifying assumption to expose the power of matched filtering, immediately flagged as untrue for real detectors (Figure 12.5).

**Prerequisites:** Gaussian detector noise

$$
\langle n_j n_{j'}\rangle=\sigma^2\delta_{jj'}
$$

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.380 (pdf 398); SCH §12.3 p.393 (pdf 411)

### signal-to-noise ratio

*physical-quantity · core* · also: SNR

The expected value of a detection statistic in the presence of a signal divided by its standard deviation in noise alone; thresholds on it set detection confidence, and for a network the matched-filter SNRs add in quadrature.

**How introduced:** Defined implicitly through filter mean and variance; applied to matched filtering, correlation searches and Table 12.1's network SNR column.

**Prerequisites:** Gaussian detector noise

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.381 (pdf 399); SCH §12.3 p.394 (pdf 412); SCH §12.4 p.398 (pdf 416)

### square-root-of-cycles gain of matched filtering

*principle · core* · also: sqrt(N) gain

Coherently integrating a known oscillating signal lowers the minimum detectable amplitude, relative to its peak value, by roughly the square root of the number of signal cycles; the same random-walk scaling appears in repeated measurements and continuous-wave searches (sensitivity improves as the square root of observation time).

**How introduced:** Derived for a pure sinusoid (squared norm B^2 N/2), then reinterpreted as cycles rather than samples via a resampling argument; applied to explain why long neutron-star chirps and pulsar signals benefit most.

**Prerequisites:** matched filtering

$$
A_{\min}=\sigma/[w_{\max}(N/2)^{1/2}]
$$

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.367 (pdf 385); SCH §12.3 p.381 (pdf 399); SCH §12.3 p.382 (pdf 400)

### detection range and volume

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: horizon distance, BNS range

Because strain falls off as the reciprocal of source distance, the farthest detectable source lies at a distance that scales as one over the smallest amplitude a search can pick out, and the surveyed volume (hence the expected rate for uniformly spread sources) grows as the cube of that distance.

**How introduced:** Used to translate the matched-filter gain into N^(3/2) more detections, to explain Figure 12.5's binary-neutron-star range legends, and the rapid growth of catalog sizes.

**Prerequisites:** signal-to-noise ratio

$$
V\propto R^3\propto h_{\min}^{-3}
$$

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.381 (pdf 399); SCH §12.4 p.406 (pdf 424)

### intrinsic and extrinsic parameters

*definition · developed*

Intrinsic parameters (component masses, spins) describe the source itself and shape the waveform, so a single detector can estimate them; extrinsic parameters (sky position, distance, inclination, polarization angle, arrival time) describe its relation to the observer and require a network.

**How introduced:** Introduced when moving from detection to measurement; ordered by accuracy (chirp mass best, others worse by five or more).

**Prerequisites:** template bank

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.383 (pdf 401)

### Bayesian parameter estimation

*technique · introduced* · also: posterior probability distribution

Combining a likelihood of the data given model parameters with prior probabilities (astrophysical and instrumental) to obtain a posterior distribution, which yields best estimates and credible intervals for each parameter.

**How introduced:** Mentioned as the practical method behind parameter values and uncertainties; its output is shown in Figure 12.6 for H0; contrasted with the frequentist significance discussion.

**Prerequisites:** matched filtering

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.383 (pdf 401); SCH §12.3 p.387 (pdf 405); SCH §12.4 p.402 (pdf 420)

### polarization reconstruction with two detectors

*technique · core* · also: two-detector inversion

Given a known source direction and a common wave-phase time, the responses of two non-aligned detectors form a linear 2x2 system in h+ and hx, which is inverted using the determinant of the antenna-factor matrix.

**How introduced:** Worked out explicitly for a sky position known from a gamma-ray burst, after shifting each detector's time to a common reference.

**Prerequisites:** antenna pattern functions, matrix inverse

$$
h_+=(F_{\times,2}\delta_1-F_{\times,1}\delta_2)/\det M_{12}
$$

**Notes:** Fails when the two detectors are nearly aligned (determinant near zero), as for the two LIGO sites.

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.384 (pdf 402); SCH §12.3 p.385 (pdf 403)

### inclination from polarization ratio

*technique · developed*

For a circular binary, the plus and cross amplitudes in the basis aligned with the projected orbit are in quadrature with ratio (1 + cos^2 iota)/(2 cos iota); rotating the sky basis until the reconstructed polarizations are 90 degrees out of phase and measuring their amplitude ratio gives the inclination iota.

**How introduced:** Built from the edge-on special case (linear polarization, one component vanishes for the right rotation), then generalized to elliptical polarization using Eq. 9.100.

**Prerequisites:** polarization reconstruction with two detectors, binary polarization versus inclination

$$
|h_+|/|h_\times|=(1+\cos^2\iota)/(2\cos\iota)
$$

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.385 (pdf 403); SCH p.410 (pdf 428)

### distance-inclination degeneracy

*phenomenon · developed*

For nearly face-on binaries the amplitude changes only at second order in inclination, so a signal can be fit almost equally well by a closer, more inclined source or a farther face-on one, inflating distance errors; since face-on systems are loudest, they dominate detected samples.

**How introduced:** Follows from the flatness of the amplitude ratio near iota = 0 (quantified in Exercise 12.7: a 1 percent ratio error allows iota up to 30 degrees); returns as the main error in the GW170817 H0 posterior; broken by higher harmonics in GW190521 and GW190814.

**Prerequisites:** inclination from polarization ratio, standard siren

$$
\epsilon=r-1\sim\iota^4/8
$$

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.385 (pdf 403); SCH §12.4 p.401 (pdf 419); SCH §12.4 p.405 (pdf 423)

### higher harmonics in binary waveforms

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: higher multipoles, higher modes

Gravitational radiation from a binary beyond the leading quadrupole at twice the orbital frequency, arising from post-Newtonian corrections and mass asymmetry; they have different frequencies and angular dependences, so detecting them improves inclination, polarization and distance estimates.

**How introduced:** Mentioned as the cure for the distance-inclination degeneracy, then shown in action in GW190521 (distance revised from 4.1 to 5.3 Gpc) and GW190814 (large mass ratio).

**Prerequisites:** quadrupole formula, post-Newtonian approximation

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.386 (pdf 404); SCH §12.4 p.405 (pdf 423)

### false alarm probability

*physical-quantity · core* · also: Gaussian tail probability

The probability that noise alone exceeds a detection threshold; for zero-mean Gaussian noise and a threshold of a standard deviations it is 2 times the integral of the unit normal density from a to infinity, bounded above by (twice) phi(a)/a, which becomes accurate for large a.

**How introduced:** Introduced to answer 'what is a detection?', converted into expected false-alarm counts using realistic sample numbers.

**Prerequisites:** Gaussian detector noise

$$
p(|n|>a\sigma)=2\int_a^\infty\phi(z)\,dz
$$

**Notes:** The printed bounds in Eq. 12.29 omit the factor 2 of Eq. 12.27, although the quoted numbers include it.

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.386 (pdf 404); SCH §12.3 p.387 (pdf 405)

### trials factor

*principle · developed* · also: look-elsewhere effect

When a statistic is evaluated many independent times (samples, templates, sky positions), the chance that at least one noise value exceeds a threshold grows with the number of trials, so thresholds must be raised accordingly.

**How introduced:** Shown numerically: 5 sigma is common in a year of data; 6.5 is needed per filter sample; 8 is needed with 10,000 templates.

**Prerequisites:** false alarm probability

**Notes:** The book does not use the term 'trials factor' but the argument is exactly this.

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.386 (pdf 404); SCH §12.3 p.387 (pdf 405)

### coincidence analysis

*technique · developed* · also: coincidence mode

Accepting a candidate only when triggers occur in two or more detectors within a time window set by the light-travel time between sites (plus a margin), which suppresses uncorrelated glitches.

**How introduced:** Justified by the difficulty of setting safe single-detector thresholds against glitches; the reason LIGO was built as two detectors.

**Prerequisites:** glitches

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.387 (pdf 405)

### time slides

*technique · developed* · also: time-shifted background estimation

Estimating the false-alarm background by artificially shifting one detector's data relative to another by more than the physical coincidence window and repeating the search many times; any coincidences found are necessarily accidental, measuring the combined glitch and Gaussian background rate.

**How introduced:** Presented as how LIGO-Virgo determines residual coincident-glitch rates and thus the false-alarm rates in Table 12.1.

**Prerequisites:** coincidence analysis

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.387 (pdf 405)

### false alarm rate

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: FAR

The expected rate (per year) at which noise, including glitches, would produce a candidate at least as strong as a given event; small values indicate high significance, and catalogs accept events below a chosen FAR threshold.

**How introduced:** Given in Table 12.1 and used to discuss GWTC-2's threshold of two per year and the trade-off between purity and completeness.

**Prerequisites:** time slides, false alarm probability

**Where:** SCH §12.4 p.398 (pdf 416); SCH §12.4 p.406 (pdf 424)

### likelihood ratio

*technique · mention* · also: Bayes factor

The ratio of the probability of the data under a signal-plus-noise hypothesis to that under noise alone (or between two signal models); thresholding it gives the optimal Neyman-Pearson detection statistic, and Bayes factors compare models such as tensor versus scalar polarization.

**How introduced:** Mentioned as the deeper Bayesian alternative to the frequentist treatment; Bayes factors of 1000 and 200 quoted for GW170814's polarization test.

**Prerequisites:** Bayesian parameter estimation

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.387 (pdf 405); SCH §12.3 p.388 (pdf 406); SCH §12.4 p.399 (pdf 417)

### coherent network analysis

*technique · developed* · also: coherent analysis

Combining the data streams of all detectors, with appropriate time shifts and antenna factors for a hypothesized direction, before applying any threshold, so that the consistency of amplitudes and phases expected from a single two-polarization wave is exploited.

**How introduced:** Contrasted with coincidence, which throws away amplitude-consistency information; attributed to Gursel and Tinto, Bose et al., and Finn, with the aperture-synthesis analogy.

**Prerequisites:** antenna pattern functions, coincidence analysis

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.388 (pdf 406); SCH §12.3 p.390 (pdf 408)

### null stream

*technique · core* · also: null combination, N_123

A linear combination of the (time-aligned) outputs of three or more detectors, weighted by antenna-factor determinants for an assumed source direction, in which any gravitational-wave signal from that direction cancels identically, leaving only instrumental noise and glitches; a network of N detectors has N - 2 null streams.

**How introduced:** Predicted by counting (two polarization functions versus three data streams), derived by eliminating h+ and hx (Exercise 12.8), then applied to glitch vetoes, glitch subtraction, source localization and calibration checks; LISA's Sagnac combination is an example.

**Prerequisites:** polarization reconstruction with two detectors, coherent network analysis

$$
N_{123}=D_{12}x_3+D_{23}x_1+D_{31}x_2
$$

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.388 (pdf 406); SCH §12.3 p.389 (pdf 407); SCH §12.3 p.390 (pdf 408)

### detector calibration

*technique · introduced* · also: calibration

Establishing, across all frequencies, the conversion between a detector's raw output and true gravitational strain in amplitude and phase; errors bias amplitudes and hence distances, and inconsistent calibrations spoil null streams.

**How introduced:** Presented as one of the hardest operational problems, with the idea of calibrating one detector precisely and transferring calibration via null streams and well-modelled binary waveforms.

**Prerequisites:** null stream

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.390 (pdf 408)

### Doppler modulation of continuous-wave signals

*phenomenon · developed*

Periodic frequency and phase modulation of a monochromatic gravitational wave caused by the detector's accelerated motion (Earth's spin and orbit), depending on source direction; it smears the signal over many frequency bins once the observation exceeds a critical duration.

**How introduced:** Critical time estimated by equating the accumulated Doppler drift f Omega^2 R T / c to the frequency resolution 2/T, giving about 70 minutes at 1 kHz for Earth's rotation.

**Prerequisites:** Doppler effect, continuous gravitational waves from spinning neutron stars

$$
T_{\max}=(2c/\Omega^2 fR)^{1/2}
$$

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.390 (pdf 408)

### barycentric demodulation

*technique · introduced* · also: resampling to the solar system barycenter

Undoing the direction-dependent Doppler modulation by re-sampling the detector's time series onto a clock that would tick uniformly for an unaccelerated observer at the Solar System's centre of mass, using the light-travel delay n.d/c for the trial sky direction.

**How introduced:** Described in words for the optimal search and quantified in Exercise 12.9, which also treats spin-down by an analogous time remapping.

**Prerequisites:** Doppler modulation of continuous-wave signals

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.391 (pdf 409); SCH p.411 (pdf 429)

### angular resolution by orbital aperture synthesis

*principle · introduced*

A long observation of a continuous signal lets the detector's motion across Earth's orbit act like a telescope of diameter 2R, giving a diffraction-limited angular resolution of order lambda/2R (about 2e-6 rad at 1 kHz for half an orbit), just as pulsar timing localizes radio pulsars.

**How introduced:** Deduced from the direction dependence of the Doppler pattern; turned into a count of about 3e12 sky patches that each need their own search.

**Prerequisites:** Doppler modulation of continuous-wave signals, diffraction limit

$$
\Delta\theta\simeq\lambda/2R
$$

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.391 (pdf 409)

### computational cost of all-sky continuous-wave searches

*principle · developed* · also: suboptimal searches

Because each resolvable sky patch and each distinguishable spin-down rate needs a separate demodulation and Fourier transform, an optimal coherent all-sky search over months of data requires vastly more computation than exists, forcing semi-coherent, suboptimal strategies.

**How introduced:** Built from sky-patch counts plus spin-down bins and estimated in Exercise 12.9 in floating-point operations and supercomputer-equivalents; contrasted with targeted searches of known pulsars.

**Prerequisites:** angular resolution by orbital aperture synthesis, spin-down limit

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.391 (pdf 409); SCH §12.3 p.392 (pdf 410); SCH p.413 (pdf 431)

### Sagnac null stream

*technique · mention* · also: Sagnac mode

For LISA's triangular constellation, a combination of the three interferometer outputs that is insensitive to gravitational waves at low frequencies, providing an in-situ measurement of instrumental noise against which an excess due to a stochastic background can be judged.

**How introduced:** Explains how a single space mission, which cannot cross-correlate independent detectors because its interferometers share arms, can still search for a background.

**Prerequisites:** null stream, LISA

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.395 (pdf 413)

### sine-Gaussian burst templates

*technique · introduced* · also: sine-Gaussian wavelets

Generic filters formed by a sinusoid multiplied by a Gaussian envelope a few cycles wide; banks spanning central frequency and width can partially match many unmodelled short transients.

**How introduced:** Proposed as a minimal-assumption compromise between pure excess-power searches and full matched filtering; GW150914's late-time waveform resembles one.

**Prerequisites:** burst search, template bank

$$
w(t)=\sin(2\pi f_0 t)\,e^{-t^2/2\tau^2}
$$

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.395 (pdf 413); SCH §12.3 p.396 (pdf 414)

### GW150914

*experiment-or-observation · core* · also: first direct detection

The first directly detected gravitational-wave signal, from a binary black hole merger (about 36 and 29 solar masses in the source frame, final mass 62, luminosity distance about 410 Mpc), recorded by both LIGO detectors in September 2015 and announced in February 2016.

**How introduced:** Used as the chapter's opening image, as a check on the merger-frequency and ringdown estimates, as an example visible without matched filtering, and told as a discovery story in 12.4. **Prerequisites:** binary inspiral, matched filtering

### event naming convention

*convention · mention* · also: GWyymmdd

Gravitational-wave events are named GW followed by the two-digit year, month and day of detection in Universal Time; later catalogs append the UTC time of day to distinguish multiple events on one date.

**How introduced:** Explained in a footnote at the first use of GW150914.

### graviton mass bound from dispersion

*technique · introduced*

A nonzero graviton mass would make gravitational-wave speed depend on frequency, so different parts of a chirp would arrive with accumulated relative delays over the propagation distance; absence of such distortion bounds the mass (1.2e-22 eV from GW150914).

**How introduced:** Presented as one of the waveform-consistency tests of general relativity, with the caveat that static-field bounds are stronger.

**Prerequisites:** GW150914

**Notes:** The text says shorter wavelengths travel faster; for a massive graviton the group velocity is closer to c at higher frequency, consistent with that statement.

**Where:** SCH §12.4 p.397 (pdf 415)

### polarization test of general relativity

*experiment-or-observation · introduced*

Checking whether the responses of three or more differently oriented detectors are consistent with only the two tensor polarizations of general relativity, as opposed to scalar (breathing) or vector modes; GW170814 favoured pure tensor over pure scalar and pure vector with Bayes factors of about 1000 and 200.

**How introduced:** Enabled by the first three-detector event; the book stresses that realistic alternatives add modes rather than replace tensor ones, which the data could not yet test.

**Prerequisites:** antenna pattern functions, likelihood ratio

**Where:** SCH §12.4 p.399 (pdf 417)

### GW170817

*experiment-or-observation · core* · also: GRB170817A, AT 2017gfo, first binary neutron star merger

The first detected binary neutron star merger (component masses about 1.44 and 1.28 solar masses, chirp mass 1.186, distance about 40 Mpc), accompanied 1.7 s later by a short gamma-ray burst and followed by a kilonova in NGC 4993; the birth of gravitational-wave multimessenger astronomy.

**How introduced:** Introduced early as a weak-but-significant matched-filter example and a glitch-plus-signal case; told in detail in 12.4 including localization, H0, tidal limits and r-process results.

**Prerequisites:** matched filtering, kilonova, standard siren

**Notes:** Text gives 31 square degrees for the initial localization; Table 12.1 lists 16 from the final analysis.

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.378 (pdf 396); SCH §12.4 p.399 (pdf 417); SCH §12.4 p.400 (pdf 418); SCH §12.4 p.401 (pdf 419); SCH §12.4 p.403 (pdf 421)

### standard siren

*technique · revisited* · also: gravitational-wave standard siren

Using a compact binary's chirp, whose frequency evolution fixes its intrinsic luminosity, to infer the luminosity distance directly from the observed amplitude, once the inclination (and sky position) are known; no cosmic distance ladder is required.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Section 9.6 and used for GW170817's 40 plus or minus 8 Mpc, with inclination identified as the dominant uncertainty.

**Prerequisites:** chirp mass, inclination from polarization ratio

**Where:** SCH §12.3 p.384 (pdf 402); SCH §12.4 p.400 (pdf 418); SCH §12.4 p.401 (pdf 419)

### Hubble-Lemaitre constant from gravitational waves

*technique · developed* · also: H0 from standard sirens, statistical standard-siren method

Dividing the Hubble-flow recession velocity of a host galaxy (from its redshift, corrected for peculiar velocity) by the standard-siren luminosity distance gives H0; without an identified host, the redshifts of all galaxies in the localization volume can be combined statistically.

**How introduced:** GW170817's posterior (Figure 12.6) lies between Planck and SH0ES values but is broad because of inclination; radio jet constraints later gave 70.3 plus or minus 5; GW190814 illustrated the host-less statistical method (Schutz 1986).

**Prerequisites:** standard siren, Hubble law

$$
H_0=v_{\rm rec}/D_L
$$

**Where:** SCH §12.4 p.401 (pdf 419); SCH §12.4 p.402 (pdf 420); SCH §12.4 p.405 (pdf 423)

### relativistic jet and viewing angle

*solution-or-model · introduced*

A narrow (roughly 5-20 degree) ultra-relativistic outflow launched along the merger's angular momentum axis powers short gamma-ray bursts; an observer slightly off-axis sees a weaker burst, and since the jet axis coincides with the orbital axis, jet-angle measurements constrain the gravitational-wave inclination.

**How introduced:** Raised by GRB170817A being ordinary in brightness though very near; radio imaging later placed the viewing angle at 14-28 degrees.

**Prerequisites:** short gamma-ray bursts from neutron star mergers

**Where:** SCH §12.4 p.401 (pdf 419); SCH §12.4 p.402 (pdf 420)

### tidal deformability

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: Love number

The response coefficient relating a star's induced quadrupole moment to an applied external tidal field; it depends on the nuclear equation of state and speeds up the final inspiral of neutron stars, leaving a phase imprint in the last orbits.

**How introduced:** Introduced as a way to learn nuclear physics from GW170817; only upper limits so far, excluding some very stiff equations of state.

**Prerequisites:** tidal acceleration, neutron star equation of state

**Where:** SCH §12.4 p.401 (pdf 419)

### r-process nucleosynthesis in neutron star mergers

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: r-process elements

Production of heavy neutron-rich nuclei (gold, platinum, thorium, uranium and others) in neutron-rich ejecta from compact mergers; spectra of the GW170817 kilonova support mergers as a major site.

**How introduced:** Told with a vivid picture of neutron-star matter breaking into ever smaller neutron-rich chunks, historical credit to Lattimer and Schramm and Eichler et al., and a connection from uranium and thorium heating to plate tectonics and Earth's magnetic field.

**Prerequisites:** kilonova

**Notes:** The book attributes heavy-element formation to fission; the standard picture is rapid neutron capture onto seed nuclei (with fission recycling only for the heaviest nuclei).

**Where:** SCH §12.4 p.402 (pdf 420); SCH §12.4 p.403 (pdf 421)

### intermediate-mass black hole

### hierarchical black hole mergers

*solution-or-model · mention*

Formation of a heavy black hole by the merger of lighter black holes, followed by a later merger of the product with another object, e.g. in dense clusters or triples; proposed to explain black holes too massive to form directly from stellar collapse.

### lower mass gap

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: mass gap between neutron stars and black holes

The apparent scarcity, in pre-LIGO electromagnetic observations, of compact objects between about 2.2 solar masses (heaviest known pulsars) and about 6 solar masses (lightest X-ray-binary black holes).

**How introduced:** Introduced to explain why GW190814's 2.6 solar-mass companion, and perhaps GW190425's primary, are surprising.

### gravitational recoil

*phenomenon · mention* · also: merger kick

The linear momentum given to a merger remnant by asymmetric emission of gravitational waves in the final orbits and merger, potentially hundreds of km/s or more; in a gaseous environment the moving remnant might produce an electromagnetic flare.

**How introduced:** Offered as the mechanism behind the proposed AGN-disk flare associated with GW190521, with a predicted repeat flare as a test.

Because cosmological redshift stretches all timescales, a binary at redshift z is observed with masses (1+z) times its source-frame masses; catalogs quote source-frame masses and luminosity distances.

### open gravitational wave data

*experiment-or-observation · mention* · also: GW Open Science Center, citizen science in gravitational wave analysis

Public release of calibrated strain data and event audio, enabling independent analyses (which have promoted marginal events to detections) and volunteer projects such as Einstein@Home for pulsar searches and Gravity Spy for glitch classification.

### merger remnant outcomes

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: post-merger evolution

What is left after compact objects touch depends on the pair: two neutron stars form a very massive star that probably collapses to a black hole unless the nuclear equation of state can support it; a neutron star plus black hole may briefly keep matter in a disk before becoming a nearly vacuum black hole; two black holes form a distorted black hole that rings down to Kerr, most likely without electromagnetic emission.

**How introduced:** Stated as the reason a merger signal does not end at first contact, leading straight into the ringdown estimate and its GW150914 check.

**Prerequisites:** Kerr black hole, neutron star equation of state

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.366 (pdf 384)

### targeted continuous-wave search

*technique · introduced* · also: known-pulsar search

A search for gravitational waves from a neutron star whose sky position (and usually spin frequency) is known from electromagnetic timing, so demodulation is fixed and only spin-down parameters (for young pulsars even the second frequency derivative) must be scanned; accreting X-ray pulsars such as Sco X-1 add poorly known binary-orbit parameters.

**How introduced:** Contrasted with blind all-sky searches as far cheaper and somewhat more sensitive; the book adds that because a Doppler-modulated signal is unlike any terrestrial artefact, such searches usually run on the single most sensitive detector and confirm candidates in the others.

**Prerequisites:** barycentric demodulation, spin-down limit

**Notes:** No detections by mid-2021; Crab limit about 4 percent of its spin-down value.

**Where:** SCH §12.2 p.368 (pdf 386); SCH §12.3 p.392 (pdf 410)

\tau_{\rm merger}=2.9\left(\frac{\mathcal M}{1\,M_\odot}\right)^{-5/3}\left(\frac{f_{\rm gw}}{100\,\mathrm{Hz}}\right)^{-8/3}\ \mathrm{s}
$$

Remaining inspiral time from a given gravitational-wave frequency; about 9-10 months from 30 mHz for a 30 solar-mass chirp mass, 2.5 minutes from 20 Hz for two 1.4 solar-mass neutron stars.

**Symbols:** M = chirp mass; f_gw = gravitational-wave frequency

### (12.4) Ringdown frequency estimate · supporting · SCH §12.2 p.366 (pdf 384)

$$
f_{\rm ringdown}\sim 16\left(\frac{1\,M_\odot}{M}\right)\ \mathrm{kHz}
$$

Characteristic quasi-normal ringing frequency of a black hole of mass M, from omega about 0.5/M; about 230 Hz for a 68 solar-mass detector-frame remnant.

**Symbols:** M = remnant mass; here f = omega/(2 pi)

### (12.5) Continuous-wave strain from a spinning neutron star · central · SCH §12.2 p.367 (pdf 385)

$$
h\sim 2\varepsilon\,\Omega^2 I_{\rm NS}/r
$$

Order-of-magnitude strain along the spin axis from a star with fractional non-axisymmetry epsilon; about 1e-26 for I = 1e38 kg m^2, f = 60 Hz, r = 1 kpc, epsilon = 1e-6 (after restoring G/c^4).

**Symbols:** epsilon = asymmetry; Omega = 2 pi f spin angular frequency; I_NS = moment of inertia; r = distance; geometrized units

### Continuous-wave sensitivity versus observation time · supporting · SCH §12.2 p.367 (pdf 385)

$$
h_{\min}\propto T^{-1/2}
$$

Coherent integration of a monochromatic signal lowers the detectable amplitude as the inverse square root of observing time; reaching 1e-26 from a 1e-22 broadband level needs about 1e8 cycles. (The book prints h_min proportional to T^(1/2); the exponent sign is corrected here, since the text says sensitivity improves with time.)

**Symbols:** T = duration of data searched

### Stochastic-background sensitivity versus observation time · supporting · SCH §12.2 p.370 (pdf 388)

$$
h_{\min}\propto T^{-1/4}
$$

Cross-correlation lowers the detectable r.m.s. background amplitude only as the fourth root of observing time. (Printed in the book as T^(1/4); sign corrected here.)

**Symbols:** T = duration of correlated data

### (12.6) Polarization basis tensors · supporting · SCH §12.3 p.374 (pdf 392)

$$
\mathbf e_+=\hat e^R_x\otimes\hat e^R_x-\hat e^R_y\otimes\hat e^R_y,\qquad \mathbf e_\times=\hat e^R_x\otimes\hat e^R_y+\hat e^R_y\otimes\hat e^R_x
$$

Plus and cross tensors built from an orthonormal basis in the plane of the sky, independent of any detector.

**Symbols:** e^R_x, e^R_y = sky-plane basis vectors perpendicular to the propagation direction N

### (12.7) Wave tensor · supporting · SCH §12.3 p.374 (pdf 392)

$$
\mathbf h(t)=h_+(t)\,\mathbf e_++h_\times(t)\,\mathbf e_\times
$$

The spatial TT metric perturbation of the incoming wave written in the sky-plane polarization basis.

**Symbols:** h+, hx = polarization amplitudes

### (12.8) Detector tensor · central · SCH §12.3 p.374 (pdf 392)

$$
\mathbf d=L\,(\hat e_x\otimes\hat e_x-\hat e_y\otimes\hat e_y)
$$

Encodes an L-shaped interferometer's arm directions and length in one tensor.

**Symbols:** L = arm length; e_x, e_y = unit vectors along the arms

### (12.9) Interferometer response as a double contraction · central · SCH §12.3 p.375 (pdf 393)

$$
\Delta_{\rm int}(t)=\mathbf d:\mathbf h:=d^{jk}h_{jk}
$$

The difference between the stretchings of the two arms is the full contraction of detector and wave tensors, valid for any direction and polarization. Normalization check (ours): because a TT wave changes an arm of length L by (1/2) h L, the literal d:h with d = L(e_x e_x - e_y e_y) equals twice the arm-length difference; conventional treatments put a factor 1/2 in the detector tensor. Ratios such as those in Exercise 12.3 and Figure 12.4 are unaffected.

**Symbols:** Delta_int = arm-length difference; ':' = double inner product

### (12.10) Strain response with antenna patterns · central · SCH §12.3 p.375 (pdf 393)

$$
\delta_{\rm int}(t):=\Delta_{\rm int}/L=F_+(\theta,\phi)\,h_+(t)+F_\times(\theta,\phi)\,h_\times(t),\qquad F_{+,\times}:=\mathbf d:\mathbf e_{+,\times}/L
$$

Detector strain separates into geometry-only antenna factors times the two polarization waveforms.

**Symbols:** theta, phi = source direction in detector-based spherical coordinates (zenith = pole); F also depends on the sky-basis orientation

### Antenna power pattern · supporting · SCH §12.3 p.375 (pdf 393)

$$
P(\theta,\phi)=F_+^2+F_\times^2
$$

Polarization-averaged directional sensitivity plotted in Figure 12.4; network patterns add for identical detectors.

### (12.11) Data model: noise plus shifted template · central · SCH §12.3 p.379 (pdf 397)

$$
x_j=n_j+A\,w_{j-D}
$$

Sampled detector output containing Gaussian noise and a signal of known shape, unknown amplitude A and unknown arrival index D.

**Symbols:** x_j = data (M samples); n_j = zero-mean Gaussian noise, std sigma; w_j = template (N samples, zero outside); D = arrival sample

### (12.12) Naive threshold-crossing criterion · supporting · SCH §12.3 p.379 (pdf 397)

$$
A_{\min}=\frac{\sigma}{w_K}
$$

Watching raw data, a signal is noticed only if its peak value exceeds the noise level; w_K is the template maximum.

**Symbols:** w_K = largest template sample

### (12.13) Filter output (correlation with template) · central · SCH §12.3 p.379 (pdf 397)

$$
(x*w)_k=\sum_j x_j\,w_{j-k}
$$

Slide the template along the data and take the inner product at each lag k (called a convolution in the book; it is a cross-correlation).

**Symbols:** k = trial arrival index

### (12.14) Filter output split into noise and signal parts · derivation-step · SCH §12.3 p.380 (pdf 398)

$$
(x*w)_k=\sum_j n_j\,w_{j-k}+A\sum_j w_{j-D}\,w_{j-k}
$$

Linear in the noise, so the output is Gaussian; the second term is deterministic.

### (12.15) Mean filter output · derivation-step · SCH §12.3 p.380 (pdf 398)

$$
\langle (x*w)_k\rangle=A\,(w*w)_{k-D}
$$

The expected output is the template's autocorrelation, scaled by A and centred at the true arrival index. The book's middle expression carries an extra factor A and writes x where the signal part is meant; only the final equality is right.

**Symbols:** angle brackets = ensemble expectation

### (12.16) Peak mean output · derivation-step · SCH §12.3 p.380 (pdf 398)

$$
\langle (x*w)_k\rangle_{\max}=A\sum_j w_j^2=A\,\|w\|^2
$$

At k = D the mean output equals amplitude times the template's squared norm.

**Symbols:** ||w||^2 = sum of squared template samples

### (12.17) Variance of the filter output · derivation-step · SCH §12.3 p.380 (pdf 398)

$$
\mathrm{var}[(x*w)_k]=\Big\langle\Big[\sum_j x_j w_{j-k}\Big]\Big[\sum_{j'} x_{j'} w_{j'-k}\Big]\Big\rangle-A^2\big[(w*w)_{k-D}\big]^2
$$

Definition of variance applied to the filter statistic; only the noise-noise term survives.

### (12.18) Surviving noise term · derivation-step · SCH §12.3 p.380 (pdf 398)

$$
\mathrm{var}=\sum_j\sum_{j'}\langle n_j n_{j'}\rangle\,w_{j-k}\,w_{j'-k}
$$

The variance is the noise covariance weighted by the template at the chosen lag. Written here with the template weights restored; the book prints only the double sum of noise covariances, var[n] = sum_j sum_j' <n_j n_j'>, dropping the w factors.

### White-noise covariance · supporting · SCH §12.3 p.380 (pdf 398)

$$
\langle n_j n_{j'}\rangle=\sigma^2\,\delta_{j,j'}
$$

No correlation between distinct samples; a simplification real detector noise violates.

**Symbols:** delta = Kronecker delta

### (12.19) Filter variance for white noise · derivation-step · SCH §12.3 p.381 (pdf 399)

$$
\mathrm{var}[(x*w)_k]=\sigma^2\,\|w\|^2=\sigma^2(w*w)_0
$$

The noise in the filter output is sigma times the template norm, the same at every lag. Corrected form. The book prints sigma^2 (w*w)_(k-D), which agrees with sigma^2 ||w||^2 only at the peak lag k = D, so Eq. 12.20 is unaffected.

### (12.20) Matched-filter detection threshold · central · SCH §12.3 p.381 (pdf 399)

$$
A_{\min}=\frac{\sigma}{\|w\|}
$$

Signal-to-noise ratio A||w||/sigma reaches one at this amplitude: the template's integrated norm replaces its peak value, so long signals are detectable at much lower amplitude.

**Symbols:** ||w|| = template norm

### Norm of a sinusoidal template · derivation-step · SCH §12.3 p.381 (pdf 399)

$$
B^2\sum_j\sin^2(\omega\Delta t\,j)=\tfrac12 B^2\sum_j\big[1-\cos(2\omega\Delta t\,j)\big]\approx \tfrac12 B^2 N
$$

Over many cycles the oscillating term averages away, leaving half the sample count.

**Symbols:** B = template amplitude; N = number of template samples; Delta t = sampling interval

### (12.21) Matched-filter gain for a sinusoid · central · SCH §12.3 p.381 (pdf 399)

$$
A_{\min}=\frac{\sigma}{w_{\max}(N/2)^{1/2}}
$$

Compared with Eq. 12.12, the threshold drops by a factor proportional to the square root of the signal length (properly, of its number of cycles); range improves by that factor, volume by its cube.

**Symbols:** w_max = template peak

### Effective filtered noise level · supporting · SCH §12.3 p.382 (pdf 400)

$$
h_c=\sigma\,(N/2)^{-1/2}
$$

Amplitude at which a matched-filtered signal has SNR = 1: an effective noise level for detectors.

### (12.22) Two-detector responses at common time · supporting · SCH §12.3 p.384 (pdf 402)

$$
\delta_1(T)=F_{+,1}h_+(T)+F_{\times,1}h_\times(T),\qquad \delta_2(T)=F_{+,2}h_+(T)+F_{\times,2}h_\times(T)
$$

Each detector's strain, time-shifted to a common wave phase, is a different linear combination of the same two polarizations.

**Symbols:** T = common reference time; subscripts 1, 2 label detectors; F evaluated at each detector's own angles

### (12.23) Two-detector response matrix · derivation-step · SCH §12.3 p.384 (pdf 402)

$$
M_{12}=\begin{pmatrix}F_{+,1}&F_{\times,1}\\F_{+,2}&F_{\times,2}\end{pmatrix}
$$

Matrix mapping (h+, hx) to (delta1, delta2); the subscript names the detector pair, not matrix indices.

### (12.24) Inverse response matrix · derivation-step · SCH §12.3 p.384 (pdf 402)

$$
M_{12}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det M_{12}}\begin{pmatrix}F_{\times,2}&-F_{\times,1}\\-F_{+,2}&F_{+,1}\end{pmatrix}
$$

Standard 2x2 inverse; singular when the detectors are aligned.

### (12.25) Polarization reconstruction · central · SCH §12.3 p.385 (pdf 403)

$$
h_+(T)=\frac{F_{\times,2}\delta_1(T)-F_{\times,1}\delta_2(T)}{\det M_{12}},\qquad h_\times(T)=\frac{-F_{+,2}\delta_1(T)+F_{+,1}\delta_2(T)}{\det M_{12}}
$$

Two non-aligned detectors recover both polarization waveforms for a known sky position.

### (12.26) Polarization amplitude ratio and inclination · central · SCH §12.3 p.385 (pdf 403)

$$
\frac{|h_+(T)|}{|h_\times(T)|}=\frac{1+\cos^2\iota}{2\cos\iota}
$$

In the sky basis where the two polarizations are in quadrature, their amplitude ratio fixes the orbital inclination; it tends to 1 face-on and diverges edge-on.

**Symbols:** iota = inclination of orbital angular momentum to line of sight

### Inclination from the ratio (Exercise 12.7) · supporting · SCH p.410 (pdf 428)

$$
z^2-2rz+1=0,\quad z=\cos\iota=r-(r^2-1)^{1/2};\qquad r-1\equiv\epsilon\sim\iota^4/8\ (\iota\to0)
$$

Solving Eq. 12.26; the quartic flatness near face-on shows why inclination (and therefore distance) is poorly measured for loud face-on systems.

**Symbols:** r = |h+|/|hx| >= 1

### (12.27) Two-sided Gaussian false-alarm probability · central · SCH §12.3 p.386 (pdf 404)

$$
p(|n_j|>a\sigma)=2\int_a^\infty\phi(z)\,dz
$$

Probability that a zero-mean Gaussian noise sample exceeds a sigma in magnitude.

**Symbols:** a = threshold in units of sigma

### (12.28) Unit normal density · supporting · SCH §12.3 p.386 (pdf 404)

$$
\phi(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-z^2/2}
$$

Gaussian probability density with unit standard deviation.

### (12.29) Gaussian tail bounds · central · SCH §12.3 p.386 (pdf 404)

$$
\frac{2a}{1+a^2}\,\phi(a)<p(|n_j|>a\sigma)<\frac{2\phi(a)}{a}
$$

The upper bound approaches the exact tail for large a and gives the quoted numbers: 6e-7 at a = 5, 2e-9 at 6, 8e-11 at 6.5, 1.3e-15 at 8. The factor 2 is restored here: the book prints the bounds as a phi(a)/(1+a^2) and phi(a)/a, although its quoted probabilities include the two-sided factor.

### (12.30) Three-detector responses · supporting · SCH §12.3 p.388 (pdf 406)

$$
\delta_a(T)=F_{+,a}h_+(T)+F_{\times,a}h_\times(T),\quad a=1,2,3
$$

Three data streams depending on only two unknown waveforms, so one combination must be signal-free.

### (12.31) Null combination of signal strains · central · SCH §12.3 p.388 (pdf 406)

$$
D_{12}\,\delta_3(T)+D_{23}\,\delta_1(T)+D_{31}\,\delta_2(T)=0
$$

The gravitational-wave-induced strains in three detectors satisfy an exact linear identity for a given source direction (Exercise 12.8).

**Symbols:** D_ab = det M_ab

### (12.32) Pair determinants · supporting · SCH §12.3 p.389 (pdf 407)

$$
D_{ab}:=\det M_{ab}=\det\begin{pmatrix}F_{+,a}&F_{\times,a}\\F_{+,b}&F_{\times,b}\end{pmatrix}
$$

Weights of the null combination; antisymmetric in a and b.

### (12.33) Null stream · central · SCH §12.3 p.389 (pdf 407)

$$
N_{123}(T):=D_{12}\,x_3(T)+D_{23}\,x_1(T)+D_{31}\,x_2(T)
$$

The same combination applied to real outputs x = n + delta contains only noise and glitches; used as a veto, for localization and for calibration checks.

**Symbols:** x_a = detector outputs

### Doppler frequency drift from detector acceleration · derivation-step · SCH §12.3 p.390 (pdf 408)

$$
\Delta f_{\rm shift}=f\,\Omega^2 R\,T/c,\qquad \Delta f_{\rm obs}=2/T
$$

Frequency change accumulated in time T from circular motion of radius R and angular velocity Omega, compared with the frequency resolution of a T-long observation (book's factor-2 convention).

**Symbols:** f = source frequency; Omega, R = rotation or orbital angular velocity and radius

### (12.34) Maximum unmodulated observation time · supporting · SCH §12.3 p.390 (pdf 408)

$$
T_{\max}=\left(\frac{2c}{\Omega^2 fR}\right)^{1/2}
$$

Longest coherent observation before Doppler corrections are needed: about 70 (f/1 kHz)^(-1/2) minutes for Earth's spin and a few hours for its orbit at 1 kHz.

### (12.35) Angular resolution of a long continuous-wave observation · supporting · SCH §12.3 p.391 (pdf 409)

$$
\Delta\theta=2\times10^{-6}\left(\frac{f}{1\,\mathrm{kHz}}\right)^{-1}
$$

Diffraction limit lambda/2R of the aperture synthesized by half an orbit around the Sun; about 0.4 arcsec at 1 kHz.

**Symbols:** Delta theta in radians

### Number of sky patches · supporting · SCH §12.3 p.391 (pdf 409)

$$
N_{\rm patch}\approx 4\pi/(\Delta\theta)^2\approx3\times10^{12}
$$

Count of independent demodulations needed for an optimal six-month all-sky search at 1 kHz, before multiplying by spin-down bins.

### (12.36) Correlation of two detector outputs · supporting · SCH §12.3 p.393 (pdf 411)

$$
\mathrm{Corr}(1,2):=\sum_j x^1_j\,x^2_j
$$

Sum of products of simultaneous samples from two detectors.

**Symbols:** x^1_j = n^1_j + h_j, x^2_j = n^2_j + h_j

### (12.37) Mean correlation · derivation-step · SCH §12.3 p.393 (pdf 411)

$$
\mathrm{Corr}(1,2)=N\langle x^1x^2\rangle=N\langle n^1n^2+n^1h+hn^2+hh\rangle=N|h|^2
$$

Independent noises and noise-signal cross terms average to zero; only the common background survives.

**Symbols:** |h|^2 = mean square background amplitude; N = number of samples

### (12.38) Variance of the correlation · derivation-step · SCH §12.3 p.393 (pdf 411)

$$
\mathrm{var}[\mathrm{Corr}(1,2)]=N\,\mathrm{var}(n^1n^2)=N\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2
$$

When the background is much weaker than instrumental noise, the noise-noise product dominates the scatter.

### (12.39) Stochastic power signal-to-noise ratio · central · SCH §12.3 p.394 (pdf 412)

$$
\mathrm{SNR}_{\rm stochastic\text{-}power}=\frac{N|h|^2}{N^{1/2}\sigma_1\sigma_2}=N^{1/2}\frac{|h|^2}{\sigma_1\sigma_2}
$$

Correlation multiplies the product of the raw per-sample SNRs by the square root of the number of samples.

### (12.40) Stochastic amplitude signal-to-noise ratio · central · SCH §12.3 p.394 (pdf 412)

$$
\mathrm{SNR}_{\rm stochastic\text{-}amplitude}=N^{1/4}\frac{|h|}{\sqrt{\sigma_1\sigma_2}}
$$

In amplitude terms the gain is only the fourth root of N: a background 1e4 below the noise needs 1e16 samples; a year at an effective 100 Hz reaches about a factor 100.

### (12.41) Discrete Fourier transform (Exercise 12.4) · supporting · SCH p.409 (pdf 427)

$$
\tilde x_a=\sum_j x_j\,e^{-2\pi i aj/M}
$$

Frequency-domain representation of an M-sample data vector; term-by-term cost of order M^2 versus M ln M for the FFT. The book's printed formula contains a stray factor w_j inside the sum; it is dropped here.

**Symbols:** a = frequency index; j = time index

### (12.42) Correlation theorem (Exercise 12.4) · supporting · SCH p.409 (pdf 427)

$$
\widetilde{(x*w)}_a=\tilde x_a\,\tilde w_a^*
$$

The transform of the filter output is a simple product, which is why matched filtering is done in the frequency domain with FFTs (and where coloured-noise weighting naturally enters).

**Symbols:** * on w = complex conjugate

### (12.43) Six-month frequency resolution (Exercise 12.9) · supporting · SCH p.411 (pdf 429)

$$
\Delta f=2/T=1.3\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{Hz},\qquad T=1.5\times10^7\ \mathrm{s}
$$

Frequency bin width for a half-year observation, used to count spin-down bins.

### (12.44) Slowing pulsar clock (Exercise 12.9) · supporting · SCH p.412 (pdf 430)

$$
t_{\rm p}=t\,(1-t/\tau)
$$

Models spin-down as a pulsar clock running slow relative to barycentric time; resampling removes the linear frequency drift.

**Symbols:** t = barycentric time; tau = slowdown time

### (12.45) Inverse resampling map (Exercise 12.9) · derivation-step · SCH p.412 (pdf 430)

$$
t=t_{\rm p}(1+t_{\rm p}/\tau)+O\big([t_{\rm p}/\tau]^2\big)
$$

Times at which to resample barycentred data to make a spinning-down signal monochromatic.

### (12.46) Chirp rate (Exercise 12.10) · supporting · SCH p.413 (pdf 431)

$$
\frac{df}{dt}=12\left(\frac{\mathcal M}{M_\odot}\right)^{5/3}\left(\frac{f}{100\,\mathrm{Hz}}\right)^{11/3}\ \mathrm{s}^{-2}
$$

Leading-order frequency derivative of a quasi-circular inspiral, used to read the chirp mass off the GW150914 trace.

**Symbols:** f = gravitational-wave frequency

### (12.47) Component mass from total and reduced mass (Exercise 12.11) · supporting · SCH p.413 (pdf 431)

$$
\frac{m_1}{M}=\frac12+\frac12\left(1-\frac{4\mu}{M}\right)^{1/2}
$$

Larger component mass given total mass M and reduced mass mu (itself fixed by chirp mass and M). Corrected form: the book omits the 1/2 in front of the square root, which would allow m1 > M; the book's Eq. 12.48 follows from the corrected version.

**Symbols:** mu = m1 m2 / M

### (12.48) Sensitivity of component mass to total mass (Exercise 12.11) · supporting · SCH p.414 (pdf 432)

$$
\frac{\delta m_1}{\delta M}=\frac12+\frac{\tfrac12-\tfrac{\mu}{3M}}{\left[1-\tfrac{4\mu}{M}\right]^{1/2}}
$$

At fixed chirp mass, a small error in total mass is amplified enormously in the component masses when they are nearly equal (4 mu/M near 1).

### Hubble-Lemaitre constant from a standard siren · supporting · SCH §12.4 p.401 (pdf 419)

$$
H_0=\frac{v_{\rm rec}}{D_L}
$$

Recession velocity of the host galaxy (redshift corrected for peculiar motion) divided by the gravitational-wave luminosity distance.

**Symbols:** D_L = luminosity distance from the chirp amplitude

## Worked examples

### Merger frequency of GW150914 and of a binary neutron star (in-text) · intro · SCH §12.2 p.363 (pdf 381)

**Problem:** Estimate the highest gravitational-wave frequency reached before merger for GW150914 (chirp mass 30.8 solar masses) and for two 1.4 solar-mass neutron stars, and find the largest chirp mass whose merger lies in LIGO's band.

**Method:** Invert the Chapter 9 phase-to-merger relation for f_gw = omega/pi, normalise to one solar mass (Eq. 12.1), and evaluate at half an orbit remaining, psi = pi (Eq. 12.2). Compare with observation and with neutron-star tidal disruption.

**Key insight:** Frequency scales inversely with mass, so a detector's low-frequency cutoff is an upper mass limit; point-particle estimates are good to a factor of about two at merger.

**Result:** GW150914: about 130 Hz predicted versus about 250 Hz observed. Neutron stars: a few kHz formally, but the signal weakens near 1 kHz as the stars merge. A 20 Hz cutoff excludes chirp masses above about 200 solar masses.

**Concepts:** merger frequency estimate, chirp mass, orbital phase remaining before merger

### LISA-to-ground handoff time (in-text) · intro · SCH §12.2 p.364 (pdf 382)

**Problem:** How long after LISA sees a 30 solar-mass chirp-mass binary at 30 mHz will it merge in the ground-based band?

**Method:** Rescale the time-to-merger formula (Eq. 12.3) and substitute the mass and frequency.

**Key insight:** The steep f^(-8/3) dependence makes months of LISA warning translate into seconds of ground-band signal, enabling advance alerts.

**Result:** About 9-10 months.

**Concepts:** time to merger, multiband gravitational wave astronomy

### Ringdown frequency of the GW150914 remnant (in-text) · intro · SCH §12.2 p.366 (pdf 384)

**Problem:** Estimate the ringdown frequency of a black hole of about 68 solar masses (detector frame).

**Method:** Take the dominant quasi-normal angular frequency as about 0.5/M, convert geometrized mass to seconds and divide by 2 pi (Eq. 12.4).

**Key insight:** A single dimensional estimate lands near the observed peak frequency, supporting a black-hole remnant, even though the ringdown itself was not isolated.

**Result:** About 230 Hz.

**Concepts:** ringdown, redshifted masses

### Strain from an asymmetric pulsar and required integration (in-text) · standard · SCH §12.2 p.367 (pdf 385)

**Problem:** Estimate the continuous-wave amplitude from a neutron star with I = 1e38 kg m^2, spin 60 Hz, distance 1 kpc and asymmetry 1e-6, and how many cycles are needed to detect it with a detector of broadband sensitivity 1e-22.

**Method:** Apply h ~ 2 epsilon Omega^2 I / r (with G/c^4 restored); then require the square-root-of-cycles gain to bridge the ratio of broadband sensitivity to signal amplitude.

**Key insight:** Tiny amplitudes are reachable only through very long coherent integration, since gain grows as the square root of the cycle count.

**Result:** h about 1e-26; needs about 1e8 cycles, of order a month of data.

**Concepts:** continuous gravitational waves from spinning neutron stars, neutron star ellipticity, square-root-of-cycles gain of matched filtering

### Crab pulsar spin-down fraction (in-text) · intro · SCH §12.2 p.368 (pdf 386)

**Problem:** If the Crab's gravitational-wave amplitude limit is about 4 percent of its spin-down limit, what fraction of its rotational energy loss can be gravitational?

**Method:** Energy flux scales as amplitude squared; square the amplitude ratio.

**Key insight:** Amplitude limits tighten quadratically in energy.

**Result:** Roughly (0.04)^2, about 0.16 percent (the book rounds this to below 0.1 percent).

**Concepts:** spin-down limit

### Matched filter versus threshold crossing (in-text derivation) · standard · SCH §12.3 p.379 (pdf 397)

**Problem:** For sampled white Gaussian noise containing a known template of unknown amplitude and arrival time, find the minimum detectable amplitude with and without using the template.

**Method:** Model x_j = n_j + A w_(j-D). Naive: peak must exceed sigma, giving sigma/w_max. Filter: correlate with w; compute mean (A times autocorrelation, peaking at A||w||^2) and variance (sigma^2 ||w||^2) using the white-noise Kronecker delta; set SNR = 1. Specialise to a sinusoid whose squared norm is B^2 N/2.

**Key insight:** The template's norm integrates over the whole signal, so the threshold drops by the square root of the signal length; long neutron-star chirps gain most.

**Result:** A_min = sigma/||w|| = sigma/[w_max (N/2)^(1/2)]; range grows by N^(1/2), volume by N^(3/2).

**Concepts:** matched filtering, white noise, signal-to-noise ratio, square-root-of-cycles gain of matched filtering, detection range and volume

### Does faster sampling help? (in-text argument) · standard · SCH §12.3 p.382 (pdf 400)

**Problem:** Equation 12.21 seems to promise better sensitivity simply by sampling faster; is that true?

**Method:** Halve the sampling rate: each new sample averages two old ones, so sigma falls by sqrt(2) while N halves, leaving sigma/(N/2)^(1/2) unchanged; push to the extreme of one sample to see the limit; the natural rate is about twice the signal frequency.

**Key insight:** The gain counts independent pieces of signal, i.e. cycles, not samples.

**Result:** No; the improvement factor is best read as the square root of the number of waveform cycles.

**Concepts:** square-root-of-cycles gain of matched filtering, white noise

### Two-detector polarization and inclination (in-text derivation) · challenging · SCH §12.3 p.384 (pdf 402)

**Problem:** With a sky position known from a gamma-ray burst, recover h+ and hx from two detectors and infer the orbital inclination of a binary.

**Method:** Time-shift both streams to a common wave phase; write the responses as a 2x2 linear system with matrix M_12 of antenna factors and invert it. Rotate the sky-plane basis until the recovered polarizations are in quadrature (the edge-on case makes one vanish); then equate their amplitude ratio to (1 + cos^2 iota)/(2 cos iota) from Eq. 9.100 and solve the quadratic (Exercise 12.7).

**Key insight:** Polarization content is geometric information about the orbit, but near face-on the ratio is quartically flat in iota, so inclination and distance are poorly determined for the loudest systems.

**Result:** Eqs. 12.25-12.26; a 1 percent ratio uncertainty near r = 1 allows iota up to about 30 degrees.

**Concepts:** polarization reconstruction with two detectors, inclination from polarization ratio, distance-inclination degeneracy

### False-alarm arithmetic for a year of data (in-text) · standard · SCH §12.3 p.386 (pdf 404)

**Problem:** How high must a signal-to-noise threshold be so that Gaussian noise produces at most about one false alarm per year (or per two years) in a matched-filter search with many templates?

**Method:** Use the tail bound (with the two-sided factor) for a = 5, 6, 6.5, 8; count raw samples (8 kHz for a year, 2.4e11) and independent filter samples (5 ms period, 6e9); multiply by 1e4 independent templates.

**Key insight:** Rare-per-sample events are common in big data; thresholds must scale with the number of independent trials, but the Gaussian tail falls so fast that modest increases in a suffice.

**Result:** 5 sigma: probability 6e-7, about 140,000 raw excursions per year; 6 sigma: 2e-9, 12 filter false alarms per year; 6.5 sigma: 8e-11, one per two years; with 1e4 templates, a threshold of about 8 (1.3e-15).

**Concepts:** false alarm probability, trials factor

### Deriving the three-detector null stream (in-text and Exercise 12.8) · standard · SCH §12.3 p.388 (pdf 406)

**Problem:** Find a linear combination of three detector strains that contains no gravitational-wave signal from a given direction.

**Method:** Solve the first two response equations for h+ and hx (Eq. 12.25), substitute into the third, clear the determinant and rearrange cyclically.

**Key insight:** Counting unknown waveforms against data streams guarantees N - 2 null combinations; their weights are pairwise antenna determinants.

**Result:** D12 delta3 + D23 delta1 + D31 delta2 = 0; applied to outputs this is the null stream N_123.

**Concepts:** null stream, polarization reconstruction with two detectors

### Doppler coherence time and sky resolution for pulsar searches (in-text) · standard · SCH §12.3 p.390 (pdf 408)

**Problem:** How long can a 1 kHz continuous signal be observed before Earth's motion must be corrected, and how finely must the sky be gridded for a six-month search?

**Method:** Velocity change Omega^2 R T gives frequency drift f Omega^2 R T/c; set equal to resolution 2/T to get T_max. Treat half an orbit as an aperture of size 2R with diffraction limit lambda/2R; divide 4 pi steradians by the patch size squared.

**Key insight:** The same Doppler pattern that spoils naive integration is an exquisitely precise direction finder, at the price of an astronomically large search.

**Result:** About 70 minutes (Earth rotation) and a few hours (orbit); about 2e-6 rad (0.4 arcsec) resolution and about 3e12 sky patches.

**Concepts:** Doppler modulation of continuous-wave signals, angular resolution by orbital aperture synthesis, computational cost of all-sky continuous-wave searches

### Cross-correlation for a stochastic background (in-text derivation) · standard · SCH §12.3 p.393 (pdf 411)

**Problem:** Compute the signal-to-noise ratio for detecting a random background common to two co-located, aligned detectors with independent white noise, and estimate what one year of LIGO correlation can reach.

**Method:** Sum products of simultaneous samples: expectation N|h|^2; variance dominated by noise products N sigma1^2 sigma2^2 when the background is weak. Divide and take a square root for amplitude. Use an effective sample rate of about 100 Hz for the LIGO pair.

**Key insight:** Correlation gains in power as N^(1/2) but in amplitude only as N^(1/4), so a random field far below the noise is much harder to find than a coherent signal of the same amplitude.

**Result:** SNR_amplitude = N^(1/4)|h|/sqrt(sigma1 sigma2); 1e4 below noise needs 1e16 samples (about 100,000 years at kHz rates); a year at 100 Hz (3e9 samples) reaches about 100 below broadband noise, 1e4 lower in energy.

**Concepts:** cross-correlation search, stochastic gravitational wave background, signal-to-noise ratio

### Standard-siren Hubble constant from GW170817 (in-text) · standard · SCH §12.4 p.401 (pdf 419)

**Problem:** Measure H0 from a neutron-star merger whose host galaxy is identified.

**Method:** Obtain the luminosity distance from the gravitational-wave amplitude and inclination posterior, the recession velocity from the host redshift corrected for peculiar motion, and form their ratio as a posterior distribution; later add a radio constraint on the jet viewing angle.

**Key insight:** Inclination uncertainty for nearly face-on systems dominates the error; an electromagnetic jet-angle measurement sharply narrows it.

**Result:** A broad posterior peaking near 70 km/s/Mpc between Planck and SH0ES; with radio jet constraints, 70.3 plus or minus 5 km/s/Mpc.

**Concepts:** Hubble-Lemaitre constant from gravitational waves, standard siren, relativistic jet and viewing angle

### Detection-rate growth from sensitivity (in-text) · intro · SCH §12.4 p.406 (pdf 424)

**Problem:** Why did detections jump from 3 in O1 to 8 in O2 to 39 in half of O3?

**Method:** Amplitude sensitivity improvement by a factor k extends range by k and volume (rate) by k^3; add better glitch rejection with three detectors and a lower significance threshold.

**Key insight:** Cubic volume scaling makes modest noise improvements pay off dramatically.

**Result:** A factor 2 in noise gives 8 times the rate.

**Concepts:** detection range and volume, gravitational wave transient catalog


## Analogies and intuitions

### A coherent detector network is like aperture synthesis in radio interferometry: separated detectors combined with correct phases act as one larger instrument. → coherent network analysis · useful · SCH §12.3 p.388 (pdf 406)

Cited (after Finn) to explain why combining data before thresholding is more powerful than coincidence and how directional information emerges.

**Where it breaks down:** Gravitational-wave wavelengths are comparable to or larger than Earth, so the network gains localization mainly from timing and antenna patterns rather than true imaging.

**App idea:** Side-by-side radio array and GW network: moving detectors apart sharpens a sky-localization ellipse just as baseline length sharpens a radio beam.

### Earth's orbit as a synthesized telescope aperture for a continuous gravitational wave. → angular resolution by orbital aperture synthesis · strong · SCH §12.3 p.391 (pdf 409)

Turns the direction-dependent Doppler modulation into a diffraction-limit estimate lambda/2R and a count of sky patches.

**Where it breaks down:** Only works for signals coherent over months and assumes perfect phase models including spin-down.

**App idea:** Animate a detector circling the Sun while a wavefront sweeps past; show phase residuals for a slightly wrong sky position growing through the year.

### The cosmological gravitational-wave background is the gravitational counterpart of the cosmic microwave background, but from a far earlier epoch because gravity has never been obscured. → stochastic gravitational wave background · strong · SCH §12.2 p.369 (pdf 387)

Motivates why a relic background would be scientifically priceless and why its present intensity is so uncertain.

**Where it breaks down:** Unlike the CMB it is not thermal, need not be detectable with any planned instrument, and is likely masked by astrophysical backgrounds.

**App idea:** Timeline of the Universe with a 'last scattering' wall for photons and none for gravitons, letting the learner see which epoch each background reveals.

### The square-root-of-N matched-filter gain is the same random-walk scaling that governs averaging repeated measurements. → square-root-of-cycles gain of matched filtering · strong · SCH §12.3 p.381 (pdf 399)

Reassures the reader that a result derived for a sinusoid is general.

**Where it breaks down:** Holds for stationary Gaussian noise and a perfectly known template; mismatched templates and coloured noise reduce the gain.

**App idea:** Random-walk plot of accumulated noise versus linearly accumulated signal, with the crossing point marking detection.

### Building LIGO required theory-experiment partnership like building large particle accelerators, where theory both justifies the cost and enables recognition of new phenomena. → gravitational wave astronomy · weak · SCH §12.1 p.362 (pdf 380)

Explains why source modelling preceded and shaped detection.

**Where it breaks down:** Sociological parallel only.

### Each pulsar in a timing array is like a separate gravitational-wave detector whose noise is independent, so correlating pulsars mirrors correlating interferometers. → pulsar timing array · useful · SCH §12.3 p.395 (pdf 413)

Links nHz background searches to the cross-correlation method just derived.

**Where it breaks down:** Pulsar-Earth baselines give a specific angular correlation pattern (Hellings-Downs) not discussed; data are irregularly sampled times of arrival, not strain.

**App idea:** Sky map of pulsars with pairwise correlation plotted against angular separation as a simulated background is switched on.

### A neutron star is essentially one enormous atomic nucleus, so flinging off part of it is like breaking a nucleus into ever smaller pieces. → r-process nucleosynthesis in neutron star mergers · weak · SCH §12.4 p.403 (pdf 421)

Gives a vivid picture of how merger ejecta become heavy elements.

**Where it breaks down:** Misleading as nuclear physics: heavy elements form mainly by rapid neutron capture on seed nuclei in decompressed ejecta, with fission only recycling the heaviest; a neutron star is gravitationally, not strongly, bound.

**App idea:** Nuclide-chart animation of neutron capture racing along the neutron-rich side, then beta decays to stability, to replace the fission picture.

### The detector response as a double contraction d:h, in the spirit of Schutz's tensors as machines that eat vectors or tensors and return numbers. → detector tensor · strong · SCH §12.3 p.375 (pdf 393)

Makes the complicated angular dependence of interferometer response a single frame-free operation.

**Where it breaks down:** Valid in the long-wavelength limit (arm length much shorter than wavelength), not for LISA at high frequencies.

**App idea:** Visual 'machine' with two input slots (detector tensor, wave tensor) and a strain output dial, with components lighting up as they contribute.

### LISA as an early-warning system for ground-based detectors and telescopes. → multiband gravitational wave astronomy · useful · SCH §12.2 p.364 (pdf 382)

Communicates the practical payoff of seeing the same binary months before merger.

**Where it breaks down:** Requires sources close enough to be above LISA's noise; rates are uncertain.

**App idea:** Frequency-time track of a binary crossing from the LISA band to the LIGO band with a countdown clock.


## Misconceptions addressed

### Gravitational-wave detectors can be pointed at a source like telescopes. · SCH §12.1 p.360 (pdf 378)

**Correction:** Waves cannot be focused because they barely interact with matter; interferometers are nearly omnidirectional with broad quadrupolar patterns, and localization comes from networks.

**Why tempting:** Other astronomy is done with aimed instruments.

### Every gravitational-wave event should have an electromagnetic counterpart, and EM surveys tell us which GW sources exist. · SCH §12.1 p.361 (pdf 379)

**Correction:** Binary black holes in vacuum emit essentially no light, and GW sources are selected by mass and compactness, not luminosity; counterparts are prized exceptions.

**Why tempting:** GW170817 set a spectacular precedent.

### A signal must stand visibly above the noise to be detected. · SCH §12.3 p.378 (pdf 396)

**Correction:** Matched filtering detects known waveforms far below the raw noise; GW170817 never exceeded 1e-22 in strain yet is the highest-SNR event of its time.

**Why tempting:** GW150914's trace was visible by eye, and intuition equates detection with seeing.

### Sampling data faster improves matched-filter sensitivity because N grows. · SCH §12.3 p.382 (pdf 400)

**Correction:** Noise per sample changes in step with N, leaving the threshold unchanged; the gain counts signal cycles.

**Why tempting:** The formula contains N explicitly.

### A 5 sigma excursion is so rare it must be real. · SCH §12.3 p.386 (pdf 404)

**Correction:** In a year of kHz data with many templates, 5 sigma noise excursions occur hundreds of thousands of times; thresholds near 8 are needed, and glitches push them higher.

**Why tempting:** 5 sigma is the particle-physics discovery standard.

### A stochastic background 1e4 times below the noise is as detectable as a pulsar 1e4 times below the noise. · SCH §12.3 p.394 (pdf 412)

**Correction:** Coherent signals gain as the square root of time in amplitude, random backgrounds only as the fourth root; the background would need about 100,000 years of correlation.

**Why tempting:** Both are 'long integrations of a weak signal'.

### If the chirp mass is known to 0.1 percent and the total mass well, the component masses are also precise. · SCH p.413 (pdf 431)

**Correction:** Near equal masses the mapping from (chirp mass, total mass) to (m1, m2) is extremely sensitive, so small total-mass errors become large component errors (Exercise 12.11).

**Why tempting:** Two equations in two unknowns seem to fix the answer cleanly.

### The loudest (face-on) binaries give the best distance measurements. · SCH §12.3 p.385 (pdf 403)

**Correction:** Face-on amplitudes are insensitive to inclination at second order, so distance is degenerate with inclination; moderately inclined systems like GW190814 constrain distance better.

**Why tempting:** Higher SNR normally means smaller errors.

### Two detectors can locate a source on the sky. · SCH §12.3 p.383 (pdf 401)

**Correction:** One time delay only restricts the source to a ring; at least three detectors (two independent delays), antenna-pattern amplitudes, or an EM counterpart are needed.

**Why tempting:** Two ears localize sound (but using head shadowing and spectral cues).

### A detector that barely responds to an event contributes nothing. · SCH §12.4 p.400 (pdf 418)

**Correction:** Virgo's weak response to GW170817 showed the source lay near its antenna-pattern null, sharply reducing the sky area.

**Why tempting:** Information seems tied to signal strength.

### GW170814's polarization test ruled out alternative theories of gravity. · SCH §12.4 p.399 (pdf 417)

**Correction:** It only disfavoured pure scalar or pure vector polarization, which no viable theory predicts; realistic alternatives add extra modes that the data could not test.

**Why tempting:** Headline results sound more sweeping than they are.

### Black-hole mergers can never produce light. · SCH §12.4 p.404 (pdf 422)

**Correction:** In a gas-rich environment (such as an AGN disk) a recoiling remnant could shock the gas and produce a flare, as proposed for GW190521.

**Why tempting:** Black holes are black and binary black holes in vacuum are dark.

### Detector noise is white, so the simple matched filter derived in the text is what LIGO uses. · SCH §12.3 p.380 (pdf 398)

**Correction:** Noise is strongly frequency dependent; real searches weight the correlation by the inverse noise spectrum in the frequency domain.

**Why tempting:** The derivation assumes white noise for clarity.

### Newtonian (gravity-gradient) noise can be removed with better vibration isolation. · SCH §12.3 p.378 (pdf 396)

**Correction:** It acts through gravity on the test masses exactly as a wave does and cannot be shielded, only reduced by moving underground or to space.

**Why tempting:** Seismic noise is handled by isolation, and the two share a source.

### Most heavy elements such as gold and uranium come from ordinary supernovae. · SCH §12.4 p.402 (pdf 420)

**Correction:** GW170817's kilonova supports compact mergers as a major r-process site.

**Why tempting:** Older textbooks attribute all heavy elements to supernovae.

### The LIGO signals directly prove that the merging objects have event horizons. · SCH §12.4 p.397 (pdf 415)

**Correction:** The black-hole label rests on the measured masses and the extreme compactness implied by the orbital frequency reached, which no conventional object could have; a direct test would require resolving the remnant's quasi-normal mode spectrum, which LIGO-Virgo did not claim for GW150914.

**Why tempting:** Popular accounts present the detection as 'seeing' black holes.


## Thought experiments

### Point particles with half an orbit left · SCH §12.2 p.363 (pdf 381)

**Setup:** Follow a point-mass inspiral whose frequency formally diverges and ask at what frequency meaningful power stops.

**Lesson:** Cutting at orbital phase pi gives a usable merger-frequency scale inversely proportional to chirp mass and an upper mass limit for any detector band.

**App idea:** Animated spiral with a phase-remaining counter and frequency readout that stops at psi = pi, with the learner dragging the mass slider.

### Halving the sampling rate · SCH §12.3 p.382 (pdf 400)

**Setup:** Resample a data stream at half the rate, averaging adjacent samples, and recompute the matched-filter threshold.

**Lesson:** The threshold is invariant; sensitivity depends on cycles of signal, not bookkeeping of samples.

**App idea:** Toggle sample rates on a noisy sinusoid while an SNR meter stays constant until the rate drops below twice the signal frequency.

### Rotate the sky basis until the polarizations are in quadrature · SCH §12.3 p.385 (pdf 403)

**Setup:** Reconstruct h+ and hx from two detectors in an arbitrary sky basis, then rotate the basis; start with an edge-on binary where one component can be made to vanish.

**Lesson:** The orbit's projected orientation and inclination are encoded in the polarization ellipse; the right basis exposes them.

**App idea:** Polarization-ellipse dial: rotating the basis mixes two sinusoids until they become 90 degrees out of phase; the ellipse's axis ratio gives iota.

### Wrong direction leaks signal into the null stream · SCH §12.3 p.389 (pdf 407)

**Setup:** Build a null stream for an assumed sky position when a real signal comes from elsewhere.

**Lesson:** Residual signal power in the null stream is a direction finder: the true direction nulls the signal.

**App idea:** Sky heat map of null-stream power updating as the learner drags a trial direction; a dark spot appears at the true source.

### Time slides · SCH §12.3 p.387 (pdf 405)

**Setup:** Shift one detector's data by more than the light-travel time and rerun the coincidence search many times.

**Lesson:** All coincidences found must be accidental, so their rate calibrates the background of real searches including glitches.

**App idea:** Two glitchy data strips with a draggable offset; count accidental coincidences per slide and build a background histogram.

### Two co-located, aligned detectors · SCH §12.3 p.393 (pdf 411)

**Setup:** Place two identical detectors at the same spot so they see identical background strain but independent instrumental noise, and multiply their outputs.

**Lesson:** The common random signal survives averaging while independent noise cancels, with SNR growing as N^(1/2) in power and N^(1/4) in amplitude.

**App idea:** Two noisy traces sharing a faint hidden common component; a running product-sum crawls upward out of a widening noise band.

### GW170817 at 150 Mpc · SCH §12.4 p.403 (pdf 421)

**Setup:** Move the observed event to a more typical distance.

**Lesson:** The gamma-ray burst would have gone undetected, localization would have ballooned and a days-long kilonova would likely have been missed: the first multimessenger event was lucky.

**App idea:** Distance slider showing SNR, sky-area ellipse and number of candidate galaxies growing, with a fading kilonova light curve.


## Applications and observations

- **LISA Galactic white-dwarf binaries and confusion noise** (astrophysical-system, SCH §12.2 p.362 (pdf 380)): Many thousands of non-evolving Galactic binaries, mostly white dwarfs, will be individually detected; below about 1 mHz their overlap forms confusion noise exceeding LISA's instrumental noise. Key numbers: Confusion dominates below ~1 mHz (text also quotes 0.1 mHz for background dominance)
- **Pulsar-timing band: supermassive black-hole binaries** (astrophysical-system, SCH §12.2 p.363 (pdf 381)): At tens of nHz the first expected signal is a background from 1e8-1e9 solar-mass binaries, possibly with resolvable individual systems. Key numbers: Tens of nHz; 1e8-1e9 solar masses
- **Binary neutron star in the ground band** (numerical-estimate, SCH §12.2 p.363 (pdf 381)): Two 1.4 solar-mass stars (chirp mass 1.2) would formally merge near 4 kHz, but hydrodynamic merger weakens the signal near 1 kHz; the signal lasts about 2.5 minutes from 20 Hz (Exercise 12.2). Key numbers: ~4 kHz formal; ~1 kHz actual; ~150 s from 20 Hz
- **LISA massive black-hole mergers** (astrophysical-system, SCH §12.2 p.366 (pdf 384)): Mergers of 1e4-1e7 solar-mass black holes, visible across the Universe back to the first galaxies, tracing galaxy mergers; an equal-mass 1e6 pair spends of order a month in band from 0.1 mHz. Key numbers: LISA band 0.1-100 mHz; ~1 month from 0.1 mHz (Exercise 12.2e quotes 26 days; our own estimate with Eq. 12.3 gives ~40 days)
- **Extreme mass-ratio inspirals for LISA** (astrophysical-system, SCH §12.2 p.366 (pdf 384)): Stellar black holes of 10-50 solar masses captured by 1e6 solar-mass holes enter the LISA band with 1e5-1e6 orbits remaining; tens per year to 100 Mpc or beyond, enabling strong-field tests including spin-orbit precession. Key numbers: 1e5-1e6 orbits; tens per year; >=100 Mpc
- **Continuous-wave search limits** (observation, SCH §12.2 p.368 (pdf 386)): No detections by mid-2021. All-sky searches 20 Hz-2 kHz limit ellipticity to about 1e-7 in places and amplitude to about 1e-25; targeted searches: Crab amplitude below 1e-25, ellipticity a few times 1e-5, about 4 percent of spin-down limit; some pulsars reach a few 1e-26 and 1e-7. Sco X-1 searches also null. Key numbers: epsilon < 1e-7; h < 1e-25; Crab h < 1e-25, 4% of spin-down
- **Stochastic background limits and prospects** (observation, SCH §12.2 p.370 (pdf 388)): O1 limit Omega_gw < 1.7e-7; Advanced LIGO design reaches about 1e-9, where the binary black hole background is predicted; LISA could reach about 1e-10; cosmological predictions span 1e-15 to above 1e-8, likely beyond even third-generation detectors; best probe may be CMB polarization. Key numbers: 1.7e-7 (O1); ~1e-9 (design); ~1e-10 (LISA); LIGO pair correlated below ~100 Hz (10 ms light-travel separation)
- **Interferometer noise sources and design trade-offs** (technology, SCH §12.3 p.377 (pdf 395)): Shot noise (high f), thermal noise (mid band), seismic noise (low f), suspension resonance lines, mains lines at 60 Hz (LIGO) and 50 Hz (Virgo); higher laser power reduces shot noise but heats mirrors and adds radiation pressure; KAGRA's cryogenic mirrors limit usable power; Newtonian noise motivates underground KAGRA and Einstein Telescope, and space-based LISA below 1 Hz. Key numbers: Sampling 8 or 16 kHz; LIGO band minimum ~20 Hz
- **Computing cost of an optimal all-sky pulsar search (Exercise 12.9)** (data-analysis, SCH p.411 (pdf 429)): Six months of 4 kHz data (6e10 samples), about 1e12-3e12 sky patches, a very large number of spin-down values, FFT per combination; the book's estimate totals about 5e35 floating-point operations, 3e28 flops to keep pace, versus 4e17 flops for the fastest 2021 supercomputer. Key numbers: 5e35 operations; ~1e11 supercomputers (the exercise's spin-down count appears overstated; see gaps)
- **GW150914** (detection-event, SCH §12.4 p.397 (pdf 415)): Binary black hole seen by both LIGO detectors; peak strain about 1e-21; chirp mass 30.8 (detector frame) or 28 (source frame); components 36 and 29, remnant 62 solar masses; 410 Mpc; SNR 24; 600 sq. deg.; graviton mass below 1.2e-22 eV; heavier than any known stellar black hole (previous maximum about 20). Key numbers: SNR 24; FAR 5e-6/yr; D_L 410 Mpc; m_graviton < 1.2e-22 eV
- **O1 additional events** (detection-event, SCH §12.4 p.398 (pdf 416)): GW151012 and GW151226 with chirp masses 15.2 and 8.9 solar masses, closer to X-ray binary black-hole masses. Key numbers: Chirp masses 15.2, 8.9
- **GW170814 and O2 black-hole events** (detection-event, SCH §12.4 p.399 (pdf 417)): First three-detector detection; sky area 87 sq. deg., 15 percent of GW150914's; pure tensor polarization favoured over pure scalar (Bayes factor ~1000) and pure vector (~200). O2 also had GW170729 at 2.8 Gpc. Key numbers: 87 sq. deg.; Bayes factors 1000, 200; D_L 600 Mpc
- **GW170817 multimessenger event** (detection-event, SCH §12.4 p.399 (pdf 417)): About 100 s in band; merger 12:41:04.4 UTC on 17 August 2017; GRB170817A 1.7 s later; Livingston glitch gated out; Virgo's weak response localized the source to ~31 sq. deg.; distance 40 plus or minus 8 Mpc; fewer than 100 galaxies in the volume; kilonova in NGC 4993 after ~11 hours; ~70 observatories; SNR 32; masses 1.44 and 1.28; tidal limits exclude very stiff equations of state; jet viewing angle 14-28 degrees; about 10 percent of professional astronomers took part. Key numbers: SNR 32; 40 Mpc; 1.7 s delay; 31 sq. deg. (16 final); H0 = 70.3 plus or minus 5 km/s/Mpc with jet constraint
- **Kilonova heavy elements and Earth** (observation, SCH §12.4 p.403 (pdf 421)): Spectra of the GW170817 ejecta support mergers as the main source of r-process elements; decays of uranium-238 and thorium-232 help heat Earth's interior, driving plate tectonics and sustaining the molten core and magnetic field.
- **GW170817 as a lucky event** (numerical-estimate, SCH §12.4 p.403 (pdf 421)): Expected nearest binary neutron star merger in a year is about 100 Mpc; 40 Mpc lies in the nearest ~7 percent of that volume ((0.4)^3 about 6 percent). Key numbers: ~7% of volume
- **GW190425** (detection-event, SCH §12.4 p.403 (pdf 421)): Total mass ~3.4 solar masses, heavier than known double neutron stars; significance from Livingston alone; 8284 sq. deg.; ~150-160 Mpc; no counterpart. Key numbers: M ~3.4; 159 Mpc; SNR 12.9
- **GW190521** (detection-event, SCH §12.4 p.404 (pdf 422)): Total ~150 solar masses, 85 plus 65, remnant 142 (an intermediate-mass black hole); four cycles over 0.1 s; SNR 14.7; possible AGN flare starting 34 days and peaking 50 days after merger, with a predicted repeat in ~2 years; higher multipoles moved distance from 4.1 to 5.3 Gpc. Key numbers: SNR 14.7; 5.3 Gpc; 0.1 s in band
- **GW190814** (detection-event, SCH §12.4 p.405 (pdf 423)): 23 plus 2.6 solar masses, mass ratio ~9:1, companion in the 2.2-6 solar-mass gap; inclination near 45 degrees and higher harmonics gave a good distance (241 Mpc) and a statistical H0 of 70 km/s/Mpc when combined with GW170817. Key numbers: 241 Mpc; 18 sq. deg.; H0 ~70
- **Catalogs and detection rates** (observation, SCH §12.4 p.406 (pdf 424)): O1: 3 events; O2: 8 events in about 9 months with Virgo in the last month; O3a (GWTC-2): 39 events in 5 months with a false-alarm threshold of 2 per year (about one expected false positive). Planned A+ upgrades (factor 3-4) and LIGO-India could give a dozen events per day by the mid-2020s according to the book. Key numbers: 3, 8, 39 events; FAR threshold 2/yr

## Historical notes

- **Joseph Weber (1960s):** Built the first resonant-bar gravitational-wave detectors, aiming to catch supernovae. — Began experimental gravitational-wave research and set the supernova burst as an early target. (SCH §12.2 p.368 (pdf 386))
- **Riccardo Giacconi (2002):** Nobel Prize for pioneering X-ray astronomy, including the discovery of X-ray binaries first seen with sounding rockets in the 1960s. — X-ray binaries showed compact objects can survive in binaries after the first supernova. (SCH §12.2 p.364 (pdf 382))
- **Russell Hulse, Joseph Taylor (1974):** Discovery of the binary pulsar, the first double neutron star system. — Started decades of work on neutron-star binary formation and provided evidence of systems surviving two supernovae. (SCH §12.2 p.365 (pdf 383))
- **Arno Penzias, Robert Wilson (1965):** Accidental discovery of the cosmic microwave background at Bell Labs (Nobel Prize 1978). — Offered as the electromagnetic analogue of a cosmological gravitational-wave background. (SCH §12.2 p.371 (pdf 389))
- **Peter Kafka (1977):** First mention of matched filtering, a radar technique, for gravitational-wave (bar detector) data analysis. — The method that makes weak binary signals detectable. (SCH §12.3 p.379 (pdf 397))
- **Yekta Gursel, Massimo Tinto, Lee Samuel Finn, Linqing Wen, Bernard Schutz (1989-2005):** Coherent network analysis and the null stream were introduced (Gursel and Tinto 1989), systematized (Bose et al.; Finn 2001, with the aperture-synthesis analogy) and promoted for localization and vetoes (Wen and Schutz 2005). — The foundation of multi-detector analysis used for glitch rejection and sky localization. (SCH §12.3 p.388 (pdf 406))
- **LIGO Scientific Collaboration (2015-2016):** GW150914 recorded on 14 September 2015 during an engineering run, first seen by collaboration members at the Albert Einstein Institute in Hanover, announced on 11 February 2016. — First direct detection of gravitational waves and first observation of a binary black hole, a century after general relativity. (SCH §12.4 p.396 (pdf 414))
- **LIGO-Virgo Collaboration, Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope teams (2017):** GW170817 detected by three detectors, gamma-ray burst 1.7 s later, kilonova found in NGC 4993 about 11 hours later by six teams; about 70 observatories followed. — Birth of gravitational-wave multimessenger astronomy, confirming merger origins of short bursts and kilonovae and giving the first standard-siren H0. (SCH §12.4 p.399 (pdf 417))
- **James Lattimer, David Schramm, David Eichler, Mario Livio, Tsvi Piran (1974, 1989):** Proposals that neutron star-black hole (1974) and neutron star-neutron star (1989) mergers eject neutron-rich matter producing heavy elements and are linked to gamma-ray bursts. — Predictions confirmed by GW170817's kilonova. (SCH §12.4 p.403 (pdf 421))
- **Bernard Schutz (1986):** Proposed measuring H0 from gravitational-wave distances by statistically combining redshifts of all galaxies in a localization volume. — Method applied to GW190814 without an identified host. (SCH §12.4 p.405 (pdf 423))
- **US military satellite programs (1960s):** Gamma-ray bursts discovered by satellites monitoring nuclear test bans. — Start of the path to the short-burst/merger connection. (SCH §12.2 p.365 (pdf 383))
- **Neutrino detector collaborations (1987):** Neutrinos from supernova SN1987A in the Large Magellanic Cloud were detected. — Cited as proof that particles can reach us directly from a collapsing core; the book argues gravitational waves are the only other such probe and that neutrino timing would anchor a Galactic supernova search. (SCH §12.2 p.368 (pdf 386))
- **Alberto Sesana (2016):** Pointed out that stellar-mass black-hole binaries like the LIGO sources could be seen first by LISA and then, months later, merge in the ground-based band. — The origin of the multiband early-warning idea the book quantifies with Eq. 12.3. (SCH §12.2 p.363 (pdf 381))
- **LIGO Scientific Collaboration (2015):** Before O1, predictions favoured about one detection per year at design sensitivity, most likely a neutron-star binary near 100 Mpc; O1 began two to three times less sensitive than design. — Explains why a strong binary black hole signal during the engineering run came as a double surprise. (SCH §12.4 p.396 (pdf 414))

## Notation and conventions

- **Geometrized units with solar-mass normalization:** Formulas use G = c = 1 and are rescaled by 1 solar mass (about 4.93e-6 s) and a benchmark frequency to give numerical coefficients (8.0 kHz, 4.0 kHz, 2.9 s, 16 kHz). — Eq. 12.5 must have G/c^4 restored for SI inputs. (SCH §12.2 p.363 (pdf 381))
- **Chirp and ringdown frequency conventions:** For inspirals f_gw = omega/pi with omega the orbital angular frequency (twice the orbital frequency); for ringdown f = omega/2 pi with omega the mode angular frequency. — Two different omegas in adjacent pages. (SCH §12.2 p.363 (pdf 381))
- **Mass ratio:** epsilon = m2/m1 with m1 the larger mass, stated range 0 < epsilon <= 0.5. — Probably intended <= 1; conflicts with other uses of epsilon (neutron-star asymmetry in Eq. 12.5; r - 1 in Exercise 12.7). (SCH §12.2 p.362 (pdf 380))
- **Sky-plane and detector bases:** Detector frame: arms along e_x and e_y, zenith e_z; source direction (theta, phi) measured from zenith and x-arm. Wave frame: z along propagation direction N, sky-plane basis e_x^R, e_y^R defining h+ and hx. (SCH §12.3 p.374 (pdf 392))
- **Double inner product:** A : B := A^{jk} B_{jk}, full contraction of two rank-2 spatial tensors. (SCH §12.3 p.375 (pdf 393))
- **Response symbols:** Delta_int = arm-length difference (displacement); delta_int = Delta_int/L = dimensionless strain; delta_a for detector a. — Delta_ab is not a Kronecker delta; D_ab are determinants. (SCH §12.3 p.375 (pdf 393))
- **Discrete data notation:** x_j data samples (M of them), w_j template samples (N nonzero), n_j noise with standard deviation sigma, Delta t sampling interval, D arrival index, ||w||^2 = sum of w_j^2, angle brackets = expectation, var = variance, (x*w)_k = sum_j x_j w_(j-k). — M is also mass and the response matrix; N is also the null stream label and samples count in the correlation section; the operation called convolution is a correlation. (SCH §12.3 p.379 (pdf 397))
- **Stochastic amplitude:** |h|^2 denotes the mean square amplitude <h^2> of a random background; sigma_1, sigma_2 are per-sample instrumental noise standard deviations. (SCH §12.2 p.370 (pdf 388))
- **Frequency resolution:** The book takes the frequency resolution of an observation of length T as 2/T. — Most signal-processing texts use 1/T for bin width; factors of two in the Doppler and spin-down estimates follow from this choice. (SCH §12.3 p.390 (pdf 408))
- **Common time T:** T denotes a common reference time at which the same wave phase passes each detector (or the geocentre or barycentre). — T also denotes signal duration, observation length and, in Exercise 12.9, the pulsar clock. (SCH §12.3 p.384 (pdf 402))
- **Inclination:** iota is the angle between the orbital angular momentum and the line of sight (0 face-on, 90 degrees edge-on). — In Exercise 12.4 the same Greek iota glyph is used for the imaginary unit. (SCH §12.3 p.385 (pdf 403))
- **Event names and times:** Events are named GWyymmdd; all times are in UTC. — From the footnote on p. 396. (SCH §12.4 p.396 (pdf 414))
- **Catalog quantities:** Table 12.1 lists source-frame masses (corrected with Eq. 9.163), luminosity distance D_L, network matched-filter SNR, false-alarm rate FAR per year, and 90 percent sky area Omega_sky in square degrees; M_f is the remnant mass. — Omega_sky is unrelated to Omega_gw or the spin angular velocity Omega. (SCH §12.4 p.398 (pdf 416))
- **Observing run labels:** O1, O2, O3 (O3a/O3b), O4, O5 denote successive LIGO-Virgo observing runs; A+ is the planned LIGO upgrade; 3G denotes third-generation ground detectors. (SCH §12.3 p.377 (pdf 395))

## Teaching gems

### Derive the matched-filter gain from a sampled sinusoid in white noise: the threshold goes from sigma/w_max to sigma/[w_max sqrt(N/2)], then argue via resampling that N really counts cycles. · SCH §12.3 p.381 (pdf 399)

**Why it works:** A two-line calculation delivers the central idea of modern GW data analysis, and the resampling twist prevents the most common misreading.

**App idea:** Noisy data strip with a hidden sinusoidal chirp; learner slides a template across it and watches the correlation peak rise out of the noise as signal length grows, with a sample-rate toggle that leaves SNR unchanged.

### Turn false-alarm probabilities into counts using real sampling rates, run lengths and template numbers (140,000 five-sigma excursions per year). · SCH §12.3 p.386 (pdf 404)

**Why it works:** Converts abstract tail probabilities into a visceral sense of why thresholds near SNR 8 are required.

**App idea:** False-alarm calculator with sliders for threshold a, sample rate, duration and number of templates, plotting expected false alarms on a log scale with a Gaussian tail shading.

### Count unknowns against data streams: two polarizations need two detectors; a third gives direction plus redundancy; each further detector adds a null stream. · SCH §12.3 p.388 (pdf 406)

**Why it works:** Structural insight before algebra, and it generalizes immediately to any network.

**App idea:** Network builder where adding detectors updates a counter of 'unknowns solved' and 'null streams available', with a demo glitch that shows up only in the null stream.

### Write detector response as the double contraction of a detector tensor with the wave tensor, then check special directions. · SCH §12.3 p.375 (pdf 393)

**Why it works:** Leverages the book's tensor-as-machine picture to tame messy angular formulas and makes network analysis frame-independent.

**App idea:** 3D interferometer with test-mass arms responding to a user-oriented plus/cross wave, showing the contraction numerically component by component.

### Contrast coherent and random integration: a pulsar 1e4 below the noise is routine, a background 1e4 below the noise needs 100,000 years. · SCH §12.3 p.394 (pdf 412)

**Why it works:** A single startling comparison makes N^(1/2) versus N^(1/4) unforgettable.

**App idea:** Race between two integrators on the same noise: one matched filter, one cross-correlator, with a time axis in years and a finish line at SNR 5.

### Treat the Doppler modulation of continuous waves as both curse and blessing: it breaks naive integration after about an hour, but makes Earth's orbit a telescope with sub-arcsecond resolution, which in turn explodes the search cost. · SCH §12.3 p.390 (pdf 408)

**Why it works:** Chains three consequences of one physical effect into a coherent story about why pulsar searches are computationally limited.

**App idea:** Orbiting detector and a sky grid that refines as observation time grows, with a counter of templates and a computing-time gauge compared against a supercomputer.

### Estimate merger frequency by stopping a point-particle inspiral at half an orbit, then confront the estimate with GW150914 and neutron stars. · SCH §12.2 p.363 (pdf 381)

**Why it works:** Shows how far a crude but principled cut-off gets you and why the detector band acts as a mass filter.

**App idea:** Mass slider moving a binary's chirp track across overlaid PTA, LISA and LIGO sensitivity bands, marking f_final and time in band.

### Explain that face-on binaries are loudest yet measure distance worst because the amplitude ratio is flat (iota^4/8) near face-on. · SCH §12.3 p.385 (pdf 403)

**Why it works:** A counterintuitive, quantitative result that directly explains the broad GW170817 H0 posterior.

**App idea:** Plot of |h+|/|hx| versus inclination with a 1 percent error band showing the allowed inclination range, linked to a distance-error bar and an H0 posterior.

### Use Virgo's near-silence during GW170817 as information: a null in one antenna pattern localizes the source. · SCH §12.4 p.400 (pdf 418)

**Why it works:** Memorable real story that teaches antenna patterns and Bayesian reasoning about non-detections.

**App idea:** Sky map where the learner sees LIGO-only localization band, then adds Virgo's weak signal to carve it down to the true patch.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **The sensitivity scalings are printed with the wrong sign of the exponent: h_min proportional to T^(1/2) for continuous waves and T^(1/4) for stochastic backgrounds, although the text says sensitivity improves with time.** — Readers may think detectable amplitude grows with observation time. *Suggestion:* State h_min proportional to T^(-1/2) and T^(-1/4) explicitly and show both as falling lines on a log plot.
- **Matched-filter derivation slips: Eq. 12.15 carries a spurious factor A in the middle expression; the text says the autocorrelation vanishes for |k - D| >= M (should be the template length N); Eq. 12.18 omits the template weights; Eq. 12.19 gives var = sigma^2 (w*w)_(k-D), whereas for white noise the variance is sigma^2 ||w||^2 at every lag (the two agree only at k = D, so Eq. 12.20 is still right); the 'integral' is a sum.** — Careful readers trying to reproduce the derivation get stuck or conclude the noise vanishes away from the peak. *Suggestion:* Present the corrected chain (mean A(w*w)_(k-D), variance sigma^2||w||^2, SNR A||w||/sigma) and animate that noise level is constant across lags while the mean peaks.
- **Normalization of the detector response: with d = L(e_x e_x - e_y e_y) and e+ = e^R_x e^R_x - e^R_y e^R_y, a wave from the zenith gives d:h = 2 L h+, whereas the physical difference of arm-length changes is L h+ (each arm changes by hL/2). The book's Eq. 12.9 therefore overstates the displacement by a factor 2 and its F+, Fx are twice the conventional antenna functions (our check).** — Learners comparing with standard antenna-pattern formulas (maximum 1) or computing absolute arm displacements get a factor 2 discrepancy. *Suggestion:* Use D = (1/2)(e_x e_x - e_y e_y) for the dimensionless detector tensor, or say explicitly that only ratios are used; Exercise 12.3's relative results are unaffected.
- **Eq. 12.29's bounds omit the factor 2 from the two-sided probability in Eq. 12.27, yet the numbers quoted (6e-7 at 5 sigma, etc.) include it; Exercise 12.5(a) writes exp(-z^2/2) on the right side where exp(-a^2/2) is meant.** — Computed false-alarm probabilities come out a factor of two smaller than the text's. *Suggestion:* Write 2 phi(a)/a and flag one-sided versus two-sided tails explicitly.
- **Exercise 12.11's Eq. 12.47 is missing a factor 1/2 in front of the square root (m1/M = 1/2 + (1/2)(1 - 4 mu/M)^(1/2)); Eq. 12.48 is consistent with the corrected form. Eq. 12.41's Fourier transform contains a stray factor w_j, and it asks for the transform of w while defining that of x.** — Students cannot verify the stated derivative or obtain m1 <= M. *Suggestion:* Use corrected forms in any app or tutor derivation; check limiting cases (equal masses give m1 = M/2).
- **Exercise 12.9's spin-down parametrization is dimensionally inconsistent: with t_p = t(1 - t/tau), df/dt = -2 f0/tau (not -2/tau), and a 2 kHz pulsar halving in 1000 years gives 1/tau of about 8e-12 per second, not 8e-8 s^-2; the number of spin-down bins (4e10) and final totals then look overstated by about 1e4 by our own check. Exercise 12.7(g) quotes a 7 percent change in 1 + cos^2 iota for iota between 0 and 0.53 rad, whereas it changes by about 13 percent (7 percent is the change in 1 + cos iota).** — Students following the steps get different numbers and lose confidence. *Suggestion:* Rework the estimates consistently; the qualitative conclusion (optimal all-sky search far out of reach) survives, since even 5e31 operations is hopeless.
- **Numerical mismatches in the source survey: for two 1.4 solar-mass neutron stars Eq. 12.1 gives 1 kHz at about 23 rad of orbital phase (roughly 3.7 orbits), not the 1.6 orbits stated; the same equation gives about 3.3 kHz at merger rather than 4 kHz; Exercise 12.2(e) claims 26 days where Eq. 12.3 gives about 40 days for an equal-mass 1e6 solar-mass pair from 0.1 mHz (our estimates); the stated mass-ratio range 0 < m2/m1 <= 0.5 excludes equal masses.** — Plug-in checks disagree with the text. *Suggestion:* Recompute in the app from the formulas and present book numbers as rounded order-of-magnitude values.
- **Table 12.1 lists GW190521's distance as 5.3 in a column labelled Mpc (it is 5.3 Gpc); masses in the table are source-frame (GW150914 chirp mass 28) while the text uses the detector-frame 30.8; GW170817's sky area is 16 sq. deg. in the table versus 31 in the text (initial versus final analyses).** — Apparent contradictions between table and prose. *Suggestion:* Label frames and analysis versions explicitly in any data display.
- **Historical details inconsistent or misprinted: 12.4 says LIGO was preparing on '15 September' with local times 03:50 (Livingston) and 01:50 (Hanford), whereas GW150914 occurred on 14 September 2015 at 09:50:45 UTC (04:50 CDT, 02:50 PDT, 11:50 CEST in Hanover); Virgo is said to have joined O2 on 1 August 2016 (it was 2017); O1 is described as spanning 16 months (it ran about four months, Sept 2015 to Jan 2016).** — Learners quoting the book will propagate wrong dates. *Suggestion:* Use the collaboration's published UTC times and run dates in app timelines.
- **Heuristics that are physically loose: the shot-noise f^(1/2) rise is derived from photons per sample, while real interferometer shot noise in strain rises roughly linearly above the arm-cavity pole; the r-process is described as fission of neutron-star chunks rather than rapid neutron capture; F+ and Fx are said to depend only on theta and phi though they also depend on the polarization angle.** — Plausible but incorrect mental models that later courses must undo. *Suggestion:* Tutor should present these as cartoons and give the standard mechanism (cavity response; neutron capture and beta decay; three-angle antenna functions).
- **Coloured noise and the noise-weighted inner product are essentially skipped: the derivation assumes white noise, and the frequency-domain treatment appears only as an FFT efficiency exercise.** — Learners cannot connect the toy filter to the sensitivity curves or to the standard SNR integral over |h(f)|^2/S_h(f). *Suggestion:* Add a bridge: whitening the data by the ASD turns coloured noise into white noise, recovering the toy result; show the integrand 4|h(f)|^2/S_h(f) on Figure 12.5.
- **Symbol overloading: epsilon (mass ratio, neutron-star asymmetry, r - 1), M (chirp mass script M, total mass, data length, response matrix), N (samples, template length, null stream), T (duration, common time, pulsar clock), Omega (spin rate, Omega_gw, Omega_sky), iota (inclination, imaginary unit).** — Easy to misapply formulas across subsections. *Suggestion:* Use distinct symbols in app materials (q for mass ratio, epsilon for ellipticity, N_s for samples).
- **Time-sensitive content is out of date relative to 2026: pulsar timing arrays reported evidence for a nHz background in 2023; GWTC-3 and O4 catalogs contain far more events (including further mass-gap and very massive systems); ESA adopted LISA in 2024 for a launch in the mid-2030s; the 'dozen events per day by mid-decade' projection has not materialised.** — Learners may treat mid-2021 limits and plans as current. *Suggestion:* Pair each 'as of mid-2021' statement with a live data panel or an update note maintained by the course.
- **The Crab spin-down statement (4 percent amplitude implies less than 0.1 percent of energy) is slightly off: 0.04 squared is 0.16 percent. The text also calls a cross-correlation a 'convolution' throughout.** — Minor confusion when checking numbers or reading signal-processing references. *Suggestion:* Quote about 0.2 percent; name the operation correlation and note the relation to convolution.

## Tutor notes

- Open with a question: 'If gravitational waves stretch LIGO's arms by less than a proton's width, how can anyone be sure a wiggle is real?' Use it to motivate the chapter's three pillars: predicted waveforms, statistics of noise, and multiple detectors.
- Before 12.3, check that the learner can state what h+ and hx are and how a ring of test particles responds; the detector-tensor formalism collapses without this.
- Teach matched filtering in this order: data model, naive threshold, correlation with template, mean and (correctly) constant variance, SNR = A||w||/sigma, sinusoid gives sqrt(N/2), then the resampling argument converting samples to cycles. Only then mention coloured noise and whitening.
- Check understanding with: 'Doubling the observation of a pulsar improves amplitude sensitivity by what factor? And for a stochastic background?' (sqrt 2 versus 2^(1/4)).
- When discussing significance, have the learner compute expected false alarms for their own choice of threshold, sample rate and template count before revealing why SNR about 8 is used; then introduce glitches to show why even that is not enough for one detector.
- Use the counting argument (two unknown waveforms) before deriving the null stream; ask how many null streams a five-detector network has (three).
- Common learner question: 'Why not just point more detectors at the source?' Answer with the unfocusable, all-sky nature of interferometers and antenna patterns (Figure 12.4).
- Common learner question: 'If the chirp mass is so precise, why are component masses fuzzy?' Walk through Exercise 12.11's sensitivity blow-up near equal masses (using the corrected Eq. 12.47).
- Common learner question: 'Why was GW170817 the most significant event if it was so weak?' Because it stayed in band for about 100 s, thousands of cycles, and matched filtering gains as the square root of cycles.
- For the H0 story, first make sure the learner understands luminosity distance from a chirp (Chapter 9), then show the inclination degeneracy with the amplitude-ratio plot, then Figure 12.6.
- Be explicit that numbers are as of mid-2021 and several printed equations have slips (T exponents, Eqs. 12.15, 12.18-12.19, the missing factor 2 in Eq. 12.29, Eq. 12.41, Eq. 12.47, the spin-down units in Exercise 12.9) and that Eq. 12.8 lacks the conventional factor 1/2; use corrected versions in derivations and say so if a learner quotes the book.
- For advanced learners, extend with the continuous noise-weighted inner product (h|h) = 4 Re integral h*(f)h(f)/S_h(f) df and the standard antenna functions with polarization angle; for beginners, stay with the discrete toy model and the stories.
- Suggested sequence for a 45-minute voice lesson: GW versus EM contrast (5 min), source zoo with scaling laws (10), matched filter toy model (10), false alarms and coincidence (8), null stream idea (5), GW170817 case study tying it together (7).
