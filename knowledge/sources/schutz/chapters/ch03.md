# SCH ch03 · Tensor analysis in special relativity

> Rebuilds the Minkowski dot product as a bilinear machine and from that single idea develops one-forms (pictured as stacks of surfaces), the gradient, outer products, symmetry, the metric's role in turning vectors into one-forms, general (M N) tensors, index gymnastics and the derivative of a tensor field in flat spacetime.

**Pages:** printed 56–83 · pdf 74–101 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 4/5

The algebra is elementary (linear maps, matrix inverses, chain rule) and every step is shown, but the abstraction shift from components to multilinear maps and the constant attention to index position are demanding for readers seeing dual spaces for the first time. The pictures and the 'why distinguish?' discussions make it friendlier than most tensor introductions. Heaviest spots: the transformation proofs with barred indices, the (M N) generalisation, and the terse §3.9 argument whose slot ordering is inconsistent.

## Role in the book

Chapter 2 gave four-vectors and the scalar product; Chapter 4 needs the stress-energy tensor and Chapters 5-6 need tensors in curvilinear coordinates and on manifolds. This chapter is the algebraic bridge: it installs the coordinate-free 'tensor = multilinear map' definition, the vector/one-form duality, and the metric as the extra structure that links them, all in the easy setting of Lorentz frames where basis vectors are the same everywhere. Its final remark (that differentiating components suffices only because the basis is constant) is deliberately planted as the doorway to the covariant derivative and hence to general relativity.

## Learning objectives

- State the definition of a (0 N) tensor as a real-valued map of N vectors that is linear in each slot, and verify that the dot product g(A,B) meets it.
- Compute the components of any tensor by feeding it basis vectors (and basis one-forms), and reconstruct its value on arbitrary arguments from those components.
- Explain why one-form components transform with Lambda^beta_alpha-bar (like basis vectors) while vector components use the inverse matrix, and prove that the contraction A^alpha p_alpha is frame independent.
- Construct the dual basis from a vector basis e_alpha via omega-tilde^alpha(e_beta) = delta^alpha_beta, and explain why changing one basis vector can change all four dual basis one-forms.
- Draw a one-form as a stack of parallel surfaces and evaluate it on a vector by counting surfaces crossed, including for the gradient on a contour map.
- Derive from the chain rule along a world line that the partial derivatives of a scalar field are the components of a one-form d-tilde phi, and check its transformation law via dx^beta/dx^alpha-bar = Lambda^beta_alpha-bar.
- Use the comma notation phi_{,alpha}, and identify the basis one-forms with the coordinate gradients d-tilde x^alpha.
- Define a normal one-form and an outward normal one-form without using the metric, and explain the odd behaviour of outward normal vectors on surfaces of different causal type.
- Form outer products of one-forms, show that omega-tilde^alpha tensor omega-tilde^beta is a basis for (0 2) tensors, and split any (0 2) tensor uniquely into symmetric and antisymmetric parts with the () and [] notation.
- Use the metric to map a vector to a one-form (V_alpha = eta_alpha beta V^beta) and the inverse metric to map back, recognising that in Minkowski components this just flips the sign of the time component.
- Compute magnitudes and inner products of one-forms with eta^alpha beta and classify one-forms as timelike, spacelike or null.
- Treat vectors as linear functions of one-forms, define (M N) tensors, write their component transformation law, and raise or lower any index with the metric.
- Show that the mixed components of the metric are the Kronecker delta and articulate why the metric is extra structure (Riemannian vs pseudo-Riemannian, non-metric spaces).
- Differentiate a tensor field along a world line, identify the gradient nabla T as a tensor with one extra covariant slot, and state which assumption (constant basis) will fail in curved spacetime.

## Teaching approach

A patient, geometry-first build-up that starts from something the reader already owns (the Minkowski dot product) and reinterprets it as a function of vectors, then climbs one rank at a time: (0 1), (0 2), metric map, (M N), derivative. Every new object is defined by what it does to arguments, components are derived as values on basis elements, and transformation laws are obtained rather than postulated. Pictures (sheets pierced by arrows, contour maps) carry the intuition for one-forms, and the minus sign in eta is used as the reason the vector/one-form distinction cannot be ignored in relativity. Frequent 'why distinguish?', 'circular reasoning?' and 'why the metric?' interludes anticipate learner objections.

**Style:** geometric-first, index-free-abstract, derivation-heavy, diagram-driven, analogy-driven, conversational-informal

**Narrative arc**

1. Rewrite the familiar scalar product as a double sum A^alpha B^beta eta_alpha beta and notice it is a rule turning two vectors into a number.
2. Promote that observation to the definition of a (0 N) tensor as a multilinear real-valued function; name the metric g and define components as values on basis vectors.
3. Specialise to (0 1) tensors: one-forms form a vector space (the dual space); derive their component transformation and prove contraction is invariant.
4. Build the dual basis from the vector basis, stress that components alone mean nothing without a basis, and give the transformation of basis one-forms.
5. Offer a metric-free picture of a one-form as stacked surfaces; justify it with the gradient of a scalar field sampled along a world line and with a contour map.
6. Check the gradient's transformation law, introduce comma derivatives, identify basis one-forms with coordinate gradients, and define normal one-forms.
7. Climb to (0 2) tensors: outer products, a 16-element basis, symmetric and antisymmetric parts; note that g is symmetric.
8. Let the metric with one empty slot turn a vector into a one-form; find the sign-flip rule, the inverse metric, the vector gradient, and explain via Euclidean space why elementary courses never needed the distinction.
9. Draw the duality out with row/column vectors and bra-kets, then define magnitudes and inner products of one-forms and unit normals, flagging peculiar outward normals.
10. Complete the duality by letting vectors eat one-forms, define (M N) tensors and their transformation, and defuse the 'circular definition' worry.
11. Generalise the metric map to index raising and lowering on any tensor, show eta^alpha_beta = delta, and discuss why the metric (rather than some other tensor) is the chosen extra structure.
12. Differentiate a (1 1) tensor field along a world line, extract the (1 2) gradient nabla T and the directional derivative nabla_U T, and point out that constant basis vectors are the hidden assumption that GR will remove.

**Signature moves**

- Definitions are phrased as 'feed the machine arguments'; components are always obtained by feeding basis elements, so every component formula is derived in two lines from linearity.
- Slot notation with blanks, g(V, _ ), used to manufacture new tensors by partially filling arguments (metric map in §3.6, fixing U in nabla T in §3.9).
- Transformation laws are presented as the 'only equation one can write with indices in the right places', teaching index placement as a consistency check.
- Surfaces-pierced-by-arrow picture of a one-form, justified physically through the gradient and a hiking contour map with explicit contour counts (1.5, 2, 0.5).
- Uses the lone minus sign in eta as the pedagogical lever: in Euclidean Cartesian coordinates vectors and one-forms have identical components, so the distinction only becomes visible in SR.
- Anticipatory 'objection' interludes (Why distinguish one-forms from vectors? Circular reasoning? Metric and nonmetric vector algebras) that voice and answer the reader's doubts.
- Draws on other parts of physics (matrix row/column vectors, Hilbert space with complex conjugation acting as the metric, Dirac bras and kets, phase space, spinor space) to show duality is ubiquitous.
- Ends the chapter by exposing the one simplifying assumption (constant basis) that will break, turning a technical remark into the motivation for the next part of the book.

## Section by section

### §3.1 The metric tensor — p.56 (pdf 74)

Expands two vectors on a frame basis and uses linearity of the dot product to write it as A^alpha B^beta eta_alpha beta. The numbers eta_alpha beta are thereby recast as the recipe that turns a pair of vectors into a single number, previewing the idea that such a recipe is what a tensor is.

**Concepts:** metric tensor, Einstein summation convention, components of a tensor

**Key moves**
- Use distinct dummy indices for the two expansions so the double sum is unambiguous.
- Pull the basis dot products out by linearity and recognise them as eta_alpha beta (Eq. 2.27).
- Read the result as a frame-invariant form of -A^0B^0 + A^1B^1 + A^2B^2 + A^3B^3 and as a rule mapping two vectors to a number.

### §3.2 Definition of tensors — p.56 (pdf 74)

Defines a (0 N) tensor as a real-valued function of N vectors that is linear in each argument, and checks that the dot product is a (0 2) tensor, named g. Emphasises that the definition refers to vectors, not their components, so a tensor gives frame-independent numbers; a scalar function is a (0 0) tensor. An aside separates a function f from its value f(x). Components in a frame are defined as the values on basis vectors, giving g(e_alpha, e_beta) = eta_alpha beta.

**Concepts:** tensor of type (0 N), multilinearity, metric tensor, scalar field as a (0 0) tensor, function versus value of a function, components of a tensor

**Key moves**
- State linearity in each argument separately (Eq. 3.2) and combine into g(aA + bB, C) = a g(A,C) + b g(B,C).
- Stress that the tensor acts on the vectors themselves; frame dependence enters only when components are taken.
- Define components as the tensor evaluated on basis vectors, so eta_alpha beta is the component array of g in a Lorentz frame.

### §3.3 The (0 1) tensors: one-forms — p.58 (pdf 76)

One-forms (covectors, dual vectors) are linear maps from vectors to numbers; with pointwise addition and scaling they form the dual vector space. Components p_alpha = p(e_alpha) give p(A) = A^alpha p_alpha, a contraction needing no metric. One-form components transform with the same matrix as basis vectors and hence inversely to vector components, which is proved to keep the contraction invariant; the old co/contra names are explained and retired. The dual basis omega-tilde^alpha is defined by omega-tilde^alpha(e_beta) = delta^alpha_beta, relates whole bases rather than pairs, and transforms like vector components. The section closes with the picture of a one-form as a family of surfaces whose value on a vector is the number of surfaces pierced.

**Concepts:** one-form, dual vector space, contraction, transformation law of one-form components, covariant and contravariant terminology, dual basis, Kronecker delta, picture of a one-form as stacked surfaces

**Key moves**
- Derive p(A) = A^alpha p_alpha from linearity (Eq. 3.8) and emphasise all plus signs: no metric involved.
- Evaluate p on the new basis e_beta-bar = Lambda^alpha_beta-bar e_alpha to get p_beta-bar = Lambda^alpha_beta-bar p_alpha (Eq. 3.9).
- Show Lambda^mu_alpha-bar Lambda^alpha-bar_beta = delta^mu_beta makes A^alpha-bar p_alpha-bar = A^beta p_beta (Eqs. 3.10a-d).
- Require p = p_alpha omega-tilde^alpha to reproduce Eq. 3.8 for all A, forcing omega-tilde^alpha(e_beta) = delta^alpha_beta (Eq. 3.12).
- Warn that a quadruple of numbers is meaningless without saying which basis (and which kind of basis) it refers to.
- Argue that a picture of a one-form must not depend on a metric, which rules out an arrow and leads to the sliced-space picture.

### §3.4 Gradient of a function is a one-form — p.62 (pdf 80)

A scalar field sampled along a world line changes at a rate d phi/d tau that the chain rule writes as a linear function of the four-velocity; hence the partial derivatives are the components of a one-form, the gradient d-tilde phi. A topographic map shows why: elevation gained along a displacement equals the number of contours crossed. The sliced-space picture is refined to straight parallel surfaces at a point, and it is argued that turning the gradient into an arrow needs a notion of length, i.e. a metric. The gradient's components transform correctly because dx^beta/dx^alpha-bar = Lambda^beta_alpha-bar. Comma notation is introduced, basis one-forms are identified with d-tilde x^alpha, d-tilde f is warned not to be an infinitesimal, and normal and outward normal one-forms are defined without a metric.

**Concepts:** gradient one-form, directional derivative of a scalar along a world line, four-velocity, picture of a one-form as stacked surfaces, transformation law of one-form components, comma notation for partial derivatives, coordinate basis one-forms dx^alpha, normal one-form

**Key moves**
- Parametrise a world line by proper time and apply the chain rule to phi(t(tau), x(tau), ...) (Eq. 3.14).
- Recognise d phi/d tau as linear in U, so the partials are one-form components (Eq. 3.15).
- Count contours on a map: displacement 1 gains 1.5 contours, 2 gains 2, 3 only 0.5, illustrating Delta h = (dh)(Delta x).
- Explain that 'steepest direction' requires 'per unit length', hence a metric, before a gradient vector exists.
- Compare the required one-form transformation (Eq. 3.16) with the chain-rule transformation (Eq. 3.17); they agree because x^beta = Lambda^beta_alpha-bar x^alpha-bar with constant coefficients (Eq. 3.18).
- Note x^alpha_{,beta} = delta^alpha_beta and compare with Eq. 3.12 to conclude that omega-tilde^alpha is d-tilde x^alpha (Eq. 3.20).

### §3.5 The (0 2) tensors — p.66 (pdf 84)

The outer product p tensor q is the simplest (0 2) tensor, and it is not commutative. A general (0 2) tensor has 16 components f_alpha beta = f(e_alpha, e_beta); demanding f = f_alpha beta omega-tilde^alpha beta forces the basis to be omega-tilde^alpha tensor omega-tilde^beta, so every such tensor is a sum of outer products. Symmetric and antisymmetric tensors are defined through argument exchange, symmetrisation and antisymmetrisation are introduced with (alpha beta) and [alpha beta] index brackets, and any (0 2) tensor splits uniquely into the two parts. The metric is symmetric.

**Concepts:** outer product, basis for (0 2) tensors, symmetric tensor, antisymmetric tensor, symmetric-antisymmetric decomposition, metric tensor

**Key moves**
- Define (p tensor q)(A,B) = p(A) q(B) and contrast with (q tensor p)(A,B).
- Expand f(A,B) = A^alpha B^beta f_alpha beta by bilinearity (Eq. 3.22).
- Evaluate the proposed expansion on (e_mu, e_nu) to get omega-tilde^alpha beta(e_mu, e_nu) = delta^alpha_mu delta^beta_nu, recognised as a product of dual-basis values (Eqs. 3.24-3.25).
- Define symmetry by argument exchange for all vectors, then specialise to basis vectors to get component symmetry.
- Add and subtract halves to write h_alpha beta = h_(alpha beta) + h_[alpha beta] (Eq. 3.35).

### §3.6 Metric as a mapping of vectors into one-forms — p.68 (pdf 86)

Feeding g a single vector leaves one empty slot, so g(V, _ ) is a one-form V-tilde with components V_alpha = eta_alpha beta V^beta: in a Lorentz frame the time component flips sign. Because eta has nonzero determinant an inverse eta^alpha beta exists, numerically equal to eta, making the vector/one-form map invertible and producing the vector gradient, which is orthogonal to level surfaces. Euclidean Cartesian coordinates hide the distinction because the metric is the identity. Dual spaces are illustrated by row and column vectors and by wave functions, where complex conjugation plays the metric's role (bras and kets). Magnitudes and inner products of one-forms are defined through their associated vectors and computed with eta^alpha beta; normal vectors, unit normals and the causal type of three-surfaces are defined, with surprising directions for outward normal vectors.

**Concepts:** metric as a map from vectors to one-forms, index lowering, inverse metric, vector gradient, dual vector space, magnitude of a one-form, inner product of one-forms, timelike, spacelike and null one-forms, normal vector and unit normal, causal classification of hypersurfaces, outward normal vector

**Key moves**
- Partially fill g to define V-tilde(A) = g(V, A) = V . A (Eqs. 3.37-3.38).
- Compute V_alpha = V-tilde(e_alpha) = eta_alpha beta V^beta and read off V_0 = -V^0, V_i = V^i (Eqs. 3.39-3.42).
- Invoke det(eta) = -1 to guarantee an inverse and define A^alpha = eta^alpha beta A_beta (Eq. 3.43).
- Show the vector associated with d-tilde phi is orthogonal to vectors tangent to constant-phi surfaces because its dot product equals the rate of change of phi.
- Contrast gradient components (Eq. 3.15) with vector-gradient components (Eq. 3.45) differing by the time-component sign.
- Collapse eta_alpha beta eta^beta nu = delta_alpha^nu to write the one-form magnitude with the inverse metric alone (Eqs. 3.48-3.51).
- Define the inner product of one-forms by polarisation (Eq. 3.52).

### §3.7 Finally: (M N) tensors — p.72 (pdf 90)

Completes the duality: a vector is a linear function of one-forms, V(p) = p(V) = <p, V>, so vectors themselves are (1 0) tensors. An (M 0) tensor eats M one-forms, and an (M N) tensor eats M one-forms and N vectors; its components carry M upper and N lower indices, and each index transforms with its own Lambda placed as index positions dictate. A 'circular reasoning?' interlude explains that mathematics is indifferent to which space is taken as primary; physics enters by identifying displacements (or gradients) with one of them.

**Concepts:** vectors as linear functions of one-forms, tensor of type (M N), transformation law of tensor components, covariant and contravariant terminology, contraction

**Key moves**
- Define V(p) := p(V) and introduce the symmetric pairing notation <p, V> (Eq. 3.54).
- Build V tensor W with components V^alpha W^beta and basis e_alpha tensor e_beta.
- Define R(omega-tilde^alpha; e_beta) = R^alpha_beta for a (1 1) tensor and transform it by substituting transformed basis elements (Eq. 3.55).
- Answer the circularity worry: vectors and one-forms are mutually defined but the algebra is consistent; physical meaning is imported by modelling choices.

### §3.8 Index 'raising' and 'lowering' — p.73 (pdf 91)

The metric and its inverse convert any tensor slot between vector-type and one-form-type, changing the tensor's type by moving one index down or up; the resulting tensors share a name and differ only by index position, and lowering different indices gives inequivalent tensors. In SR the rule is to flip the sign whenever a 0 index moves. Raising one index of eta gives the Kronecker delta, and raising both gives eta^alpha beta, so the metric's upper components are the matrix inverse of its lower ones. A closing discussion explains why a correspondence is needed only when scalar products matter (phase space manages without one), that the metric is added structure, distinguishes Riemannian from pseudo-Riemannian (and antisymmetric spinor) metrics, and notes that choosing other metric components leads to other spaces such as curved GR spacetime.

**Concepts:** index raising and lowering, mixed components of the metric, Kronecker delta, inverse metric, metric as added structure, Riemannian and pseudo-Riemannian metrics, nonmetric vector algebra, spinor space

**Key moves**
- Lower the second or the first upper index of T^alpha beta_gamma, or raise its lower index, to produce two inequivalent (1 2) tensors and a (3 0) tensor (Eqs. 3.56-3.58).
- State the SR sign rule for time versus space indices.
- Compute eta^alpha_beta = eta^alpha mu eta_mu beta = delta^alpha_beta as a matrix product of mutual inverses (Eqs. 3.59-3.60).
- Separate the question 'why any correspondence?' (needed for scalar products) from 'why the metric?' (whatever tensor does the job is called the metric).

### §3.9 Differentiation of tensors — p.75 (pdf 93)

Differentiating a tensor field raises its covariant rank by one, as with a function and its gradient. For a (1 1) field T expanded on a Lorentz basis, the derivative along a world line only hits the components because the basis one-forms and vectors are position independent; the chain rule then gives T^alpha_{beta,gamma} U^gamma. Since dT/d tau is a tensor for every U, the array T^alpha_{beta,gamma} defines a (1 2) tensor nabla T, and filling its derivative slot with U gives nabla_U T = dT/d tau. The chapter flags that constant basis vectors fail in curved spacetime, which is where GR begins.

**Concepts:** gradient of a tensor field, directional derivative of a tensor along a vector, constant basis in a Lorentz frame, four-velocity, comma notation for partial derivatives, tensor of type (M N)

**Key moves**
- Expand T = T^alpha_beta omega-tilde^beta tensor e_alpha and define dT/d tau as a limit of differences of tensors (Eqs. 3.61-3.62).
- Use constancy of the basis to move the derivative onto the components (Eq. 3.63).
- Apply the chain rule to get T^alpha_{beta,gamma} U^gamma (Eq. 3.64).
- Argue that because the result is a tensor for arbitrary U, stripping U off leaves a (1 2) tensor nabla T (Eqs. 3.65-3.66).
- Introduce nabla_U T for the derivative along U and flag that the constant-basis step will need replacing in GR (Eqs. 3.67-3.68).

### §3.10 Bibliography — p.76 (pdf 94)

Points readers who want more of the geometric view of tensors to the early chapters of Misner, Thorne and Wheeler, to Schutz's Geometrical Methods of Mathematical Physics and to Bishop and Goldberg, and notes that most physics texts outside relativity only treat Cartesian tensors, citing Bourne and Kendall.

**Concepts:** tensor of type (M N)

**Key moves**

**Concepts:** one-form, dual basis, gradient one-form, normal one-form, outer product, symmetric-antisymmetric decomposition, index raising and lowering, gradient of a tensor field, projection tensor, Lorentz transformation as metric-preserving map, Lorentz group, null basis

## Concepts

### metric tensor

*mathematical-object · core* · also: g, Minkowski metric, eta_alpha beta as components of g

The symmetric (0 2) tensor g whose value on two vectors is their scalar product; in any Lorentz frame its components g(e_alpha, e_beta) are eta_alpha beta = diag(-1, 1, 1, 1).

**How introduced:** Reached by rewriting the chapter-2 dot product as the double sum A^alpha B^beta eta_alpha beta and noticing it is a rule producing a number from two vectors; named g once the tensor definition is in place, and later shown to be symmetric and to double as the vector-to-one-form converter.

**Prerequisites:** scalar product of four-vectors, tensor of type (0 N), multilinearity

$$
\vec A\cdot\vec B = A^\alpha B^\beta \eta_{\alpha\beta}
$$

$$
\mathbf g(\vec A,\vec B) := \vec A\cdot\vec B
$$

$$
\mathbf g(\vec e_\alpha,\vec e_\beta)=\eta_{\alpha\beta}
$$

**Notes:** The book says the name will be 'justified later'; the justification is the tensor definition of §3.2 and the mapping role of §3.6.

**Where:** SCH §3.1 p.56 (pdf 74); SCH §3.2 p.57 (pdf 75); SCH §3.2 p.58 (pdf 76); SCH §3.5 p.68 (pdf 86); SCH §3.6 p.68 (pdf 86)

### tensor of type (0 N)

*definition · core* · also: covariant tensor of rank N, (0 N) tensor

A real-valued function of N vector arguments that is linear in each argument separately; its value does not depend on any frame, only its components do.

**How introduced:** Stated immediately after observing that eta_alpha beta encodes a two-vector-to-number rule; the dot product is checked against the definition, and a scalar function is noted as the N = 0 case.

**Prerequisites:** multilinearity, four-vector

**Notes:** The (0 N) label is used before it is explained; the upper/lower numbers are justified only in §3.7.

**Where:** SCH §3.2 p.56 (pdf 74); SCH §3.2 p.57 (pdf 75)

### multilinearity

*definition · developed* · also: linearity in each argument, bilinearity (N = 2)

The property that a function of several vector arguments is linear in every argument when the others are held fixed: scaling one argument scales the value, and additive splitting of one argument splits the value.

**How introduced:** Spelled out for the dot product (Eq. 3.2 for the first slot and its partner for the second) and summarised in g(aA + bB, C) = a g(A,C) + b g(B,C); the reader is told to study it carefully because every later component formula comes from it.

**Prerequisites:** vector space

$$
\mathbf g(\alpha\vec A+\beta\vec B,\vec C)=\alpha\,\mathbf g(\vec A,\vec C)+\beta\,\mathbf g(\vec B,\vec C)
$$

**Where:** SCH §3.2 p.57 (pdf 75)

### components of a tensor

*definition · core* · also: tensor components in a frame

The frame-dependent numbers obtained by evaluating a tensor on the basis vectors (and, for upper indices, basis one-forms) of a chosen frame; together with the basis they reconstruct the tensor.

**How introduced:** Introduced right after the aside on functions, applied first to g to recover eta_alpha beta, then reused as the uniform recipe for one-forms (Eq. 3.7), (0 2) tensors (Eq. 3.21) and (M N) tensors.

**Prerequisites:** tensor of type (0 N), basis vectors of a frame

$$
p_\alpha := \tilde p(\vec e_\alpha)
$$

$$
f_{\alpha\beta}:=\mathbf f(\vec e_\alpha,\vec e_\beta)
$$

$$
R^\alpha{}_\beta := \mathbf R(\tilde\omega^\alpha;\vec e_\beta)
$$

**Where:** SCH §3.2 p.58 (pdf 76); SCH §3.3 p.59 (pdf 77); SCH §3.5 p.66 (pdf 84); SCH §3.7 p.72 (pdf 90)

### scalar field as a (0 0) tensor

*definition · introduced* · also: function of position, scalar

A real-valued function of position such as f(t, x, y, z), classified as a tensor that takes no vector arguments at all.

**How introduced:** A one-line remark after the tensor definition; later used as the starting rung for differentiation (the gradient of a (0 0) tensor is a (0 1) tensor).

**Prerequisites:** tensor of type (0 N)

**Where:** SCH §3.2 p.57 (pdf 75); SCH §3.9 p.75 (pdf 93)

### one-form

*mathematical-object · core* · also: covector, covariant vector, dual vector, (0 1) tensor

### dual vector space

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: dual space, space of one-forms

The vector space of all linear real-valued functions on a given vector space; it has the same dimension, and each basis of the original space induces a dual basis.

**How introduced:** Named once one-forms are shown to add and scale like vectors; the word 'dual' is tied to the inverse transformation of components; later illustrated by row versus column vectors and by bras versus kets.

**Prerequisites:** one-form, vector space

### contraction

*operation · core* · also: contraction of a one-form with a vector, natural pairing, <p, V>

The metric-free number obtained by applying a one-form to a vector, p(V) = p_alpha V^alpha = sum with all plus signs; more generally, summing an upper index against a lower index.

\vec V(\tilde p):=\tilde p(\vec V)=p_\alpha V^\alpha:=\langle\tilde p,\vec V\rangle

**Notes:** The book calls contraction more fundamental than the scalar product because it needs no additional tensor.

### transformation law of one-form components

### covariant and contravariant terminology

*convention · introduced* · also: co/contra naming, M-times contravariant and N-times covariant

Older names: indices or objects whose components transform like basis vectors are 'covariant' (lower indices, one-forms); those transforming oppositely are 'contravariant' (upper indices, vectors). An (M N) tensor is M-times contravariant and N-times covariant.

**How introduced:** Explained etymologically after the proof of contraction invariance and partly retired as old-fashioned because it conflates a change of basis (new vectors from old) with a change of components (same object, new description); revived in §3.7 for index language.

**Prerequisites:** transformation law of one-form components

### dual basis

*definition · core* · also: basis one-forms, omega-tilde^alpha, basis dual to e_alpha

Given a basis e_beta of vectors, the four one-forms omega-tilde^alpha fixed by omega-tilde^alpha(e_beta) = delta^alpha_beta; in this basis any one-form expands as p = p_alpha omega-tilde^alpha with p_alpha = p(e_alpha).

**How introduced:** Motivated by wanting a one-form basis in which the already-defined components p(e_alpha) are the expansion coefficients; the defining condition is forced by requiring p(A) = p_alpha A^alpha for all A. ### mixed components of the metric

*identity · developed* · also: eta^alpha_beta

Raising one index of the metric gives the Kronecker delta, eta^alpha_beta = eta^alpha mu eta_mu beta = delta^alpha_beta; raising the other index gives back eta^alpha beta, so the inverse metric is the metric with both indices up.

**How introduced:** Computed as the matrix product of mutual inverses, prompting the observation that for g the upper-index components coincide with the inverse matrix of the lower-index components.

**Prerequisites:** inverse metric, index raising and lowering, Kronecker delta

$$
\eta^\alpha{}_\beta\equiv\eta^{\alpha\mu}\eta_{\mu\beta}=\delta^\alpha{}_\beta
$$

**Notes:** The book adds that g is the only tensor with this property, which is looser than it sounds (see gaps).

**Where:** SCH §3.8 p.74 (pdf 92)

### metric as added structure

*principle · developed* · also: metric structure, why the metric

A bare vector space and its dual carry no scalar product; choosing a nondegenerate symmetric (0 2) tensor to identify vectors with one-forms is extra structure, and whichever tensor plays that role is by definition the metric. Special relativity is the choice of components eta_alpha beta.

**How introduced:** A two-part discussion at the end of §3.8: first why any identification is needed (only if scalar products matter), then why the metric (any tensor doing the job would be called the metric), closing with the pointer that other metric components lead to other geometries, including curved GR spacetime.

**Prerequisites:** metric as a map from vectors to one-forms, dual vector space

**Where:** SCH §3.8 p.74 (pdf 92); SCH §3.8 p.75 (pdf 93)

### Riemannian and pseudo-Riemannian metrics

*definition · introduced* · also: positive-definite metric, indefinite metric, Lorentzian metric

A Riemannian metric gives every nonzero vector a positive squared magnitude; a pseudo-Riemannian one, like Minkowski's, allows negative and zero values.

**How introduced:** Named briefly while discussing the metric as structure; the book also mentions antisymmetric 'metrics' on two-dimensional spinor space as out of scope.

**Prerequisites:** metric as added structure

**Where:** SCH §3.8 p.74 (pdf 92); SCH §3.8 p.75 (pdf 93)

### nonmetric vector algebra

*definition · mention* · also: vector spaces without a metric, phase space example

A setting with vectors, one-forms and tensors but no preferred identification between vectors and one-forms, so vectors have no scalar product; classical and quantum phase space are cited as places where a metric is not important.

**How introduced:** Hypothetical scenario used to separate what the tensor algebra provides from what the metric adds.

**Prerequisites:** metric as added structure

**Where:** SCH §3.8 p.74 (pdf 92)

### spinor space

*mathematical-object · mention* · also: antisymmetric metric

A two-dimensional complex vector space equipped with an antisymmetric bilinear form that plays the role of a metric; fundamental in physics but beyond this book.

**How introduced:** Mentioned only as an example that a 'metric' need not be symmetric.

**Prerequisites:** metric as added structure

**Where:** SCH §3.8 p.75 (pdf 93)

### gradient of a tensor field

*operation · core* · also: nabla T, covariant derivative in a Lorentz frame

For a tensor field T of type (M N), the (M N+1) tensor nabla T whose components in a Lorentz frame are the partial derivatives of T's components, e.g. T^alpha_{beta,gamma} for a (1 1) field; differentiation adds one covariant slot.

**How introduced:** Derived by differentiating a (1 1) field along a world line, noting the result is a tensor for every tangent U, and removing U to leave a tensor with one more vector slot; justified via the blank-slot argument list nabla T( , U, ).

**Prerequisites:** tensor of type (M N), gradient one-form, constant basis in a Lorentz frame, comma notation for partial derivatives

$$
\nabla\mathbf T:=T^\alpha{}_{\beta,\gamma}\,\tilde\omega^\beta\otimes\tilde\omega^\gamma\otimes\vec e_\alpha
$$

**Notes:** Called nabla T rather than d-tilde T because d-tilde on tensors is reserved by mathematicians for a different operation (the exterior derivative). Exercise 3.29 proves the Leibniz rule.

**Where:** SCH §3.9 p.75 (pdf 93); SCH §3.9 p.76 (pdf 94)

### directional derivative of a tensor along a vector

*operation · core* · also: nabla_U T, dT/d tau

The rate of change of a tensor field along a curve with tangent U, nabla_U T, obtained by filling the derivative slot of nabla T with U; its components are T^alpha_{beta,gamma} U^gamma.

**How introduced:** Defined as the limit of differences of tensors at neighbouring points on a world line (Eq. 3.62), computed with the chain rule, and packaged in new notation (Eqs. 3.67-3.68).

**Prerequisites:** gradient of a tensor field, four-velocity, directional derivative of a scalar along a world line

$$
\frac{d\mathbf T}{d\tau}=\nabla_{\vec U}\mathbf T\to\{T^\alpha{}_{\beta,\gamma}U^\gamma\}
$$

**Where:** SCH §3.9 p.75 (pdf 93); SCH §3.9 p.76 (pdf 94)

### constant basis in a Lorentz frame

*principle · developed* · also: position-independent basis vectors

In an inertial (Lorentz) frame the basis vectors and basis one-forms are the same at every event, so derivatives of tensor fields act only on components; this fails for curvilinear coordinates and in curved spacetime.

**How introduced:** Used silently in the step from Eq. 3.62 to 3.63 and then highlighted as the chapter's final sentence: dropping it will be the entry point into GR.

**Prerequisites:** dual basis, basis vectors of a frame

$$
\tilde\omega^\alpha(\tau+\Delta\tau)=\tilde\omega^\alpha(\tau)
$$

**Where:** SCH §3.9 p.75 (pdf 93); SCH §3.9 p.76 (pdf 94)

### Einstein summation convention

*convention · revisited* · also: summation over repeated indices, dummy indices

A repeated index appearing once up and once down is summed over 0-3; distinct summations must use distinct dummy index names.

**How introduced:** Revisited throughout with repeated parenthetical reminders to use different dummy indices for independent sums; Exercise 3.1 contrasts M_alpha beta A^alpha B^beta with the (wrong) single sum over M_alpha alpha A^alpha B^alpha.

**Where:** SCH §3.1 p.56 (pdf 74); SCH §3.3 p.60 (pdf 78); SCH §3.5 p.66 (pdf 84); SCH §3.6 p.71 (pdf 89); SCH p.76 (pdf 94)

### Lorentz transformation matrix

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: Lambda^alpha-bar_beta

The constant matrix relating coordinates, components and basis vectors of two Lorentz frames; vector components use Lambda^alpha-bar_beta and one-form components and basis vectors use its inverse Lambda^beta_alpha-bar.

**How introduced:** Revisited from chapters 1-2 to derive every transformation law, and identified with coordinate partial derivatives, dx^beta/dx^alpha-bar = Lambda^beta_alpha-bar, because the transformation is linear with constant coefficients.

$$
\partial x^\beta/\partial x^{\bar\alpha}=\Lambda^\beta{}_{\bar\alpha}
$$

**Where:** SCH §3.3 p.59 (pdf 77); SCH §3.4 p.64 (pdf 82); SCH §3.7 p.73 (pdf 91)

### Lorentz transformation as metric-preserving map

*definition · introduced* · also: general definition of a Lorentz transformation

A Lorentz transformation is any linear change of basis whose matrix leaves the metric components unchanged: eta_alpha-bar beta-bar = Lambda^mu_alpha-bar Lambda^nu_beta-bar eta_mu nu, or in matrix form eta = Lambda^T eta Lambda.

**How introduced:** Proposed in Exercise 3.32 as a more general definition, justified because every Lorentz frame must see components eta; the reader checks that the x-boost satisfies it and that products of such matrices do too.

**Prerequisites:** transformation law of tensor components, metric tensor

$$
\eta_{\bar\alpha\bar\beta}=\Lambda^\mu{}_{\bar\alpha}\Lambda^\nu{}_{\bar\beta}\eta_{\mu\nu}
$$

$$
(\eta)=(\Lambda)^T(\eta)(\Lambda)
$$

**Where:** SCH p.82 (pdf 100); SCH p.83 (pdf 101)

### Lorentz group

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: O(1,3), L(4)

The group of all matrices preserving the Minkowski metric under congruence, with matrix multiplication as the group operation; its elements have determinant +1 or -1, the +1 elements form a subgroup, and the rotation group O(3) sits inside it.

**How introduced:** Named in Exercise 3.33 after Exercise 3.32(c) shows closure under multiplication; the exercise works through identity, inverses, determinants and subgroups.

**Prerequisites:** Lorentz transformation as metric-preserving map, orthogonal matrix

**Where:** SCH p.83 (pdf 101)

### orthogonal matrix

*definition · introduced* · also: O(3)

A matrix equal to the transpose of its inverse; such matrices are exactly those preserving the Euclidean metric delta_ij, and for them vector and one-form components transform identically.

**How introduced:** Exercise 3.20 uses it to explain why elementary Cartesian vector analysis never needs to distinguish vectors from one-forms.

**Prerequisites:** transformation law of one-form components

$$
\delta_{\bar i\bar j}=\Lambda^k{}_{\bar i}\Lambda^l{}_{\bar j}\delta_{kl}
$$

**Where:** SCH p.80 (pdf 98)

### projection tensor

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: P_mu nu = eta_mu nu + U_mu U_nu, spatial projector

For a unit timelike vector U, the tensor P with components eta_mu nu + U_mu U_nu, which projects any vector onto the subspace orthogonal to U, leaves already-orthogonal vectors unchanged, and acts as the metric on that subspace.

**How introduced:** Exercise 3.31, including the generalisation to any non-null vector q (with q_mu q_nu divided by q^alpha q_alpha) and the question of why null vectors fail.

**Prerequisites:** index raising and lowering, metric tensor, four-velocity

$$
P_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+U_\mu U_\nu
$$

$$
V_\perp^\alpha=P^\alpha{}_\beta V^\beta
$$

**Where:** SCH p.82 (pdf 100)

### null basis

*definition · introduced* · also: light-cone basis, e_u, e_v

A basis containing null vectors, such as e_u = (e_t - e_x)/2 and e_v = (e_t + e_x)/2 tied to coordinates u = t - x and v = t + x; its vectors are null and mutually non-orthogonal, so the metric components are off-diagonal.

**How introduced:** Exercise 3.34, which asks for the basis, metric components and the relevant one-forms in terms of d-tilde t and d-tilde x.

**Prerequisites:** components of a tensor, metric tensor, dual basis

**Where:** SCH p.83 (pdf 101)

## Key equations

### (3.1) Scalar product in components · central · SCH §3.1 p.56 (pdf 74)

$$
\vec A\cdot\vec B=A^\alpha B^\beta\eta_{\alpha\beta}=-A^0B^0+A^1B^1+A^2B^2+A^3B^3
$$

The dot product of two four-vectors is a double sum of their components weighted by the metric components; this is the seed from which the tensor concept grows.

**Symbols:** A^alpha, B^beta: vector components in frame O; eta_alpha beta = diag(-1,1,1,1).

### (3.2) Linearity of the dot product in the first argument · supporting · SCH §3.2 p.57 (pdf 75)

$$
(\alpha\vec A)\cdot\vec B=\alpha(\vec A\cdot\vec B),\qquad(\vec A+\vec B)\cdot\vec C=\vec A\cdot\vec C+\vec B\cdot\vec C
$$

Scaling or splitting the first argument scales or splits the value; the same holds in the second slot. This is the linearity demanded of a tensor.

**Symbols:** alpha: real number; A, B, C: vectors.

### (3.3) Metric tensor defined · central · SCH §3.2 p.57 (pdf 75)

$$
\mathbf g(\vec A,\vec B):=\vec A\cdot\vec B
$$

Names the dot-product rule as a function g of two vectors, so the metric becomes an object in its own right.

**Symbols:** g: metric tensor.

### (3.4) Bilinearity of g · supporting · SCH §3.2 p.57 (pdf 75)

$$
\mathbf g(\alpha\vec A+\beta\vec B,\vec C)=\alpha\,\mathbf g(\vec A,\vec C)+\beta\,\mathbf g(\vec B,\vec C)
$$

Compact statement of linearity in the first slot (and similarly the second).

**Symbols:** alpha, beta: real numbers.

### (3.5) Components of the metric · central · SCH §3.2 p.58 (pdf 76)

$$
\mathbf g(\vec e_\alpha,\vec e_\beta)=\vec e_\alpha\cdot\vec e_\beta=\eta_{\alpha\beta}
$$

Feeding basis vectors to g yields its components; in a Lorentz frame these are the Minkowski values.

**Symbols:** e_alpha: basis vectors of frame O.

### (3.6a,b) Vector-space operations on one-forms · supporting · SCH §3.3 p.58 (pdf 76)

$$
\tilde s=\tilde p+\tilde q,\ \tilde r=\alpha\tilde p;\qquad \tilde s(\vec A)=\tilde p(\vec A)+\tilde q(\vec A),\ \tilde r(\vec A)=\alpha\,\tilde p(\vec A)
$$

Sums and multiples of one-forms are defined by their values on vectors, making the one-forms a vector space (the dual space).

**Symbols:** p, q: one-forms; alpha: real number.

### (3.7) Components of a one-form · central · SCH §3.3 p.59 (pdf 77)

$$
p_\alpha:=\tilde p(\vec e_\alpha)
$$

A one-form's components are its values on the basis vectors; lower index signals one-form.

**Symbols:** p_alpha: components of p-tilde.

### (3.8) Contraction of a one-form with a vector · central · SCH §3.3 p.59 (pdf 77)

$$
\tilde p(\vec A)=\tilde p(A^\alpha\vec e_\alpha)=A^\alpha\tilde p(\vec e_\alpha)=A^\alpha p_\alpha
$$

By linearity the value of a one-form on a vector is the all-plus-sign sum of products of components, needing no metric.

### (3.9) Transformation of one-form components · central · SCH §3.3 p.59 (pdf 77)

$$
p_{\bar\beta}:=\tilde p(\vec e_{\bar\beta})=\tilde p(\Lambda^\alpha{}_{\bar\beta}\vec e_\alpha)=\Lambda^\alpha{}_{\bar\beta}\,p_\alpha
$$

One-form components change with the same matrix as basis vectors (e_beta-bar = Lambda^alpha_beta-bar e_alpha), i.e. inversely to vector components.

**Symbols:** Bars mark the new frame; Lambda^alpha_beta-bar: inverse of the matrix Lambda^alpha-bar_beta used for vector components.

### (3.10a-d) Frame invariance of contraction · central · SCH §3.3 p.59 (pdf 77)

$$
A^{\bar\alpha}p_{\bar\alpha}=(\Lambda^{\bar\alpha}{}_\beta A^\beta)(\Lambda^\mu{}_{\bar\alpha}p_\mu)=\Lambda^\mu{}_{\bar\alpha}\Lambda^{\bar\alpha}{}_\beta A^\beta p_\mu=\delta^\mu{}_\beta A^\beta p_\mu=A^\beta p_\beta
$$

Because vector and one-form components transform with mutually inverse matrices, their contraction is the same in every frame.

**Symbols:** The export drops the bars; the printed page (pdf 77) has barred indices on the left side and on the Lambdas.

### (3.11) Expansion in the dual basis · supporting · SCH §3.3 p.60 (pdf 78)

$$
\tilde p=p_\alpha\tilde\omega^\alpha
$$

A one-form is the component-weighted sum of four basis one-forms.

**Symbols:** omega-tilde^alpha: basis one-forms.

### (3.12) Dual basis condition · central · SCH §3.3 p.60 (pdf 78)

$$
\tilde\omega^\alpha(\vec e_\beta)=\delta^\alpha{}_\beta
$$

The basis one-forms are defined by giving 1 on their partner basis vector and 0 on the others, which makes Eq. 3.11 reproduce Eq. 3.8.

### Components of the dual basis · derivation-step · SCH §3.3 p.60 (pdf 78)

$$
\tilde\omega^0\to_{\mathcal O}(1,0,0,0),\ \tilde\omega^1\to_{\mathcal O}(0,1,0,0),\ \tilde\omega^2\to_{\mathcal O}(0,0,1,0),\ \tilde\omega^3\to_{\mathcal O}(0,0,0,1)
$$

Each dual basis one-form has a single unit component in its own frame.

### (3.13) Transformation of basis one-forms · supporting · SCH §3.3 p.61 (pdf 79)

$$
\tilde\omega^{\bar\alpha}=\Lambda^{\bar\alpha}{}_\beta\,\tilde\omega^\beta
$$

Basis one-forms transform like vector components, opposite to one-form components; proof is Exercise 3.7.

### (3.14) Rate of change of a scalar along a world line · central · SCH §3.4 p.63 (pdf 81)

$$
\frac{d\phi}{d\tau}=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\frac{dt}{d\tau}+\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{d\tau}+\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{d\tau}+\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}\frac{dz}{d\tau}=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}U^t+\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}U^x+\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}U^y+\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}U^z
$$

The chain rule expresses the change of phi seen along a world line as a linear function of the four-velocity, which is exactly what a one-form does.

**Symbols:** phi: scalar field; tau: proper time; U: four-velocity.

### (3.15) Gradient one-form components · central · SCH §3.4 p.63 (pdf 81)

$$
\tilde d\phi\to_{\mathcal O}\left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t},\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x},\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y},\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}\right)
$$

The gradient of a scalar is the one-form whose components are the partial derivatives.

### (3.16) Required transformation of gradient components · derivation-step · SCH §3.4 p.64 (pdf 82)

$$
(\tilde d\phi)_{\bar\alpha}=\Lambda^\beta{}_{\bar\alpha}(\tilde d\phi)_\beta
$$

What the one-form law demands of the gradient.

### (3.17) Chain-rule transformation of partial derivatives · derivation-step · SCH §3.4 p.64 (pdf 82)

$$
(\tilde d\phi)_{\bar\alpha}=\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x^{\bar\alpha}}(\tilde d\phi)_\beta
$$

How partial derivatives actually change under a change of coordinates.

### (3.18) Coordinate derivative equals the inverse Lorentz matrix · central · SCH §3.4 p.64 (pdf 82)

$$
\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x^{\bar\alpha}}=\Lambda^\beta{}_{\bar\alpha}
$$

Since old coordinates are constant linear combinations of new ones, the Jacobian is the transformation matrix, reconciling Eqs. 3.16 and 3.17; the book calls this identity fundamental.

**Symbols:** Follows from x^beta = Lambda^beta_alpha-bar x^alpha-bar with constant coefficients.

### (3.19) Comma notation · supporting · SCH §3.4 p.65 (pdf 83)

$$
\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x^\alpha}:=\phi_{,\alpha}
$$

Shorthand for partial derivatives, with the index lowered to reflect one-form behaviour.

### Derivatives of coordinates · derivation-step · SCH §3.4 p.65 (pdf 83)

$$
x^\alpha{}_{,\beta}\equiv\delta^\alpha{}_\beta
$$

Each coordinate changes only along its own direction, at unit rate.

### (3.20) Basis one-forms are coordinate gradients · central · SCH §3.4 p.65 (pdf 83)

$$
\tilde dx^\alpha=\tilde\omega^\alpha
$$

Comparing the previous identity with Eq. 3.12 identifies each dual basis one-form with the gradient of a coordinate.

**Symbols:** The book writes ':=' but the relation is derived, not a definition.

### Gradient in the coordinate basis · supporting · SCH §3.4 p.65 (pdf 83)

$$
\tilde df=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^\alpha}\,\tilde dx^\alpha
$$

Expansion of any gradient on the basis d-tilde x^alpha; resembles the infinitesimal differential but is a tensor.

### (3.21) Components of a (0 2) tensor · supporting · SCH §3.5 p.66 (pdf 84)

$$
f_{\alpha\beta}:=\mathbf f(\vec e_\alpha,\vec e_\beta)
$$

Sixteen numbers obtained by feeding pairs of basis vectors.

### (3.22) Value of a (0 2) tensor · supporting · SCH §3.5 p.66 (pdf 84)

$$
\mathbf f(\vec A,\vec B)=A^\alpha B^\beta f_{\alpha\beta}
$$

Bilinearity reconstructs the value on any vectors from the components.

### (3.23) Proposed basis expansion · derivation-step · SCH §3.5 p.66 (pdf 84)

$$
\mathbf f=f_{\alpha\beta}\,\tilde\omega^{\alpha\beta}
$$

Ansatz for a basis of (0 2) tensors.

### (3.24) Condition on the (0 2) basis · derivation-step · SCH §3.5 p.66 (pdf 84)

$$
\tilde\omega^{\alpha\beta}(\vec e_\mu,\vec e_\nu)=\delta^\alpha{}_\mu\,\delta^\beta{}_\nu
$$

Required values of the basis tensors on basis vector pairs (reasoning is Exercise 3.15).

### (3.25) (0 2) basis as outer products · supporting · SCH §3.5 p.66 (pdf 84)

$$
\tilde\omega^{\alpha\beta}=\tilde\omega^\alpha\otimes\tilde\omega^\beta
$$

The required values are products of dual-basis values, so the basis consists of outer products.

### (3.26) Expansion of a (0 2) tensor · central · SCH §3.5 p.67 (pdf 85)

$$
\mathbf f=f_{\alpha\beta}\,\tilde\omega^\alpha\otimes\tilde\omega^\beta
$$

Every (0 2) tensor is a sum of simple outer products.

### (3.27)-(3.28) Symmetric tensor · supporting · SCH §3.5 p.67 (pdf 85)

$$
\mathbf f(\vec A,\vec B)=\mathbf f(\vec B,\vec A)\ \ \forall\vec A,\vec B\quad\Rightarrow\quad f_{\alpha\beta}=f_{\beta\alpha}
$$

Symmetry under argument exchange implies a symmetric component matrix.

### (3.29)-(3.30) Symmetric part of a tensor · supporting · SCH §3.5 p.67 (pdf 85)

$$
\mathbf h_{(s)}(\vec A,\vec B)=\tfrac12\mathbf h(\vec A,\vec B)+\tfrac12\mathbf h(\vec B,\vec A),\qquad h_{(s)\alpha\beta}=\tfrac12(h_{\alpha\beta}+h_{\beta\alpha})
$$

Averaging over argument order produces a symmetric tensor from any (0 2) tensor.

### (3.31) Symmetrisation brackets · supporting · SCH §3.5 p.67 (pdf 85)

$$
h_{(\alpha\beta)}:=\tfrac12(h_{\alpha\beta}+h_{\beta\alpha})
$$

Round brackets on indices denote the symmetric part.

### (3.32)-(3.33) Antisymmetric tensor · supporting · SCH §3.5 p.67 (pdf 85)

$$
\mathbf f(\vec A,\vec B)=-\mathbf f(\vec B,\vec A)\ \ \forall\vec A,\vec B\quad\Rightarrow\quad f_{\alpha\beta}=-f_{\beta\alpha}
$$

Sign change under argument exchange; components form an antisymmetric matrix.

### (3.34) Antisymmetrisation brackets · supporting · SCH §3.5 p.67 (pdf 85)

$$
h_{[\alpha\beta]}:=\tfrac12(h_{\alpha\beta}-h_{\beta\alpha})
$$

Square brackets denote the antisymmetric part (from h_(A)(A,B) = h(A,B)/2 - h(B,A)/2).

### (3.35) Symmetric-antisymmetric split · central · SCH §3.5 p.68 (pdf 86)

$$
h_{\alpha\beta}=\tfrac12(h_{\alpha\beta}+h_{\beta\alpha})+\tfrac12(h_{\alpha\beta}-h_{\beta\alpha})=h_{(\alpha\beta)}+h_{[\alpha\beta]}
$$

Any (0 2) tensor decomposes uniquely into symmetric plus antisymmetric parts.

### (3.36) Symmetry of the metric · supporting · SCH §3.5 p.68 (pdf 86)

$$
\mathbf g(\vec A,\vec B)=\mathbf g(\vec B,\vec A)
$$

The metric is a symmetric tensor.

**Symbols:** The book says this follows from Eq. 3.26; it really follows from the symmetry of the dot product or of eta_alpha beta.

### (3.37) Metric with one slot filled · central · SCH §3.6 p.68 (pdf 86)

$$
\mathbf g(\vec V,\;\cdot\;):=\tilde V(\;\cdot\;)
$$

A vector plugged into one slot of g leaves a linear function of the remaining vector: a one-form.

**Symbols:** Blank = slot awaiting a vector.

### (3.38) Associated one-form · central · SCH §3.6 p.68 (pdf 86)

$$
\tilde V(\vec A):=\mathbf g(\vec V,\vec A)=\vec V\cdot\vec A
$$

The one-form paired with V gives, on any A, the dot product of V and A.

### (3.39) Index lowering · central · SCH §3.6 p.68 (pdf 86)

$$
V_\alpha:=\tilde V(\vec e_\alpha)=\vec e_\alpha\cdot(V^\beta\vec e_\beta)=\eta_{\alpha\beta}V^\beta
$$

The associated one-form's components are the metric contracted with the vector components; index position alone distinguishes them.

### (3.40)-(3.41) Explicit lowered components · derivation-step · SCH §3.6 p.69 (pdf 87)

$$
V_0=V^\beta\eta_{\beta0}=-V^0,\qquad V_1=V^\beta\eta_{\beta1}=+V^1
$$

Only the time component changes sign when an index is lowered in a Lorentz frame.

### (3.42) Sign-flip rule · supporting · SCH §3.6 p.69 (pdf 87)

$$
\vec V\to(a,b,c,d)\ \Rightarrow\ \tilde V\to(-a,b,c,d)
$$

Mnemonic summary of lowering in Minkowski components.

### (3.43) Index raising with the inverse metric · central · SCH §3.6 p.69 (pdf 87)

$$
A^\alpha:=\eta^{\alpha\beta}A_\beta,\qquad A_\beta=\eta_{\beta\alpha}A^\alpha
$$

The inverse matrix of eta maps one-form components back to vector components, making the correspondence one-to-one.

**Symbols:** eta^alpha beta: inverse of eta_alpha beta, which exists since det eta = -1.

### (3.44) Components of the inverse metric · supporting · SCH §3.6 p.69 (pdf 87)

$$
\eta^{00}=-1,\qquad\eta^{0i}=0,\qquad\eta^{ij}=\delta^{ij}
$$

In a Lorentz frame the inverse metric has the same numerical entries as the metric.

### (3.45) Vector gradient components · supporting · SCH §3.6 p.70 (pdf 88)

$$
\vec d\phi\to\left(-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t},\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x},\dots\right)
$$

The vector normal to constant-phi surfaces differs from the gradient one-form by the sign of its time component.

**Symbols:** The printed page uses an arrow over d; the export shows a tilde.

### (3.46) Row vector acting on a column vector · supporting · SCH §3.6 p.70 (pdf 88)

$$
(a\ b\ \cdots)\begin{pmatrix}p\\q\\\vdots\end{pmatrix}=ap+bq+\cdots
$$

Matrix algebra's row vectors are one-forms on column vectors: a familiar dual pair.

### Hilbert-space inner product · supporting · SCH §3.6 p.70 (pdf 88)

$$
\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\int\phi^*(\vec x)\,\psi(\vec x)\,d^3x
$$

The complex-conjugated function acts as a one-form on wave functions; conjugation plays the role of the metric map.

**Symbols:** The book writes only the integral of phi* psi and the bra-ket symbols separately; the equality with the bra-ket is our shorthand.

### (3.47) Magnitude of a one-form · supporting · SCH §3.6 p.71 (pdf 89)

$$
\tilde p^2=\vec p^{\,2}=\eta_{\alpha\beta}p^\alpha p^\beta
$$

A one-form's squared magnitude is defined as that of its associated vector.

### (3.48) Substituting raised components · derivation-step · SCH §3.6 p.71 (pdf 89)

$$
\tilde p^2=\eta_{\alpha\beta}(\eta^{\alpha\mu}p_\mu)(\eta^{\beta\nu}p_\nu)
$$

Intermediate step using Eq. 3.43 for both vector components.

### (3.49) Metric times inverse · supporting · SCH §3.6 p.71 (pdf 89)

$$
\eta_{\alpha\beta}\eta^{\beta\nu}=\delta_\alpha{}^\nu
$$

The sum on beta collapses because the matrices are inverses.

### (3.50)-(3.51) One-form magnitude from the inverse metric · central · SCH §3.6 p.71 (pdf 89)

$$
\tilde p^2=\eta^{\alpha\mu}p_\alpha p_\mu=-(p_0)^2+(p_1)^2+(p_2)^2+(p_3)^2
$$

The inverse metric computes one-form magnitudes directly; same sign pattern as for vectors, and frame invariant.

### (3.52) Inner product of one-forms by polarisation · supporting · SCH §3.6 p.71 (pdf 89)

$$
\tilde p\cdot\tilde q:=\tfrac12\left[(\tilde p+\tilde q)^2-\tilde p^2-\tilde q^2\right]
$$

Dot product of one-forms defined from magnitudes.

### (3.53) Inner product of one-forms in components · supporting · SCH §3.6 p.71 (pdf 89)

$$
\tilde p\cdot\tilde q=-p_0q_0+p_1q_1+p_2q_2+p_3q_3
$$

Component form of Eq. 3.52 (derivation is Exercise 3.19b).

### (3.54) Vector as a function of one-forms · central · SCH §3.7 p.72 (pdf 90)

$$
\vec V(\tilde p):=\tilde p(\vec V)=p_\alpha V^\alpha:=\langle\tilde p,\vec V\rangle
$$

Vectors act on one-forms by the same contraction, and the angle-bracket notation expresses the equal standing of the two.

### Components of a (1 1) tensor · supporting · SCH §3.7 p.72 (pdf 90)

$$
R^\alpha{}_\beta:=\mathbf R(\tilde\omega^\alpha;\vec e_\beta)
$$

Mixed tensor components come from inserting basis one-forms in one-form slots and basis vectors in vector slots.

**Symbols:** Semicolon separates one-form arguments from vector arguments.

### (3.55) Transformation of mixed tensor components · central · SCH §3.7 p.73 (pdf 91)

$$
R^{\bar\alpha}{}_{\bar\beta}=\mathbf R(\tilde\omega^{\bar\alpha};\vec e_{\bar\beta})=\mathbf R(\Lambda^{\bar\alpha}{}_\mu\tilde\omega^\mu;\Lambda^\nu{}_{\bar\beta}\vec e_\nu)=\Lambda^{\bar\alpha}{}_\mu\Lambda^\nu{}_{\bar\beta}R^\mu{}_\nu
$$

Each index picks up its own Lambda with placement fixed by the summation convention.

### (3.56) Lowering the second upper index · supporting · SCH §3.8 p.73 (pdf 91)

$$
T^\alpha{}_{\beta\gamma}:=\eta_{\beta\mu}T^{\alpha\mu}{}_\gamma
$$

Turns a (2 1) tensor into a (1 2) tensor by converting its second one-form slot.

### (3.57) Lowering the first upper index · supporting · SCH §3.8 p.73 (pdf 91)

$$
T_\alpha{}^\beta{}_\gamma:=\eta_{\alpha\mu}T^{\mu\beta}{}_\gamma
$$

A different, inequivalent (1 2) tensor from converting the first slot.

**Symbols:** The export garbles the left side as T^beta_alpha beta; restored from the printed page (pdf 91).

### (3.58) Raising the lower index · supporting · SCH §3.8 p.74 (pdf 92)

$$
T^{\alpha\beta\gamma}:=\eta^{\gamma\mu}T^{\alpha\beta}{}_\mu
$$

The inverse metric converts the vector slot into a one-form slot, giving a (3 0) tensor.

### (3.59)-(3.60) Mixed metric equals Kronecker delta · central · SCH §3.8 p.74 (pdf 92)

$$
\eta^\alpha{}_\beta\equiv\eta^{\alpha\mu}\eta_{\mu\beta}=\delta^\alpha{}_\beta
$$

Raising one index of the metric yields the identity map.

### (3.61) Mixed tensor field on the Lorentz basis · derivation-step · SCH §3.9 p.75 (pdf 93)

$$
\mathbf T=T^\alpha{}_\beta\,\tilde\omega^\beta\otimes\vec e_\alpha
$$

Expansion of a (1 1) field whose components vary with position.

### (3.62) Derivative of a tensor along a world line · supporting · SCH §3.9 p.75 (pdf 93)

$$
\frac{d\mathbf T}{d\tau}=\lim_{\Delta\tau\to0}\frac{\mathbf T(\tau+\Delta\tau)-\mathbf T(\tau)}{\Delta\tau}
$$

Difference quotient of tensors at neighbouring events; a tensor because it is built from differences of tensors.

### (3.63) Derivative acts only on components · derivation-step · SCH §3.9 p.75 (pdf 93)

$$
\frac{d\mathbf T}{d\tau}=\left(\frac{dT^\alpha{}_\beta}{d\tau}\right)\tilde\omega^\beta\otimes\vec e_\alpha
$$

Valid because the Lorentz-frame basis is the same everywhere.

### (3.64) Chain rule for tensor components · derivation-step · SCH §3.9 p.75 (pdf 93)

$$
\frac{dT^\alpha{}_\beta}{d\tau}=\frac{\partial T^\alpha{}_\beta}{\partial x^\gamma}\frac{dx^\gamma}{d\tau}=T^\alpha{}_{\beta,\gamma}U^\gamma
$$

Component derivative along the curve in terms of partials and four-velocity.

### (3.65) Tensor derivative as linear in U · derivation-step · SCH §3.9 p.75 (pdf 93)

$$
\frac{d\mathbf T}{d\tau}=\left(T^\alpha{}_{\beta,\gamma}\,\tilde\omega^\beta\otimes\vec e_\alpha\right)U^\gamma
$$

Shows dT/d tau depends linearly on the tangent vector.

### (3.66) Gradient of a tensor · central · SCH §3.9 p.75 (pdf 93)

$$
\nabla\mathbf T:=T^\alpha{}_{\beta,\gamma}\,\tilde\omega^\beta\otimes\tilde\omega^\gamma\otimes\vec e_\alpha
$$

Removing U leaves a (1 2) tensor whose components are the partial derivatives of T's components.

**Symbols:** The written product order puts the one-form slot (e_alpha) last, while the text calls the first argument a one-form; see gaps.

### (3.67)-(3.68) Directional derivative notation · central · SCH §3.9 p.76 (pdf 94)

$$
\frac{d\mathbf T}{d\tau}=\nabla_{\vec U}\mathbf T,\qquad\nabla_{\vec U}\mathbf T\to\{T^\alpha{}_{\beta,\gamma}U^\gamma\}
$$

Derivative along U is nabla T with U inserted in its derivative slot.

### (3.69)-(3.70) Symmetric and antisymmetric parts (exercise restatement) · supporting · SCH p.79 (pdf 97)

$$
\mathbf h_{(s)}(\vec A,\vec B)=\tfrac12\mathbf h(\vec A,\vec B)+\tfrac12\mathbf h(\vec B,\vec A),\qquad\mathbf h_{(A)}(\vec A,\vec B)=\tfrac12\mathbf h(\vec A,\vec B)-\tfrac12\mathbf h(\vec B,\vec A)
$$

Restated in Exercise 3.16 for proof that they have the claimed symmetry.

### Transformation of (0 2) components in matrix form · supporting · SCH p.82 (pdf 100)

$$
f_{\bar\alpha\bar\beta}=\Lambda^\mu{}_{\bar\alpha}\Lambda^\nu{}_{\bar\beta}f_{\mu\nu},\qquad(\bar f)=(\Lambda)^T(f)(\Lambda)
$$

A (0 2) tensor's component matrix transforms by congruence (Exercise 3.32a).

**Symbols:** (Lambda): matrix with entries Lambda^mu_alpha-bar.

### (3.71) Metric-preserving definition of a Lorentz transformation · central · SCH p.83 (pdf 101)

$$
\eta_{\bar\alpha\bar\beta}=\Lambda^\mu{}_{\bar\alpha}\Lambda^\nu{}_{\bar\beta}\eta_{\mu\nu}
$$

Lorentz transformations are exactly the linear maps that keep the metric components equal to eta in the new frame; they form the Lorentz group.

### Projection tensor orthogonal to a four-velocity · supporting · SCH p.82 (pdf 100)

$$
P_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}+U_\mu U_\nu,\qquad V_\perp^\alpha=P^\alpha{}_\beta V^\beta,\qquad \eta_{\mu\nu}-\frac{q_\mu q_\nu}{q^\alpha q_\alpha}\ \text{for non-null }\vec q
$$

Projects vectors onto the 3-space orthogonal to U (or to any non-null q); acts as the induced metric there (Exercise 3.31).

## Worked examples

### Dot product as a (0 2) tensor (§3.1-3.2) · intro · SCH §3.1 p.56 (pdf 74)

**Problem:** Show that the Minkowski scalar product qualifies as a tensor and find its components.

**Method:** Expand both vectors on the basis with separate dummy indices, pull out coefficients by linearity, recognise e_alpha . e_beta = eta_alpha beta, then define g(A,B) and evaluate on basis vectors.

**Key insight:** The metric components are simply the values of the dot-product machine on basis vector pairs, so the familiar matrix eta is one frame's snapshot of a frame-free object.

**Result:** g(A,B) = A^\alpha B^\beta \eta_{\alpha\beta}; g(e_\alpha, e_\beta) = \eta_{\alpha\beta}

**Concepts:** metric tensor, multilinearity, components of a tensor

### Invariance of contraction (Eqs. 3.9-3.10) · standard · SCH §3.3 p.59 (pdf 77)

**Problem:** Find how one-form components transform and show that p(A) computed from components is the same in every Lorentz frame.

**Method:** Evaluate p on the transformed basis vectors to get p_beta-bar = Lambda^alpha_beta-bar p_alpha; then substitute both transformation laws into A^alpha-bar p_alpha-bar, reorder the sums, and use the inverse relation Lambda^mu_alpha-bar Lambda^alpha-bar_beta = delta^mu_beta.

**Key insight:** Opposite (inverse) transformation of vector and one-form components is precisely what makes their pairing a scalar; index placement makes the cancellation automatic.

**Result:** A^{\bar\alpha}p_{\bar\alpha}=A^\beta p_\beta

**Concepts:** transformation law of one-form components, contraction, Kronecker delta

### Deriving the dual basis (Eqs. 3.11-3.12) · standard · SCH §3.3 p.60 (pdf 78)

**Problem:** Find the one-form basis in which the components of p are the numbers p(e_alpha).

**Method:** Postulate p = p_alpha omega-tilde^alpha, evaluate on an arbitrary vector expanded as A^beta e_beta, and demand agreement with p_alpha A^alpha for all components; this forces omega-tilde^alpha(e_beta) = delta^alpha_beta, whose components are then listed.

**Key insight:** The dual basis is fixed by the whole vector basis jointly, not pair by pair.

**Result:** \tilde\omega^\alpha(\vec e_\beta)=\delta^\alpha{}_\beta

**Concepts:** dual basis, one-form

### Gradient as a one-form along a world line (Eqs. 3.14-3.15) · intro · SCH §3.4 p.62 (pdf 80)

**Problem:** Show that the partial derivatives of a scalar field are components of a one-form.

**Method:** Parametrise a world line by proper time, write phi along it as phi(t(tau), x(tau), y(tau), z(tau)), apply the chain rule, and recognise the derivatives of the coordinates as components of U; the result is linear in U, hence a one-form with components phi_{,alpha}.

**Key insight:** Anything that eats a velocity and returns a rate of change linearly is a one-form; the gradient is the canonical example.

**Result:** d\phi/d\tau = \phi_{,\alpha}U^\alpha

**Concepts:** gradient one-form, four-velocity, directional derivative of a scalar along a world line

### Counting contours on a map (Figure 3.3) · intro · SCH §3.4 p.63 (pdf 81)

**Problem:** Estimate the height gained along three displacements drawn on a contour map.

**Method:** Count the contour lines crossed by each arrow: path 1 crosses one and a half contours, path 2 crosses two, and path 3, starting where path 2 does but heading another way, gains only half a contour. Identify each count with Delta h = sum of (partial h/partial x^i) Delta x^i.

**Key insight:** Evaluating a gradient on a displacement requires no metric: only counting. The direction-of-steepest-ascent vector needs a length scale.

**Result:** Delta h = 1.5, 2 and 0.5 contour intervals for paths 1, 2 and 3.

**Concepts:** gradient one-form, picture of a one-form as stacked surfaces

### Consistency of the gradient's transformation (Eqs. 3.16-3.18) · standard · SCH §3.4 p.64 (pdf 82)

**Problem:** Check that the chain-rule transformation of partial derivatives matches the one-form law.

**Method:** Write the one-form law with Lambda^beta_alpha-bar and the chain rule with partial x^beta / partial x^alpha-bar; since x^beta = Lambda^beta_alpha-bar x^alpha-bar with constant coefficients, the Jacobian equals the matrix.

**Key insight:** For Lorentz transformations coordinate Jacobians and transformation matrices coincide; this is why gradients are 'archetypal' one-forms, and it foreshadows Jacobians replacing Lambdas in curvilinear coordinates.

**Result:** \partial x^\beta/\partial x^{\bar\alpha}=\Lambda^\beta{}_{\bar\alpha}

**Concepts:** gradient one-form, transformation law of one-form components, Lorentz transformation matrix

### Basis of (0 2) tensors (Eqs. 3.21-3.26) · standard · SCH §3.5 p.66 (pdf 84)

**Problem:** Find a basis for (0 2) tensors and show every such tensor is a sum of outer products.

**Method:** Expand f(A,B) by bilinearity; propose 16 basis tensors; evaluate on (e_mu, e_nu) to require values delta^alpha_mu delta^beta_nu; notice these are products of dual-basis values, i.e. outer products of basis one-forms.

**Key insight:** The same 'evaluate on basis elements' trick that gave the dual basis generates bases for all higher tensors.

**Result:** \mathbf f=f_{\alpha\beta}\tilde\omega^\alpha\otimes\tilde\omega^\beta

**Concepts:** basis for (0 2) tensors, outer product, dual basis

### Symmetric-antisymmetric decomposition (Eqs. 3.29-3.35) · intro · SCH §3.5 p.67 (pdf 85)

**Problem:** Build symmetric and antisymmetric tensors from an arbitrary (0 2) tensor and show they reconstruct it.

**Method:** Average h over the two argument orders for the symmetric part and take half the difference for the antisymmetric part; translate to components with round and square index brackets; add them to recover h.

**Key insight:** Any bilinear machine splits uniquely into an order-blind part and an order-sensitive part.

**Result:** h_{\alpha\beta}=h_{(\alpha\beta)}+h_{[\alpha\beta]}

**Concepts:** symmetric tensor, antisymmetric tensor, symmetric-antisymmetric decomposition

### Lowering and raising in Minkowski components (Eqs. 3.39-3.44) · intro · SCH §3.6 p.68 (pdf 86)

**Problem:** Find the components of the one-form associated with a vector, and invert the map.

**Method:** Evaluate g(V, e_alpha) with V expanded on the basis to get eta_alpha beta V^beta; compute alpha = 0 and 1 explicitly; argue det eta = -1 guarantees an inverse and find its components.

**Key insight:** In a Lorentz frame the metric map just flips the sign of the time component, and the inverse metric has the same entries; the rule will be more complex for other metrics.

**Result:** V -> (a,b,c,d) gives V-tilde -> (-a,b,c,d); eta^alpha beta = diag(-1,1,1,1).

**Concepts:** index lowering, inverse metric

### Vector gradient is orthogonal to level surfaces (§3.6) · standard · SCH §3.6 p.69 (pdf 87)

**Problem:** Show that the vector obtained from d-tilde phi by the inverse metric is normal to surfaces of constant phi, and give its components.

**Method:** Its scalar product with a vector tangent to a level surface equals, by the metric map, the value of d-tilde phi on that vector, which is the rate of change of phi within the surface and therefore zero. Components follow by flipping the time sign of (phi_{,t}, phi_{,x}, ...).

**Key insight:** Orthogonality of the gradient vector is inherited from the metric-free statement that d-tilde phi annihilates tangent vectors.

**Result:** \vec d\phi \to (-\phi_{,t}, \phi_{,x}, \phi_{,y}, \phi_{,z})

**Concepts:** vector gradient, normal one-form, metric as a map from vectors to one-forms

### Magnitude of a one-form from its components (Eqs. 3.47-3.51) · standard · SCH §3.6 p.71 (pdf 89)

**Problem:** Compute the squared magnitude of a one-form without first constructing its vector.

**Method:** Start from the definition via the associated vector, substitute p^alpha = eta^alpha mu p_mu for both factors (with independent dummy indices), collapse eta_alpha beta eta^beta nu to a Kronecker delta, and write out using eta^alpha beta = diag(-1,1,1,1).

**Key insight:** The inverse metric is the metric for one-forms.

**Result:** \tilde p^2=-(p_0)^2+(p_1)^2+(p_2)^2+(p_3)^2

**Concepts:** magnitude of a one-form, inverse metric, Kronecker delta

### Transformation of a (1 1) tensor (Eq. 3.55) · standard · SCH §3.7 p.73 (pdf 91)

**Problem:** Find how the components R^alpha_beta of a mixed tensor change between Lorentz frames.

**Method:** Write the new components as R evaluated on new basis one-form and new basis vector, substitute their expressions in terms of old basis elements, and pull the Lambdas out by multilinearity.

**Key insight:** Each slot contributes its own transformation factor; no new rule is needed beyond those for basis vectors and basis one-forms.

**Result:** R^{\bar\alpha}{}_{\bar\beta}=\Lambda^{\bar\alpha}{}_\mu\Lambda^\nu{}_{\bar\beta}R^\mu{}_\nu

**Concepts:** transformation law of tensor components, tensor of type (M N)

### Mixed components of the metric (Eqs. 3.59-3.60) · intro · SCH §3.8 p.74 (pdf 92)

**Problem:** Raise one index of eta_alpha beta and interpret the result.

**Method:** Contract eta^alpha mu with eta_mu beta, recognise the product of mutually inverse matrices, and identify the identity with the Kronecker delta; raising the second index returns eta^alpha beta.

**Key insight:** The metric with one index up is the identity map; its fully raised form is literally the inverse matrix.

**Result:** \eta^\alpha{}_\beta=\delta^\alpha{}_\beta

**Concepts:** mixed components of the metric, inverse metric, index raising and lowering

### Derivative of a (1 1) tensor field (Eqs. 3.61-3.68) · standard · SCH §3.9 p.75 (pdf 93)

**Problem:** Differentiate a position-dependent (1 1) tensor along a world line and extract a tensor that does not depend on the curve.

**Method:** Expand on the Lorentz basis, use the constancy of the basis to differentiate components only, apply the chain rule to get T^alpha_{beta,gamma} U^gamma, note the result is a tensor for every U, and strip off U to leave a (1 2) tensor; verify with the argument-slot picture nabla T( , U, ).

**Key insight:** Differentiation adds a covariant slot; in flat spacetime with a constant basis the gradient's components are plain partial derivatives, an assumption that must be abandoned in curved spacetime.

**Result:** \nabla_{\vec U}\mathbf T \to \{T^\alpha{}_{\beta,\gamma}U^\gamma\}

**Concepts:** gradient of a tensor field, directional derivative of a tensor along a vector, constant basis in a Lorentz frame


## Analogies and intuitions

### A one-form is a stack of parallel sheets; its value on an arrow is how many sheets the arrow punches through. → one-form · strong · SCH §3.3 p.61 (pdf 79)

Introduced as the picture mathematicians use, deliberately chosen because it does not rely on a metric; then used to argue that denser sheets mean a larger one-form and that the gradient cannot be an arrow.

**Where it breaks down:** Counting sheets gives the contraction but not the metric magnitude or causal type of the one-form; comparisons of 'size' by spacing only make sense for parallel stacks, and a null one-form in spacetime still has finite spacing. The picture is local (straight sheets at a point).

**App idea:** Sheet-counter canvas with draggable arrow and adjustable sheet spacing/orientation, showing the count and p_alpha A^alpha side by side.

### Hiking on a topographic map: the height climbed along a straight walk equals the number of contour lines it crosses. → gradient one-form · strong · SCH §3.4 p.63 (pdf 81)

Justifies the sheet picture physically with specific counts for three routes, and shows why 'steepest direction' needs a length scale.

**Where it breaks down:** Real contours curve and are unevenly spaced, so counts are only approximate for long arrows; a map is Euclidean so it cannot show the Minkowski sign subtleties.

**App idea:** Terrain generator with 3D surface and contour map; learner draws walks and compares contour counts with the linear estimate, then stretches the map horizontally to show the steepest-ascent arrow changes while counts do not.

### Column vectors and row vectors: a row times a column gives a number, so rows are one-forms acting on columns, and transposition is the one-to-one correspondence. → dual vector space · useful · SCH §3.6 p.70 (pdf 88)

Presented as the simplest familiar dual pair after explaining why SR forces the vector/one-form distinction.

**Where it breaks down:** Plain transposition corresponds to a Euclidean (identity) metric; it hides the sign flip that the Minkowski metric introduces and hides the difference in transformation behaviour.

**App idea:** Matrix playground: toggle between 'transpose' and 'transpose then multiply by eta' to form the row from a column and see which gives the Minkowski dot product.

### Quantum wave functions: in the inner product the complex conjugate function acts on the other function like a one-form, and complex conjugation plays the role of the metric; bras and kets make the duality explicit. → metric as a map from vectors to one-forms · useful · SCH §3.6 p.70 (pdf 88)

A less trivial example of duality, stressing that conjugated functions happen to live in the same space but act as dual objects.

**Where it breaks down:** Complex conjugation is antilinear, not linear like the metric map, and the Hilbert space is infinite-dimensional; useful only for readers who already know quantum mechanics.

### Tensors as machines with input slots: g( , ) awaits two vectors; filling one slot with V leaves a machine g(V, ) that awaits one vector, i.e. a one-form. → tensor of type (M N) · strong · SCH §3.6 p.68 (pdf 86)

Built from the aside on functions versus values, then used to define the metric map (§3.6), the vector-as-function-of-one-forms idea (§3.7) and the gradient of a tensor with its derivative slot (§3.9).

**Where it breaks down:** Slot order must be tracked carefully; the book's own ordering of slots in nabla T is inconsistent between the formula and the text.

**App idea:** Drag-and-drop tensor machine: tiles for vectors and one-forms snap into typed slots; partially filled machines change their displayed type and output.

### The differential notation d-tilde f = f_{,alpha} d-tilde x^alpha looks like the physicist's sloppy infinitesimal calculus. → coordinate basis one-forms dx^alpha · useful · SCH §3.4 p.65 (pdf 83)

Used as a deliberate mnemonic resemblance and simultaneously as a caution: d-tilde f is a finite tensor that only gives a small number on a small vector.

**Where it breaks down:** Encourages conflating tensors with small increments if the caution is not absorbed.

### Tangent one-forms are to surfaces what tangent vectors are to curves: straight and local. → picture of a one-form as stacked surfaces · useful · SCH §3.4 p.64 (pdf 82)

Explains why the sheets in the picture are drawn straight and parallel even though contour lines of a real field curve.

**Where it breaks down:** The notion of a tangent space is only gestured at here; the precise version arrives in the curved-manifold chapter.

**App idea:** Zoom slider on a curved contour field that progressively straightens the contours around a point into a uniform stack.

### Physicists model displacements as vectors and build all tensors from them, but could equally start from gradients as one-forms: the mathematics does not care which is primary. → vectors as linear functions of one-forms · useful · SCH §3.7 p.73 (pdf 91)

Defuses the worry that mutually defining vectors and one-forms is circular.

**Where it breaks down:** Postpones the geometer's modern definition of a vector, so the physical identification remains 'admittedly imprecise'.


## Misconceptions addressed

### The gradient of a function is a vector. · SCH §3.4 p.62 (pdf 80)

**Correction:** The gradient is naturally a one-form (partial derivatives transform inversely to vector components); a vector can be associated with it only after a metric supplies a notion of length, and in SR its components differ by the time-component sign.

**Why tempting:** Euclidean Cartesian vector calculus identifies the two because the metric is the identity matrix.

### Two vectors by themselves determine a number (their dot product). · SCH §3.3 p.61 (pdf 79)

**Correction:** A scalar product needs a third object, the metric; a different metric gives a different number. Only a one-form paired with a vector yields a number without extra structure.

**Why tempting:** In elementary physics the dot product is always available and feels intrinsic to arrows.

### A list of four numbers is a vector (or a one-form). · SCH §3.3 p.61 (pdf 79)

**Correction:** Numbers are components only relative to a stated basis and a stated kind of basis; (0, 2, -1, 5) means nothing until one says which vector or one-form basis it refers to.

**Why tempting:** Linear algebra courses often identify vectors with columns of numbers.

### Each basis one-form omega-tilde^alpha is determined by its matching basis vector e_alpha alone. · SCH §3.3 p.61 (pdf 79)

**Correction:** The duality condition links the whole bases: changing e_0 while fixing the other three generally changes all four dual basis one-forms.

**Why tempting:** The notation pairs indices one to one, and in orthonormal Euclidean bases the dual looks like a copy of each vector.

### The transformation of basis vectors and the transformation of components are the same kind of operation, so 'covariant' and 'contravariant' describe parallel things. · SCH §3.3 p.59 (pdf 77)

**Correction:** Changing the basis produces new vectors from old ones; transforming components re-describes the same object in the new basis. The co/contra naming mixes these, which is why the book prefers 'vector' and 'one-form'.

**Why tempting:** Both are written with Lambda matrices and look similar on the page.

### d-tilde f is a small increment of f. · SCH §3.4 p.65 (pdf 83)

**Correction:** d-tilde f is a tensor (the gradient one-form) with finite components; it produces a small number only when evaluated on a small vector.

**Why tempting:** The notation was chosen to resemble infinitesimal differentials.

### Widely spaced one-form surfaces mean a big one-form. · SCH §3.3 p.61 (pdf 79)

**Correction:** Spacing and size are inversely related: closer surfaces are pierced more often by a given vector, giving a larger value.

**Why tempting:** For arrows, bigger drawings mean bigger vectors, so learners expect 'more spread out' to mean 'larger'.

### The outer product is commutative, p tensor q = q tensor p. · SCH §3.5 p.66 (pdf 84)

**Correction:** The two products assign different values to (A, B): p(A)q(B) versus q(A)p(B). Exercise 3.14 makes this concrete.

**Why tempting:** Multiplication of numbers is commutative and p(A)q(B) looks like a simple product.

### An outward normal vector always points outward. · SCH §3.6 p.72 (pdf 90)

**Correction:** With the Minkowski metric the vector associated with an outward normal one-form points outward only when the normal is spacelike; for a timelike normal it points inward, and for a null normal it lies in the surface. The one-form is the better notion of normal.

**Why tempting:** Euclidean intuition about normals.

### Every normal can be rescaled to a unit normal. · SCH §3.6 p.71 (pdf 89)

**Correction:** Null normals have zero magnitude and cannot be normalised; timelike unit normals have magnitude -1, not +1.

**Why tempting:** Normalising is always possible in Euclidean space.

### Defining one-forms from vectors and then vectors from one-forms is circular. · SCH §3.7 p.73 (pdf 91)

**Correction:** It is a duality: the algebra is self-consistent, and physics decides which objects (displacements or gradients) to identify first; the rest inherits meaning.

**Why tempting:** Definitions are expected to have a strict order.

### The metric is part of what it means to have vectors, and no other tensor could relate vectors and one-forms. · SCH §3.8 p.74 (pdf 92)

**Correction:** A vector algebra can exist without a metric (phase space is cited); the metric is extra structure, and whichever nondegenerate tensor generates the correspondence is by definition the metric. SR is selected by the particular components eta_alpha beta.

**Why tempting:** In all earlier physics courses a dot product came for free.

### Summing over a repeated index can reuse the same letter for two independent sums, e.g. M_alpha alpha A^alpha B^alpha is the same as M_alpha beta A^alpha B^beta. · SCH p.76 (pdf 94)

**Correction:** Each independent summation needs its own dummy index; reusing a letter collapses a double sum into a single diagonal sum.

**Why tempting:** Sloppy habit when indices are treated as placeholders; the book's repeated parenthetical reminders and Exercise 3.1 target it.

### Lowering an index on a tensor with several upper indices gives the same result whichever index is lowered. · SCH §3.8 p.73 (pdf 91)

**Correction:** Lowering different indices of T^alpha beta_gamma produces inequivalent (1 2) tensors; horizontal index order records which slot was converted.

**Why tempting:** For a single vector there is only one index, so the choice never arose.

### Vector and one-form components are the same numbers in relativity, as in ordinary vector calculus. · SCH §3.6 p.69 (pdf 87)

**Correction:** In a Lorentz frame they differ by the sign of the time component; in more general metrics (later chapters) they differ in more complicated ways.

**Why tempting:** Cartesian Euclidean experience where the metric is the identity.

### Differentiating the components of a tensor always gives the components of its derivative. · SCH §3.9 p.76 (pdf 94)

**Correction:** This works here only because Lorentz-frame basis vectors and one-forms are constant; when the basis varies (curvilinear coordinates, curved spacetime) the basis must be differentiated too.

**Why tempting:** It is true in the flat, Cartesian setting where everything is first learned.

### A (1 1) tensor can be split into symmetric and antisymmetric parts just like a (0 2) tensor. · SCH p.81 (pdf 99)

**Correction:** Swapping an upper with a lower index is not a basis-independent operation without the metric; symmetry is meaningful for two slots of the same type (or after lowering an index).

**Why tempting:** Its components also form a 4 by 4 matrix, which can always be symmetrised as a matrix.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Carrying a clock through a scalar field · SCH §3.4 p.62 (pdf 80)

**Setup:** An observer moves along a world line through a spacetime filled with a scalar field (such as temperature), reading both a clock and a thermometer.

**Lesson:** The rate at which the reading changes per unit proper time is linear in the observer's four-velocity, so the field's partial derivatives are the components of a one-form.

**App idea:** Animated observer on a t-x diagram with a heat-map field; strip chart of phi(tau); change the observer's speed to see d phi/d tau change linearly in U.

### Walking across contours · SCH §3.4 p.63 (pdf 81)

**Setup:** Three walkers set off along different straight routes on a hillside map; each counts contour lines crossed.

**Lesson:** Height change equals the gradient evaluated on the displacement and is obtained by counting, without measuring distance; steepest ascent needs a length scale.

**App idea:** Three draggable walkers on a contour map with live counters.

### Tensor algebra without a metric · SCH §3.8 p.74 (pdf 92)

**Setup:** Imagine a world with vectors, one-forms and all (M N) tensors but no preferred tensor linking vectors to one-forms.

**Lesson:** Contractions still produce numbers, but vectors have no dot product; the metric is an added choice, and choosing its components (eta, or something else) selects SR, a Riemannian space, or curved GR spacetime.

**App idea:** Toggle metric on/off in a 2D vector playground: with metric off, angles and lengths disappear from the display but sheet counts remain; switch between Euclidean and Minkowski metric to watch 'perpendicular' directions change.

### Starting from one-forms instead of vectors · SCH §3.7 p.73 (pdf 91)

**Setup:** Rebuild the whole algebra taking stacked-surface one-forms as primary and defining vectors as linear functions on them (Exercise 3.22 and the 'circular reasoning?' discussion).

**Lesson:** The duality is symmetric: either space can be taken as fundamental and yields the same algebra.

(SCH §3.3 p.59 (pdf 77))

## Notation and conventions

- **Metric signature:** eta_alpha beta = diag(-1, +1, +1, +1); scalar product -A^0B^0 + A^1B^1 + A^2B^2 + A^3B^3; timelike vectors and one-forms have negative squared magnitude. — Inherited from ch1-2 with c = 1. (SCH §3.1 p.56 (pdf 74))
- **Typography of objects:** Arrow over a symbol = vector; tilde over a symbol = one-form; boldface = general tensor (g, f, h, T, R). The vector associated with a one-form keeps the same letter (V and V-tilde); the vector gradient is written with an arrow over d, the gradient one-form with a tilde over d. — The markdown export sometimes renders arrows as tildes (e.g. Eq. 3.45 and p.69); check the printed page. (SCH §3.3 p.58 (pdf 76))
- **Tensor type label:** (M N) written as a stacked binomial: M = number of one-form arguments (upper indices), N = number of vector arguments (lower indices). A scalar is (0 0), a one-form (0 1), a vector (1 0). — §3.8 briefly uses (N M) with the letters swapped; the meaning (upper number = upper indices) is unchanged. (SCH §3.7 p.72 (pdf 90))
- **Index placement:** Upper indices label vector components and basis one-forms; lower indices label one-form components and basis vectors. Any single lower index is by convention a one-form component. Summation only pairs an upper with a lower index. (SCH §3.3 p.59 (pdf 77))
- **Components in a frame:** An arrow with O beneath, X ->_O (a, b, c, d), means 'has components ... in frame O'. (SCH §3.3 p.60 (pdf 78))
- **Barred indices for the new frame:** Indices carrying a bar refer to frame O-bar; Lambda^alpha-bar_beta transforms vector components and Lambda^beta_alpha-bar (its inverse) transforms one-form components and basis vectors. — The export often drops or mangles the bars. (SCH §3.3 p.59 (pdf 77))
- **Comma derivative:** phi_{,alpha} := partial phi / partial x^alpha; for tensors T^alpha_{beta,gamma}. — Footnote 1: the partial_alpha phi notation exists but is not used in this book. (SCH §3.4 p.65 (pdf 83))
- **Symmetrisation brackets:** h_(alpha beta) = (h_alpha beta + h_beta alpha)/2 and h_[alpha beta] = (h_alpha beta - h_beta alpha)/2, with factor one-half included. (SCH §3.5 p.67 (pdf 85))
- **Argument slots:** Blanks in g( , ) or T( , U, ) mark unfilled arguments; a semicolon separates one-form arguments from vector arguments, R(p; A). The pairing of a one-form and a vector is also written <p, V>. (SCH §3.7 p.72 (pdf 90))
- **Derivative of tensors:** nabla T denotes the gradient of a tensor field (one extra covariant slot) and nabla_U T the derivative along U; the symbol d-tilde T is avoided because mathematicians reserve it for another operation. (SCH §3.9 p.76 (pdf 94))
- **Raising/lowering sign rule in SR:** Moving a 0 index up or down changes the component's sign; moving a spatial index does not. (SCH §3.8 p.74 (pdf 92))
- **Surface classification:** A three-surface is called timelike, spacelike or null according to the type of its normal. — Opposite to the common convention that names a hypersurface after its tangent vectors (t = const is usually 'spacelike'). (SCH §3.6 p.71 (pdf 89))

## Teaching gems

### Start tensor theory from the dot product the learner already trusts: rewrite it as A^alpha B^beta eta_alpha beta and ask 'what kind of thing takes two vectors and returns a number?' · SCH §3.1 p.56 (pdf 74)

**Why it works:** The abstract definition arrives as a name for something familiar, and the metric is immediately the first example rather than a later special case.

**App idea:** Opening interactive: two draggable arrows on a Minkowski diagram with a live readout of their dot product; a 'look inside' button reveals the 16-entry double-sum table with eta weights lighting up.

**Why it works:** Gives a visual meaning to a lower index and a memorable argument for why gradients and vectors are different kinds of object.

**App idea:** Side-by-side panels: sheet-stack picture (unchanged when the metric toggles) versus the 'gradient arrow' (which swings when the metric switches from Euclidean to Minkowski).

### Derive that the gradient is a one-form by walking a world line through a field: d phi/d tau is linear in U. · SCH §3.4 p.62 (pdf 80)

**Why it works:** Grounds a formal claim in a physical measurement by a moving observer, and reuses four-velocity from the previous chapter.

**App idea:** Observer-in-a-field animation where speeding up the observer scales d phi/d tau linearly, with the chain-rule decomposition shown as stacked bars.

### Contour-map counting with explicit numbers (1.5, 2 and 0.5 contours) for three walks. · SCH §3.4 p.63 (pdf 81)

**Why it works:** Concrete, checkable numbers turn contraction into an everyday skill and reveal that direction matters as much as length.

**App idea:** Hiking challenge: learner predicts contour counts for random walks before the app reveals them; scoring rewards noticing parallel walks gain nothing.

### Explain why elementary vector calculus never distinguished vectors from one-forms: the Euclidean Cartesian metric is the identity, so only the Minkowski minus sign exposes the difference. · SCH §3.6 p.69 (pdf 87)

**Why it works:** Resolves the learner's nagging 'why did nobody tell me this before?' and turns the sign flip into a feature rather than a nuisance.

**App idea:** Metric dial morphing eta from diag(1,1) to diag(-1,1): watch the vector associated with a fixed one-form rotate or reflect while the one-form's sheets stay fixed.

### Manufacture new tensors by partially filling slots: g(V, ) is a one-form; nabla T( , U, ) is dT/d tau. · SCH §3.6 p.68 (pdf 86)

**Why it works:** One uniform mechanism explains index lowering, vectors as functions of one-forms, and the directional derivative, so learners stop memorising separate rules.

## Gaps and pitfalls

- **The book names a three-surface timelike, spacelike or null after its normal. Most other GR texts name it after its tangent vectors, so a surface t = const (timelike normal) is usually called spacelike. The statements about outward normal vectors in §3.6 and Exercise 3.21 are consistent with the book's own naming but read backwards against standard usage.** — Learners carrying this vocabulary into later chapters or other books (Cauchy surfaces, 'spacelike hypersurface', horizons) will invert statements and get confused about which surfaces are initial-data surfaces. *Suggestion:* Teach the standard convention explicitly and translate: 'timelike normal = spacelike surface'. Work the t = 1 edge of a slab: outward one-form +d-tilde t, associated vector (-1,0,0,0) pointing back into the region.
- **Eq. 3.36 claims the symmetry of g 'can be deduced from Eq. 3.26', but Eq. 3.26 is just the basis expansion of any (0 2) tensor; symmetry follows from the symmetry of the dot product or of eta_alpha beta.** — A careful reader may search Eq. 3.26 for a symmetry argument and fail to find one. *Suggestion:* Cite the commutativity of the scalar product (ch2) or eta_alpha beta = eta_beta alpha together with Eq. 3.28.
- **Slot ordering in §3.9 is inconsistent: Eq. 3.61 writes T = T^alpha_beta omega-tilde^beta tensor e_alpha (vector slot first) and Eq. 3.66 writes nabla T with omega-tilde^beta tensor omega-tilde^gamma tensor e_alpha (one-form slot last), yet the text says the first argument of nabla T is a one-form, and §3.7 wrote R(p; A) with the one-form first. The text also slips into calling nabla T( , U, ) a 'gradient vector'.** — Learners who take slot order seriously (as the chapter trains them to) cannot reconcile the formula with the words. *Suggestion:* Adopt one ordering (for example, the order of the basis elements in the outer product) and state that the derivative index is the last lower slot; keep the name 'gradient of T' for the tensor.
- **The remark that g is 'the only tensor' whose upper-index components equal the matrix inverse of its lower-index components is imprecise: -g, for instance, has the same property.** — Minor, but a curious learner who tests it will find counterexamples and lose confidence. *Suggestion:* Say instead that for the metric the raised-index components coincide with the inverse matrix by construction, in every basis.
- **The one-form picture's 'magnitude from spacing' is only a relative comparison for parallel stacks; the metric magnitude of a one-form (and its causal type) cannot be read from sheet spacing, and a null one-form has perfectly visible sheets.** — Learners may think a picture tells them whether a one-form is timelike or how large its norm is, then clash with Eq. 3.51. *Suggestion:* Separate two notions explicitly: value on a vector (counting, metric-free) versus norm (needs eta^alpha beta). Demo a null one-form whose sheets contain their own associated vector.
- **The markdown export loses or garbles notation: Figure 3.1's caption, bars on new-frame indices in Eqs. 3.9-3.10 and 3.13, the arrow-d of the vector gradient (shown as tilde-d near Eq. 3.45), the left side of Eq. 3.57, and commas in Exercise 3.30.** — Anyone building from the export (authors, the tutor's retrieval) could propagate wrong formulas such as treating the vector gradient as a one-form. *Suggestion:* Use the cleaned LaTeX in this dossier's key_equations. The verifier checked it against rendered pages 77, 79, 83, 88, 91 and 100: the bars in Eqs. 3.9-3.10, the arrow-d in Eq. 3.45, T_alpha^beta_gamma in Eq. 3.57, and the comma derivatives in Exercise 3.30 (U^alpha_{,beta}, D^beta_{,beta}, rho_{,alpha}, rho^{,alpha} := eta^{alpha beta} rho_{,beta}).
- **The chapter's notion of a vector remains 'things like Delta x', admittedly imprecise; tangent vectors as derivatives along curves are postponed.** — Learners may later struggle to see why a finite displacement cannot be a vector on a curved manifold. *Suggestion:* Plant the curve-tangent picture now, using the world-line derivative of §3.4 as the model of what a vector does.
- **Dummy-index hygiene and horizontal index spacing are taught only through parentheticals and one exercise; §3.8's lowering examples depend on reading index order precisely.** — Novices write ambiguous expressions like T^alpha_beta without spacing, or reuse dummy indices, and then 'prove' false identities. *Suggestion:* Add a short index-grammar drill (free vs dummy indices, legal contractions, horizontal order) before §3.8, with an interactive index checker.
- **§3.9 contains no worked numerical example, and divergence and contraction of nabla T are introduced only through Exercise 3.30.** — The idea that differentiation adds a slot stays abstract, and the constant-basis warning lacks a concrete counterexample until ch5. *Suggestion:* Pair the derivation with a small explicit field (e.g. a rotating vector field in t-x) and show nabla T components and one contraction; preview how polar basis vectors break the component-only rule.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'You have used the dot product for years. What does it need as input, and what does it give back?' Then ask whether two arrows alone determine their dot product if you are not told the metric; this sets up both the tensor definition and the one-form idea.
