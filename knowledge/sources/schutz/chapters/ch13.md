# SCH ch13 · Cosmology

> The closing chapter builds the Friedmann-Robertson-Walker description of a homogeneous, isotropic, expanding Universe, converts it into observable redshift-distance relations (luminosity and angular-diameter distance, the H0 measurement problem, the discovery of acceleration), derives the Friedmann and acceleration equations with a cosmological constant treated as a dark-energy fluid, and then surveys physical cosmology as of mid-2021: thermal history, decoupling and CMB anisotropies, dark matter and structure formation, CMB B-mode polarization as a gravitational-wave detector, nucleosynthesis, inflation and quantum-gravity ideas.

**Pages:** printed 415–467 · pdf 433–485 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 4/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

The GR derivations (FRW metric, redshift, distances, Friedmann equations) are short and use tools already built, so the mathematics is moderate; the second-order cosmography series and the photon-counting derivation require care. Conceptual load is high: multiple distance definitions, horizons, the meaning of a preferred frame, non-conservation of energy, negative pressure, and a large survey of physical cosmology with many unfamiliar numbers and particle-physics terms. Section 13.4 is readable but qualitative and dated, with several numerical slips, so learners need guidance to separate robust ideas from snapshot values.

## Role in the book

This is the last chapter of the book and acts as a capstone that reuses almost every tool built earlier: the Einstein tensor of a spherically symmetric metric from the stellar-structure chapter becomes the route to the spatial FRW geometry; conserved covariant momenta from the Schwarzschild-orbit chapters give the cosmological redshift; the perfect-fluid stress-energy tensor and its conservation law give the energy equation; the active mass rho + 3p from chapter 8 explains cosmic acceleration; the energy-method treatment of orbits returns as an effective-potential view of R(t); and the gravitational-wave chapters (standard sirens, LISA, transverse area-preserving waves) reappear as tools for H0, dark energy and CMB B-modes. Sections 13.1-13.3 are a compact but genuine GR derivation; section 13.4 shifts into a qualitative, observation-rich survey that closes the book on open problems (Hubble tension, dark matter, dark energy, inflation, quantum gravity).

## Learning objectives

- Explain, with the M/R estimate, why the Universe as a whole requires general relativity while local physics does not, and why Newtonian gravity cannot give a consistent edge-free cosmology.
- State the observational basis for homogeneity, isotropy and uniform expansion, distinguish homogeneity from isotropy, and define the cosmic rest frame via the CMB dipole.
- Distinguish the particle horizon from the optical (decoupling) horizon and articulate the cosmological principle and what rests on it.
- Derive the FRW line element from comoving coordinates, a common scale factor, g_0i = 0, and a spatially constant curvature scalar, including the normalization of k to +1, 0, -1.
- Describe the three spatial geometries (flat, three-sphere, hyperboloid in Minkowski space) and argue why each is homogeneous and isotropic.
- Derive 1 + z = R(t_0)/R(t_e) from conservation of p_chi, show H = Rdot/R, and recover v = z = H_0 d for nearby sources.
- Define luminosity and angular-diameter distance operationally, derive d_L = R_0 r (1+z) by photon counting and d_A = d_L/(1+z)^2, and expand both to second order in z.
- Explain the distance ladder (parallax, Cepheids, Type Ia supernovae, TRGB), standard sirens, CMB/BAO methods, and the origin of the Hubble tension.
- Interpret a magnitude-redshift diagram and explain why curvature of the high-z data, not the low-z slope, reveals acceleration.
- Derive the cosmological energy equation, the Friedmann equation with Lambda, and the acceleration equation, and explain why negative pressure can accelerate expansion.
- Solve the Friedmann equation for radiation-dominated (R proportional to t^{1/2}) and matter-dominated (t^{2/3}) eras and classify future and past behaviour with the effective-potential picture.
- Show that Einstein's static universe needs rho_Lambda = rho_m/2 and is unstable.
- Define the critical density and Omega parameters, compute rho_c numerically, and quote the measured Omega_Lambda, Omega_m, Omega_b, Omega_k.
- Narrate the thermal history: matter-radiation equality, decoupling, dark ages, Population III stars, reionization, and nucleosynthesis, with order-of-magnitude energies and times.
- Explain how CMB polarization separates into E and B modes and why primordial gravitational waves are the only primordial source of B modes.
- Explain the horizon and perturbation problems and how exponential inflation driven by vacuum energy addresses both.

## Teaching approach

Schutz treats cosmology as the place where every earlier idea pays off. The GR core (13.2-13.3) is short and economical: he borrows the spherically symmetric Einstein tensor from the stellar chapter instead of using symmetry-group machinery, proves homogeneity of each FRW slice with a concrete embedding picture, and keeps kinematics (cosmography) strictly separate from dynamics (Einstein equations). Throughout he forces the reader back to what is actually measured: flux, redshift, angular size. Dynamics are made intuitive by rewriting the Friedmann equation as a particle energy equation and by treating Lambda as a fluid with tension. After that the chapter becomes a narrative survey of physical cosmology, heavily observational and explicitly dated (mid-2021), with gravitational-wave astronomy woven in as a cosmological tool.

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, observational-astrophysics, survey-overview, geometric-first, analogy-driven, spiral-revisit, historical-narrative, conversational-informal

**Narrative arc**

1. Motivating question: at what scale does M/R approach 1 for uniformly distributed matter, and why can the rest of science ignore this? (local flatness versus Newtonian boundary dependence)
2. Observed picture: homogeneity, isotropy, uniform expansion, a 2.725 K black-body background, and the headline puzzles (inflation, dark matter, dark energy)
3. Kinematic description: v = H d, the CMB dipole and cosmic rest frame, the relativity-of-simultaneity objection answered by a preferred but physically defined cosmic time
4. Assumptions made explicit: particle horizon versus optical horizon, the cosmological principle justified by early observers outside each other's horizons
5. Model building: comoving coordinates, a single scale factor, g_0i = 0, then a constant 3D curvature scalar forces the FRW spatial metric; normalize k
6. Check: the three geometries are shown to be genuinely homogeneous and isotropic via Euclidean and Minkowski embeddings
7. Link to observables: redshift from a conserved momentum, H = Rdot/R, Taylor expansions of R(t), z(t), H(z); Newtonian expanding-cloud cross-check and the fate of photon energy
8. Cosmography: rebuild distance from flux (photon-counting derivation of d_L), angular-diameter distance, the distance ladder, H0 history and tension, supernova discovery of acceleration
9. Dynamics: fluid energy equation, one independent Einstein equation, Lambda as dark-energy fluid, the rho + 3p acceleration equation, energy-analogy fates, Big Bang inevitability and its caveats, Einstein's unstable static model
10. Parametrize our Universe: critical density, Omega sum rule, measured values, baryons versus dark matter, LISA as a future dark-energy probe
11. Application as thermal history: equality, decoupling, CMB anisotropy map and spectrum, Lambda-CDM, baryon cooling into dark-matter wells, hierarchical mergers and Gaia streams, Population III and reionization
12. Further applications: evidence for dark matter, CMB plus BAO route to H0, CMB polarization E/B decomposition as a primordial gravitational-wave detector
13. Back toward t = 0: nucleosynthesis and neutrino counting, pair annihilation and matter-antimatter asymmetry, quark-gluon plasma, then the smoothness and perturbation puzzles resolved by inflation
14. Frontier: loop quantum gravity, strings and branes; closing reflection that cosmic-scale mysteries may require Planck-scale physics

**Signature moves**

- Recycles the Chapter 10 Einstein-tensor formulas with Phi = 0 to get the curvature of a spherically symmetric 3-space, then imposes constant trace to derive g_rr = 1/(1 - k r^2) in a few lines.
- Visualizes k = +1 as a three-sphere in 4D Euclidean space and k = -1 as a constant-interval hyperboloid in Minkowski space, using rotations and boosts respectively to make homogeneity obvious, while warning that the extra dimension is not physical.
- Rebuilds v = H d from what is measured: distance enters only as (L/F)^{1/2}, so a luminosity distance is defined and computed by counting photons through a sphere, with one factor of (1+z) from energy and one from arrival rate.
- Keeps cosmography (H_0, dH/dt, Taylor series in z) separate from dynamics, so the discovery of acceleration is presented before Einstein's equations explain it.
- Treats the cosmological constant as a fluid with rho_Lambda and p_Lambda = -rho_Lambda, then uses the chapter-8 active mass rho + 3p and a rubber-band tension picture to make antigravity plausible.
- Rewrites the Friedmann equation as kinetic plus potential energy with -k/2 as total energy, echoing the Schwarzschild effective-potential method, to reason qualitatively about Big Bang, recollapse, and the instability of Einstein's static model.
- Repeatedly contrasts with a Newtonian model (boundary-dependent fields, an expanding cloud where redshift is a chain of Doppler shifts) to show what GR adds.
- Uses temperature/energy rather than time as the clock for the early Universe, walking backward and forward through energy thresholds.
- Integrates gravitational-wave astronomy into cosmology: standard sirens for H_0, LISA for dark energy, B-mode polarization as the CMB acting as a gravitational-wave detector.
- Openly time-stamps results (mid-2021) and presents unresolved tensions rather than a closed story.

## Section by section

### §13.1 What is cosmology? — p.415 (pdf 433)

Defines cosmology as the study of the Universe as a whole and argues it needs GR: for uniform density M/R grows like R^2, reaching order unity near 6 Gpc, while galaxies (1e-6) and clusters (1e-4) stay Newtonian. Newtonian gravity cannot handle an edgeless uniform medium, whereas GR's local flatness explains why local science can ignore cosmology. The second part lists the observed arena (homogeneity and isotropy above ~10 Mpc, uniform expansion, 2.725 K CMB), notes Olbers' paradox is resolved by finite age, and previews inflation, dark matter and dark energy.

**Concepts:** cosmology, relativistic regime at cosmological scales, boundary-condition ambiguity of Newtonian cosmology, separation of local physics from cosmology, homogeneity, isotropy, cosmic microwave background, Olbers' paradox, inflation, dark matter, dark energy

**Key moves**
- Scale ladder of M/R from Sun to galaxy to cluster to Gpc scales
- M = 4 pi rho R^3/3 equals R at about 6 Gpc for rho ~ 1e-26 kg m^-3
- Poisson equation needs an outer boundary; GR does not
- Converse question: local flatness decouples local experiments from global geometry

### §13.2 Cosmological kinematics: observing our expanding Universe — p.418 (pdf 436)

Opens with the observed homogeneity, isotropy (not implied by homogeneity) and uniform expansion, illustrated by the inflating balloon, and the Copernican principle. The Hubble-Lemaitre flow is defined by the local cosmological rest frame, the frame with no CMB dipole; Planck's dipole gives our speed 369.82 km/s. The CMB is expanded in spherical harmonics, anisotropies of ~1e-5 justify homogeneity. H_0 ~ 71 km/s/Mpc is converted to SI and geometrized units and a Hubble time of ~14 Gyr. Objections from simultaneity and v > c beyond 4200 Mpc are answered: v = Hd is local, and a preferred cosmic time exists without violating relativity.

**Concepts:** homogeneity, isotropy, Hubble-Lemaitre law, Hubble-Lemaitre parameter, Copernican principle, local cosmological rest frame, CMB dipole, multipole expansion of the CMB sky, Hubble time, preferred cosmic time

**Key moves**
- Counterexample: uniform magnetic field gives homogeneity without isotropy
- Balloon: speed proportional to separation keeps dot distribution homogeneous, so every dot sees the same law
- Rest frame defined by vanishing CMB dipole rather than galaxy averages
- Unit conversions of H_0 and v = c at d = c/H_0
- Preferred frame is a fact about the contents, not the laws (Jupiter-origin comparison)

### Models of the Universe: the cosmological principle — p.422 (pdf 440)

Separates two inaccessible regions: beyond our past light cone (bounded by the particle horizon, which grows so cosmology is retrospective) and inside the cone but unobservable (dim objects, events before decoupling behind the optical horizon, which gravitational waves can penetrate). The cosmological principle, or assumption of mediocrity, is motivated by observing two early observers mutually outside their horizons who nonetheless look statistically alike.

**Concepts:** particle horizon, cosmological principle, optical horizon, horizon problem

**Key moves**
- Past light cone to t = 0 defines what can have influenced us
- Early observers with disjoint past cones would have been right to assume mediocrity, so we adopt it too
- Optical horizon is not fundamental: gravitational waves cross it

### Cosmological metrics and the three types of cosmological model — p.423 (pdf 441)

Two idealizations (homogeneous isotropic slices; comoving frame agrees with slicing) lead to comoving coordinates with t as proper time, a single scale factor R(t) multiplying a fixed 3-metric, and g_0i = 0. Isotropy about the origin gives the Chapter 10 form e^{2Lambda}dr^2 + r^2 dOmega^2; demanding a constant trace of the 3D Einstein tensor and regularity at r = 0 gives 1/(1 - k r^2), hence the FRW metric, with k normalizable to +1, 0, -1. Each case is then checked: flat Euclidean space, a three-sphere (r = sin chi) and a hyperbolic space (r = sinh chi) realized as a Lorentz-invariant hyperboloid in Minkowski spacetime.

**Concepts:** comoving coordinates, scale factor, orthogonality of cosmic time slices, homogeneity from a constant curvature scalar, Friedmann-Robertson-Walker metric, curvature parameter k, flat FRW model, closed FRW model, three-sphere, open FRW model

**Key moves**
- dl^2(t) = R^2(t) h_ij dx^i dx^j from equal growth of all h_ij
- g_0i = e_0 . e_i = 0 from agreement of simultaneity
- G_rr and G_thetatheta of the 3-metric from Eqs. 10.15-10.17 with Phi = 0
- G = -(1/r^2)[1 - (r e^{-2Lambda})'] set equal to constant kappa, integrate, A = 0 by local flatness, k = -kappa/3
- Rescale r and R to set |k| = 1; sign is invariant
- Embeddings: rotations of R^4 and Lorentz boosts move the origin

### Cosmological redshift as a distance measure — p.427 (pdf 445)

Present-day distance to a far galaxy is ill-defined observationally, so redshift is used as a monotonic label. Homogeneity makes p_chi conserved along a radial photon path, giving locally measured energy proportional to 1/R and 1 + z = R(t_0)/R(t_e), with extra Doppler factors for peculiar motion. Differentiating d_0 = R_0 chi gives v = (Rdot/R)_0 d_0 so H = Rdot/R. The scale factor, redshift, look-back time and H(z) are expanded to second order using H_0 and Hdot_0 (or q_0). A Newtonian expanding cloud reproduces the local picture but depends on its edge; the redshifted photon's energy is simply not conserved in a time-dependent metric, though locally the redshift is a sequence of Doppler shifts.

**Concepts:** redshift as a cosmological distance label, cosmological redshift, conserved momentum from metric symmetry, Hubble-Lemaitre parameter, deceleration parameter, look-back time, Newtonian expanding-cloud cosmology, non-conservation of photon energy in cosmology

**Key moves**
- Null radial line: 0 = -dt^2 + R^2 dchi^2; p_chi conserved
- Locally measured wavelength scales with R
- d_0 = R_0 chi differentiated gives Hubble law with H = Rdot/R
- R(t) = R_0 exp(integral of H), Taylor series to second order
- Invert z(t) to look-back time, then H(z)
- Exact H = -zdot/(1+z)

### Cosmography: measures of distance in the Universe — p.431 (pdf 449)

Asks how distance entered v = Hd in the first place: through flux of objects of known luminosity. Defines luminosity distance, recalls standard candles and the distance ladder, and notes that standard sirens need inclination because flux up the orbital axis is four times that in the plane. A photon-counting derivation (sphere area 4 pi R_0^2 r^2, photon energy and arrival rate each reduced by 1+z) gives d_L = R_0 r (1+z); the radial null equation relates r to z and yields a second-order expansion. Angular-diameter distance d_A = D/theta = d_L/(1+z)^2 is introduced, with the CMB's intrinsic scale as a key application. The section closes with a methodological moral: physics rests on measurements.

**Concepts:** cosmography, luminosity distance, standard candle, cosmic distance ladder, standard siren, angular-diameter distance

**Key moves**
- L = 4 pi d^2 F inverted to define d_L
- N = L delta t_e / h nu_e photons spread over A = 4 pi R_0^2 r^2
- Energy per photon and arrival interval each carry a factor 1+z, so F = L/[A(1+z)^2]
- dr/sqrt(1 - k r^2) = dz/(R_0 H(z)); curvature enters only at second order
- d_A = R_e r

### Measuring the Hubble-Lemaitre constant: not a simple matter — p.434 (pdf 452)

Historical sweep from Hubble's ~500 km/s/Mpc through the 50-versus-100 dispute to ~70 by 2000. Explains Cepheids (Leavitt's period-luminosity law, Gaia parallax calibration, possible composition drift), Type Ia supernovae (white dwarf incineration near the Chandrasekhar mass, calibrated by Cepheids in nearby hosts), TRGB and lensing time-delay methods, standard sirens (GW170817 and statistical use of binary black holes, first suggested by Schutz 1986), and early-Universe methods (CMB anisotropies and BAO via angular-diameter distance). States the mid-2021 tension: 74.03 +/- 1.42 from supernovae versus 67.4 +/- 0.5 from the CMB.

**Concepts:** Cepheid period-luminosity relation, Type Ia supernova, tip of the red giant branch, standard siren, baryon acoustic oscillations, Hubble tension

**Key moves**
- Distance errors, not velocity errors, drove historical H_0 changes
- Each rung calibrated on the previous one
- Supernova luminosity set by degeneracy physics, largely epoch-independent
- Sirens bypass calibration; inclination is the limiting uncertainty
- CMB/BAO measure through d_A, supernovae through d_L

### The Universe is accelerating! — p.436 (pdf 454)

Two supernova teams (High-z, 1998; Supernova Cosmology Project, 1999) extended Type Ia measurements to z ~ 1 and found distant supernovae fainter than expected, best fit by accelerating expansion. The magnitude-redshift diagram (Figure 13.3) is explained: m - M tracks log d_L, the low-z slope gives H_0 and the high-z bending gives acceleration, which cannot be mimicked by rescaling H_0. Omega_M and Omega_Lambda are introduced informally as decelerating and accelerating parameters; the result challenges the belief that gravity always attracts.

**Concepts:** accelerating expansion of the Universe, magnitude-redshift diagram, density parameters Omega, critical density, dark energy

**Key moves**
- Dimmer-than-expected at high z means farther than expected, hence faster recent expansion
- Residual plot relative to Omega_M = 0.2, Omega_Lambda = 0 isolates the curvature
- Acceleration depends on how H changes with z, independent of overall H_0 scale

### §13.3 Cosmological dynamics: understanding the expanding Universe — p.438 (pdf 456)

The cosmic contents are a homogeneous perfect fluid at rest in comoving coordinates. The time component of the conservation law gives d(rho R^3) = -p d(R^3): constant rho R^3 for dust and constant rho R^4 for radiation. Isotropy and the Bianchi identity leave one independent Einstein equation, G_tt = 3(Rdot/R)^2 + 3k/R^2, which with Lambda becomes the Friedmann equation; Lambda is recast as dark energy with rho_Lambda = Lambda/8 pi and p_Lambda = -rho_Lambda. The acceleration equation shows rho + 3p drives deceleration, so strong tension accelerates. The energy-equation analogy gives the possible fates; with positive matter and Lambda >= 0 a Big Bang is unavoidable, radiation gives R proportional to t^{1/2}, matter t^{2/3}. Caveats: singularity theorems, quantum effects within a Planck time. Einstein's static model needs rho_Lambda = rho_0/2 and is unstable.

**Concepts:** perfect-fluid model of the cosmic contents, cosmological energy equation, matter-dominated era, radiation-dominated era, Friedmann equation, cosmological constant, dark energy, acceleration equation, active gravitational mass density rho + 3p, negative pressure (tension), effective potential for the scale factor, fate of the Universe, Big Bang, singularity theorems, Planck time, Einstein static universe

**Key moves**
- Isotropy kills spatial components of T^{mu nu}_{;nu} = 0; time component is first law
- Only G_tt needed because Bianchi identity links it to G_rr
- Move Lambda to the right as T_Lambda = -(Lambda/8 pi) g
- Combine energy equation with derivative of Friedmann equation to get Rddot/R = -(4 pi/3)(rho + 3p)
- Kinetic-plus-potential reading: R^2 rho_m falls, R^2 rho_Lambda grows
- Solve Rdot^2 = (8/3) pi B R^{-2} to get R^2 proportional to t
- Static solution sits at a potential maximum

### Critical density and the parameters of our Universe — p.444 (pdf 462)

Dividing the Friedmann equation by 4 pi R^2/3 turns every term into an energy density: a Hubble density 3H^2/8 pi, a curvature density -3k/8 pi R^2, matter and Lambda. The Hubble density is the critical density separating open from closed; dividing by it gives Omega_k + Omega_m + Omega_Lambda = 1 with measured values 0, 0.31, 0.69. rho_c = 9.2e-27 h_70^2 kg m^-3; baryons contribute only Omega_b = 0.04, the rest being dark matter. Figure 13.4 summarizes model space and constraints. LISA could measure luminosity distances of high-redshift black-hole mergers, with host identification (possibly aided by Athena) supplying redshifts, to test whether dark energy evolves; weak lensing will limit accuracy.

**Concepts:** critical density, curvature energy density, density parameters Omega, h_70 parametrization, baryonic matter, dark matter, standard siren

**Key moves**
- rho_H = rho_k + rho_m + rho_Lambda
- Threshold logic: physical density below rho_H implies k = -1
- Omega sum rule evaluated today
- Split Omega_m = Omega_b + Omega_d

### §13.4 Physical cosmology: the evolution of the Universe we observe — p.447 (pdf 465)

Framed as a thermal history using temperature as the clock, reliable back to TeV energies. The decoupling subsection runs backward: dark energy is negligible beyond z ~ 3; radiation (R^{-4}) overtakes matter (R^{-3}) at the equality epoch; decoupling occurs once hydrogen is neutral enough, well below 13.6 eV, releasing the CMB. Dark matter is assumed inert. CMB anisotropies of 1e-5 (Figure 13.5) trace dark-matter density perturbations; slow random velocities are needed for growth, hence cold dark matter and Lambda-CDM. The angular power spectrum (Figure 13.6) carries the parameter information.

**Concepts:** thermal history of the Universe, matter-radiation equality, decoupling, CMB temperature anisotropies, cold dark matter and Lambda-CDM, CMB angular power spectrum, multipole expansion of the CMB sky

**Key moves**
- Scale ratio for equality = Omega_m/Omega_gamma
- Decoupling set by residual free electrons, not by the full ionization energy
- Positions of spots are random; amplitudes versus l carry physics

### Dark matter and galaxy formation: the Universe after decoupling — p.449 (pdf 467)

Simulations started from CMB-like perturbations show dark-matter wells growing and neutral baryons falling in after decoupling. Baryons radiate collisional excitation energy, sinking deeper while heating (negative specific heat), whereas collisionless dark matter forms extended halos. Structure builds hierarchically from ~1e6 solar-mass clumps; mergers continue (Omega Centauri, Magellanic Clouds, future Andromeda merger). Gaia's velocity maps preserve formation history because stars conserve phase-space density (Figure 13.7), revealing a major merger ~10 Gyr ago. Population III stars (100-1000 solar masses) enriched the gas, likely left black holes, and reionized hydrogen between z ~ 20 and 8, ending the dark ages; LISA may see black-hole mergers from that era.

**Concepts:** radiative cooling of baryons in dark matter wells, dark matter halo, hierarchical structure formation, galactic stellar streams and phase-space conservation, Population III stars, reionization, cosmic dark ages

**Key moves**
- Collision time longer than radiative decay time lets photons carry energy out of the well
- Liouville theorem makes the Galaxy a fossil record
- Low opacity of metal-free gas requires massive first stars

### What is the dark matter? — p.452 (pdf 470)

Evidence independent of structure formation: Zwicky's 1933 virial analysis of Coma, Rubin's rotation curves indicating about six times more dark than visible mass, and lensing by cluster halos. Composition is unknown; candidates include undiscovered weakly interacting particles, primordial black holes or other compact objects; laboratory searches so far are negative. A footnote notes Zwicky also proposed neutron stars with Baade in the same period.

**Concepts:** evidence for dark matter, dark matter, dark matter halo

**Key moves**
- Virial theorem: velocities too large for luminous mass
- Flat rotation curves
- Lensing maps mass directly

### Using the CMB to measure H_0 — p.453 (pdf 471)

The CMB route needs a physical length D to turn angular scales into a distance. The book's version: rather than computing acoustic travel distances directly, one uses BAO. After decoupling, photon drag briefly stops baryons collapsing (decoupling at z = 1090, drag ending at z = 1060), and the imprint of the linear oscillations survives in today's galaxy distribution. Measuring that imprint now and extrapolating back to z = 1060 and then 1090 gives D, hence d_A to last scattering and H_0 = 67.4 +/- 0.5, nearly five sigma from the supernova value. Each method has a weak point: distance-ladder systematics for supernovae, and assumptions about post-decoupling perturbation physics and the sound speed for the CMB. Standard sirens could arbitrate. (The book's description of the CMB H_0 method is non-standard; see gaps.)

**Concepts:** baryon acoustic oscillations, photon drag, angular-diameter distance, Hubble tension

**Key moves**
- Standard ruler plus angle gives d_A
- BAO linear scale today back-extrapolated to the drag and decoupling epochs
- Each method's Achilles heel identified

### How a stochastic gravitational wave background affects the CMB — p.454 (pdf 472)

Primordial gravitational waves reach us nearly unaltered, and the CMB can detect them. Because the waves are transverse and area-preserving they create no density or temperature perturbation, but they create velocity patterns that polarize scattered light. Scattering from an anisotropic radiation field (hot spot next to cold spot) produces net linear polarization; acoustic waves produce a curl-free pattern that stays curl-free under superposition. A wave travelling toward us squeezes plasma along one sky axis and stretches along the other, Doppler-heating the inflowing gas so polarization aligns with the stretch axis, frozen in at last scattering; a random background gives a pattern with a curl component, the B mode. Complications: vorticity and obliquely travelling waves give curl-free patterns, and polarized Galactic dust is a foreground.

**Concepts:** primordial gravitational waves, CMB polarization from anisotropic scattering, E-mode and B-mode polarization

**Key moves**
- Transverse area-preserving waves: no temperature signal
- Scattering polarization perpendicular to the plane of incoming and outgoing directions
- Linear superposition preserves zero curl for scalar perturbations
- Last scattering freezes the gravitational-wave imprint
- Take the curl to remove acoustic and E contributions

### The early Universe: fundamental physics meets cosmology — p.457 (pdf 475)

Runs the clock back from equality: at roughly 200 s and ~50 keV nuclear reactions equilibrate and light nuclei form, mostly helium-4 with traces of helium-3 and lithium; yields depend on the expansion rate and so on the number of relativistic species, limiting neutrinos to three and bounding primordial gravitational waves; dark matter must be heavier than ~50 keV. At ~1 s and ~500 keV electron-positron pairs are in equilibrium; their annihilation leaves one electron per 1e9 photons, implying a matter-antimatter asymmetry also present for protons at a few hundred MeV. Before 1e-5 s there is a quark-gluon plasma, and colliders test physics to ~10 TeV (Higgs found, no supersymmetry). Two things standard physics does not supply: the initial perturbations and the large-scale smoothness of causally disconnected regions (horizon problem).

**Concepts:** big bang nucleosynthesis, nucleosynthesis bound on relativistic species, electron-positron annihilation and photon-to-baryon ratio, matter-antimatter asymmetry, quark-gluon plasma, horizon problem

**Key moves**
- More massless species means stronger gravity and faster expansion, changing helium and lithium yields
- Annihilation bookkeeping reveals asymmetry
- Uniform helium in regions outside each other's horizons motivates a causal smoothing mechanism

### Inflation — p.459 (pdf 477)

Inflation (Starobinsky, Guth, Linde) posits a temporary large vacuum-energy cosmological constant near 1e-35 s giving exponential expansion with growth time tau = (3/8 pi rho_Lambda)^{1/2}. A small, causally smoothed patch is blown up to contain the observable Universe, explaining homogeneity. Lorentz invariance forces vacuum stress-energy to be proportional to the metric, so vacuum energy acts as Lambda; a high-energy phase transition could release it and later convert it to particles. Exponential growth amplifies quantum fluctuations into classical density perturbations and a gravitational-wave background. The model fits the fluctuation amplitude and spectrum but remains a model until its underlying fields are identified.

**Concepts:** inflation, vacuum energy as a cosmological constant, inflationary generation of perturbations, horizon problem, primordial gravitational waves

**Key moves**
- H^2 = (8/3) pi rho_Lambda gives Rdot/R = constant
- Many e-folds map a tiny smoothed region onto today's horizon
- Only Lorentz-invariant symmetric (0,2) tensor is g

### Beyond general relativity — p.461 (pdf 479)

Inflation stays within classical GR, but quantum gravity is needed eventually. Loop quantum gravity replaces the manifold at the Planck scale with tangled loops and can turn the Big Bang into a bounce. String theory aims to unify all forces in higher dimensions; extra dimensions could be Planck-sized or large, with our Universe a brane on which only gravity leaks off, modifying the inverse-square law below a scale now bounded at about 40 micrometres. Brane collisions, shadow matter and extra gravitational radiation are illustrative model possibilities. Closing theme: the youngest cosmic puzzles (dark matter, dark energy, inflation) may require physics near the Planck scale, 1e16 TeV away from collider energies.

**Concepts:** loop quantum gravity, string theory and braneworlds, shadow matter

**Key moves**
- Classify ideas as model theories: neither predictions nor idle speculation
- Short-range gravity tests as windows on extra dimensions

## Concepts

### cosmology

*definition · introduced*

The science of the Universe treated as one physical system: its large-scale geometry, contents, expansion history, and the origin of the structures that formed within it.

**How introduced:** Opening paragraph of the chapter presents it as both the backdrop for every smaller structure and a laboratory for physics at energies beyond accelerators.

**Where:** SCH §13.1 p.415 (pdf 433)

### relativistic regime at cosmological scales

*principle · developed* · also: M/R criterion for cosmology

For matter spread with roughly constant density, enclosed mass grows as R^3 so the compactness M/R (geometrized units) grows as R^2; it must eventually approach 1. With rho ~ 1e-26 kg m^-3 this happens around R ~ 6 Gpc, inside the observable Universe, so GR is unavoidable for cosmology.

**How introduced:** Contrasted with compact objects, where R shrinks at fixed M. A ladder of estimates (Sun, Milky Way with M/R ~ 1e-6, clusters ~ 1e-4) shows nothing below cluster scales needs GR, then the uniform-density estimate crosses unity.

**Prerequisites:** geometrized units, Newtonian limit

$$
M = \frac{4\pi}{3}\rho R^3 = R \;\Rightarrow\; R \sim 6\,\mathrm{Gpc}
$$

**Where:** SCH §13.1 p.415 (pdf 433); SCH §13.1 p.416 (pdf 434)

### boundary-condition ambiguity of Newtonian cosmology

*phenomenon · developed* · also: Newtonian cosmology edge problem

Poisson's equation for a uniform infinite medium has no unique solution without a boundary condition at some outer edge; in Newtonian gravity the local field inside a uniform region depends on the shape of the distant boundary, even beyond any horizon, so a consistent edgeless Newtonian cosmology does not exist.

**How introduced:** Stated in 13.1 as the reason cosmology became physics only after Einstein, reinforced when the Newtonian expanding cloud is discussed (instantaneous forces make dynamics depend on the edge), and worked out in Exercise 13.3.

**Prerequisites:** Poisson equation, particle horizon

**Where:** SCH §13.1 p.416 (pdf 434); SCH §13.2 p.430 (pdf 448); SCH p.463 (pdf 481)

### separation of local physics from cosmology

*principle · developed* · also: local flatness in a cosmological spacetime

Because spacetime is locally flat in GR, experiments confined to a small region need no knowledge of the global geometry; stars, planets and black holes can be modelled in asymptotically flat spacetime even though the Universe as a whole is strongly relativistic.

**How introduced:** Posed as the converse question (how can biology or stellar astrophysics ignore cosmology?) and answered by contrast with Newtonian theory, where far boundaries influence local fields. Framed as GR explaining why the rest of science does not need GR.

**Prerequisites:** local inertial frame, equivalence principle

**Where:** SCH §13.1 p.416 (pdf 434)

### cosmic microwave background

*phenomenon · core* · also: CMB, microwave background radiation

Nearly perfect black-body radiation filling space, now at 2.725 K, released when the primordial plasma became neutral and transparent; its small direction-dependent temperature and polarization variations record conditions around decoupling and earlier.

**How introduced:** Listed among the basic observed facts in 13.1, then used operationally to define the cosmic rest frame and constant-time slices (13.2), and finally treated physically in 13.4 as the relic of decoupling with anisotropies and polarization.

**Prerequisites:** black-body radiation, decoupling

$$
T_0 = 2.725\,\mathrm{K}
$$

**Where:** SCH §13.1 p.417 (pdf 435); SCH §13.2 p.419 (pdf 437); SCH §13.2 p.420 (pdf 438); SCH §13.4 p.448 (pdf 466); SCH §13.4 p.449 (pdf 467)

### Olbers' paradox

*historical-idea · mention* · also: dark night sky paradox

The old puzzle that in an infinite, eternal universe uniformly filled with stars every line of sight would end on a star and the sky would be bright; it is resolved because the Universe has a finite age (and expands), so we receive light only from sources near enough for it to have arrived.

**How introduced:** Mentioned briefly as the oldest cosmological puzzle that expansion and finite age resolve.

**Prerequisites:** Hubble time

**Where:** SCH §13.1 p.417 (pdf 435)

### homogeneity

*definition · core* · also: spatial homogeneity

The property that on scales well above about 10 Mpc the Universe, at a given cosmic time, has the same average density and the same statistical properties (galaxy types, clustering, composition, evolutionary stage) everywhere.

**How introduced:** Inferred observationally, taking care that deep observations look back in time: the evolution seen is the same in all directions, which supports homogeneity on constant-time slices.

**Where:** SCH §13.1 p.417 (pdf 435); SCH §13.2 p.418 (pdf 436)

### isotropy

*definition · core* · also: spatial isotropy

The property that the averaged Universe looks the same in every direction around every point, with no preferred spatial direction.

**How introduced:** Distinguished from homogeneity by a counterexample (a uniform magnetic field pointing one way everywhere would be homogeneous but anisotropic); conversely inhomogeneity would make most observers see a lumpy, anisotropic sky.

**Prerequisites:** homogeneity

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.418 (pdf 436)

### Hubble-Lemaitre law

*law · core* · also: Hubble law, Hubble-Lemaitre flow, Hubble flow

On average, galaxies recede at a speed proportional to their proper distance, v = H d. It holds for nearby objects (v much less than c) where v is read from redshift; proportionality to distance keeps a homogeneous distribution homogeneous, so every comoving observer sees the same law.

**How introduced:** Introduced as the third observed regularity, visualized with dots on an inflating balloon, then derived in FRW as the small-distance limit of d_0 = R_0 chi. A footnote records the 2018 IAU renaming.

**Prerequisites:** homogeneity, Doppler shift

$$
v = H d
$$

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.418 (pdf 436); SCH §13.2 p.419 (pdf 437); SCH §13.2 p.420 (pdf 438); SCH §13.2 p.429 (pdf 447)

### Hubble-Lemaitre parameter

*physical-quantity · core* · also: Hubble parameter, Hubble-Lemaitre constant H_0, Hubble constant

The fractional expansion rate H(t) = Rdot/R of the scale factor. Its present value H_0 is about 70 km s^-1 Mpc^-1, i.e. about 2.3e-18 s^-1 or 7.7e-27 m^-1 in geometrized units.

**How introduced:** First as the slope of v = H d with unit conversions from astronomers' units; then shown in FRW to equal Rdot/R at any time; later expanded in redshift via H_0 and its derivative.

**Prerequisites:** scale factor, Hubble-Lemaitre law

$$
H(t) = \dot R/R
$$

$$
H_0 \approx 71\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}} = 2.3\times10^{-18}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}
$$

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.420 (pdf 438); SCH §13.2 p.421 (pdf 439); SCH §13.2 p.428 (pdf 446); SCH §13.2 p.429 (pdf 447)

### Hubble time

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: Hubble-Lemaitre time

The inverse of the present expansion rate, t_H = 1/H_0 (about 4.3e17 s or 14 billion years), setting the order of magnitude of the age of the Universe; the actual age differs because the expansion rate has varied.

**How introduced:** Computed directly after converting H_0 and compared with the 4.54-billion-year age of Earth.

**Prerequisites:** Hubble-Lemaitre parameter

$$
t_H = H_0^{-1} \approx 4.3\times10^{17}\,\mathrm{s}
$$

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.421 (pdf 439)

### Copernican principle

*principle · developed*

The working assumption that we do not occupy a special location; for cosmology, that the expansion is not centred on us and other observers see the same recession pattern.

**How introduced:** Drawn from the balloon picture: velocity proportional to separation means any dot sees all others receding the same way.

**Prerequisites:** Hubble-Lemaitre law

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.419 (pdf 437)

### local cosmological rest frame

*definition · core* · also: CMB rest frame, cosmological reference frame

At each event, the local inertial frame in which the CMB temperature shows no dipole (no systematic warmer-ahead, cooler-behind pattern); it moves with the Hubble-Lemaitre flow and differs from the motion of any individual galaxy.

**How introduced:** The naive choice (zero mean velocity of neighbouring galaxies) is rejected because of large-scale galaxy flows; the CMB supplies a clean definition.

**Prerequisites:** cosmic microwave background, local inertial frame

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.419 (pdf 437); SCH §13.2 p.421 (pdf 439)

### CMB dipole

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: kinematic dipole

The l = 1 component of the CMB temperature map, produced by our motion relative to the cosmic rest frame; Planck measures the Solar System barycentre moving at 369.82 +/- 0.11 km/s toward Galactic longitude 264.021 deg, latitude 48.253 deg.

**How introduced:** Introduced as the practical way to find our own cosmic rest frame, after describing COBE, WMAP and Planck mapping.

**Prerequisites:** local cosmological rest frame, multipole expansion of the CMB sky

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.420 (pdf 438)

### multipole expansion of the CMB sky

*technique · introduced* · also: spherical harmonic decomposition of CMB temperature, T_lm coefficients

Writing the sky temperature as a sum over spherical harmonics Y_lm with coefficients T_lm; the index l labels angular scale (roughly 180 deg / l), the dipole encodes our motion, l >= 2 carries intrinsic anisotropy, and resolution sets the maximum usable l (beyond 2000 for Planck).

**How introduced:** Presented with Galactic coordinates (l, b); a footnote warns not to confuse longitude l with the multipole index. Revisited to explain that phases encode random positions while squared amplitudes give the power spectrum.

**Prerequisites:** spherical harmonics

$$
T(l,b) = \sum_{\ell}\sum_{m} T_{\ell m} Y_{\ell m}(l,b)
$$

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.420 (pdf 438); SCH §13.4 p.449 (pdf 467)

### preferred cosmic time

*definition · core* · also: cosmic time, cosmological time

A global time coordinate whose constant-time hypersurfaces are homogeneous and isotropic (equivalently, have uniform CMB temperature) and which equals proper time for observers moving with the Hubble-Lemaitre flow.

**How introduced:** Raised as an objection: expansion statements seem to ignore relativity of simultaneity, and v = Hd would exceed c beyond 4200 Mpc. Resolved by locality of v = Hd and by noting a preferred frame reflects the matter content, not the laws.

**Prerequisites:** local cosmological rest frame, relativity of simultaneity

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.421 (pdf 439); SCH §13.2 p.423 (pdf 441)

### particle horizon

*definition · core*

The boundary between events that could have sent signals to an observer since the Big Bang and those that could not; drawn as the observer's past light cone extended to t = 0. It grows with time, so formerly unknown regions become observable.

**How introduced:** Figure 13.2 (left) shows the unknown regions outside the cone; the growth of the horizon makes cosmology a retrospective science whose future predictions rely on assumptions.

**Prerequisites:** light cone, Big Bang

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.422 (pdf 440)

### cosmological principle

*principle · core* · also: assumption of mediocrity

The assumption that the Universe is homogeneous and isotropic everywhere, including beyond our particle horizon, so our observable region is typical.

**How introduced:** Motivated by imagining two early observers whose past light cones were disjoint: we can see both and find them statistically alike, so their assumption of mediocrity would have been correct, and we adopt it too. Flagged as mathematically very restrictive.

**Prerequisites:** particle horizon, homogeneity, isotropy

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.422 (pdf 440); SCH §13.2 p.423 (pdf 441)

### optical horizon

*definition · introduced* · also: last-scattering surface, decoupling horizon

The limit on electromagnetic observation set by decoupling: earlier than that the ionized plasma was opaque, so no light reaches us from before it. It is not a fundamental horizon because gravitational waves (and neutrinos) passed freely.

**How introduced:** Introduced as the second kind of inaccessible region, inside our past light cone but hidden from instruments.

**Prerequisites:** decoupling, particle horizon

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.423 (pdf 441)

### comoving coordinates

*definition · core*

Spatial coordinates attached to observers at rest in the local cosmological rest frame, each keeping fixed x^i, with time t equal to their proper time; expansion then appears only as time dependence of the spatial metric.

**How introduced:** Adopted as a simplification after stating two idealizations about slicing and the Hubble-Lemaitre flow.

**Prerequisites:** local cosmological rest frame, preferred cosmic time

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.423 (pdf 441)

### scale factor

*physical-quantity · core* · also: expansion factor R(t), a(t) in many other texts

The single function of time R(t) multiplying a fixed spatial metric, dl^2 = R^2(t) h_ij dx^i dx^j, so proper distances between all comoving points grow by the same factor; a direction-dependent growth would violate isotropy.

**How introduced:** Derived by requiring all h_ij to evolve with a common factor (anisotropic growth would give a matrix-valued Hubble law, Exercise 13.4); normalized to 1 at a reference time.

**Prerequisites:** comoving coordinates, isotropy

$$
\mathrm{d}l^2(t) = R^2(t) h_{ij}\,\mathrm{d}x^i\mathrm{d}x^j
$$

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.423 (pdf 441); SCH §13.2 p.424 (pdf 442)

### orthogonality of cosmic time slices

*definition · introduced* · also: vanishing shift g_0i, g_0i = 0 in comoving coordinates

If simultaneity defined by t = const is to match that of comoving observers, the time basis vector must be orthogonal to the spatial ones, so g_0i = 0, while g_00 = -1 because t is proper time along comoving world lines.

**How introduced:** One-line argument from idealization (ii); Exercise 13.5 connects it to homogeneity as seen by galaxies on fixed x^i.

**Prerequisites:** comoving coordinates, basis vectors and metric components

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.424 (pdf 442)

### homogeneity from a constant curvature scalar

*technique · developed*

Starting from the general isotropic 3-metric e^{2Lambda(r)} dr^2 + r^2 dOmega^2, homogeneity requires every scalar, in particular the curvature scalar (or the trace of the 3D Einstein tensor, which equals minus half of it), to be position independent; integrating this condition and demanding regularity at r = 0 forces g_rr = 1/(1 - k r^2).

**How introduced:** Uses the Chapter 10 Einstein-tensor components with Phi set to zero rather than a fresh curvature computation; the condition is necessary, and sufficiency is then checked separately for each sign of k.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein tensor, Ricci scalar, spherically symmetric spacetime

$$
G = -\frac{1}{r^2}\left[1 - (r e^{-2\Lambda})'\right] = \kappa
$$

$$
g_{rr} = \frac{1}{1-kr^2},\; k = -\kappa/3
$$

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.424 (pdf 442); SCH §13.2 p.425 (pdf 443)

### Friedmann-Robertson-Walker metric

*solution-or-model · core* · also: FRW metric, FLRW metric, Robertson-Walker metric

The spacetime line element ds^2 = -dt^2 + R^2(t)[dr^2/(1 - k r^2) + r^2 dOmega^2], the general form for a spacetime foliated by homogeneous, isotropic spatial slices orthogonal to comoving world lines; R(t) carries all dynamics and k the spatial geometry.

**How introduced:** Assembled from the scale-factor form and the constant-curvature spatial metric; general proofs are deferred to Weinberg and Schutz (1980b), with case-by-case checks in the text.

**Prerequisites:** scale factor, homogeneity from a constant curvature scalar, orthogonality of cosmic time slices

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = -\mathrm{d}t^2 + R^2(t)\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}r^2}{1-kr^2} + r^2\mathrm{d}\Omega^2\right]
$$

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.425 (pdf 443); SCH §13.3 p.438 (pdf 456)

### curvature parameter k

*physical-quantity · core* · also: spatial curvature constant

The constant in the FRW metric that fixes the sign of spatial curvature; rescaling r and R can set its magnitude to 1, leaving three distinct cases k = +1 (closed), 0 (flat), -1 (open), but no rescaling changes its sign.

**How introduced:** Shown by explicitly rescaling an example with k = -3.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann-Robertson-Walker metric

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.425 (pdf 443)

### flat FRW model

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: k = 0 universe, Einstein-de Sitter geometry (when matter-only)

The k = 0 case, where each constant-time slice is ordinary Euclidean 3-space with all distances scaled by R(t); homogeneity and isotropy are evident.

**How introduced:** Shown by absorbing R(t_0) into a rescaled radial coordinate.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann-Robertson-Walker metric

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.426 (pdf 444)

### closed FRW model

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: spherical FRW model, k = +1 universe

The k = +1 case: with r = sin chi each slice is a three-sphere of radius R(t), having finite volume but no boundary; rotations of the embedding 4D Euclidean space carry any point to any other, so it is homogeneous and isotropic.

**How introduced:** Coordinate change dchi^2 = dr^2/(1 - r^2), link to Exercise 6.33, and the remark that the balloon analogy fits this case best; the reader is warned the radial direction into 4D is only a visual aid.

**Prerequisites:** three-sphere, curvature parameter k

$$
r = \sin\chi
$$

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.426 (pdf 444)

### three-sphere

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: 3-sphere, S^3

The three-dimensional set of points at fixed distance a from the origin of 4D Euclidean space, with line element a^2[dchi^2 + sin^2 chi dOmega^2]; the area of spheres about any point first grows, then shrinks to zero at chi = pi.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapter 6 as the geometry of closed FRW slices.

**Prerequisites:** metric of a sphere

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.426 (pdf 444)

### open FRW model

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: hyperbolic FRW model, k = -1 universe

The k = -1 case with slices R^2[dchi^2 + sinh^2 chi dOmega^2]: sphere areas grow faster with proper radius than in flat space, so the slice cannot be embedded in Euclidean space, but it is exactly the hyperboloid of events at fixed timelike interval from an origin of 4D Minkowski spacetime, whose invariance under Lorentz transformations makes it homogeneous and isotropic.

**How introduced:** Contrasted with the three-sphere; the Minkowski embedding (Exercise 13.8) is used to prove the symmetry in the same way rotations did for k = +1.

**Prerequisites:** curvature parameter k, Lorentz transformation, hyperbolic geometry

$$
\mathrm{d}l^2 = R^2(\mathrm{d}\chi^2 + \sinh^2\chi\,\mathrm{d}\Omega^2)
$$

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.426 (pdf 444); SCH §13.2 p.427 (pdf 445)

### redshift as a cosmological distance label

*technique · introduced*

Using the measured redshift z, which grows monotonically with separation in an expanding universe, to label how far away (and how far back) a source is, because its present-day distance is not directly observable and the source may no longer exist; peculiar velocities spoil this only for nearby objects.

**How introduced:** Motivated by the ambiguity of 'distance now' for light that travelled for billions of years.

**Prerequisites:** Hubble-Lemaitre law

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.427 (pdf 445)

### conserved momentum from metric symmetry

*principle · revisited* · also: Killing-vector conservation law

If the metric components do not depend on some coordinate, the covariant component of a geodesic's momentum conjugate to that coordinate stays constant along the geodesic.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Section 7.4 to show p_chi is conserved for a radial photon in a homogeneous cosmology.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic, one-form components of momentum

$$
p_\chi = \mathrm{const}
$$

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.428 (pdf 446)

### cosmological redshift

*phenomenon · core*

The increase of photon wavelength in proportion to the scale factor between emission and reception by comoving observers, 1 + z = R(t_0)/R(t_e); motions of source or observer relative to the cosmic frame multiply in extra Doppler factors. For nearby sources it reduces to an ordinary Doppler shift with z = v.

**How introduced:** Derived from conservation of p_chi and the locally measured energy -p^0 proportional to 1/R (Exercise 13.9); Exercise 13.10 shows z = v locally; the Newtonian cloud gives a Doppler interpretation.

**Prerequisites:** conserved momentum from metric symmetry, scale factor, Friedmann-Robertson-Walker metric

$$
1+z = R(t_0)/R(t_e)
$$

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.427 (pdf 445); SCH §13.2 p.428 (pdf 446); SCH §13.2 p.429 (pdf 447); SCH §13.2 p.430 (pdf 448)

### deceleration parameter

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: q_0

The dimensionless measure of how the expansion rate changes, q_0 = -R_0 Rddot_0/Rdot_0^2 = -(1 + Hdot_0/H_0^2); named when gravity was expected to slow expansion (q_0 > 0), it is negative for today's accelerating Universe.

**How introduced:** Introduced as the traditional replacement for Hdot_0 in the Taylor series of R(t), with the remark that the name is now out of fashion.

**Prerequisites:** Hubble-Lemaitre parameter, scale factor

q_0 = -R_0\ddot R_0/\dot R_0^2

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.429 (pdf 447)

### look-back time

*physical-quantity · introduced*

The cosmic proper time between emission of light now received with redshift z and its reception, approximately t_0 - t = H_0^{-1}[z - (1/2)(1 - Hdot_0/H_0^2) z^2] for small z.

**How introduced:** Obtained by inverting the Taylor series for z(t), since emission time is not directly measured.

**Prerequisites:** cosmological redshift, Hubble-Lemaitre parameter

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.430 (pdf 448)

### Newtonian expanding-cloud cosmology

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: Newtonian cosmology

A uniform Newtonian ball of galaxies started with radial velocities proportional to distance from its centre; far from the edge it expands homogeneously and isotropically and shows redshift as accumulated Doppler shifts between diverging galaxies, but instantaneous gravity makes its dynamics depend on the unseen edge.

**How introduced:** Offered as a heuristic cross-check after the redshift derivation, used to explain where photon energy seems to go, and to argue only a relativistic theory (with dynamics determined inside past light cones) makes cosmic dynamics well posed.

**Prerequisites:** boundary-condition ambiguity of Newtonian cosmology, Doppler shift

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.430 (pdf 448)

### non-conservation of photon energy in cosmology

*principle · introduced*

In a time-dependent metric there is no conserved energy along a photon's geodesic, so the energy a cosmologically redshifted photon loses is not transferred elsewhere; it simply has no conservation law to obey.

**How introduced:** Answer to the natural question of where redshifted energy goes, paired with the Newtonian picture in which successive observers simply move faster away.

**Prerequisites:** conserved momentum from metric symmetry, cosmological redshift

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.430 (pdf 448)

### cosmography

*technique · developed*

Mapping the expansion history of the Universe, via distance measures and the redshift dependence of H, without yet using Einstein's equations to explain it.

### luminosity distance

*definition · core* · also: d_L

d_L = (L/4\pi F)^{1/2}

d_L = R_0 r(1+z)

### standard candle

*definition · revisited*

A class of sources whose intrinsic luminosity is known, from theory or from calibration on nearby examples, so that measured flux yields luminosity distance.

### cosmic distance ladder

*technique · developed* · also: distance ladder

A chain of distance methods starting from geometric parallax of nearby stars, where each brighter standard candle is calibrated with distances from the previous rung so that it can be used farther away.

**How introduced:** Described generically, then made concrete by the Cepheid-to-supernova calibration and its systematic risks.

**Prerequisites:** standard candle, parallax

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.431 (pdf 449); SCH §13.2 p.434 (pdf 452); SCH §13.2 p.435 (pdf 453)

### standard siren

*definition · revisited* · also: gravitational-wave standard siren

A compact binary coalescence whose gravitational waveform directly reveals its luminosity distance without astronomical calibration; because the flux along the orbital axis is four times that in the orbital plane, the inclination must be inferred (from polarization measured by a detector network), and a redshift must come from a host galaxy or statistically.

**How introduced:** Revisited from Sections 9.6 and 12.4: GW170817's H_0, statistical use of binary black holes (Schutz 1986), O4 expectations, and LISA mergers at high redshift as dark-energy probes affected by weak lensing.

**Prerequisites:** luminosity distance, gravitational wave polarization

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.432 (pdf 450); SCH §13.2 p.435 (pdf 453); SCH §13.2 p.436 (pdf 454); SCH §13.3 p.445 (pdf 463); SCH §13.3 p.446 (pdf 464)

### angular-diameter distance

*definition · developed* · also: d_A

d_A = D/theta, the distance at which an object of known proper transverse size D would subtend the observed angle theta in Euclidean space; in FRW d_A = R_e r = d_L/(1+z)^2, where R_e is the scale factor at emission.

**How introduced:** Introduced by analogy with luminosity distance; the CMB's intrinsic fluctuation scale and BAO are its key uses for H_0.

**Prerequisites:** luminosity distance, scale factor

$$
d_A = D/\theta = d_L/(1+z)^2

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.433 (pdf 451); SCH §13.2 p.436 (pdf 454); SCH §13.4 p.453 (pdf 471)

### Cepheid period-luminosity relation

*law · developed* · also: Leavitt law

The strong correlation between a Cepheid variable's pulsation period and its mean intrinsic luminosity, found by Henrietta Leavitt in 1908 from Magellanic Cloud stars at a common distance; calibrated by parallax (now from Gaia), it anchors the extragalactic distance ladder, though it may drift slightly with chemical composition.

**How introduced:** Presented as historically the most trusted candle whose recalibrations explain changes in H_0 estimates.

**Prerequisites:** standard candle

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.434 (pdf 452); SCH §13.2 p.435 (pdf 453)

### Type Ia supernova

*phenomenon · developed* · also: SN Ia, thermonuclear supernova

The explosion of a white dwarf, usually driven by accretion from a companion, in which nuclear burning ignited near the Chandrasekhar mass incinerates the whole star; since degeneracy physics fixes the progenitor mass, peak luminosities are similar and largely independent of cosmic epoch, making them bright standard candles once calibrated with Cepheids in nearby hosts.

**How introduced:** Contrasted with core-collapse (Type II) supernovae that leave neutron stars or black holes; the calibration chain and the discovery of acceleration follow.

**Prerequisites:** Chandrasekhar limit, white dwarf, standard candle

### tip of the red giant branch

### Hubble tension

### perfect-fluid model of the cosmic contents

*solution-or-model · introduced*

Idealization of matter and radiation as a homogeneous perfect fluid at rest in comoving coordinates, with rho and p depending only on time; any bulk flow would single out a direction and break isotropy.

### cosmological energy equation

*law · core* · also: fluid equation, first law for the expanding Universe, continuity equation in FRW

The time component of T^{mu nu}_{;nu} = 0 in FRW: d(rho R^3)/dt = -p d(R^3)/dt, equivalently rhodot + 3(rho + p) Rdot/R = 0; energy in a comoving volume changes by the -p dV work done as it expands.

**How introduced:** Spatial components vanish by isotropy; the result is derived via Christoffel symbols in Exercise 13.14 and interpreted thermodynamically.

**Prerequisites:** perfect-fluid model of the cosmic contents, stress-energy conservation, Christoffel symbols

$$
\frac{d}{dt}(\rho R^3) = -p\frac{d}{dt}(R^3)
$$

### Friedmann equation

*law · core* · also: tt-component of Einstein's equations for FRW, energy equation for the scale factor

(1/2) Rdot^2 = -k/2 + (4 pi/3) R^2 (rho_m + rho_Lambda), the one independent Einstein equation for an FRW model (built from G_tt = 3(Rdot/R)^2 + 3k/R^2), linking expansion rate, spatial curvature and total energy density; equivalently 3H^2/8 pi = -3k/(8 pi R^2) + rho_m + rho_Lambda.

**How introduced:** Isotropy leaves G_tt and G_rr, and the Bianchi identity relates them, so only G_tt needs computing (Exercise 13.16); Lambda is moved into the source as a fluid.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann-Robertson-Walker metric, Einstein field equations, cosmological constant

$$
\tfrac12\dot R^2 = -\tfrac12 k + \tfrac43\pi R^2(\rho_m + \rho_\Lambda)
$$

**Where:** SCH §13.3 p.439 (pdf 457); SCH §13.3 p.440 (pdf 458); SCH §13.3 p.444 (pdf 462)

### cosmological constant

*law · core* · also: Lambda

A constant term added to Einstein's equations, G_{mu nu} + Lambda g_{mu nu} = 8 pi T_{mu nu}; it is equivalent to a stress-energy -(Lambda/8 pi) g^{alpha beta}, i.e. a fluid with constant energy density Lambda/8 pi and pressure equal to minus that density.

**How introduced:** Introduced in the tt field equation, reinterpreted as a contribution to the stress-energy that quantum gravity might one day compute, discussed historically (Einstein's static universe and later disavowal), and reused as the engine of inflation.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein field equations, perfect fluid stress-energy tensor

$$
T_\Lambda^{\alpha\beta} = -(\Lambda/8\pi) g^{\alpha\beta}
$$

**Where:** SCH §13.3 p.440 (pdf 458); SCH §13.3 p.443 (pdf 461); SCH §13.4 p.459 (pdf 477)

### dark energy

*phenomenon · core*

The energy component that is not tied to any known matter field and drives accelerating expansion; in its simplest form it is the cosmological-constant fluid with rho_Lambda constant in time and p_Lambda = -rho_Lambda, though it might instead be a field whose density and pressure evolve.

**How introduced:** Previewed in 13.1 as a relativistic energy with negative pressure, named formally in 13.3 via Eq. 13.54, and discussed as an open problem with LISA as a possible test of time variation.

**Prerequisites:** cosmological constant, negative pressure (tension)

$$
\rho_\Lambda = \Lambda/8\pi,\; p_\Lambda = -\rho_\Lambda
$$

**Where:** SCH §13.1 p.417 (pdf 435); SCH §13.3 p.440 (pdf 458); SCH §13.3 p.445 (pdf 463); SCH §13.3 p.446 (pdf 464)

### acceleration equation

*law · core* · also: second Friedmann equation, Raychaudhuri equation for FRW

Rddot/R = -(4 pi/3)(rho + 3p), with rho and p totals including dark energy; the expansion decelerates when rho + 3p > 0 and accelerates when it is negative.

**How introduced:** Derived (Exercise 13.17) from the energy equation and the time derivative of the Friedmann equation, instead of from Christoffel symbols, to answer how positive energy can cause acceleration.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann equation, cosmological energy equation

*physical-quantity · revisited* · also: active gravitational mass

The combination of energy density and (isotropic) pressure that sources gravitational attraction in GR; for dark energy rho + 3p = -2 rho_Lambda, so it gravitates repulsively.

**How introduced:** Linked back to Exercise 8.20, where rho + 3p was the source of the distant Newtonian field, now controlling cosmic deceleration or acceleration.

### negative pressure (tension)

*phenomenon · developed* · also: tension

A negative diagonal stress component, i.e. tension, as along a stretched rubber band; it is physically ordinary, but dark energy's tension is isotropic and equal in magnitude to its energy density, and tension does not produce any net local force in a homogeneous medium.

### effective potential for the scale factor

*technique · developed* · also: energy-method analysis of cosmic expansion

Reading the Friedmann equation as kinetic energy (1/2) Rdot^2 plus a potential -(4 pi/3) R^2 (rho_m + rho_Lambda) equal to a constant total energy -k/2, so qualitative past and future behaviour follows from how R^2 rho scales with R, as with orbits in an effective potential.

### fate of the Universe

*phenomenon · developed* · also: Big Crunch, eternal expansion, recollapse

For positive matter density and Lambda >= 0: an expanding open model expands forever; a flat model expands forever if Lambda > 0, and with Lambda = 0 slows toward zero rate as R grows; a closed Lambda = 0 model reaches maximum size and recollapses to a Big Crunch; a closed Lambda > 0 model's fate depends on the balance of matter and dark energy. All rely on the cosmological principle.

**How introduced:** Deduced directly from the energy form of the Friedmann equation; Figure 13.4 shows the dividing line between expanding forever and recollapse.

**Prerequisites:** effective potential for the scale factor, curvature parameter k

**Where:** SCH §13.3 p.441 (pdf 459); SCH §13.3 p.446 (pdf 464)

### Big Bang

*phenomenon · core* · also: cosmological singularity, initial singularity

The state R = 0 reached a finite time in the past in any FRW model with positive matter density and nonnegative Lambda; curvature and tidal forces diverge there and classical GR cannot be extended to earlier times.

**How introduced:** Argued via the energy equation (matter term dominates at small R, so R never turned around) and made explicit by the radiation-dominated solution; robustness discussed via singularity theorems and quantum caveats.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann equation, radiation-dominated era, curvature singularity

**Where:** SCH §13.3 p.441 (pdf 459); SCH §13.3 p.442 (pdf 460); SCH §13.3 p.443 (pdf 461)

### singularity theorems

*theorem · mention* · also: Penrose-Hawking singularity theorems

Theorems showing that, given conditions on the stress-energy (such as positive energy) and the validity of Einstein's equations, a universe like ours must have a past singularity even without homogeneity or isotropy, though they do not determine its nature.

**How introduced:** Cited (Hawking and Ellis 1973) to argue the Big Bang does not hinge on symmetry, followed by the caveats on their assumptions.

**Prerequisites:** Big Bang

**Notes:** The book overstates the theorems. They prove that past-directed timelike geodesics are incomplete, and only under energy conditions. The cosmological version needs the strong energy condition rho + 3p >= 0, which dark energy and the inflaton violate. So inflation plus dark energy weakens, though does not remove, the case for a generic initial singularity. The book does concede that the theorems assume Einstein's equations without Lambda.

**Where:** SCH §13.3 p.442 (pdf 460); SCH §13.3 p.443 (pdf 461)

### Planck time

*physical-quantity · mention* · also: t_Pl

The characteristic time of quantum gravity, t_Pl = sqrt(hbar G/c^5), about 5e-44 s (order 1e-43 s), within which classical conclusions about the Big Bang are unreliable because quantum fluctuations can violate energy positivity.

**How introduced:** Introduced when questioning the singularity conclusion, referencing the Planck mass of Chapter 11; the practical point is that cosmology can start from about 100 Planck times.

**Prerequisites:** Planck mass, Hawking radiation

$$
t_{\rm Pl} = G M_{\rm Pl}/c^3 \sim 10^{-43}\,\mathrm{s}
$$

**Notes:** The book prints G M_Pl/c^2, which has dimensions of length; the time needs c^3.

**Where:** SCH §13.3 p.443 (pdf 461)

### cold dark matter and Lambda-CDM

### radiative cooling of baryons in dark matter wells

After decoupling, infalling gas atoms collide and are excited, and because the density is low they radiate before the next collision; the photons escape the well, draining energy so the baryons sink deeper and actually heat up (negative specific heat), concentrating at the bottom while collisionless dark matter stays extended.

**How introduced:** Named in the H_0 methods list, explained after photon drag in 13.4.

**Prerequisites:** angular-diameter distance, photon drag

**Notes:** The book says the CMB value of H_0 comes from measuring BAO scales today and extrapolating them back to z = 1060 and 1090. It also calls the acoustic waves waves 'in the dark matter'. Both need correcting. The sound waves travel in the photon-baryon plasma, since collisionless dark matter supports no pressure waves. Planck's H_0 = 67.4 comes from the CMB spectra alone: a Lambda-CDM fit computes the sound horizon at last scattering from early-Universe physics and compares it with the measured acoustic angular scale. Galaxy-survey BAO is an independent standard ruler, often combined with the CMB.

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.436 (pdf 454); SCH §13.4 p.454 (pdf 472)

### photon drag

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: drag epoch, baryon drag

In the book's account, the short period after decoupling when gas in overdense regions, heated by compression, was re-ionized and coupled back to the photons, so baryons could not yet fall into dark-matter wells. The book quotes Planck's decoupling at z = 1090 and the end of effective drag at z = 1060.

**How introduced:** Introduced to explain why the BAO scale is anchored to a slightly later epoch than decoupling.

**Prerequisites:** decoupling, baryon acoustic oscillations

**Notes:** The standard picture is different. The 'drag epoch' is when Compton scattering off the still-numerous photons stops holding the baryons back, which happens slightly after last scattering because photons outnumber baryons about 1e9 to 1. It does not involve re-ionization by compressional heating. Teach the z_drag of about 1060 value, but not the book's mechanism.

**Where:** SCH §13.4 p.453 (pdf 471); SCH §13.4 p.454 (pdf 472)

### CMB polarization from anisotropic scattering

*phenomenon · developed* · also: Thomson-scattering polarization

When electrons scatter radiation, the outgoing polarization is perpendicular to the plane containing incoming and outgoing directions; if the incident radiation is brighter from some directions than others (as between a hot and a cold region) the light scattered toward us acquires net linear polarization.

**How introduced:** Built from a 90-degree scattering argument: isotropic radiation cancels, while a hot spot next to a cold spot leaves a net polarization perpendicular to the line joining them.

**Prerequisites:** transverse electromagnetic waves, CMB temperature anisotropies

**Where:** SCH §13.4 p.455 (pdf 473)

### E-mode and B-mode polarization

*definition · core* · also: E modes, B modes, curl-free and curl polarization patterns

Split of the CMB polarization pattern into a gradient-like, curl-free part (E) and a curl-like part (B); scalar density perturbations (acoustic waves), even when superposed, produce only E, while gravitational waves produce both, so primordial B modes are a signature of gravitational waves at decoupling.

**How introduced:** Argued with vector-field language on the sky: take the curl to remove acoustic polarization; complications (vorticity, obliquely travelling waves, Galactic dust) noted.

**Prerequisites:** CMB polarization from anisotropic scattering, primordial gravitational waves

**Where:** SCH §13.4 p.455 (pdf 473); SCH §13.4 p.456 (pdf 474)

### primordial gravitational waves

*phenomenon · developed* · also: stochastic gravitational wave background from the early Universe, inflationary gravitational waves

Gravitational waves produced in the very early Universe (for example amplified during inflation) that propagate essentially unaltered except for redshift; they could be seen directly by LISA or Einstein Telescope, or indirectly through CMB B-mode polarization, and their energy density is bounded by nucleosynthesis.

**How introduced:** Motivated as the cleanest probe of the earliest times; area-preserving transverse action means no temperature signal, only polarization.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational waves, inflation, E-mode and B-mode polarization

**Where:** SCH §13.4 p.454 (pdf 472); SCH §13.4 p.455 (pdf 473); SCH §13.4 p.456 (pdf 474); SCH §13.4 p.458 (pdf 476); SCH §13.4 p.460 (pdf 478)

### big bang nucleosynthesis

*phenomenon · developed* · also: primordial nucleosynthesis, BBN

Formation of light nuclei, mainly helium-4 with traces of helium-3, lithium and others, in the first few minutes as the plasma cooled through tens of keV; heavier nuclei hardly formed because expansion cooled the plasma too fast. Yields depend on the baryon density and the expansion rate, fixing Omega_b about 0.04.

**How introduced:** Reached by running the clock back to about 200 s; stars destroy rather than make these light elements, so present abundances are primordial.

**Prerequisites:** radiation-dominated era, thermal history of the Universe

**Where:** SCH §13.3 p.445 (pdf 463); SCH §13.4 p.457 (pdf 475)

### nucleosynthesis bound on relativistic species

*principle · developed* · also: neutrino counting from BBN

Extra massless species would add energy density, strengthen gravity and speed up the early expansion, changing helium and lithium yields; matching observations limits neutrino families to the three known, forces other relativistic backgrounds (including primordial gravitational waves) to be well below the photon density, and requires dark matter to have been nonrelativistic (mass well above 50 keV).

**How introduced:** Argued qualitatively from reaction-network computations.

**Prerequisites:** big bang nucleosynthesis, radiation-dominated era

**Where:** SCH §13.4 p.457 (pdf 475); SCH §13.4 p.458 (pdf 476)

### electron-positron annihilation and photon-to-baryon ratio

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: specific entropy of the Universe

Around kT ~ 500 keV (t ~ 1 s) electron-positron pairs were continually created and annihilated; as the Universe cooled creation stopped and the pairs annihilated, leaving about one electron per 1e9 photons, a ratio that measures the entropy per particle of the Universe.

**How introduced:** Part of the backward walk in energy.

**Prerequisites:** thermal history of the Universe

**Where:** SCH §13.4 p.458 (pdf 476)

### matter-antimatter asymmetry

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: baryon asymmetry

No significant antimatter exists today, so in the early plasma matter must have slightly outnumbered antimatter (about one extra particle per billion photons) for both electrons and protons; the physical cause is an open problem of particle physics.

**How introduced:** Inferred from the leftover electrons after annihilation and the absence of anti-galaxies.

**Prerequisites:** electron-positron annihilation and photon-to-baryon ratio

**Where:** SCH §13.4 p.458 (pdf 476)

### quark-gluon plasma

*definition · mention*

The state of matter at times earlier than about 1e-5 s, when temperatures and densities were so high that quarks and gluons behaved as free particles rather than being bound into individual protons and neutrons.

**How introduced:** One step in the energy ladder toward collider-tested physics.

**Prerequisites:** thermal history of the Universe

**Where:** SCH §13.4 p.458 (pdf 476)

### horizon problem

*phenomenon · developed* · also: smoothness problem

In standard Big Bang models, regions on opposite sides of our sky were outside each other's particle horizons when, for example, helium was made, yet they show the same abundances and conditions; without a causal smoothing mechanism the uniformity must be put in as an initial condition.

**How introduced:** Posed with the right-hand panel of Figure 13.2 alongside the missing origin of density perturbations, as motivation for inflation.

**Prerequisites:** particle horizon, big bang nucleosynthesis

**Where:** SCH §13.2 p.422 (pdf 440); SCH §13.4 p.459 (pdf 477)

### inflation

*solution-or-model · core* · also: inflationary scenario, inflationary cosmology

A hypothesized very early phase (near 1e-35 s) dominated by a large temporary vacuum energy acting as a cosmological constant, giving exponential expansion with growth time (3/8 pi rho_Lambda)^{1/2}; it stretches a small, causally smoothed region to encompass the observable Universe and amplifies quantum fluctuations into density perturbations and gravitational waves, after which the vacuum energy converts to particles.

**How introduced:** Previewed in 13.1; developed after the horizon and perturbation problems, credited to Starobinsky, Guth and Linde, and classed as a model rather than a theory until its fields are identified.

**Prerequisites:** cosmological constant, horizon problem, vacuum energy as a cosmological constant

$$
R \propto e^{t/\tau_\Lambda},\; \tau_\Lambda = (3/8\pi\rho_\Lambda)^{1/2}
$$

**Where:** SCH §13.1 p.417 (pdf 435); SCH §13.4 p.459 (pdf 477); SCH §13.4 p.460 (pdf 478)

### vacuum energy as a cosmological constant

*principle · developed*

Since the quantum vacuum must look the same to every inertial observer, its stress-energy tensor must be Lorentz invariant, and the only invariant symmetric (0,2) tensor is the metric; hence any vacuum energy behaves exactly like a cosmological constant with p = -rho.

**How introduced:** Used to explain why attempts to compute Lambda focus on vacuum energy and why a high-energy vacuum phase transition could drive inflation.

**Prerequisites:** cosmological constant, vacuum fluctuations, Lorentz invariance

**Where:** SCH §13.4 p.460 (pdf 478)

### inflationary generation of perturbations

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: amplification of quantum fluctuations

During exponential expansion, microscopic vacuum fluctuations are stretched and amplified into classical perturbations, producing both the density fluctuations seen in the CMB (with the right amplitude and spectrum in calculations) and a background of gravitational waves.

**How introduced:** Explained with a loose comparison to a nonlinear oscillator pumping up its amplitude.

**Prerequisites:** inflation, vacuum fluctuations

**Where:** SCH §13.4 p.460 (pdf 478)

### loop quantum gravity

*solution-or-model · mention*

An approach that quantizes spacetime geometry directly, replacing the smooth manifold at the Planck scale by a network of nested, tangled loops; in some cosmological applications the Big Bang becomes a bounce from an earlier collapsing phase.

**How introduced:** Surveyed briefly as one route beyond classical GR (Bojowald 2008).

**Prerequisites:** Big Bang, Planck time

**Where:** SCH §13.4 p.461 (pdf 479)

### string theory and braneworlds

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: brane cosmology, large extra dimensions

An approach unifying all interactions, gravity included, using strings in higher-dimensional spacetime; extra dimensions may be Planck-sized or large, with our Universe a four-dimensional brane on which non-gravitational forces are confined while gravity extends into the bulk, altering the inverse-square law below some scale (experimentally below about 40 micrometres).

**How introduced:** Surveyed with the caution that dramatic scenarios come from deliberately simplified model theories.

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian inverse-square law

**Where:** SCH §13.4 p.461 (pdf 479); SCH §13.4 p.462 (pdf 480)

### shadow matter

*phenomenon · mention*

Hypothetical matter on a nearby parallel brane that would interact with our world only through gravity.

**How introduced:** Mentioned among possible brane-world consequences.

**Prerequisites:** string theory and braneworlds

**Where:** SCH §13.4 p.462 (pdf 480)

## Key equations

### (13.1) Multipole expansion of the CMB temperature · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.420 (pdf 438)

$$
T(l,b) = \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell} T_{\ell m}\, Y_{\ell m}(l,b)
$$

Sky temperature decomposed into spherical harmonics; l = 1 terms encode our motion (dipole), higher l the intrinsic anisotropy on angular scales of roughly 180 deg / l.

**Symbols:** l, b: Galactic longitude and latitude; ell, m: multipole indices; T_lm: complex coefficients; Y_lm: spherical harmonics

### (13.2) Hubble-Lemaitre law · central · SCH §13.2 p.420 (pdf 438)

$$
v = H d
$$

Local recession velocity is proportional to distance with a direction-independent constant; exact only for v much less than 1.

**Symbols:** v: recession speed; H: Hubble-Lemaitre parameter; d: proper distance

### H_0 in several unit systems and the Hubble time · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.421 (pdf 439)

$$
H_0 = 71\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}} = 2.3\times10^{-18}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} = 7.7\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}},\quad t_H = H_0^{-1} = 4.3\times10^{17}\,\mathrm{s}
$$

Conversions using 1 Mpc = 3.1e22 m and division by c; the Hubble time is about 14 Gyr, and v = c at d = c/H_0 = 1.3e26 m = 4200 Mpc.

**Symbols:** t_H: Hubble-Lemaitre time

### (13.3) · derivation-step · SCH §13.2 p.423 (pdf 441)

$$
\mathrm{d}l^2(t_0) = h_{ij}(t_0)\,\mathrm{d}x^i\mathrm{d}x^j
$$

Line element of a constant-time slice in comoving coordinates at a reference time.

**Symbols:** h_ij: spatial metric (unrelated to linearized-theory h)

### (13.4) · derivation-step · SCH §13.2 p.423 (pdf 441)

$$
\mathrm{d}l^2(t_1) = f(t_1,t_0)\,h_{ij}(t_0)\,\mathrm{d}x^i\mathrm{d}x^j = h_{ij}(t_1)\,\mathrm{d}x^i\mathrm{d}x^j
$$

Isotropic expansion requires every spatial metric component to grow by the same factor; otherwise the Hubble law becomes a matrix (Exercise 13.4).

**Symbols:** f: common growth factor

### (13.5) Scale-factor form of the spatial metric · central · SCH §13.2 p.423 (pdf 441)

$$
\mathrm{d}l^2(t) = R^2(t)\,h_{ij}\,\mathrm{d}x^i\mathrm{d}x^j
$$

All proper distances between comoving points scale with one function R(t), normalized to 1 at t_0; h_ij is now time independent.

**Symbols:** R(t): scale factor

### (13.6) · derivation-step · SCH §13.2 p.424 (pdf 442)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = -\mathrm{d}t^2 + g_{0i}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x^i + R^2(t)\,h_{ij}\,\mathrm{d}x^i\mathrm{d}x^j
$$

General spacetime extension with g_00 = -1 because t is proper time along comoving world lines.

**Symbols:** g_0i: time-space metric components

### (13.7) · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.424 (pdf 442)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = -\mathrm{d}t^2 + R^2(t)\,h_{ij}\,\mathrm{d}x^i\mathrm{d}x^j
$$

Requiring constant-t slices to match comoving observers' simultaneity sets g_0i = e_0 . e_i = 0.

### (13.8) · derivation-step · SCH §13.2 p.424 (pdf 442)

$$
\mathrm{d}l^2 = e^{2\Lambda(r)}\,\mathrm{d}r^2 + r^2\,\mathrm{d}\Omega^2
$$

Most general 3-metric isotropic about one point, borrowed from the spherical-star form (Eq. 10.5).

**Symbols:** Lambda(r): metric function (not the cosmological constant); dOmega^2: unit-sphere line element

### (13.9) Einstein tensor of the isotropic 3-metric · derivation-step · SCH §13.2 p.424 (pdf 442)

$$
G_{rr} = -\frac{1}{r^2}e^{2\Lambda}\left(1-e^{-2\Lambda}\right),\quad G_{\theta\theta} = -r\,e^{-2\Lambda}\Lambda',\quad G_{\phi\phi} = \sin^2\theta\,G_{\theta\theta}
$$

Obtained from the Chapter 10 components (Eqs. 10.15-10.17) by setting Phi = 0, since a static product metric has the same spatial Einstein tensor.

**Symbols:** prime: d/dr

### (13.10) Trace of the 3D Einstein tensor · derivation-step · SCH §13.2 p.425 (pdf 443)

$$
G = G_{ij}g^{ij} = -\frac{1}{r^2} + \frac{1}{r^2}e^{-2\Lambda}\left(1-2r\Lambda'\right) = -\frac{1}{r^2}\left[1-\left(r e^{-2\Lambda}\right)'\right]
$$

A scalar that must be the same everywhere on a homogeneous slice; in three dimensions it equals minus half the Ricci scalar.

**Symbols:** G: trace of Einstein tensor

### (13.11) Homogeneity condition · derivation-step · SCH §13.2 p.425 (pdf 443)

$$
\kappa = -\frac{1}{r^2}\left[r\left(1-e^{-2\Lambda}\right)\right]'
$$

Setting the trace equal to a constant kappa gives a first-order ODE for e^{-2Lambda}.

**Symbols:** kappa: constant

### (13.12) · derivation-step · SCH §13.2 p.425 (pdf 443)

$$
g_{rr} = e^{2\Lambda} = \frac{1}{1 + \tfrac13\kappa r^2 - A/r}
$$

Integrated solution; regularity (local flatness) at r = 0 demands the integration constant A = 0 (Exercise 13.7).

**Symbols:** A: integration constant

### (13.13) Homogeneous isotropic 3-metric · central · SCH §13.2 p.425 (pdf 443)

$$
g_{rr} = \frac{1}{1-kr^2},\qquad \mathrm{d}l^2 = \frac{\mathrm{d}r^2}{1-kr^2} + r^2\,\mathrm{d}\Omega^2
$$

The unique spatial metric compatible with a constant curvature scalar and isotropy about the origin, with k = -kappa/3.

**Symbols:** k: curvature parameter

### (13.14) Friedmann-Robertson-Walker metric · central · SCH §13.2 p.425 (pdf 443)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = -\mathrm{d}t^2 + R^2(t)\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}r^2}{1-kr^2} + r^2\,\mathrm{d}\Omega^2\right]
$$

Line element of every homogeneous isotropic cosmology; R(t) holds the dynamics, k the spatial geometry.

**Symbols:** t: cosmic time; r: comoving radial coordinate

### (13.15) Normalization of k · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.425 (pdf 443)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = -\mathrm{d}t^2 + \tilde R^2(t)\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde r^2}{1+\tilde r^2} + \tilde r^2\,\mathrm{d}\Omega^2\right],\quad \tilde r = \sqrt3\,r,\ \tilde R = R/\sqrt3\quad(k=-3\to\tilde k=-1)
$$

Worked rescaling for k = -3 showing any nonzero k can be normalized to magnitude 1 while its sign cannot change. Printed error: the book shows 1 - r~^2 in the denominator, but the substitution gives 1 + r~^2 (k~ = -1), as transcribed here.

### (13.16) Flat slice · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.426 (pdf 444)

$$
\mathrm{d}l^2 = R^2(t_0)\left[\mathrm{d}r^2 + r^2\mathrm{d}\Omega^2\right] = \mathrm{d}r'^2 + r'^2\,\mathrm{d}\Omega^2,\quad r' = R(t_0)\,r
$$

For k = 0 each slice is Euclidean space with a rescaled radius.

### (13.17) · derivation-step · SCH §13.2 p.426 (pdf 444)

$$
\mathrm{d}\chi^2 = \frac{\mathrm{d}r^2}{1-r^2}
$$

Defines proper radial coordinate chi for k = +1 with chi = 0 at r = 0.

**Symbols:** chi: proper radial angle

### (13.18) · derivation-step · SCH §13.2 p.426 (pdf 444)

$$
r = \sin\chi
$$

Integral of Eq. 13.17.

### (13.19) Three-sphere slice (k = +1) · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.426 (pdf 444)

$$
\mathrm{d}l^2 = R^2(t_0)\left[\mathrm{d}\chi^2 + \sin^2\chi\left(\mathrm{d}\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi^2\right)\right]
$$

Metric of a three-sphere of radius R(t_0): finite volume, no edge.

### (13.20) Hyperbolic slice (k = -1) · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.426 (pdf 444)

$$
\mathrm{d}l^2 = R^2(t_0)\left(\mathrm{d}\chi^2 + \sinh^2\chi\,\mathrm{d}\Omega^2\right)
$$

Sphere circumferences grow like sinh chi, faster than in flat space; realized as a constant-interval hyperboloid in Minkowski spacetime (Exercise 13.8).

### (13.21) Radial null line · derivation-step · SCH §13.2 p.428 (pdf 446)

$$
0 = -\mathrm{d}t^2 + R^2(t)\,\mathrm{d}\chi^2
$$

Line element restricted to a radial photon path in any of the three geometries; the metric is independent of chi so p_chi is conserved.

### (13.22) Cosmological redshift · central · SCH §13.2 p.428 (pdf 446)

$$
1 + z = \frac{R(t_0)}{R(t)}
$$

Locally measured photon energy scales as 1/R, so wavelength stretches with the scale factor between emission at t and reception at t_0; peculiar motions add further (1 + z) factors.

**Symbols:** z: redshift; t_0: reception time

### (13.23) Hubble-Lemaitre parameter as expansion rate · central · SCH §13.2 p.428 (pdf 446)

$$
H(t) = \frac{\dot R(t)}{R(t)}
$$

Instantaneous fractional expansion rate at any cosmic time.

**Symbols:** dot: d/dt

### (13.24) · derivation-step · SCH §13.2 p.428 (pdf 446)

$$
d_0 = R(t_0)\,\chi
$$

Present proper distance to a comoving galaxy at coordinate chi.

### (13.25) · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.429 (pdf 447)

$$
v = \dot R(t_0)\,\chi = \left(\dot R/R\right)_0 d_0
$$

Rate of change of proper distance, reproducing v = Hd and identifying H_0 = (Rdot/R)_0.

### (13.26) · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.429 (pdf 447)

$$
R(t) = R_0\exp\left[\int_{t_0}^{t} H(t')\,\mathrm{d}t'\right]
$$

Formal integral of H = Rdot/R.

### (13.27) Taylor expansion of the scale factor · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.429 (pdf 447)

$$
R(t) = R_0\left[1 + H_0(t-t_0) + \tfrac12\left(H_0^2 + \dot H_0\right)(t-t_0)^2 + \dots\right]
$$

Second-order expansion; the Hdot_0 term carries acceleration or deceleration information.

### (13.28) Deceleration parameter · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.429 (pdf 447)

$$
q_0 = -\frac{R_0\ddot R_0}{\dot R_0^2} = -\left(1 + \frac{\dot H_0}{H_0^2}\right)
$$

Dimensionless replacement for Hdot_0; positive for deceleration, negative today.

### (13.29) · derivation-step · SCH §13.2 p.429 (pdf 447)

$$
1 + z(t) = \exp\left[-\int_{t_0}^{t} H(t')\,\mathrm{d}t'\right]
$$

Redshift of light emitted at t, combining Eqs. 13.22 and 13.26.

### (13.30) Redshift versus emission time · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.429 (pdf 447)

$$
z(t) = H_0(t_0-t) + \tfrac12\left(H_0^2 - \dot H_0\right)(t_0-t)^2 + \dots
$$

Taylor expansion of Eq. 13.29 (Exercise 13.11).

### (13.31) Look-back time · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.430 (pdf 448)

$$
t_0 - t(z) = H_0^{-1}\left[z - \tfrac12\left(1 - \dot H_0/H_0^2\right)z^2 + \cdots\right]
$$

Series inversion giving elapsed proper time to an event of redshift z.

### Equation · derivation-step · SCH §13.2 p.430 (pdf 448)

$$
H(t) = H_0 + \dot H_0(t-t_0) + \cdots
$$

Linear expansion of H used with Eq. 13.31 to express H in terms of z.

### (13.32) Hubble-Lemaitre parameter versus redshift · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.430 (pdf 448)

$$
H(z) = H_0\left(1 - \frac{\dot H_0}{H_0^2}z + \cdots\right)
$$

First-order redshift dependence of the expansion rate.

### (13.33) Exact H from redshift drift · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.430 (pdf 448)

$$
H(t) = -\frac{\dot z}{1+z}
$$

Exact inversion of Eq. 13.29, with z regarded as a function of emission time t.

### (13.34) Euclidean flux law · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.431 (pdf 449)

$$
L = 4\pi d^2 F
$$

Luminosity inferred from flux for an isotropic emitter at known distance in flat static space.

**Symbols:** L: intrinsic luminosity; F: received energy flux

### (13.35) Luminosity distance · central · SCH §13.2 p.431 (pdf 449)

$$
d_L = \left(\frac{L}{4\pi F}\right)^{1/2}

Operational definition of distance for any source of known luminosity, at any redshift.

N = \frac{L\,\delta t_e}{h\nu_e}

Photons emitted in interval delta t_e at frequency nu_e.

### (13.37) · derivation-step · SCH §13.2 p.432 (pdf 450)

A = 4\pi R_0^2 r^2

Proper area of the sphere of photons when it reaches the observer's comoving radius r at t_0.

$$
h\nu_0 = \frac{h\nu_e}{1+z}

Each photon's energy is reduced by the redshift factor.

### (13.39) · derivation-step · SCH §13.2 p.432 (pdf 450)

\delta t_0 = \delta t_e(1+z)

The burst arrives stretched in time by the same factor.

$$
F = \frac{L}{A(1+z)^2}
$$

Two powers of 1+z: one from energy per photon, one from arrival rate.

### (13.41) Luminosity distance in FRW · central · SCH §13.2 p.433 (pdf 451)

$$
d_L = R_0\,r\,(1+z)

Relates the observable d_L to the comoving coordinate of the source and redshift.

### (13.42) Radial photon equation · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.433 (pdf 451)

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{(1-kr^2)^{1/2}} = -\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{R(t)} = \frac{\mathrm{d}z}{R_0 H(z)}

Connects source coordinate r to redshift; curvature enters only at second order for small r.

### (13.43) Second-order luminosity distance-redshift relation · central · SCH §13.2 p.433 (pdf 451)

$$
d_L = R_0 r(1+z) = \frac{z}{H_0}\left[1 + \left(1 + \frac12\frac{\dot H_0}{H_0^2}\right)z\right] + \dots

Equivalent to d_L = (z/H_0)[1 + (1 - q_0)z/2]; measuring many d_L, z pairs yields H_0 and Hdot_0 (hence acceleration).

### (13.44) Angular-diameter distance · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.433 (pdf 451)

$$
d_A = D/\theta
$$

Distance inferred from the angle subtended by a known proper transverse size.

**Symbols:** D: proper diameter; theta: angular size

### (13.45) Angular-diameter versus luminosity distance · central · SCH §13.2 p.433 (pdf 451)

$$
d_A = R_e\,r = (1+z)^{-2}\,d_L
$$

Transverse size is fixed at emission, when the Universe was smaller by 1+z; Exercise cited as 13.17 in text.

**Symbols:** R_e: scale factor at emission

### (13.46) · supporting · SCH §13.2 p.433 (pdf 451)

$$
d_A = \frac{R_0 r}{1+z} = \frac{z}{H_0}\left[1 + \left(-1 + \frac12\frac{\dot H_0}{H_0^2}\right)z\right] + \dots

Small-z expansion of d_A; unlike d_L it eventually turns over at high z.

### (13.47) Cosmological energy equation · central · SCH §13.3 p.439 (pdf 457)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\rho R^3\right) = -p\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(R^3\right)

Time component of stress-energy conservation: energy in a comoving volume changes by -p dV work.

**Symbols:** rho: energy density; p: pressure

### (13.48) Matter domination · supporting · SCH §13.3 p.439 (pdf 457)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\rho R^3\right) = 0

Dust (p = 0): energy density falls as R^{-3}.

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\rho R^3\right) = -\tfrac13\rho\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(R^3\right)

### (13.50) Radiation domination · supporting · SCH §13.3 p.439 (pdf 457)

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\rho R^4\right) = 0
$$

Radiation energy density falls as R^{-4}: number dilution plus redshift of each quantum.

### (13.51) tt component of the FRW Einstein tensor · supporting · SCH §13.3 p.439 (pdf 457)

$$
G_{tt} = 3\left(\frac{\dot R}{R}\right)^2 + \frac{3k}{R^2}
$$

The only Einstein-tensor component needed, since isotropy and the Bianchi identity make the rest redundant (Exercise 13.16).

### (13.52) · supporting · SCH §13.3 p.440 (pdf 458)

$$
G_{tt} + \Lambda g_{tt} = 8\pi T_{tt}
$$

Einstein equation with cosmological constant.

**Symbols:** Lambda: cosmological constant

### (13.53) Cosmological constant as stress-energy · supporting · SCH §13.3 p.440 (pdf 458)

$$
T_\Lambda^{\alpha\beta} = -\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi}g^{\alpha\beta}
$$

Moving Lambda to the source side reinterprets it as a contribution to the stress-energy tensor.

### (13.54) Dark energy density and pressure · central · SCH §13.3 p.440 (pdf 458)

$$
\rho_\Lambda = \frac{\Lambda}{8\pi},\qquad p_\Lambda = -\rho_\Lambda
$$

The cosmological-constant fluid has constant density and equal-magnitude negative pressure.

### (13.55) Friedmann equation · central · SCH §13.3 p.440 (pdf 458)

$$
\tfrac12\dot R^2 = -\tfrac12 k + \tfrac43\pi R^2\left(\rho_m + \rho_\Lambda\right)
$$

Energy-like first integral for R(t): kinetic term, constant 'total energy' -k/2, and a potential term; rho_m includes radiation.

**Symbols:** rho_m: matter plus radiation density

### (13.56) Acceleration equation · central · SCH §13.3 p.441 (pdf 459)

$$
\frac{\ddot R}{R} = -\frac{4\pi}{3}\left(\rho + 3p\right)
$$

Expansion accelerates when total rho + 3p < 0, as for dominant dark energy (Exercise 13.17).

### Equation · derivation-step · SCH §13.3 p.442 (pdf 460)

$$
\dot R^2 = \tfrac83\pi B R^{-2}
$$

Friedmann equation for radiation (rho = B R^{-4}) with k and Lambda neglected.

**Symbols:** B: constant

### (13.57) · derivation-step · SCH §13.3 p.442 (pdf 460)

\frac{\mathrm{d}R}{\mathrm{d}t} = \left(\tfrac83\pi B\right)^{1/2}R^{-1}
$$

Separable ODE for the radiation era.

### (13.58) Radiation-era solution · supporting · SCH §13.3 p.442 (pdf 460)

$$
R^2 = \left(\tfrac{32}{3}\pi B\right)^{1/2}(t - T)
$$

R reaches zero a finite time ago; choosing T = 0 puts the Big Bang at t = 0, giving R proportional to t^{1/2}.

**Symbols:** T: integration constant

### Power-law expansion · supporting · SCH §13.3 p.442 (pdf 460)

R \propto t^{1/2}\ \text{(radiation)},\qquad R \propto t^{2/3}\ \text{(matter)}

Scale-factor growth with Lambda = 0 and negligible curvature (matter case: Exercise 13.19).

Epoch within which quantum gravity undermines classical singularity conclusions. The book prints G M_Pl/c^2, which is the Planck length; c^3 is needed for a time.

**Symbols:** M_Pl: Planck mass (Eq. 11.111)

### Einstein static universe condition · supporting · SCH §13.3 p.443 (pdf 461)

## Worked examples

### Scale where GR becomes necessary · intro · SCH §13.1 p.416 (pdf 434)

**Problem:** Estimate the radius at which a uniform cosmic density makes M/R of order 1, after checking M/R for the Sun, the Galaxy and clusters.

**Method:** Use M/R ~ 1e-6 for the Galaxy (1e11 stars in about 15 kpc) and ~1e-4 for clusters; then set M = 4 pi rho R^3/3 equal to R (geometrized units) with rho ~ 1e-26 kg m^-3.

**Key insight:** M/R grows as R^2 at fixed density, so relativity must matter on large enough scales even though every smaller system is Newtonian.

**Result:** R ~ 6 Gpc, well inside the observable Universe (seen to beyond 10 Gpc).

**Concepts:** relativistic regime at cosmological scales, homogeneity

### Converting the Hubble-Lemaitre constant · intro · SCH §13.2 p.421 (pdf 439)

**Problem:** Express H_0 = 71 km/s/Mpc in s^-1 and m^-1, find the Hubble time, and find the distance at which v = Hd would reach c.

**Method:** Use 1 Mpc = 3.1e22 m; divide by c for geometrized units; invert for t_H; set Hd = c.

**Key insight:** The expansion timescale is comparable to the age of the Universe, and a naive global v = Hd breaks down at the Hubble distance, forcing a relativistic reinterpretation.

**Result:** 2.3e-18 s^-1, 7.7e-27 m^-1, t_H = 4.3e17 s (about 14 Gyr); v = c at 1.3e26 m = 4200 Mpc.

**Concepts:** Hubble-Lemaitre parameter, Hubble time, preferred cosmic time

### Deriving the homogeneous isotropic 3-metric · standard · SCH §13.2 p.424 (pdf 442)

**Problem:** Find all spherically symmetric 3-metrics whose curvature scalar is the same at every point and regular at the origin.

**Method:** Take G_ij for e^{2Lambda}dr^2 + r^2 dOmega^2 from the Chapter 10 formulas with Phi = 0, form the trace G = -(1/r^2)[1 - (r e^{-2Lambda})'], set it to a constant kappa, integrate to e^{-2Lambda} = 1 + kappa r^2/3 - A/r, impose g_rr(0) = 1 so A = 0, and define k = -kappa/3.

**Key insight:** Homogeneity can be imposed through a single scalar condition, and machinery already built for stars delivers the answer without group theory.

**Result:** dl^2 = dr^2/(1 - k r^2) + r^2 dOmega^2, hence the FRW metric (13.14).

**Concepts:** homogeneity from a constant curvature scalar, Friedmann-Robertson-Walker metric, curvature parameter k

### Normalizing k by rescaling · intro · SCH §13.2 p.425 (pdf 443)

**Problem:** Show that a metric with k = -3 is the same model as one with k = -1.

**Method:** Substitute r~ = sqrt(3) r and R~ = R/sqrt(3): R^2 dr^2/(1 + 3r^2) becomes R~^2 dr~^2/(1 + r~^2) and R^2 r^2 dOmega^2 becomes R~^2 r~^2 dOmega^2, i.e. the k = -1 form. (The printed Eq. 13.15 shows 1 - r~^2 in the denominator, which would be k = +1; the plus sign is correct.)

**Key insight:** Only the sign of k is physical; its magnitude trades off against the normalization of R.

**Result:** Three distinct cases k = +1, 0, -1.

**Concepts:** curvature parameter k

### Identifying the three spatial geometries · standard · SCH §13.2 p.426 (pdf 444)

**Problem:** Show each sign of k gives a homogeneous isotropic space and identify it.

**Method:** k = 0: absorb R(t_0) into r. k = +1: substitute r = sin chi to get a three-sphere, symmetric under 4D rotations. k = -1: substitute r = sinh chi and recognize the hyperboloid of fixed timelike interval in Minkowski spacetime, symmetric under Lorentz transformations (Exercise 13.8).

**Key insight:** Embedding spaces make symmetry manifest: rotations or boosts move any point to any other while preserving the slice.

**Result:** Flat Euclidean, spherical (closed, finite volume) and hyperbolic (open) slices.

**Concepts:** flat FRW model, closed FRW model, open FRW model, three-sphere

### Cosmological redshift and H = Rdot/R · standard · SCH §13.2 p.428 (pdf 446)

**Problem:** Relate observed redshift to the scale factor and identify the Hubble-Lemaitre parameter in FRW.

**Method:** Place the observer at the origin; radial null path 0 = -dt^2 + R^2 dchi^2; metric independent of chi so p_chi conserved; locally measured energy -p^0 then scales as 1/R (Exercise 13.9). Then differentiate proper distance d_0 = R_0 chi at fixed chi.

**Key insight:** Redshift measures how much the Universe expanded while the light travelled, and Hubble's constant is the fractional expansion rate.

**Result:** 1 + z = R(t_0)/R(t_e); v = (Rdot/R)_0 d_0 so H(t) = Rdot/R; locally z = v (Exercise 13.10).

**Concepts:** cosmological redshift, conserved momentum from metric symmetry, Hubble-Lemaitre parameter

### Small-redshift cosmography expansions · standard · SCH §13.2 p.429 (pdf 447)

**Problem:** Expand the scale factor, redshift, look-back time and H to second order about the present.

**Method:** Write R = R_0 exp(integral of H), Taylor expand; combine with 1 + z = R_0/R to get z(t); invert the series for t_0 - t(z); substitute into H(t) = H_0 + Hdot_0 (t - t_0) to get H(z); note the exact inversion H = -zdot/(1+z).

**Key insight:** At second order the only new cosmological information is Hdot_0 (or q_0), which is exactly what acceleration measurements target.

**Result:** Eqs. 13.27-13.33, e.g. t_0 - t = H_0^{-1}[z - (1 - Hdot_0/H_0^2) z^2/2] and H(z) = H_0(1 - Hdot_0 z/H_0^2).

**Concepts:** deceleration parameter, look-back time, cosmography

### Luminosity distance by photon counting · standard · SCH §13.2 p.432 (pdf 450)

**Problem:** Compute the flux received from a source of luminosity L at comoving coordinate r and redshift z, and express d_L.

**Method:** Emit N = L delta t_e/(h nu_e) photons; at reception they cover a sphere of proper area 4 pi R_0^2 r^2; each photon's energy is divided by 1+z and the burst duration multiplied by 1+z; divide energy by area and time; use the radial null equation dr/sqrt(1-kr^2) = dz/(R_0 H) to first order to relate r to z.

**Key insight:** The two separate (1+z) factors (energy and arrival rate) make d_L exceed the naive distance; the emitted frequency drops out, so the result is general.

**Result:** F = L/[4 pi R_0^2 r^2 (1+z)^2], d_L = R_0 r (1+z) = (z/H_0)[1 + (1 + Hdot_0/(2H_0^2)) z] + ...; d_A = d_L/(1+z)^2.

**Concepts:** luminosity distance, angular-diameter distance, cosmological redshift

### Energy equation for dust and radiation · intro · SCH §13.3 p.439 (pdf 457)

**Problem:** Specialize d(rho R^3)/dt = -p d(R^3)/dt to pressureless matter and to radiation.

**Method:** Set p = 0 to get rho R^3 constant; set p = rho/3 and rearrange to get rho R^4 constant.

**Key insight:** Radiation dilutes faster because each quantum also loses energy to redshift (the pdV work), so radiation dominated early and matter later.

**Result:** rho_matter proportional to R^{-3}; rho_radiation proportional to R^{-4}.

**Concepts:** cosmological energy equation, matter-dominated era, radiation-dominated era

### Radiation-dominated expansion and the Big Bang · intro · SCH §13.3 p.442 (pdf 460)

**Problem:** Solve the Friedmann equation for a radiation-dominated universe with k and Lambda negligible.

**Method:** Insert rho = B R^{-4} into the Friedmann equation to get Rdot^2 = (8/3) pi B R^{-2}; separate variables R dR = sqrt(8 pi B/3) dt and integrate.

**Key insight:** R vanishes at a finite past time, so the Big Bang follows from the matter term dominating at small R; matter domination gives t^{2/3} similarly (Exercise 13.19).

**Result:** R^2 = (32 pi B/3)^{1/2} (t - T); choose T = 0 so R proportional to t^{1/2}.

**Concepts:** radiation-dominated era, Friedmann equation, Big Bang

### Acceleration equation and the role of pressure · standard · SCH §13.3 p.441 (pdf 459)

**Problem:** Show how a positive dark-energy density can make Rddot positive.

**Method:** Differentiate the Friedmann equation in time and eliminate rhodot with the energy equation (Exercise 13.17, using p_Lambda = -rho_Lambda), giving Rddot/R = -(4 pi/3)(rho + 3p); for dark energy rho + 3p = -2 rho_Lambda.

**Key insight:** In GR pressure gravitates; large isotropic tension turns the active mass negative and gravity repulsive.

**Result:** Acceleration whenever rho_Lambda exceeds half the matter density (for dust plus Lambda).

**Concepts:** acceleration equation, active gravitational mass density rho + 3p, negative pressure (tension), dark energy

### Qualitative histories from the energy equation · standard · SCH §13.3 p.441 (pdf 459)

**Problem:** Without solving, determine which FRW models expand forever, recollapse, or began with R = 0.

**Method:** Treat (1/2)Rdot^2 as kinetic energy, -k/2 as total energy and -(4 pi/3) R^2 (rho_m + rho_Lambda) as potential; track how R^2 rho_m (falls as R^{-1} or R^{-2}) and R^2 rho_Lambda (grows as R^2) behave at large and small R.

**Key insight:** Signs and scalings alone decide fates: k = -1 never stops, k = +1 with Lambda = 0 turns around, and at small R the matter term always wins so a Big Bang is unavoidable if densities are positive.

**Result:** Classification of fates; Big Bang for rho_m > 0 and Lambda >= 0.

**Concepts:** effective potential for the scale factor, fate of the Universe, Big Bang

### Einstein's static universe · standard · SCH §13.3 p.443 (pdf 461)

**Problem:** Find the dark-energy density that allows a static matter-filled model and test its stability.

**Method:** With rho_m = rho_0 (R_0/R)^3, differentiate the Friedmann equation to get Rddot = (8/3) pi rho_Lambda R - (4/3) pi rho_0 R_0^3 R^{-2}; set it to zero at R_0; check the second derivative of the potential term (Exercise 13.20).

**Key insight:** The static solution is a potential maximum, so it is unstable: slightly less matter runs away into acceleration, slightly more into collapse.

**Result:** rho_Lambda = rho_0/2; unstable equilibrium.

**Concepts:** Einstein static universe, effective potential for the scale factor

### Critical density and the Omega sum rule · intro · SCH §13.3 p.444 (pdf 462)

**Problem:** Rewrite the Friedmann equation in terms of energy densities and evaluate the critical density numerically.

**Method:** Divide Eq. 13.55 by 4 pi R^2/3, name 3H^2/8 pi the Hubble density and -3k/(8 pi R^2) the curvature density, divide by rho_c at the present time; insert H_0 = 70 h_70 km/s/Mpc.

**Key insight:** Whether space is open or closed is decided by comparing the physical density with a density built from the expansion rate.

**Result:** rho_c = 3H_0^2/8 pi = 9.2e-27 h_70^2 kg m^-3; Omega_k + Omega_m + Omega_Lambda = 1 with measured values 0, 0.31, 0.69.

**Concepts:** critical density, density parameters Omega, curvature energy density

### Epoch of matter-radiation equality · intro · SCH §13.4 p.448 (pdf 466)

**Problem:** Estimate when radiation and matter densities were equal.

**Method:** Radiation scales as R^{-4}, matter as R^{-3}; their ratio today Omega_gamma/Omega_m (~1e-5/0.3) equals R_eq/R_0; scale the present CMB temperature up by the same factor.

**Key insight:** Different dilution laws make any radiation-matter ratio a clock; redshift and scale factor are interchangeable labels.

**Result:** Book: scale factor ~3e4 smaller, T ~ 1e5 K, ~10 eV, ~3000 yr. Modern inputs including neutrinos give z_eq about 3400, T about 9000 K, t about 50,000 yr (own check).

**Concepts:** matter-radiation equality, thermal history of the Universe

### Exponential inflation · intro · SCH §13.4 p.459 (pdf 477)

**Problem:** Find the expansion law when a vacuum energy density dominates.

**Method:** Drop matter and curvature from the Friedmann equation: H^2 = (8/3) pi rho_Lambda is constant, so Rdot/R = 1/tau_Lambda.

**Key insight:** A constant energy density that does not dilute gives a constant expansion rate and therefore exponential growth, capable of stretching a tiny smoothed patch to cosmic size.

**Result:** R proportional to exp(t/tau_Lambda), tau_Lambda = (3/(8 pi rho_Lambda))^{1/2}.

**Concepts:** inflation, cosmological constant, horizon problem

### Standard-siren flux anisotropy · intro · SCH §13.2 p.432 (pdf 450)

**Problem:** Why must the inclination of a binary be known to get its luminosity distance from gravitational waves?

**Method:** Wave amplitude along the orbital axis is twice that in the orbital plane, so the energy flux, quadratic in amplitude, is four times larger; the emission is not isotropic, so L/4 pi F must use the flux emitted in our direction, inferred from the measured polarization ellipticity.

**Key insight:** Luminosity distance generalizes to anisotropic emitters only if the directional emission is known; polarization supplies that information.

**Result:** Factor 4 flux contrast; inclination-distance degeneracy limits single-event H_0 precision (as for GW170817).

**Concepts:** standard siren, luminosity distance


## Analogies and intuitions

### Dots painted on an inflating balloon → Hubble-Lemaitre law and the absence of a centre · strong · SCH §13.2 p.418 (pdf 436)

Separation along the surface grows in proportion to separation, so every dot sees others receding with v proportional to d; later noted as especially apt for k = +1 and as an example of a medium under tension.

**Where it breaks down:** The balloon's interior and exterior (the third spatial direction) have no counterpart in a real three-sphere; it suggests a centre in the embedding space and a finite size even for flat or open models; galaxies themselves do not expand with the rubber.

**App idea:** Balloon demo with a 'hide embedding' button that flattens the view into what surface-dwellers can measure, reinforcing that only in-surface distances count.

### Choosing a Solar System origin at Jupiter's position on a particular date → preferred cosmic time · useful · SCH §13.2 p.421 (pdf 439)

A frame adapted to the matter content is a convenience, just as nobody would set Solar System coordinates on a moving planet at an arbitrary date; the laws stay frame independent.

**Where it breaks down:** The cosmic frame is not merely convenient but physically defined by the CMB and homogeneity everywhere; the analogy understates that the whole foliation is singled out.

### Three-sphere as the surface of a 4D ball; hyperbolic space as a hyperboloid in Minkowski spacetime → closed and open FRW spatial geometries · strong · SCH §13.2 p.426 (pdf 444)

Rotations of the 4D ball and Lorentz boosts of Minkowski space carry any point of the slice to any other, proving homogeneity and isotropy visually.

**Where it breaks down:** The embedding dimension is not physical and nothing can move through it; the hyperboloid picture requires comfort with Minkowski geometry and uses a timelike embedding direction.

**App idea:** 2D analogues: a sphere in 3D and a hyperboloid in 2+1 Minkowski space, with drag-to-rotate and drag-to-boost controls that slide a marked point around while triangles drawn on the surface keep their shape.

### An expanding Newtonian cloud of galaxies where a photon passes ever-faster receding observers → cosmological redshift as accumulated Doppler shift · useful · SCH §13.2 p.430 (pdf 448)

Explains why redshifted photons seem to lose energy: each successive galaxy is moving away faster relative to the previous frame, so each measures a lower energy.

**Where it breaks down:** Only valid locally; globally the redshift is a property of the time-dependent geometry, and the Newtonian cloud's dynamics depend on its edge.

**App idea:** Chain-of-observers animation: a photon hops between comoving galaxies, with a small Doppler shift at each hop multiplying to the total 1 + z = R_0/R_e.

### Friedmann equation as the energy equation of a particle in a potential → effective potential for the scale factor · strong · SCH §13.3 p.441 (pdf 459)

Kinetic term (1/2)Rdot^2, total energy -k/2, and potential built from R^2 rho; used to read off fates, inevitability of a Big Bang, and the instability of Einstein's static universe as a potential maximum.

**Where it breaks down:** k is fixed by initial data, not an energy one can add; the 'potential' depends on the equation of state, and the analogy obscures that there is no true conserved energy in cosmology.

**App idea:** Ball-on-a-hill widget: plot V(R) for chosen matter and Lambda, set the energy line -k/2, and roll a ball to generate R(t) alongside.

### Tension in a stretched rubber band (and in the balloon skin) → negative pressure (tension) · useful · SCH §13.3 p.441 (pdf 459)

Shows negative pressure is familiar physics; what is special about dark energy is that its tension is isotropic and enormous.

**Where it breaks down:** A rubber band's tension pulls things together locally, which suggests dark energy should decelerate; the cosmic effect comes instead from tension's contribution to the active gravitational mass, not from any mechanical force (Exercise 13.18).

**App idea:** Side-by-side: a stretched rubber sheet (local forces cancel in the interior) and a gauge showing rho + 3p going negative as tension increases.

### Galaxies and clusters as atoms of the cosmic fluid → perfect-fluid model of the cosmic contents · weak · SCH §13.1 p.416 (pdf 434)

On cosmological scales, galaxies are small enough to be treated as the particles of a smooth fluid.

**Where it breaks down:** Galaxy velocities and clustering are not thermal; large-scale flows exist; the analogy says nothing about dark matter.

### Inflation amplifying fluctuations like a pumped nonlinear oscillator → inflationary generation of perturbations · weak · SCH §13.4 p.460 (pdf 478)

Offered as an intuition for how rapid expansion enlarges tiny quantum fluctuations into classical perturbations.

**Where it breaks down:** Loose: the real mechanism is modes being stretched beyond the Hubble radius and freezing, closer to parametric amplification in an expanding background than to a driven nonlinear oscillator.

**App idea:** Mode-stretching animation: wavelengths of quantum ripples grow exponentially while the Hubble radius stays fixed; modes that cross it freeze in amplitude.

### The CMB as a gravitational-wave detector → E-mode and B-mode polarization · useful · SCH §13.4 p.454 (pdf 472)

Primordial gravitational waves passing through the last-scattering plasma leave a polarization record, much as a detector records strain; the wry note that the same waves pass through the CMB antenna now.

**Where it breaks down:** Only waves with wavelengths comparable to the horizon at decoupling leave a signal; the 'detector' is read statistically via B-mode power, not as a time series.

### Stars as a collisionless gas that remembers its past → galactic stellar streams and phase-space conservation · useful · SCH §13.4 p.451 (pdf 469)

Liouville's theorem means stars born together stay together in phase space, so the Galaxy is a ball of strings encoding its history.

**Where it breaks down:** Scattering by giant molecular clouds, spiral arms and bars slowly erodes coherence.

### Temperature as the clock of the early Universe → thermal history of the Universe · strong · SCH §13.4 p.447 (pdf 465)

Epochs are labelled by the energy scale of the plasma so that each era is tied to the physics active at that energy (ionization, nuclear binding, pair creation, quark confinement).

**Where it breaks down:** Temperature is monotonic only while equilibrium holds and species decouple at different times; after decoupling matter and radiation temperatures differ.

**App idea:** Cosmic thermometer timeline: a log-scale slider in energy that shows t, z, R, the dominant species, and which processes are active.


## Misconceptions addressed

### Since all galaxies recede from us, we must be at the centre of the expansion. · SCH §13.2 p.419 (pdf 437)

**Correction:** A velocity proportional to distance is the same law seen from every comoving point; no location is special.

**Why tempting:** Everyday explosions have a centre, and we only observe from one place.

### The Hubble-Lemaitre flow velocity at a location is the velocity of the galaxy there. · SCH §13.2 p.419 (pdf 437)

**Correction:** The flow is the velocity of the local cosmological rest frame; individual galaxies have peculiar velocities (typically under 100 km/s) and large-scale flows, and the frame is defined by the CMB.

**Why tempting:** Hubble diagrams plot individual galaxies.

### A preferred cosmological frame contradicts the principle of relativity. · SCH §13.2 p.421 (pdf 439)

**Correction:** The laws are still the same in every frame; the matter distribution of our one Universe singles out a convenient frame, as any physical system does.

**Why tempting:** Special relativity is often summarized as 'no preferred frames'.

### Galaxies beyond about 4200 Mpc recede faster than light, violating relativity, or v = Hd must hold exactly at all distances. · SCH §13.2 p.421 (pdf 439)

**Correction:** v = Hd is a local statement for v much less than c; at large distances velocity is not well defined, and one must use redshift and operational distance measures (superluminal recession of comoving coordinates does not violate local causality).

**Why tempting:** The law looks like a universal linear formula.

### Homogeneity guarantees isotropy. · SCH §13.2 p.418 (pdf 436)

**Correction:** A universe with a uniform field pointing one way everywhere is homogeneous but anisotropic; isotropy about every point is an independent (stronger) property, though it does imply homogeneity.

**Why tempting:** In everyday uniform media both properties usually hold together.

### A closed universe has an edge or boundary, and the fourth dimension of its embedding is physically real. · SCH §13.2 p.426 (pdf 444)

**Correction:** The three-sphere has finite volume but no edge; the embedding direction is only a visualization device inaccessible to measurement (the book notes braneworlds as a speculative exception).

**Why tempting:** Finite suggests bounded, and pictures of spheres always show the surrounding space.

### The energy lost by redshifted photons must be going into something, since energy is conserved. · SCH §13.2 p.430 (pdf 448)

**Correction:** Energy conservation along geodesics requires a time-independent metric; FRW depends on time, so there is no conserved photon energy and nothing receives it. Locally the loss matches Doppler shifts between receding observers.

**Why tempting:** Global energy conservation is deeply ingrained from flat-space physics.

### The distance astronomers quote for a distant galaxy is its present separation from us. · SCH §13.2 p.431 (pdf 449)

**Correction:** Present proper distance is unmeasurable (the object may no longer exist); what is measured are luminosity distance, angular-diameter distance or redshift, which differ from one another by powers of 1+z.

**Why tempting:** In local astronomy distance is unambiguous.

### The apparent acceleration could be an artifact of an error in H_0. · SCH §13.2 p.438 (pdf 456)

**Correction:** Rescaling H_0 shifts the whole magnitude-redshift curve; acceleration shows up as its bending at z ~ 1, which a change of scale cannot produce.

**Why tempting:** H_0 itself is uncertain at the several-percent level.

### Positive energy density always attracts, so dark energy with positive density cannot accelerate the expansion. · SCH §13.3 p.441 (pdf 459)

**Correction:** In GR the source of acceleration is rho + 3p; dark energy's pressure -rho_Lambda makes this -2 rho_Lambda, so it acts repulsively.

**Why tempting:** Newtonian intuition makes mass density the only source of gravity.

### Negative pressure is unphysical, or tension should pull the Universe inward and slow it down. · SCH §13.3 p.441 (pdf 459)

**Correction:** Tension is ordinary (rubber bands); in a homogeneous medium it exerts no net force on any element (Exercise 13.18). Its effect on expansion is purely gravitational, through rho + 3p.

**Why tempting:** Pressure is pictured as a push on walls, and tension as a pull.

### The Big Bang singularity is an artifact of assuming perfect homogeneity and isotropy. · SCH §13.3 p.442 (pdf 460)

**Correction:** Singularity theorems show a past singularity even without symmetry, given positive energy and classical GR; the real caveats are quantum effects and unknown high-energy physics.

**Why tempting:** Symmetric collapse models often exaggerate focusing.

### The optical horizon at decoupling is a fundamental limit on what we can learn about the early Universe. · SCH §13.2 p.423 (pdf 441)

**Correction:** Only light is blocked; gravitational waves and neutrinos travelled freely, and CMB polarization can reveal earlier gravitational waves. The particle horizon is the fundamental limit.

**Why tempting:** Most of astronomy is electromagnetic.

### Newtonian gravity can describe an infinite uniform universe just as well as GR. · SCH §13.1 p.416 (pdf 434)

**Correction:** Poisson's equation in an unbounded uniform medium needs a boundary condition; results depend on the shape of a distant edge, so there is no consistent edge-free Newtonian cosmology.

**Why tempting:** Newtonian cosmology reproduces the Friedmann equation form in textbook derivations.

### Primordial gravitational waves would show up as extra temperature fluctuations in the CMB. · SCH §13.4 p.455 (pdf 473)

**Correction:** The book's argument: gravitational waves are transverse and area preserving, so they do not compress the plasma and leave no density signature at decoupling; instead they create velocity patterns that polarize scattered light, detectable as B modes. (Caveat not in the book: tensor modes do add a small large-angle temperature anisotropy through the integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect, but it cannot be separated from scalar anisotropy, which is why B modes are the clean signature.)

**Why tempting:** Density perturbations produce temperature spots, so any perturbation seems likely to.

### Most of the matter in the Universe is in stars and gas. · SCH §13.3 p.445 (pdf 463)

**Correction:** Baryons contribute Omega_b about 0.04 of the critical density, versus Omega_m about 0.31; most matter is nonbaryonic dark matter.

**Why tempting:** Luminous matter is all we see directly.

### Astrophysical 'cooling' of gas falling into dark-matter halos makes the gas colder. · SCH §13.4 p.450 (pdf 468)

**Correction:** Radiating energy away lets the gas sink deeper, and because self-gravitating systems have negative specific heat it actually gets hotter.

**Why tempting:** In the lab, radiating energy lowers temperature.

### The cosmological redshift is just a Doppler shift, so z = v/c at all distances. · SCH §13.2 p.429 (pdf 447)

**Correction:** Only nearby does z equal v; in general 1 + z = R_0/R_e, a statement about expansion during transit, and z can exceed 1 without any superluminal velocity in a local frame.

**Why tempting:** The book itself stresses the local Doppler interpretation.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Decoupling happens when the temperature drops to the 13.6 eV ionization energy of hydrogen. · SCH §13.4 p.448 (pdf 466)

**Correction:** Because photons vastly outnumber baryons and even a small ionized fraction scatters light, decoupling waits until kT is far below 13.6 eV (well under 1 eV).

**Why tempting:** Matching thermal energy to binding energy is the obvious estimate.


## Thought experiments

### Growing sphere of uniform matter · SCH §13.1 p.415 (pdf 433)

**Setup:** Start at the centre of the Sun and keep increasing the radius of a sphere, tracking the enclosed mass and M/R through star, galaxy and cluster scales into a uniformly filled Universe.

**Lesson:** For uniform density M/R grows as R^2, so GR must become necessary at cosmological radii (~6 Gpc) even though all intermediate systems are Newtonian.

**App idea:** Zoom-out slider from solar to Gpc scales with a live M/R gauge that stays near 1e-6 until homogeneity kicks in and then climbs to 1.

### Local experiment revealing a distant boundary (Newtonian) · SCH §13.1 p.416 (pdf 434)

**Setup:** In a Newtonian universe of uniform density bounded by a non-spherical edge far beyond our horizon, measure the local gravitational field at the centre.

**Lesson:** The local field would reveal the edge's shape, an unphysical dependence on unobservable regions that GR avoids through local flatness and causal propagation.

**App idea:** Draw an arbitrary boundary around a uniform 2D mass distribution and show the computed tidal field at the centre changing as the far boundary is reshaped.

### Early observers outside each other's horizons · SCH §13.2 p.422 (pdf 440)

**Setup:** Two observers at an early epoch lie inside our past light cone but their own past light cones do not intersect.

**Lesson:** We see both regions as statistically alike, so the principle that unseen regions resemble seen ones would have been right for them; this supports the cosmological principle and highlights the horizon problem.

**App idea:** Part of the Figure 13.2 redesign: drag observers and see cone overlap.

### Surprising news from beyond the horizon · SCH §13.2 p.422 (pdf 440)

**Setup:** Suppose tomorrow light arrives from a region previously outside our particle horizon showing it to be very inhomogeneous.

**Lesson:** Our past would then look specially arranged, raising hard questions; predictions about the future depend on assuming mediocrity.

### Counting photons from a distant flash · SCH §13.2 p.432 (pdf 450)

**Setup:** A source emits a short burst of photons of one frequency; follow them until they cross the sphere through the observer's comoving position.

**Lesson:** Area grows with R_0^2 r^2, and energy per photon and arrival rate each drop by 1+z, giving d_L = R_0 r (1+z).

**App idea:** Animated photon shell expanding in comoving coordinates while each photon's colour reddens and the spacing between photons stretches; counters show area, energy and rate factors.

### Photon crossing an expanding Newtonian cloud · SCH §13.2 p.430 (pdf 448)

**Setup:** A photon travels outward through a cloud of galaxies whose outward speeds increase with distance from the centre; each galaxy measures its energy as it passes.

**Lesson:** Each successive observer recedes faster, so measured energies fall; locally the cosmological redshift is a sequence of Doppler shifts.

### Perturbing Einstein's static universe · SCH §13.3 p.443 (pdf 461)

**Setup:** Start with the balanced static model (rho_Lambda = rho_m/2) and slightly decrease or increase the matter density.

**Lesson:** Less matter lets dark energy win and expansion runs away; more matter triggers collapse, so the static model is unstable.

**App idea:** Ball balanced on the top of the Friedmann potential hill with a nudge button.

### Hot spot next to a cold spot, seen by scattering · SCH §13.4 p.455 (pdf 473)

**Setup:** Before last scattering, an electron between a hot and a cold region scatters photons through 90 degrees toward us.

**Lesson:** More photons arrive from the hot side, so the scattered light has a net linear polarization perpendicular to the line joining the spots, and none at spot centres.

**App idea:** Interactive scattering diagram: move a hot and a cold blob around an electron and see the polarization bar we receive rotate and change length.

### Gravitational wave heading toward us through the last-scattering plasma · SCH §13.4 p.455 (pdf 473)

**Setup:** A primordial gravitational wave propagating along our line of sight stretches plasma along x and squeezes it along y on the sky.

**Lesson:** Inflowing gas along y is Doppler-heated, raising scattering intensity with polarization along x; last scattering freezes this, producing polarization patterns with a curl component (B modes) when many random waves add.

**App idea:** Sky-patch simulator: generate polarization maps from random acoustic waves (curl-free) versus random gravitational waves, then apply an E/B decomposition and show that only the gravitational-wave map has B power.

### B-mode antenna meets its own source · SCH §13.4 p.456 (pdf 474)

**Setup:** A CMB telescope receives microwaves carrying a B-mode imprint.

**Lesson:** The same primordial gravitational waves that created the imprint are passing through the antenna at the same moment, since both travel at the speed of light along the same path.


## Applications and observations

- **M/R across astronomical scales** (numerical-estimate, SCH §13.1 p.415 (pdf 433)): Sun and Milky Way have M/R of about 1e-6, rich clusters about 1e-4; a uniform density of 1e-26 kg m^-3 gives M/R = 1 near 6 Gpc, compared with telescope reach beyond 10 Gpc. Key numbers: Galaxy: 1e11 stars within ~15 kpc; 1 pc = 3e16 m; R(M/R=1) ~ 6 Gpc
- **Planck measurement of the CMB dipole** (observation, SCH §13.2 p.420 (pdf 438)): Our barycentric velocity relative to the cosmic rest frame from the l = 1 moments; Planck measured multipoles beyond l = 2000, with anisotropies around 1e-5 K. Key numbers: v = 369.82 +/- 0.11 km/s toward l = 264.021 deg, b = 48.253 deg
- **Hubble-Lemaitre constant and timescales** (numerical-estimate, SCH §13.2 p.421 (pdf 439)): A representative H_0 = 71 km/s/Mpc gives 2.3e-18 s^-1, 7.7e-27 m^-1, Hubble time 4.3e17 s (~14 Gyr); Earth is 4.54 Gyr old. Peculiar velocities are typically below 100 km/s. Key numbers: c/H_0 = 1.3e26 m = 4200 Mpc
- **Cepheid calibration with Gaia parallaxes** (observation, SCH §13.2 p.435 (pdf 453)): Direct parallaxes of Galactic Cepheids from Gaia (2018) now calibrate the period-luminosity law; possible composition-dependent drift is a systematic concern.
- **Type Ia supernova distance ladder** (data-analysis, SCH §13.2 p.435 (pdf 453)): Supernova luminosities are calibrated with Cepheids in nearby host galaxies and then used to measure H_0 and the expansion history. Key numbers: H_0 = 74.03 +/- 1.42 km/s/Mpc (Riess et al. 2019)
- **Gravitational-wave standard sirens for H_0** (detection-event, SCH §13.2 p.435 (pdf 453)): GW170817 gave the first siren H_0 with large errors from inclination; binary black holes without counterparts can be used statistically; O4 was expected to yield 200-300 binaries per year; third-generation detectors and LISA could give the most accurate values. Key numbers: 200-300 binary black holes per year projected for O4 (mid-2021 expectation)
- **Hubble tension** (data-analysis, SCH §13.2 p.436 (pdf 454)): Supernova ladder versus CMB (with BAO) values differ by nearly five standard deviations as the text states; explanations range from systematics to new physics. Key numbers: 74.03 +/- 1.42 vs 67.4 +/- 0.5 km/s/Mpc (about 4.4 sigma combined, own calculation)
- **Discovery of cosmic acceleration** (observation, SCH §13.2 p.437 (pdf 455)): High-z Supernova Search Team and Supernova Cosmology Project measured Type Ia supernovae to z ~ 1; distant ones were fainter than in a decelerating model; best fit in Figure 13.3 has Omega_M = 0.24, Omega_Lambda = 0.76. Key numbers: Residuals of order +0.2 to +0.3 mag at z ~ 0.5-1 relative to an Omega_M = 0.2 empty-Lambda model
- **Cosmological parameters of our Universe** (data-analysis, SCH §13.3 p.444 (pdf 462)): Supernovae, CMB and cluster studies combine to a flat Lambda-dominated Universe; baryons from nucleosynthesis are a small part of the matter. Key numbers: Omega_Lambda = 0.69, Omega_m = 0.31, Omega_k = 0, Omega_b = 0.04, Omega_d = 0.27; rho_c = 9.2e-27 h_70^2 kg/m^3
- **LISA cosmography and dark energy** (technology, SCH §13.3 p.445 (pdf 463)): LISA should measure luminosity distances of high-redshift massive black-hole mergers to a few percent; host galaxies may be identified by disturbed morphology, jets or X-ray changes (possibly with ESA's Athena), enabling H(z) at high redshift and tests of time-varying dark energy; weak lensing of the waves adds distance errors. Key numbers: Distance errors at the few-percent level (projection)
- **Matter-radiation equality** (numerical-estimate, SCH §13.4 p.448 (pdf 466)): From Omega_gamma ~ 1e-5 and Omega_m = 0.3 the text estimates equality at a scale factor ~3e4 times smaller than today. Key numbers: Book: T ~ 1e5 K, ~10 eV, ~3000 yr after the Big Bang (modern: z ~ 3400, ~50,000 yr, own note)
- **Decoupling and the CMB** (numerical-estimate, SCH §13.4 p.448 (pdf 466)): The CMB was released when hydrogen became neutral enough for photons to stream freely; CMB temperature today 2.725 K. Key numbers: z = 1090, ~380,000 yr (p. 434); text on p. 448 gives z ~ 2000 and 4e5 yr; decoupling kT below 1 eV
- **Planck CMB maps and power spectrum** (observation, SCH §13.4 p.449 (pdf 467)): All-sky temperature fluctuation map (Figure 13.5) and angular power spectrum (Figure 13.6) from Planck 2018; low-l errors come from subtracting Galactic emission. Key numbers: Fluctuation range +/-300 microkelvin; first acoustic peak near l ~ 220 (read from figure)
- **Galactic mergers and Gaia streams** (astrophysical-system, SCH §13.4 p.450 (pdf 468)): Omega Centauri is likely the core of an absorbed dwarf and hosts a massive black hole; the Magellanic Clouds will merge with the Milky Way; Gaia (launched December 2013, ~1e9 stars, ~1.5e8 radial velocities) revealed the Gaia-Enceladus Sausage merger, which thickened the disc; Andromeda will merge with the Galaxy. Key numbers: Gaia-Enceladus ~10 Gyr ago; Andromeda merger in ~3 Gyr; building-block clumps ~1e6 solar masses
- **Population III stars and reionization** (astrophysical-system, SCH §13.4 p.452 (pdf 470)): Metal-free first stars were very massive, short-lived, enriched the gas, likely left many black holes, and reionized hydrogen; LISA may see resulting black-hole mergers at z up to 20. Key numbers: 100-1000 solar masses; reionization between z ~ 20 and 8; Sun formed at z ~ 0.3-0.4, age ~5 Gyr
- **Evidence for dark matter** (observation, SCH §13.4 p.453 (pdf 471)): Coma cluster virial analysis, galactic rotation curves, lensing by cluster halos and interacting galaxies; direct laboratory searches so far negative; primordial black holes remain a candidate. Key numbers: Dark-to-visible mass around galaxies about 6:1
- **CMB plus BAO determination of H_0** (data-analysis, SCH §13.4 p.454 (pdf 472)): As the book describes it, the BAO length scale measured in today's galaxy distribution is extrapolated back to the drag epoch and then to decoupling. That gives a physical size for the CMB fluctuations, hence the angular-diameter distance to last scattering, hence H_0. In practice Planck's H_0 comes from fitting the CMB spectra with a sound horizon computed in Lambda-CDM, and BAO surveys give a complementary ruler (see gaps_and_pitfalls). Key numbers: Decoupling z = 1090, drag ends z = 1060; H_0 = 67.4 +/- 0.5 km/s/Mpc
- **Searches for primordial B-mode polarization** (experiment, SCH §13.4 p.456 (pdf 474)): Multiple CMB experiments (as of mid-2021) seek the small curl component of CMB polarization; polarized emission from magnetically aligned Galactic dust must be mapped and removed.
- **Big Bang nucleosynthesis and neutrino counting** (numerical-estimate, SCH §13.4 p.457 (pdf 475)): Light elements formed a few minutes after the Big Bang; yields restrict the number of light neutrino species to three and bound any extra relativistic background, including primordial gravitational waves, to below the photon density. Key numbers: ~200 s, ~50 keV (book); Omega_gamma ~ 1e-5; dark matter mass > 50 keV
- **Pair annihilation and entropy per baryon** (numerical-estimate, SCH §13.4 p.458 (pdf 476)): Electron-positron pairs annihilated after about 1 s (~500 keV), leaving one electron per 1e9 photons; proton-antiproton equilibrium at a few hundred MeV about 10 microseconds after the Big Bang; quark-gluon plasma before 1e-5 s. Key numbers: Photon-to-electron ratio ~1e9
- **Collider frontier** (experiment, SCH §13.4 p.458 (pdf 476)): The LHC probes about 10 TeV, found the Higgs boson in 2012 but no supersymmetry by mid-2021; the Planck scale lies at about 1e16 TeV. Key numbers: 10 TeV vs 1e16 TeV
- **Inflation timescale** (numerical-estimate, SCH §13.4 p.460 (pdf 478)): Inflation is placed near 1e-35 s; the text says 20-30 e-foldings could expand a smoothed patch to the observable Universe. Key numbers: ~1e-35 s; 20-30 e-folds in text (standard estimates ~60)
- **Short-range tests of the inverse-square law** (experiment, SCH §13.4 p.461 (pdf 479)): Laboratory torsion-balance experiments constrain extra-dimension scales in brane models. Key numbers: Deviations excluded above about 40 micrometres (Lee et al. 2020)
- **Planck time as the limit of classical cosmology** (numerical-estimate, SCH §13.3 p.443 (pdf 461)): Classical conclusions about the Big Bang fail within about a Planck time; models can start reliably from ~100 Planck times. Key numbers: t_Pl ~ 1e-43 s

## Historical notes

- **Edwin Hubble, Georges Lemaitre (1920s (renamed 2018)):** Hubble discovered the linear velocity-distance relation that Lemaitre had predicted; in 2018 the IAU recommended the name Hubble-Lemaitre law. — Recognizes the theoretical prediction alongside the observation; explains the book's naming (footnote 1). (SCH §13.2 p.418 (pdf 436))
- **Henrietta Swan Leavitt (1908):** Found that Cepheid variables in the Large Magellanic Cloud have mean brightness correlated with pulsation period. — Created the first reliable extragalactic standard candle and the basis of the distance ladder. (SCH §13.2 p.434 (pdf 452))
- **Edwin Hubble:** Hubble's own estimate of the expansion rate was about 500 km/s/Mpc because his distances were too small by a factor of about 7; values of about 50 and 100 competed until around 1990, converging near 70 by 2000. — Shows that distance calibration, not redshift, dominated H_0 uncertainty. (SCH §13.2 p.434 (pdf 452))
- **Adam Riess, Saul Perlmutter, High-z Supernova Search Team, Supernova Cosmology Project (1998-1999):** Type Ia supernova surveys to z ~ 1 found the expansion accelerating. — Revived the cosmological constant and established dark energy as dominant. (SCH §13.2 p.436 (pdf 454))
- **Albert Einstein (1917 (disavowed after 1929)):** Introduced the cosmological constant to permit a static universe, later calling it his biggest mistake once expansion was discovered. — Ironically, Lambda returned as the simplest explanation of acceleration; the static model is unstable anyway. (SCH §13.3 p.443 (pdf 461))
- **Roger Penrose, Stephen Hawking:** Singularity theorems (reviewed in Hawking and Ellis 1973) showing a past singularity without symmetry assumptions. — Makes the Big Bang robust within classical GR with positive energy. (SCH §13.3 p.442 (pdf 460))
- **Fritz Zwicky (1933):** Applied the virial theorem to the Coma cluster and found far more mass than starlight implies, naming the missing component dark matter. — First evidence for dark matter; a footnote notes Zwicky and Baade proposed neutron stars around the same time. (SCH §13.4 p.453 (pdf 471))
- **Vera Rubin, Kent Ford (1970s):** Galaxy rotation-curve measurements implying about six times more dark than visible mass around galaxies. — Made dark matter mainstream; the Vera C. Rubin Observatory honours her. (SCH §13.4 p.453 (pdf 471))
- **Walter Baade, Fritz Zwicky (1933-1934):** Proposed neutron stars at an American Physical Society meeting. — Footnote linking dark matter's discoverer to compact-object history from Chapter 10. (SCH §13.4 p.453 (pdf 471))
- **Alexei Starobinsky, Alan Guth, Andrei Linde (1980-1982):** Proposed inflationary cosmology. — Supplied a mechanism for homogeneity and for the origin of perturbations. (SCH §13.4 p.459 (pdf 477))
- **Peter Higgs, Francois Englert (2012 (Nobel 2013)):** Discovery of the Higgs boson at the LHC. — Confirms the standard model to about 10 TeV, bounding the energies where cosmology rests on tested physics. (SCH §13.4 p.458 (pdf 476))
- **Bernard Schutz (1986):** Proposed determining H_0 statistically from gravitational-wave standard sirens without identified counterparts. — Basis for using numerous binary black hole events for cosmography. (SCH §13.2 p.436 (pdf 454))
- **John Mather, George Smoot (1989 launch; Nobel 1996):** COBE measured the CMB black-body spectrum and first detected its anisotropies. — Beginning of precision cosmology. (SCH §13.5 p.462 (pdf 480))
- **James Peebles (Nobel 2019):** Pioneered physical cosmology and predicted the CMB. — Cited as the founder of the field treated in 13.4. (SCH §13.5 p.462 (pdf 480))
- **Amina Helmi (2018):** Gaia data revealed the Gaia-Enceladus merger about 10 Gyr ago. — Direct evidence of hierarchical assembly of the Milky Way. (SCH §13.4 p.451 (pdf 469))

## Notation and conventions

- **Units and signature:** Geometrized units G = c = 1 with metric signature (-,+,+,+); energy densities and mass densities are interchangeable, H_0 can be quoted in m^-1. — SI and astronomers' units (km/s/Mpc, kpc, Mpc, Gpc, magnitudes) are mixed in numerical passages. (SCH §13.2 p.421 (pdf 439))
- **R(t):** Scale factor, dimensionful in the k = +1 case (radius of the three-sphere), normalized to 1 at t_0 in Eq. 13.5. — Many texts use a(t). R also denotes the radius of a sphere in 13.1 and the letter recalls the Ricci scalar (written R^i_i); R_0 and R_e are present and emission values. (SCH §13.2 p.423 (pdf 441))
- **k:** Curvature parameter normalized to +1, 0, -1. — k_B is Boltzmann's constant in 13.4; the text explicitly warns not to confuse them. (SCH §13.2 p.425 (pdf 443))
- **h_ij and h_70:** h_ij is the time-independent comoving spatial metric; h_70 = H_0/(70 km/s/Mpc). — Neither is related to linearized-theory or gravitational-wave amplitude h; the book says so both times. (SCH §13.2 p.423 (pdf 441))
- **Lambda:** Cosmological constant in Eqs. 13.52-13.54; rho_Lambda = Lambda/8 pi. — Earlier in the same section Lambda(r) is the radial metric function of Eq. 13.8, borrowed from Chapter 10. (SCH §13.3 p.440 (pdf 458))
- **chi:** Proper radial coordinate with r = sin chi (k = +1), r = chi (k = 0), r = sinh chi (k = -1). (SCH §13.2 p.426 (pdf 444))
- **Subscript 0:** Quantities evaluated at the present cosmic time t_0 (H_0, R_0, q_0). — In the static-universe discussion and Exercise 13.20, rho_0 and R_0 refer to the static solution instead. (SCH §13.2 p.429 (pdf 447))
- **Dots:** Overdots denote derivatives with respect to cosmic time t, e.g. Hdot_0; zdot in Eq. 13.33 is the derivative of redshift with respect to emission time. (SCH §13.2 p.429 (pdf 447))
- **Galactic coordinates versus multipole index:** l and b are Galactic longitude and latitude; ell (script l) and m are spherical-harmonic indices. — Footnote 2 warns against confusing l with ell; the exported text sometimes loses the distinction (e.g. T_lm). (SCH §13.2 p.420 (pdf 438))
- **Density parameters:** Omega_x = rho_x/rho_c with subscripts m (all matter, sometimes M), b (baryons), d (dark matter), Lambda, k (curvature), gamma (photons). — rho_m in Eq. 13.55 includes radiation; Figure 13.3 uses capital M subscripts. (SCH §13.3 p.444 (pdf 462))
- **Redshift conventions:** 1 + z = R(t_0)/R(t_e) for the cosmological part; motions multiply additional (1 + z_motion) factors. (SCH §13.2 p.428 (pdf 446))
- **Magnitudes:** m = -2.5 log10(F/F_s), M = apparent magnitude at 10 pc; larger magnitude means fainter. — Defined in Exercise 13.13; used in Figure 13.3. (SCH p.464 (pdf 482))
- **Temperature as energy:** Epochs in 13.4 are labelled by E = k_B T in eV, keV, MeV, TeV. (SCH §13.4 p.447 (pdf 465))
- **Naming:** Uses 'Hubble-Lemaitre' law, parameter, constant and time throughout, and 'Friedmann-Robertson-Walker' (not FLRW). — Footnote 1 explains the 2018 IAU renaming. (SCH §13.2 p.418 (pdf 436))
- **Einstein tensor of 3-space:** G_ij and its trace G computed for the 3-metric alone; G = -R/2 in three dimensions. (SCH §13.2 p.424 (pdf 442))

## Teaching gems

### Estimate M/R for ever-larger uniform spheres to show cosmology must be relativistic, then flip the question: local flatness explains why the rest of science never needs cosmology. · SCH §13.1 p.415 (pdf 433)

**Why it works:** Motivates GR for cosmology with one line of arithmetic and immediately gives learners a deep payoff about the equivalence principle.

**App idea:** Scale-zoom slider with an M/R meter and a 'local lab' inset that stays flat no matter how relativistic the global scale becomes.

### Derive the FRW spatial metric by demanding a constant curvature scalar in the already-solved spherical-star metric, with regularity at the centre fixing the last constant. · SCH §13.2 p.424 (pdf 442)

**Why it works:** Avoids Killing vectors and group theory yet feels like a genuine derivation, and it reuses familiar machinery so the new result is anchored.

**App idea:** Step-through derivation panel where each line highlights which Chapter 10 formula it borrows, ending with a plot of g_rr(r) for k = +1, 0, -1 including the singular r = 1 for k = +1 explained by r = sin chi.

### Show the k = -1 space is a Lorentz-invariant hyperboloid in Minkowski spacetime, so boosts play the role of rotations on a sphere. · SCH §13.2 p.427 (pdf 445)

**Why it works:** Converts an unpicturable geometry into special relativity the learner already owns, and proves homogeneity visually.

**App idea:** 2+1D Minkowski viewer with a hyperboloid; a rapidity slider boosts the picture and slides a marked point across the surface while local triangle shapes stay invariant.

### Ask how distance got into v = Hd at all, answer 'through flux', and define luminosity distance, then count photons to get two separate factors of 1+z. · SCH §13.2 p.431 (pdf 449)

**Why it works:** Models the core epistemology of relativity (define quantities by measurement) and makes the (1+z)^2 in the flux memorable as energy loss plus time dilation.

**App idea:** Photon-shell animation with three meters (area, energy per photon, arrival rate) whose product updates the flux and d_L as the learner changes z.

### Read the Friedmann equation as a particle energy equation with -k/2 as total energy and R^2 rho as a potential. · SCH §13.3 p.441 (pdf 459)

**Why it works:** Transfers intuition from orbits to cosmology; fates, the inevitability of the Big Bang, and the instability of Einstein's static model all become visual.

**App idea:** Potential-well playground linked to R(t): adjust Omega_m, Omega_Lambda and radiation, set k, and watch the ball-on-hill and the scale-factor history move together.

### Explain cosmic acceleration with active mass rho + 3p and the everyday fact that tension is negative pressure (rubber band, balloon skin). · SCH §13.3 p.441 (pdf 459)

**Why it works:** Resolves 'how can positive energy repel?' without new physics and ties back to an earlier exercise, while Exercise 13.18 blocks the wrong mechanical picture.

**App idea:** Equation-of-state dial w from 1/3 to -1 with live readout of rho + 3p, sign of Rddot, and a small universe animation that decelerates or accelerates.

### Divide the Friedmann equation so that expansion, curvature, matter and Lambda all appear as energy densities; the Hubble density is the critical density. · SCH §13.3 p.444 (pdf 462)

## Gaps and pitfalls

- **The Planck time is printed as t_Pl = G M_Pl/c^2, which has dimensions of length; the time is G M_Pl/c^3 = sqrt(hbar G/c^5) ~ 5e-44 s.** — Dimensional-analysis-minded learners will be confused, or will propagate the wrong formula. *Suggestion:* Give both Planck length and Planck time with a units check.
- **Exercise cross-references in the text do not match the exercise list: the energy equation is attributed to Exercise 13.24 (it is 13.14), angular-diameter distance to Exercise 13.17 (13.17 is the acceleration equation; no d_A exercise exists), and the tension discussion to Exercise 13.25 (it is 13.18). Exercise 13.22(c) cites Eq. 12.2, apparently meaning the Friedmann equation.** — Students following pointers land on unrelated problems. *Suggestion:* Use the corrected mapping in any course material and add a d_A derivation exercise.
- **Nucleosynthesis is placed at ~50 keV described as the neutron-proton mass difference; that difference is 1.29 MeV. Weak-interaction freeze-out is near 0.8 MeV, and deuterium-bottleneck nucleosynthesis near 60-80 keV at about 3 minutes.** — Mixes two distinct thresholds and plants an incorrect particle-physics number. *Suggestion:* Separate neutron-to-proton freeze-out, free neutron decay, and the deuterium bottleneck on a timeline.
- **Matter-radiation equality numbers (Omega_gamma ~ 1e-5, scale factor 3e4 smaller, 1e5 K, 10 eV, 3000 years) are off; with photons plus neutrinos z_eq is about 3400, T about 9000 K (0.8 eV), t about 50,000 yr. The text's argument that the equality energy lies near 13.6 eV therefore does not hold numerically.** — Wrong epoch ordering intuition and inconsistent numbers relative to decoupling. *Suggestion:* Recompute with Omega_r h^2 ~ 4.2e-5 and emphasize that equality precedes decoupling by a factor ~3 in redshift.
- **Decoupling redshift is given as about 2000 (and 'a little below 1 eV') on one page, but z = 1090 elsewhere; actual decoupling temperature is ~0.26 eV (3000 K).** — Conflicting numbers in the same chapter undermine confidence. *Suggestion:* Standardize on z = 1090, T = 3000 K, t = 380,000 yr and explain the photon-to-baryon ratio reason for the low temperature.
- **Inflation is said to need 20-30 e-foldings; standard estimates require roughly 50-60 to solve the horizon and flatness problems.** — Underestimates the enormity of inflation. *Suggestion:* Show the e-fold estimate from the ratio of today's Hubble radius to the pre-inflation causal patch.
- **The statement that our Universe is 'near' Einstein's static solution because dark energy is about twice matter is misleading: static needs rho_Lambda = rho_m/2, ours has about 2.2 rho_m, a factor of about 4 difference, and ours is accelerating (q_0 ~ -0.5).** — Could suggest our Universe is nearly static or nearly balanced. *Suggestion:* Compute q_0 = Omega_m/2 - Omega_Lambda for both cases.
- **Eq. 13.15, the worked rescaling of k = -3, prints the radial term as dr~^2/(1 - r~^2); substituting r~ = sqrt(3) r into 1 - kr^2 = 1 + 3r^2 gives dr~^2/(1 + r~^2), the k = -1 form. The printed sign contradicts the very next sentence that the sign of k cannot change. Separately, Exercise 13.4's Eq. 13.67 is printed as v^i = H^i_j without the position x^j.** — A careful learner checking the algebra finds the sign flips, which seems to contradict the claim being illustrated. *Suggestion:* Show the corrected substitution step by step and make the invariance of sign(k) explicit: rescaling multiplies k by a positive factor.
- **The FRW Christoffel symbol Gamma^theta_{phi phi} is printed as +sin theta cos theta in Exercise 13.14; it should be -sin theta cos theta.** — Sign errors in geodesic or divergence calculations. *Suggestion:* Provide a symbolic-algebra check of the connection coefficients.
- **The distance-redshift relations are given only to second order in z, while the chapter emphasizes z ~ 1 supernovae and z = 1090 CMB; the exact integral d_L = (1+z) R_0 S_k(integral of dz/(R_0 H(z))) with H(z) from the Friedmann equation is never written.** — Learners cannot reproduce Figure 13.3 curves or the CMB distance. *Suggestion:* Add the exact comoving-distance integral and H(z) = H_0 sqrt(Omega_r(1+z)^4 + Omega_m(1+z)^3 + Omega_k(1+z)^2 + Omega_Lambda).
- **The age of the Universe is never computed for the Lambda-CDM parameters; only 1/H_0 (14 Gyr) and the matter-only 2/(3H_0) appear.** — No connection between Omega values and the familiar 13.8 Gyr. *Suggestion:* Include the closed-form flat Lambda-CDM age formula and a numerical evaluation.
- **The explanation of E and B modes treats polarization as an ordinary vector field and says about half of gravitational-wave polarization is divergence-free; real CMB polarization is a headless spin-2 field whose E/B split is defined by second derivatives, and the terminology 'divergence-free' versus 'curl-free' is used loosely.** — Advanced learners may be confused when meeting the standard formalism. *Suggestion:* Present the vector picture as a heuristic, then show headless sticks and the tangential/radial pattern around hot spots (E) versus swirling patterns (B).
- **The text states that gravitational waves produce no temperature variations in the CMB. Tensor perturbations do generate large-angle temperature anisotropy (integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect along the photon path); what they cannot do is make E-only polarization, so B modes are the distinguishing signature.** — Learners may conclude tensor modes are invisible in temperature data, and then be confused by tensor-to-scalar ratio constraints that use temperature spectra. *Suggestion:* Say gravitational waves add no density perturbation at last scattering but do redshift photons anisotropically; show a TT spectrum with a tensor contribution confined to low l.
- **String theory is said to require eleven spacetime dimensions; superstring theories are ten-dimensional and eleven dimensions belong to M-theory/supergravity.** — Minor factual slip in a survey passage. *Suggestion:* State 10 for superstrings, 11 for M-theory.
- **The loss of photon energy is dismissed as 'just goes away' without explaining that cosmological spacetimes lack a timelike Killing vector, or showing the conformal Killing vector reasoning.** — Leaves learners uneasy about energy conservation in GR. *Suggestion:* Contrast Schwarzschild (static, conserved E) with FRW and discuss the local conservation law nabla_mu T^{mu nu} = 0 versus global energy.
- **The 'balloon' and embedding pictures, and the v > c objection, are not followed by a discussion of the Hubble sphere, event horizon, or superluminal recession; the text defers to Davis and Lineweaver.** — Common confusions about galaxies receding faster than light persist. *Suggestion:* Add an interactive conformal diagram showing Hubble sphere, particle horizon and event horizon in Lambda-CDM.
- **The chapter is a mid-2021 snapshot; later developments (LVK O4 results, JWST high-redshift galaxies, DESI BAO analyses hinting at evolving dark energy, updated H_0 measurements, Athena's re-scoping) are absent.** — Numbers and outlook will look dated. *Suggestion:* Keep the conceptual framework but attach a maintained 'current values' panel to observational sections.
- **The homogeneity derivation shows a constant curvature scalar is necessary but proves sufficiency only by checking the three cases; the statement that it is 'remarkably sufficient' in general is not proven and relies on isotropy about every point.** — Careful readers may wonder whether other spaces with constant scalar curvature exist (they do, without isotropy). *Suggestion:* Clarify that isotropy about every point plus constant curvature scalar implies maximal symmetry, and cite the Killing-vector argument.
- **The book's account of how the CMB measures H_0 is physically muddled. The acoustic waves are said to travel in the dark matter. Photon drag is explained as compressional heating re-ionizing the gas. The Planck value is presented as coming from extrapolating today's BAO scale back to z = 1090. In fact the oscillations are sound waves in the tightly coupled photon-baryon fluid. The drag epoch is when Compton drag on baryons ends. Planck infers H_0 from the CMB alone, calibrating the acoustic angular scale with a sound horizon computed in Lambda-CDM.** — Learners come away with a wrong picture of acoustic peaks and the drag epoch, and cannot see why the Hubble tension is model-dependent on the CMB side (the computed sound horizon is exactly what 'new early-Universe physics' proposals change). *Suggestion:* Teach the sound horizon r_s = integral of c_s dt up to last scattering as the standard ruler and theta_* = r_s / D_M(z_*) as the measured angle. Present galaxy BAO as the same ruler seen at low redshift, and note that shrinking r_s would raise the CMB value of H_0.
- **The book says the singularity theorems prove our Universe certainly had a singularity whatever its asymmetry. The theorems establish geodesic incompleteness only under energy conditions. The one relevant here, the strong energy condition rho + 3p >= 0, is violated by the very dark energy and inflation the chapter goes on to use.** — Readers may think the Big Bang singularity is a theorem-level certainty of the actual Universe rather than a consequence of assumptions. *Suggestion:* Link the singularity argument to the acceleration equation. The same rho + 3p that decides deceleration also decides focusing, so an accelerating phase is exactly where the argument weakens.

## Tutor notes

- Open with a question: if the Universe is expanding, where is the centre? Use the balloon (with the embedding hidden) to show there is none, then ask what 'distance' could mean for a galaxy whose light left 10 billion years ago.
- Order of explanation that works: observed facts (homogeneity, isotropy, v = Hd, CMB) -> cosmic time and comoving coordinates -> FRW metric and k -> redshift = expansion ratio -> operational distances -> only then Einstein's equations (Friedmann, acceleration) -> Omega budget -> thermal history.
- Check understanding of redshift by asking the learner to predict wavelength of a photon emitted when the Universe was one-third its present size (z = 2) and whether that means the source moved at twice the speed of light (no).
- When introducing the Friedmann equation, have the learner identify kinetic, potential and total-energy terms and predict the fate of a closed, matter-only universe before showing Figure 13.4.
- For dark energy, ask: how can something with positive energy make expansion speed up? Guide toward rho + 3p and tension; then use Exercise 13.18 to kill the idea that tension 'pulls' the Universe outward or inward mechanically.
- Distinguish carefully the particle horizon (causal, fundamental), the optical horizon (decoupling, not fundamental), and later the Hubble radius; learners conflate them. A spacetime diagram in conformal time helps.
- Use the photon-counting derivation of d_L as a formative check: ask which factor of (1+z) comes from energy and which from arrival rate, and why d_A has (1+z)^{-2} relative to d_L.
- When quoting numbers from 13.4, flag the known slips (Planck time formula, nucleosynthesis energy, equality epoch, decoupling redshift, e-fold count) and give modern values; encourage learners to recompute equality and decoupling from scaling laws.
- Common learner question: 'Does space expand inside atoms or the Solar System?' Answer with the chapter's local-flatness point: bound systems are governed by local dynamics; the FRW expansion describes averages over scales above ~10 Mpc.
- Common learner question: 'What came before the Big Bang?' Explain that classical GR cannot continue past R = 0, singularity theorems make this generic, but within a Planck time quantum gravity may change the story (loop cosmology bounces).
- For the Hubble tension, have learners explain why supernovae measure d_L while the CMB and BAO measure d_A, and what each method assumes; avoid presenting either value as 'the right one'.
- Link back to gravitational waves: ask why standard sirens need no distance ladder, why inclination matters (factor 4 in flux), and why primordial gravitational waves show up as B-mode polarization but not as temperature spots.
- For learners with less mathematics, lean on the energy-budget bar chart and the balloon; for advanced learners, derive the Christoffel symbols (Exercise 13.14) and the exact d_L(z) integral.
- When covering 'Using the CMB to measure H_0', do not repeat the book's mechanism. Explain acoustic peaks as sound waves in the photon-baryon fluid, the sound horizon as a ruler computed from early-Universe physics, and galaxy BAO as the same ruler seen later. Then ask why a smaller sound horizon would ease the Hubble tension.
