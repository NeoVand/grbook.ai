# SCH ch11 · Schwarzschild geometry and black holes

> Uses the conserved energy and angular momentum of Schwarzschild geodesics to build effective potentials and classify orbits (ISCO at 6M, photon orbit at 3M, perihelion advance, light bending by 4M/b, lensing). It then shows that r = 2M is only a coordinate singularity but is a one-way null horizon, using infall times and Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates. From there it generalizes to event horizons and the black-hole theorems, explores the Kerr solution (frame dragging, ergosphere, horizon area, Penrose process), surveys the black holes seen in astronomy, and closes with a heuristic uncertainty-principle derivation of Hawking radiation and black-hole thermodynamics.

**Pages:** printed 296–359 · pdf 314–377 · **Difficulty:** math 4/5, conceptual 4/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

The orbit sections are standard central-force mathematics, but the perturbative precession and deflection derivations are algebra-heavy and contain printed slips. The Kruskal section is conceptually demanding and gives the coordinate change without derivation. Kerr requires careful index raising with an off-diagonal metric and a subtle forward-in-time argument. The general-theorems and astronomy sections are descriptive and easy to read but assert much without proof, and the Hawking section is short but mixes local and distant energies in ways that confuse novices. Overall it is long (64 pages) and uneven in density.

## Role in the book

Chapter 10 derived the Schwarzschild exterior of a static star. This chapter takes that metric and applies the whole toolkit built so far: conserved momenta from symmetries (ch7), the weak-field limit and far-field mass and spin (ch8), curvature and geodesic deviation (ch6), and spacetime diagrams (ch1). It is the book's strong-field centerpiece. It delivers the classical solar-system tests (perihelion shift, light deflection), connects to the binary pulsar met in ch9, and builds the black-hole physics (horizons, Kerr, no-hair, area theorem) that ch12 needs for gravitational waves from black-hole mergers and ringdown. It also prepares ch13 by introducing dark matter from lensing and the naked Big Bang singularity. Like other late chapters it moves from derivation into a long observational survey with 2021-era numbers.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can derive the conserved specific energy and angular momentum of Schwarzschild geodesics and reduce the motion to a one-dimensional radial energy equation for both massive particles and photons.
- Reader can sketch the effective potentials for particles and photons and read off turning points, scattering, bound, circular (stable and unstable) and plunging orbits from an energy line.
- Reader can derive the radii of circular orbits, show that stable circular orbits require J^2 > 12M^2, and obtain the ISCO at r = 6M and the unstable photon orbit at r = 3M.
- Reader can compute the coordinate-time period of a circular orbit and explain why it coincides with Kepler's law and is what a distant observer measures.
- Reader can perturb the orbit equation in u = 1/r about a circular orbit to obtain the perihelion advance 6 pi M^2/J^2 ~ 6 pi M/r and evaluate it for Mercury (43 arcsec per century) and for binary pulsars.
- Reader can explain the post-Newtonian idea, relate Schwarzschild to isotropic coordinates at large r, and argue that Newtonian dynamics of slow bodies is controlled by g_00 alone.
- Reader can derive the light-deflection angle 4M/b, evaluate 1.74 arcsec at the solar limb, and explain physically why the Newtonian corpuscular value is half as large.
- Reader can describe gravitational lensing phenomena (multiple images, arcs, magnification, microlensing) and what they reveal about dark matter.
- Reader can show that a radially infalling particle reaches r = 2M in finite proper time but infinite Schwarzschild coordinate time, and interpret this as a coordinate singularity.
- Reader can explain why r becomes timelike inside r = 2M and why everything inside must reach r = 0, making r = 2M a horizon.
- Reader can write down Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates, state the metric in them, and read a Kruskal diagram: 45-degree light cones, hyperbolae of constant r, lines of constant t, horizon, singularity, and which regions are physical for a collapsing star.
- Reader can state the global definition of an event horizon, explain why it is teleological, and distinguish it from a locally trapped surface.
- Reader can summarize the key black-hole theorems and conjectures: uniqueness (Kerr), area theorem, singularity theorems, cosmic censorship, and linear stability results.
- Reader can work with the Kerr metric in Boyer-Lindquist coordinates to find the frame-dragging angular velocity, the ergosphere (static limit), the horizon radius r_+ and the horizon area.
- Reader can use the factored equatorial photon potentials V_+ and V_- with a ZAMO's energy condition to decide which trajectories are physical, and explain how negative-energy orbits in the ergoregion allow the Penrose process.
- Reader can describe how stellar-mass and supermassive black holes are identified observationally (X-ray binaries, mass gap, stellar orbits at Sgr A*, EHT imaging, gravitational waves).
- Reader can reproduce the heuristic derivation of the Hawking photon energy hbar/8M from the uncertainty principle, state T_H = hbar/(8 pi k_B M), derive the M^3 evaporation lifetime scaling, and identify S = kA/(4 hbar) through dM = T_H dS.

## Teaching approach

A long chapter that starts from physics and ends in astronomy. Schutz opens with the Newtonian dark star as a foil. He then does orbit theory the way an undergraduate does central-force problems: symmetry gives two conserved quantities, and a squared effective potential turns the motion into a one-dimensional energy diagram, with particles and photons handled side by side. The classic tests are derived perturbatively and immediately converted into numbers for Mercury, the Sun, Jupiter and pulsars. For r = 2M he uses a disciplined 'ask invariant questions' method: an analogy with the pole of spherical coordinates, proper versus coordinate infall time, finite local curvature, the interior signature flip, and then a picture problem (closing light cones) solved by Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates, toured in eight numbered remarks. Kerr is treated only as far as frame dragging, the ergosphere, horizon area and equatorial photon potentials. A recurring idea is that the sign of energy is set by the local forward direction of time, and it links the Penrose process to the heuristic Hawking argument. General theorems are listed rather than proved, and the observational sections are descriptive and heavily referenced.

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, derivation-heavy, observational-astrophysics, diagram-driven, thought-experiment, historical-narrative, geometric-first, survey-overview

**Narrative arc**

1. Motivating question: can gravity trap light? Michell and Laplace's escape-velocity dark star gives R = 2GM/c^2 and a density that falls as M^-2. Relativity then rejects light rising and falling back, because a local inertial observer would see the photon stop.
2. Model: time independence and axial symmetry give conserved E and J; normalizing the four-momentum yields a radial energy equation with squared effective potentials for particles and photons.
3. Qualitative reading: turning points, scattering, stable and unstable circular orbits and plunges are read off labelled potential diagrams; extrema give the ISCO at 6M and the photon orbit at 3M.
4. First check against Newton: the circular-orbit period is Kepler's law; perturbing about a circular orbit in u = 1/r shows the radial frequency differs from the angular one, so perihelia advance by 6 pi M/r. This is evaluated for Mercury and binary pulsars and linked to S2 at Sgr A*.
5. Framing: the post-Newtonian regime (weak field, slow motion). Isotropic coordinates reconcile the Schwarzschild far field with the ch8 Newtonian metric, and Newtonian dynamics is shown to be governed by g_00, the curvature of time.
6. Photons: the orbit equation gives deflection 4M/b (1.74 arcsec at the Sun). The Newtonian half-value is explained by light's speed giving g_rr equal weight, so bending is evidence for curved space. This leads to lensing, dark matter, microlensing and the EHT image.
7. Puzzle of r = 2M: coordinate singularities in ordinary geometry (sphere poles); finite proper infall time versus divergent coordinate time; finite curvature; inside, r is timelike, so nothing escapes; the Earth-horizon analogy.
8. Picture: Schwarzschild-coordinate light cones close up, which motivates Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates with 45-degree cones; eight remarks read the diagram (hyperbolae, t = const lines, horizon as a null line, redshift, regions I-IV, collapsing-star caveat).
9. Generalization: the event horizon is defined globally as a boundary in spacetime; horizon growth during collapse; a later gas infall shows its teleological nature; locally trapped surfaces and Penrose's theorem.
10. Theorems survey: uniqueness and no-hair, charges, stability, merger settling, area theorem, thermodynamic analogy, singularities, cosmic censorship.
11. Kerr: metric and Boyer-Lindquist coordinates; g_t phi leads to frame dragging and gravitomagnetism (Lense-Thirring tests); tangential photons at g_tt = 0 give the ergosphere; horizon at Delta = 0 and its area; equatorial photon potentials V_+ and V_-; ZAMO energy condition; negative-energy orbits; Penrose process, Blandford-Znajek jets, ergoregion instability of stars.
12. Application: real black holes (X-ray binaries and the mass gap, Sgr A* stellar orbits, M87 and merging galaxies, intermediate-mass holes, quasi-normal ringdown, numerical relativity and recoil kicks).
13. Quantum coda: a vacuum-fluctuation pair near the horizon, where the negative-energy partner becomes real inside because E is a spatial momentum there; the uncertainty principle plus infall time gives E = hbar/8M, compared with Hawking's T_H; M^3 lifetime, primordial holes, Planck mass; rearranging dM gives the first and second laws with S = kA/4 hbar; the information question.

**Signature moves**

- Uses the Newtonian dark star as a foil. It is right about the radius, but the local-inertial-observer argument shows why it is wrong about light trying to leave and falling back.
- Handles massive and massless particles in strict parallel (tilde versus untilded E, J, V), so photon results appear as limits of the same machinery.
- Puts letters (A, B, C, D, F, G) on points of the effective-potential plots and walks through the orbit type associated with each energy line.
- Introduces a deviation-from-circularity variable y, keeps the u^3 term while dropping y^3, and completes the square to show the radial and azimuthal periods differ (k not equal to 1).
- Explains the factor of two in light bending with a slow-versus-fast argument: slow bodies feel only g_00, light feels g_00 and g_rr equally, so bending measures spatial curvature.
- Tests whether a singularity is real by asking coordinate-independent questions: circumferences at a sphere's pole, proper time to fall in, curvature components in the infaller's frame.
- Gives a structured tour of the Kruskal diagram as eight numbered remarks instead of a derivation (the metric transformation is left to an exercise).
- Uses a thought experiment in which gas falls in later to make the global, future-dependent nature of the event horizon concrete.
- Anchors the idea of negative energy in the local forward direction of time for an observer (a ZAMO outside the horizon, an infalling observer inside), reusing it for both the Penrose process and Hawking radiation.
- Replaces the full quantum field theory with a one-page uncertainty-principle estimate, then compares it with the exact black-body result (0.860 agreement).
- Turns results into current observational numbers and missions right away (Mercury, PSR B1913+16, J0737-3039, S2, Gaia, EHT, LAGEOS-type satellites, GP-B, GW170817).

## Section by section

### §11.1 Trajectories in the Schwarzschild spacetime — p.296 (pdf 314)

The longest section. It opens with the pre-relativistic dark star and why relativity changes the picture. It then derives conserved quantities and effective potentials, classifies orbits, finds the ISCO and photon orbit, and goes on through perihelion precession, post-Newtonian ideas, light deflection and lensing (subsections listed separately below).

**Concepts:** Newtonian dark star, Schwarzschild radius, specific energy, specific angular momentum, effective potential, turning point, innermost stable circular orbit, photon sphere, plunging orbit

**Key moves**
- Escape-velocity balance with v = c reproduces R = 2GM/c^2; mean density scales as M^-2, so very massive dark stars need only low density.
- A photon turning around would be at rest for a comoving local inertial observer, which is impossible, so in relativity trapped light cannot even leave the surface.
- Stationarity and axial symmetry give conserved E = -p_0 and J = p_phi; spherical symmetry lets the orbit lie in the equatorial plane.
- Normalization p.p = -m^2 (or 0) gives (dr/dtau)^2 = E^2 - V^2 with V^2 = (1 - 2M/r)(1 + J^2/r^2) for particles and (1 - 2M/r)J^2/r^2 for photons.
- Differentiating gives d^2r/dtau^2 = -(1/2) dV^2/dr, the analogue of ma = -grad phi, so extrema of V^2 are circular orbits (minimum stable, maximum unstable).
- Setting dV^2/dr = 0 gives two circular radii when J^2 > 12M^2, merging at 6M (ISCO); photons have a single unstable orbit at 3M independent of E and J.
- An energy line above the potential peak (point F) plunges through r = 2M; for a star of radius R the diagrams apply only for r > R.

### Perihelion shift: noncircular orbits — p.302 (pdf 320)

Finds E and J of circular orbits and the coordinate-time period, which coincides with Kepler's law. Then perturbs the orbit equation about circularity to show that the orbit closes only after the azimuth advances by 2 pi/k, with k = (1 - 6M^2/J^2)^{1/2}. This gives an advance of 6 pi M/r per orbit, evaluated for Mercury, and notes the much larger shifts for the Hulse-Taylor pulsar and S2.

**Concepts:** orbital period of circular Schwarzschild orbits, perihelion precession

**Key moves**
- From the circular-orbit radius formula: J^2 = Mr/(1 - 3M/r) and E^2 = (1 - 2M/r)^2/(1 - 3M/r).
- The ratio (dt/dtau)/(dphi/dtau) gives dt/dphi = (r^3/M)^{1/2} and P = 2 pi (r^3/M)^{1/2}, a coordinate time that equals the distant observer's time.
- Divide the radial equation by (dphi/dtau)^2 and set u = 1/r; dropping u^3 gives the Newtonian orbit, a harmonic oscillator in y = u - M/J^2 with period 2 pi in phi (an ellipse).
- Keeping the u^3 term to second order in y gives a shifted oscillator with frequency k < 1, so the pericenter returns after Delta phi = 2 pi/k.
- Expand for J >> M: advance 6 pi M^2/J^2, and with J^2 ~ Mr this is 6 pi M/r = 4.99e-7 rad per orbit for Mercury, or 43 arcsec per century.

### Binary pulsars and post-Newtonian gravity — p.307 (pdf 325)

Applies the precession estimate to PSR B1913+16 and the double pulsar, explaining how periastron shift plus the combined gravitational/transverse-Doppler redshift and the mass function yield the stellar masses. It then introduces the post-Newtonian expansion, shows that Schwarzschild's far field matches the Newtonian metric of ch8 after a shift to isotropic radius, and argues that Newtonian gravity lives entirely in g_00.

**Concepts:** binary pulsar timing, post-Newtonian approximation, isotropic coordinates, Newtonian gravity as curvature of time

**Key moves**
- Crude Hulse-Taylor estimate with mean separation 1.2e9 m and M = 1.4 solar masses gives 2.1 degrees per year; a careful two-body, eccentric calculation gives the observed 4.2 degrees per year.
- The measured periastron advance is turned around to measure neutron-star masses, combined with the redshift term and mass function.
- The pericenter shift is an expansion in M/r ~ v^2: a post-Newtonian correction.
- Weak-field Schwarzschild has g_rr = 1 + 2M/r but an undistorted angular part; the substitution r-bar = r - M makes the spatial metric conformally flat, matching Eq. 8.50.
- For slow motion dr << dt, so the proper time along a world line is dominated by g_00; Newtonian gravity is therefore equivalent to gravitational redshift.

### Gravitational deflection of light — p.309 (pdf 327)

Derives the photon orbit equation in terms of the impact parameter b = J/E and integrates it to first order in M/b, finding a total deflection 4M/b. Evaluates it for the Sun (1.74 arcsec) and Jupiter, and notes astrometric satellites (Hipparcos, Gaia) and deflection away from the limb. Recounts the Cavendish-Soldner corpuscular prediction of half this value and explains the factor of two physically.

**Concepts:** impact parameter, gravitational deflection of light, Newtonian corpuscular deflection

**Key moves**
- dphi/du = (b^-2 - u^2 + 2Mu^3)^{-1/2}; with M = 0 the solution r sin(phi - phi_0) = b is a straight line.
- Substitute y = u(1 - Mu) so the cubic term cancels at first order, leaving dphi/dy = (1 + 2My)/(b^-2 - y^2)^{1/2}.
- Integration gives phi = phi_0 + 2M/b + arcsin(by) - 2M(b^-2 - y^2)^{1/2}; closest approach at y = 1/b, reached after pi/2 + 2M/b, so by symmetry the total sweep is pi + 4M/b.
- Numbers: 8.45e-6 rad at the solar limb, 6.4e-8 rad at Jupiter's limb.
- Factor of two: light moves with |dr| ~ |dt|, so g_rr's deviation from flatness counts as much as g_00's; Newtonian corpuscles feel only g_00.

### Gravitational lensing — p.312 (pdf 330)

Explains multiple images from rays passing on both sides of a mass and why lensing is universal but usually unnoticed. It then surveys cluster lensing (arcs and multiple images), weak lensing and magnification, dark matter inferred from lens mass models, microlensing and MACHOs, and the Event Horizon Telescope image of M87's black hole, where lensing brings light from behind the hole into view.

**Concepts:** gravitational lensing, weak lensing, microlensing, dark matter, black hole shadow

**Key moves**
- Two rays from a distant star bent around opposite sides of a lens can cross at an observer, who sees two images separated by about the deflection angle.
- Double images are rare for stars because alignment is improbable and extended sources blur them; for clusters the probability and deflections are large enough.
- Lens mass models show clusters hold roughly ten times more mass than their stars, distributed smoothly: dark matter, which must be neutral and non-luminous.
- Microlensing appears as temporary brightening, not image splitting, as a compact dark object passes in front of a star.
- EHT: the ring's inner edge lies within 6M, subtending under 1e-10 rad for M87's hole, which needed Earth-sized radio interferometry.

### §11.2 Nature of the surface r = 2M — p.315 (pdf 333)

Asks whether the misbehaviour of the metric at r = 2M is geometric or coordinate-induced. Compares it with the poles of spherical coordinates, computes proper and coordinate times for radial infall, shows curvature is finite there, and demonstrates that inside, r is a time coordinate so everything moves inexorably to r = 0. This makes r = 2M a causal horizon with no local marker.

**Concepts:** coordinate singularity, curvature singularity, proper time of radial infall, Schwarzschild horizon, interchange of timelike and spacelike coordinates inside the horizon

**Key moves**
- On a sphere, theta = 0 is a line on a coordinate map but a single point; invariant measurements (vanishing circumferences) reveal this. In Lorentzian geometry, null separations make such tests subtler.
- Radial infall: dtau = -dr/(E^2 - 1 + 2M/r)^{1/2} integrates to a finite value down to and through 2M; for E = 1 the result is (4M/3)[(r/2M)^{3/2}].
- Coordinate time dt = E dtau/(1 - 2M/r) diverges logarithmically near r = 2M for every E, because the divergence comes from g^00.
- Riemann components in the infalling frame are finite at 2M (Exercise 11.21), so the particle continues inward.
- With epsilon = 2M - r, the metric shows g_rr < 0 and g_tt > 0 inside: r is timelike, forcing decreasing r for particles and photons alike, so r = 0 lies in every future.
- The Earth-horizon analogy: nothing is locally special at a horizon, but unlike Earth's, this one cannot be recrossed outward.

### Coordinate systems and Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates — p.318 (pdf 336)

Draws radial light cones in Schwarzschild coordinates, which narrow to vertical at r = 2M, and interprets the infinite-coordinate-time result as a coordinate distortion that stretches a single event into a line. It then introduces Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates, in which radial light rays are always at 45 degrees, and gives eight observations about the Kruskal diagram, including the true horizon, redshift seen from afar, the four regions, and why regions III and IV are irrelevant for a collapsed star.

**Concepts:** light cones in Schwarzschild coordinates, Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates, Kruskal diagram, infinite redshift at the horizon, horizon as a null surface, maximally extended Schwarzschild spacetime

**Key moves**
- Radial null lines satisfy dt/dr = +/-(1 - 2M/r)^{-1} (printed without the inverse), so cones close as r -> 2M and world lines reach 2M only at t = infinity.
- Guess: the whole line r = 2M in (t, r) might represent less than it seems, as with the pole of a sphere.
- Define u, v via cosh/sinh of t/4M with factors (r/2M - 1)^{1/2} e^{r/4M} outside and the swapped form inside; the metric becomes conformally flat in (u, v) with no singularity at 2M.
- r(u, v) is implicit, via (r/2M - 1)e^{r/2M} = u^2 - v^2, and keeps its meaning as the areal radius of the symmetry sphere.
- Lines of constant r are hyperbolae (timelike outside, spacelike inside); lines of constant t are straight lines through the origin, all collapsing into the origin as in a (t, r) chart; the true horizon is r = 2M, t = +infinity, a null line.
- A falling clock emits finitely many ticks before crossing, spread over infinite distant time, which is seen as infinite redshift.
- For a star that really collapsed, only the part of the diagram outside the stellar surface's world line applies, so regions III and IV are discarded.

### §11.3 General black holes — p.322 (pdf 340)

Generalizes from Schwarzschild to black holes formed dynamically. Defines the event horizon globally, illustrates its growth during collapse and its dependence on the future, introduces locally trapped surfaces and Penrose's theorem, and then lists the main theorems and conjectures: uniqueness, charges, stability and ringdown, the area theorem, thermodynamics, singularities, cosmic censorship. The Kerr material follows (listed separately).

**Concepts:** event horizon, teleological nature of the event horizon, locally trapped surface, black hole uniqueness theorem, Hawking area theorem, black hole thermodynamics, curvature singularity, cosmic censorship conjecture, asymptotic flatness

**Key moves**
- Event horizon defined as the spacetime boundary between events that can and cannot send signals to distant observers in an asymptotically flat spacetime; it is ruled by marginally trapped null rays.
- Spherical collapse: outgoing photons from the center are progressively delayed; the last one that just stays at 2M traces the horizon, which grows from zero radius before the star crosses 2M.
- Late gas infall example: rays hovering at 2M_0 were actually trapped, and the true horizon was already expanding toward 2M_1 before the gas arrived, so locating a horizon requires the entire future.
- Practical substitute: locally trapped surfaces defined on one time slice; Penrose proved they lie inside true horizons, so black-hole formation needs no symmetry.
- No-hair: a stationary vacuum hole is fully specified by M and J measured from the far field; the complexity of about 1e60 atoms is erased.
- Area theorem (with positive energy density) forbids spontaneous bifurcation of a hole; quantum Hawking emission evades it because quantum energies can be negative.

### Kerr black hole: metric, frame dragging, ergoregion and horizon — p.328 (pdf 346)

Presents the Kerr metric in Boyer-Lindquist coordinates with a = J/M. Shows how the off-diagonal g_t phi makes zero-angular-momentum particles co-rotate (frame dragging, gravitomagnetism, Lense-Thirring measurements), finds the static limit where g_tt = 0 and counter-rotation becomes impossible, locates the horizon at Delta = 0, and computes its area.

**Concepts:** Kerr metric, Boyer-Lindquist coordinates, Kerr parameter, frame dragging, gravitomagnetism, Lense-Thirring effect, ergosphere, ergoregion, Kerr horizon, Kerr horizon area

**Key moves**
- Stationary axisymmetric metrics fix t and phi but leave r and theta to choice; Boyer-Lindquist coordinates have the minimum number of nonzero components, with g_t phi unavoidably nonzero.
- With p_phi = 0 the raised momentum still has p^phi nonzero because g^{phi t} is nonzero, so dphi/dt = g^{phi t}/g^{tt} = omega: an infalling zero-angular-momentum particle is swept around.
- Tangential photons satisfy a quadratic in dphi/dt; where g_tt = 0 one root is zero, so no photon (hence no particle) can counter-rotate or stay static: the static limit r_0 = M + (M^2 - a^2 cos^2 theta)^{1/2}.
- Horizon where g_rr blows up (Delta = 0): r_+ = M + (M^2 - a^2)^{1/2}, inside r_0 except at the poles.
- The induced metric on t, r = const has a theta-independent determinant factor, giving A = 4 pi (r_+^2 + a^2).

### Equatorial photon motion in Kerr and the Penrose process — p.332 (pdf 350)

Inverts the t-phi block of the Kerr metric to get omega = -g_t phi/g_phi phi explicitly. It writes the equatorial photon energy equation, which contains an EJ cross term and so must be factored into (E - V_+)(E - V_-), and uses a ZAMO to show that only E > V_+ is forward in time. For counter-rotating photons V_+ dips below zero inside the ergosphere, allowing negative-energy orbits, the Penrose energy-extraction process, Blandford-Znajek jets, and the ergoregion instability of rotating stars.

**Concepts:** factored effective potentials, zero-angular-momentum observer, negative-energy orbits, Penrose process, Blandford-Znajek mechanism, ergoregion instability

**Key moves**
- 2x2 block inverse gives g^{tt} = g_phi phi/D and g^{t phi} = -g_t phi/D, hence omega = -g_t phi/g_phi phi = 2Mra/[(r^2 + a^2)^2 - a^2 Delta sin^2 theta], which is positive for a > 0 and falls off as r^-3.
- Equatorial p.p = 0 gives (dr/dlambda)^2 proportional to (E - V_+)(E - V_-), with V_+ and V_- meeting at the horizon value omega_+ J and becoming complex for Delta < 0 (no turning points inside).
- Direction of time: reversing a geodesic's tangent flips the sign of every observer's measured energy, so forwardness must be judged by a local observer, not by E at infinity.
- ZAMO four-velocity (A, 0, 0, omega A) gives measured energy A(E - omega J); positivity requires E > omega J, which selects E > V_+.
- For aJ < 0 the curves flip and V_+ becomes negative for r < r_0, so forward-moving photons with E < 0 exist only inside the ergosphere.
- A particle decaying inside the ergosphere can send a negative-E, negative-J fragment into the hole and eject a fragment with more energy than the original, spinning the hole down.
- In a star with an ergotoroid and no horizon, a trapped negative-energy wave leaks positive energy outward and grows exponentially; in Kerr, the horizon can swallow the wave, and small-a linear stability is proven.

### §11.4 Real black holes in astronomy — p.337 (pdf 355)

Argues that uniqueness makes black holes astrophysically simple (with Chandrasekhar's reaction), then surveys evidence: X-ray binaries with 6-20 solar-mass holes distinguished from neutron stars by mass and lack of pulsations, the neutron star-black hole mass gap, Sgr A* weighed by stellar orbits and confined by GRAVITY, M87, galaxy mergers and ejected holes, intermediate-mass holes, quasi-normal ringdown, the history of numerical relativity, and merger recoil kicks.

**Concepts:** X-ray binary, accretion disk, neutron star-black hole mass gap, supermassive black hole, Sagittarius A*, intermediate-mass black hole, quasi-normal modes, numerical relativity, gravitational recoil kick

**Key moves**
- Identifying a compact accretor: steady X-ray pulsations imply a neutron star (a hole cannot carry a misaligned field); an estimated mass above about 5 solar masses implies a hole, since causal equations of state cap neutron stars near 3 solar masses.
- Selection effect in the mass diagram: X-ray holes are nearby binaries; LIGO-Virgo holes are distant and must be massive to be detected; isolated objects in the gap would be invisible.
- Sgr A*: decades of infrared astrometry plus radial velocities give Keplerian orbits sharing one dark focus and M = 4.15e6 solar masses, confined within roughly ISCO size.
- Stationary holes ring down in a few cycles; matching the Kerr quasi-normal spectrum would confirm the final object is Kerr.
- Recoil velocity depends only on dimensionless data (mass ratio, spins and their directions), because velocity carries no mass scale.

### §11.5 Hawking radiation — p.345 (pdf 363)

Gives a plausibility argument for Hawking radiation. Virtual photon pairs near the horizon can become real if the negative-energy member crosses the horizon before recombining, because inside, the conserved E is a spatial momentum and may have either sign. Matching the infall proper time of a freely falling frame to hbar/2(energy) gives an escaping energy hbar/8M independent of where the pair formed, close to the black-body mean energy for T_H = hbar/(8 pi k M). The section derives lifetime scaling, notes primordial holes and the Planck-mass limit, obtains black-hole first and second laws with S = kA/4 hbar, and raises the information problem.

**Concepts:** Hawking radiation, vacuum fluctuations, Hawking temperature, black hole evaporation, primordial black holes, Planck mass, Bekenstein-Hawking entropy, black hole information problem

**Key moves**
- Inside the horizon choose an observer momentarily with U^t = 0, so U^r = -(2M/r - 1)^{1/2}; a radial photon's measured energy is -(2M/r - 1)^{-1/2} p^r, positive for any ingoing photon regardless of the sign of E.
- A frame released from rest at 2M + epsilon has E-tilde ~ (epsilon/2M)^{1/2} and reaches the horizon after proper time 2(2M epsilon)^{1/2}.
- Equating this to hbar/2(energy) gives local energy (1/4)hbar(2M epsilon)^{-1/2}; redshifting to infinity with g^00 and U_0 gives E = hbar/8M, independent of epsilon.
- Exact result: black body with mean photon energy 2.701 kT = 0.860 hbar/8M; the local wavelength exceeds epsilon but is much less than M, so the flat-space treatment is self-consistent.
- Luminosity ~ A T^4 ~ M^-2 gives lifetime ~ M^3; 1e12 kg lasts about the age of the Universe; the final second releases about 1e23 J at 1e11 K.
- A = 16 pi M^2 gives dM = (hbar/8 pi k M) d(kA/4 hbar), i.e. dE = T_H dS, and the area theorem becomes the second law.
- Warns that Hawking's negative-energy partners lie inside the horizon and exist without rotation. Penrose-process orbits sit outside the horizon in an ergoregion. Because the partner is already inside, there is no ergoregion-style runaway, only steady thermal emission.
- Closes with open questions: whether weak correlations in the radiation carry information, and the use of Hawking radiation and black-hole entropy as a necessary test for any proposed quantum-gravity method.

## Concepts

### Schwarzschild metric

*solution-or-model · revisited* · also: Schwarzschild geometry, Schwarzschild line element

The unique spherically symmetric vacuum solution of Einstein's equations, ds^2 = -(1 - 2M/r)dt^2 + (1 - 2M/r)^{-1}dr^2 + r^2 dOmega^2 in geometrized units, with M the total mass and r the areal radius. It describes the exterior of any spherical star and, in full, a non-rotating black hole.

**How introduced:** Recalled from ch10 as the starting point. The chapter's stated strategy is to understand its physical meaning by studying its timelike and null geodesics.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, geodesic, geometrized units

$$
ds^2 = -(1-2M/r)dt^2 + (1-2M/r)^{-1}dr^2 + r^2 d\Omega^2
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 11.21 computes its Christoffel symbols and Riemann components and shows the curvature invariant diverges only at r = 0.

**Where:** SCH §11.1 p.296 (pdf 314)

### Newtonian dark star

*historical-idea · developed* · also: Michell-Laplace dark star, Newtonian black hole

An 18th-century idea: a body so compact that the Newtonian escape speed from its surface exceeds c, so corpuscular light cannot reach infinity. The critical radius R = 2GM/c^2 coincides with the Schwarzschild radius, but in this picture light still leaves the surface and falls back.

**How introduced:** Opening motivation. Since trajectories depend only on velocity (weak equivalence principle), trapping light is just an escape-velocity question. The chapter then contrasts it with GR, where trapped light never gets out at all.

**Prerequisites:** escape velocity, weak equivalence principle

$$
\tfrac12 v^2 = GM/R
$$

$$
R = 2GM/c^2
$$

**Notes:** Reused later: Cavendish and Soldner applied the same corpuscular reasoning to predict half the GR light deflection.

**Where:** SCH §11.1 p.296 (pdf 314); SCH §11.1 p.297 (pdf 315); SCH §11.2 p.318 (pdf 336)

### escape velocity

*physical-quantity · mention*

The minimum launch speed at which a projectile's kinetic energy balances its Newtonian gravitational potential energy, so that it just reaches infinity; v_esc = (2GM/R)^{1/2} from radius R.

**How introduced:** Used as the Newtonian route to the dark-star radius by setting v_esc = c.

$$
v_{esc}^2 = 2GM/R
$$

**Where:** SCH §11.1 p.297 (pdf 315)

### Schwarzschild radius

*physical-quantity · core* · also: gravitational radius, horizon radius r = 2M, R = 2GM/c^2

The areal radius r = 2M (2GM/c^2 in SI units) at which the Schwarzschild metric's g_tt vanishes and g_rr diverges. It is about 3 km per solar mass and marks the horizon of a non-rotating black hole.

**How introduced:** Obtained first from the Newtonian dark-star calculation, then identified in §11.2 with the horizon of the relativistic solution.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild metric

$$
R_S = 2GM/c^2 \approx 2.95\,\mathrm{km}\,(M/M_\odot)
$$

**Where:** SCH §11.1 p.297 (pdf 315); SCH §11.2 p.318 (pdf 336)

### mean density at the Schwarzschild radius

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: black-hole formation density

The Newtonian mean density of a mass M compressed into radius 2GM/c^2, rho = 3c^6/(32 pi G^3 M^2). Because it scales as M^-2, very massive objects become black holes at modest, even sub-water, densities.

**How introduced:** Used to argue that forming supermassive holes needs no exotic physics; Exercise 11.1 checks the 1e9 solar-mass case.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild radius

$$
\bar\rho = 3c^6/(32\pi G^3 M^2)
$$

**Where:** SCH §11.1 p.297 (pdf 315)

### conserved momentum components from metric symmetries

*theorem · revisited* · also: constants of geodesic motion, Killing conserved quantities

If the metric components do not depend on a coordinate x^beta, then the covariant component p_beta of a freely moving particle's four-momentum is constant along its geodesic. For Schwarzschild, t-independence conserves p_0 and phi-independence conserves p_phi.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Eq. 7.29. The chapter stresses that Schwarzschild has so much symmetry that these constants fully determine the orbits.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic, four-momentum, one-form components

$$
g_{\alpha\beta,t}=0 \Rightarrow p_0 = \text{const}
$$

**Notes:** In Kerr the same argument still conserves p_t and p_phi, but raising indices mixes them through g^{t phi}.

**Where:** SCH §11.1 p.298 (pdf 316); SCH §11.3 p.329 (pdf 347)

### specific energy

*physical-quantity · core* · also: energy per unit rest mass, E-tilde, conserved energy at infinity

The conserved quantity E-tilde = -p_0/m for a massive particle (E = -p_0 for a photon). It equals the energy per unit rest mass that an observer at rest at infinity would measure; E-tilde < 1 means bound, E-tilde = 1 means released from rest at infinity.

**How introduced:** Defined from the time-independence of the metric so that massive and massless cases can be treated in parallel.

**Prerequisites:** conserved momentum components from metric symmetries

$$
\tilde E := -p_0/m
$$

$$
E := -p_0
$$

**Where:** SCH §11.1 p.298 (pdf 316)

### specific angular momentum

*physical-quantity · core* · also: angular momentum per unit rest mass, J-tilde

The conserved quantity J-tilde = p_phi/m for a massive particle (J = p_phi for a photon), arising from the metric's independence of the azimuthal angle phi.

**How introduced:** Introduced alongside specific energy; with the orbit placed in the equatorial plane it completes the set of constants needed.

**Prerequisites:** conserved momentum components from metric symmetries

$$
\tilde J := p_\phi/m
$$

$$
d\phi/d\tau = \tilde J/r^2
$$

**Notes:** The text on printed p.316 briefly writes L-tilde for the same quantity.

**Where:** SCH §11.1 p.298 (pdf 316)

### affine parameter for photons

*definition · introduced* · also: lambda

A parameter lambda along a null geodesic in terms of which the geodesic equation keeps its standard form. Proper time is unavailable for light, so lambda is fixed by setting the photon momentum equal to the tangent, p^alpha = dx^alpha/dlambda.

**How introduced:** Stated briefly: the photon equation p^r = dr/dlambda is taken as defining lambda.

**Prerequisites:** null geodesic

$$
p^r = dr/d\lambda
$$

**Where:** SCH §11.1 p.299 (pdf 317)

### effective potential

*technique · core* · also: squared effective potential V^2(r), V-tilde squared

The function V^2(r) such that the radial motion obeys (dr/dtau)^2 = E^2 - V^2(r). For Schwarzschild particles V^2 = (1 - 2M/r)(1 + J^2/r^2); for photons V^2 = (1 - 2M/r)J^2/r^2. Motion is allowed only where E^2 >= V^2, and radial acceleration is -(1/2)dV^2/dr.

**How introduced:** Obtained by solving the four-momentum normalization for the radial velocity after inserting conserved E and J, then plotted with labelled energy lines (Figures 11.1-11.3) to classify orbits.

**Prerequisites:** specific energy, specific angular momentum, four-momentum normalization

$$
\tilde V^2 = (1-2M/r)(1+\tilde J^2/r^2)
$$

$$
V^2 = (1-2M/r)J^2/r^2
$$

$$
d^2r/d\tau^2 = -\tfrac12 dV^2/dr
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 11.12 traces the u^3 (i.e. -2MJ^2/r^3) term, absent in Newtonian theory, and links it to the potential's maximum.

**Where:** SCH §11.1 p.299 (pdf 317); SCH §11.1 p.300 (pdf 318); SCH §11.1 p.301 (pdf 319); SCH §11.1 p.302 (pdf 320)

### turning point

*definition · developed* · also: periapsis radius, radial turning point

A radius where the energy line meets the effective potential, E^2 = V^2, so dr/dtau = 0. Radial motion reverses there while the angular motion continues.

**How introduced:** Illustrated by point G in Figures 11.1 and 11.2: a particle from infinity reaches minimum r there and is pushed back out.

**Prerequisites:** effective potential

**Notes:** The text warns that the potential equation governs only the radial part of the motion.

**Where:** SCH §11.1 p.299 (pdf 317); SCH §11.1 p.301 (pdf 319)

### stable and unstable circular orbits

*phenomenon · core* · also: circular geodesics

Constant-r geodesics, possible only where dV^2/dr = 0. At a minimum of V^2 a small radial displacement is pushed back (stable); at a maximum it runs away (unstable). For particles with J^2 > 12M^2 there are two circular radii r = (J^2/2M)[1 +/- (1 - 12M^2/J^2)^{1/2}].

**How introduced:** Read from the extrema A (maximum) and B (minimum) of the potential diagrams, then quantified by differentiating V^2.

**Prerequisites:** effective potential

$$
r = \frac{\tilde J^2}{2M}\left[1 \pm (1 - 12M^2/\tilde J^2)^{1/2}\right]
$$

$$
\tilde J^2 = Mr/(1-3M/r)
$$

$$
\tilde E^2 = (1-2M/r)^2/(1-3M/r)
$$

**Where:** SCH §11.1 p.301 (pdf 319); SCH §11.1 p.302 (pdf 320)

### innermost stable circular orbit

*phenomenon · core* · also: ISCO, last stable circular orbit, r_min = 6M

The smallest radius, r = 6M in Schwarzschild, at which a massive particle can orbit circularly and stably. It occurs when J^2 = 12M^2, where the stable and unstable circular radii merge at an inflection point of V^2; below it, circular orbits are unstable or absent.

**How introduced:** Follows from the condition for real circular-orbit radii; Figure 11.3 shows the potential flattening to an inflection and then losing its well.

**Prerequisites:** stable and unstable circular orbits

r_{ISCO} = 6M

**Notes:** Emphasized as a property of the geometry, independent of the particle. Reappears in the EHT ring discussion and the GRAVITY confinement of Sgr A*'s mass.

**Where:** SCH §11.1 p.301 (pdf 319); SCH §11.1 p.302 (pdf 320)

### photon sphere

*phenomenon · developed* · also: unstable photon orbit, r = 3M circular photon orbit, light ring

The radius r = 3M where the photon effective potential has its only maximum, so light can circle the hole on an unstable orbit. Its location is independent of the photon's energy and angular momentum.

**How introduced:** Found by extremizing the photon V^2, with the remark that E and J change a photon's wavelength but not its path.

**Prerequisites:** effective potential, impact parameter

r_{ph} = 3M

### plunging orbit

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: capture orbit

A trajectory whose energy exceeds the maximum of the effective potential, so it has no turning point and crosses r = 2M. For fixed J such orbits are aimed more directly at the hole, with a small impact parameter.

**How introduced:** Point F in Figures 11.1-11.2; Exercise 11.11 finds the minimum energy for capture.

**Prerequisites:** effective potential, impact parameter

**Notes:** For a star of radius R > 2M, trajectories reaching R simply hit the surface.

**Where:** SCH §11.1 p.302 (pdf 320)

### orbital period of circular Schwarzschild orbits

*law · developed* · also: relativistic Kepler third law

For a circular geodesic at areal radius r the coordinate-time period is P = 2 pi (r^3/M)^{1/2}. It is exactly Kepler's Newtonian formula and equals the period measured by a distant static observer, whose proper time coincides with t.

**How introduced:** Obtained by dividing dt/dtau by dphi/dtau using the circular-orbit E and J; the coincidence with Newton is noted as accidental.

**Prerequisites:** stable and unstable circular orbits

$$
dt/d\phi = (r^3/M)^{1/2}
$$

$$
P = 2\pi (r^3/M)^{1/2}
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 11.8 contrasts this with the orbiting clock's proper time and a static clock's time.

**Where:** SCH p.302 (pdf 320); SCH p.303 (pdf 321)

### perihelion precession

*phenomenon · core* · also: pericenter advance, periastron shift, peribothon shift, apsidal precession

The steady rotation of the major axis of a nearly elliptical bound orbit. In Schwarzschild it arises because the radial oscillation frequency is lower than the orbital frequency by k = (1 - 6M^2/J^2)^{1/2}, giving an advance of about 6 pi M^2/J^2 ~ 6 pi M/r radians per orbit in the weak-field limit.

**How introduced:** Motivated by Mercury's 43 arcsec per century residual and relativistic systems (Hulse-Taylor, S2). Derived by rewriting the orbit equation in u = 1/r, perturbing about circularity with y, keeping the relativistic u^3 term, and completing the square.

**Prerequisites:** effective potential, specific angular momentum, Newtonian Kepler orbits

$$
\Delta\phi = 2\pi(1-6M^2/\tilde J^2)^{-1/2}
$$

$$
\delta\phi \approx 6\pi M^2/\tilde J^2 \approx 6\pi M/r

**Notes:** Figure 11.4 shows strong-field rosettes with advances of roughly 97 and 130 degrees per orbit. The printed intermediate Eqs. 11.34/11.36 contain a J^2 that should be J^4 (see gaps).

### binary pulsar timing

### post-Newtonian approximation

*technique · introduced* · also: post-Newtonian expansion, PN corrections

A systematic expansion of general relativity about Newtonian gravity in the small parameters M/r ~ v^2 (weak field, slow motion). First-order corrections include perihelion advance, light deflection and frame dragging; higher orders are needed for accurate inspiral waveforms.

**How introduced:** Introduced after noting that the step from Eq. 11.37 to 11.38 was a Taylor expansion in M/r, so perihelion shift is the leading correction to Newtonian motion.

### isotropic coordinates

*convention · introduced* · also: isotropic radius r-bar

Spatial coordinates in which the spatial metric is a scalar multiple of the flat metric, dl^2 = F(r-bar)(dr-bar^2 + r-bar^2 dOmega^2), so no direction is singled out. For weak-field Schwarzschild, r-bar = r - M gives F = 1 + 2M/r-bar to first order.

\bar r = r - M

$$
d\ell^2 = (1 + 2M/\bar r)(d\bar r^2 + \bar r^2 d\Omega^2)

### Newtonian gravity as curvature of time

*principle · developed* · also: g_00 dominance for slow motion

For slowly moving bodies the proper time along a world line is dominated by the g_00 dt^2 term, so Newtonian orbits are determined by g_00 alone. Since g_00 also sets gravitational redshift, Newtonian gravity and redshift carry the same information, and spatial curvature appears only in post-Newtonian corrections.

**How introduced:** Argued from dr/dt << 1 at the end of the post-Newtonian discussion, then used to explain the factor of two in light bending.

**Prerequisites:** post-Newtonian approximation, gravitational redshift

g_{00} \approx -(1 + 2\phi)

**Where:** SCH p.309 (pdf 327); SCH p.312 (pdf 330)

### impact parameter

### coordinate singularity

*definition · core* · also: bad coordinates

A place where a coordinate system fails to represent the geometry faithfully (metric components blow up or vanish, or distinct coordinate values label one point) although the geometry itself is smooth. It can be removed by changing coordinates and is diagnosed by coordinate-invariant measurements.

**How introduced:** Introduced through the poles of spherical coordinates on a sphere (Figure 11.10), where a whole line of the coordinate map is one point, then applied to r = 2M.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, coordinate transformation

### curvature singularity

*definition · developed* · also: true singularity, physical singularity, r = 0 singularity

A place where spacetime curvature, and hence tidal force as measured by a freely falling observer, becomes infinite, so it cannot be removed by any coordinate change. More generally, a spacetime is singular where geodesics are incomplete, ending after finite proper time or affine parameter.

**How introduced:** Contrasted with the coordinate singularity at 2M: Riemann components in the infaller's frame are finite at 2M but diverge at r = 0 (Exercise 11.21). Later generalized in the list of black-hole theorems.

### proper time of radial infall

*phenomenon · core* · also: finite infall time, coordinate time divergence at the horizon

A freely falling particle reaches and crosses r = 2M after a finite proper time, but the Schwarzschild coordinate time along its path diverges logarithmically as it approaches 2M. The divergence comes from the factor (1 - 2M/r)^{-1} and indicates a failure of the t coordinate.

**How introduced:** First geometrical question posed to r = 2M: integrate dtau and dt along a radial geodesic, with E = 1 worked explicitly.

**Prerequisites:** specific energy, effective potential

### interchange of timelike and spacelike coordinates inside the horizon

*phenomenon · core* · also: r becomes timelike inside 2M

For r < 2M the sign of both g_tt and g_rr flips, so curves of constant t, theta, phi are timelike and r serves as a time coordinate while t is spacelike. Every future-directed timelike or null curve must therefore move to smaller r.

**How introduced:** Seen by rewriting the metric just inside the horizon with epsilon = 2M - r; used to argue inevitable collapse to r = 0 and the impossibility of signalling out.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild metric, timelike and spacelike intervals

$$
ds^2 = \frac{\varepsilon}{2M-\varepsilon}dt^2 - \frac{2M-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}d\varepsilon^2 + (2M-\varepsilon)^2 d\Omega^2
$$

**Notes:** Reused in §11.5: inside, the conserved E = -p_t is a spatial momentum and can take either sign.

**Where:** SCH §11.2 p.317 (pdf 335); SCH §11.2 p.318 (pdf 336); SCH §11.5 p.346 (pdf 364)

### Schwarzschild horizon

*definition · core* · also: event horizon of Schwarzschild, r = 2M surface

The null surface r = 2M of a Schwarzschild black hole, separating events from which light can reach distant observers from those it cannot. It is locally unremarkable (finite curvature) but causally one-way.

**How introduced:** Named after showing that nothing inside can send a photon out, with the analogy of Earth's horizon for a ship at sea.

**Prerequisites:** interchange of timelike and spacelike coordinates inside the horizon, coordinate singularity

$$
r_H = 2M
$$

**Where:** SCH §11.2 p.318 (pdf 336); SCH §11.2 p.321 (pdf 339)

### light cones in Schwarzschild coordinates

*phenomenon · developed* · also: closing light cones

In a t-r diagram with Schwarzschild coordinates, radial null lines have slope dt/dr = +/-(1 - 2M/r)^{-1}, so the cones narrow toward the vertical as r -> 2M. This is a distortion of the chart, not of the geometry.

**How introduced:** First attempt at a picture of the geometry (Figure 11.11); it explains geometrically why infall takes infinite t and motivates finding coordinates with undistorted cones.

**Prerequisites:** light cone, Schwarzschild metric

$$
dt/dr = \pm (1-2M/r)^{-1}

### Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates

*convention · core* · also: Kruskal coordinates, u, v coordinates

Coordinates (u, v, theta, phi) covering the full Schwarzschild geometry smoothly across r = 2M. Outside, u = (r/2M - 1)^{1/2} e^{r/4M} cosh(t/4M) and v = the same prefactor times sinh(t/4M); inside, cosh and sinh swap and the prefactor becomes (1 - r/2M)^{1/2}. The metric becomes -(32M^3/r)e^{-r/2M}(dv^2 - du^2) + r^2 dOmega^2, so radial light rays are 45-degree lines.

**How introduced:** Presented as the 1960 answer to the search for coordinates that do not close the light cones; the transformation is given without derivation (Exercise 11.26 asks for the proof).

**Prerequisites:** coordinate singularity, light cones in Schwarzschild coordinates, hyperbolic functions

$$
u = (r/2M-1)^{1/2}e^{r/4M}\cosh(t/4M)
$$

$$
v = (r/2M-1)^{1/2}e^{r/4M}\sinh(t/4M)
$$

$$
(r/2M - 1)e^{r/2M} = u^2 - v^2
$$

$$
ds^2 = -\frac{32M^3}{r}e^{-r/2M}(dv^2-du^2) + r^2 d\Omega^2
$$

**Notes:** The transformation itself is singular at 2M, which is what allows it to cure the metric's singularity. The book remarks the coordinates are poor far from the hole.

**Where:** SCH §11.2 p.319 (pdf 337); SCH §11.2 p.320 (pdf 338)

### Kruskal diagram

*mathematical-object · core* · also: Kruskal-Szekeres diagram, u-v diagram

The (u, v) spacetime diagram of Schwarzschild with angles suppressed. Light cones are at 45 degrees, curves of constant r are hyperbolae u^2 - v^2 = const, curves of constant t are straight lines through the origin, the horizon is the null line u = v, and r = 0 is a spacelike hyperbola bounding region II.

**How introduced:** Figure 11.12 plus eight numbered remarks that read off causal structure, the true horizon, redshift of infalling clocks and the division into regions I-IV.

**Prerequisites:** Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates, invariant hyperbolae

### infinite redshift at the horizon

*phenomenon · developed* · also: frozen star appearance

Signals emitted by an object falling toward r = 2M reach a distant observer with ever-increasing delay and redshift. Only finitely many ticks are emitted before the crossing, so the distant observer sees the clock slow and dim toward a stop and never sees the crossing itself.

**How introduced:** Remark five on the Kruskal diagram: an infaller crosses every t = const line up to t = infinity, which reconciles the finite proper time with the observer's infinite wait.

**Prerequisites:** Kruskal diagram, gravitational redshift

**Notes:** Also applies to short-wavelength gravitational waves falling in.

**Where:** SCH §11.2 p.321 (pdf 339)

### horizon as a null surface

*theorem · developed* · also: horizon generated by null rays

An event horizon is ruled by null geodesics: its generators are the marginal outgoing light rays that neither escape nor fall inward, because the horizon is the boundary between rays that escape and rays that do not.

**How introduced:** Remark six on the Kruskal diagram, then used as a defining property of general horizons in §11.3.

**Prerequisites:** Kruskal diagram, null geodesic

**Where:** SCH §11.2 p.321 (pdf 339); SCH §11.3 p.323 (pdf 341)

### maximally extended Schwarzschild spacetime

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: regions III and IV, white hole and second exterior

The full vacuum Schwarzschild geometry covered by Kruskal coordinates. It contains four regions: our exterior (I), the black-hole interior (II), a second exterior (III) and a past interior (IV). For a black hole formed by stellar collapse, regions III and IV (and parts of I and II) are replaced by the star's interior.

**How introduced:** Remark seven: regions III and IV are labelled but declared beyond scope, with the dashed surface of a collapsing star showing why astrophysicists may ignore them.

**Prerequisites:** Kruskal diagram

### asymptotic flatness

*definition · mention* · also: asymptotically flat spacetime

The property that spacetime approaches Minkowski space far from the sources, which allows 'distant observers', total mass and total angular momentum to be defined from the far field.

**How introduced:** Invoked as a precondition of the event-horizon definition and of the uniqueness theorem, referring back to §8.4.

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian limit of general relativity

### event horizon

*definition · core* · also: black-hole horizon, absolute horizon

In an asymptotically flat spacetime, the three-dimensional boundary between events that can send a signal (by at least one future light ray) to arbitrarily distant observers and events that cannot. It is a null hypersurface ruled by marginally trapped light rays.

**How introduced:** Abstracted from the one-way property of the Schwarzschild horizon; illustrated by collapse (Figure 11.13) where the horizon grows from zero radius before the star's surface reaches 2M.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild horizon, asymptotic flatness, horizon as a null surface

**Where:** SCH §11.3 p.322 (pdf 340); SCH §11.3 p.323 (pdf 341)

### teleological nature of the event horizon

*principle · developed* · also: global nature of horizons

Because the event horizon depends on which light rays escape to infinity eventually, its location at any moment depends on the entire future evolution. For example, a horizon begins expanding before matter that will later fall in has arrived.

**How introduced:** Thought experiment: gas falls into a hole of mass M_0 long after it settles. Rays that seemed to hover at 2M_0 were secretly trapped, and the real horizon had been growing toward 2M_1.

**Prerequisites:** event horizon

**Notes:** This is why simulations, which cannot run forever, need a locally defined substitute.

**Where:** SCH §11.3 p.324 (pdf 342)

### locally trapped surface

*definition · introduced* · also: marginally trapped surface, apparent horizon

A closed two-surface, defined on a single time slice, whose outgoing future light rays are momentarily neither expanding nor contracting in area. Such surfaces can be located without knowing the future and always lie inside (or on) the true event horizon.

**How introduced:** Offered as the practical tool for numerical relativity after the teleological example; linked to Penrose's 1965 theorem.

**Prerequisites:** event horizon

**Notes:** Standard usage distinguishes trapped surfaces (outgoing expansion negative) from marginally trapped surfaces (zero expansion); the book's wording corresponds to the marginal case.

**Where:** SCH §11.3 p.324 (pdf 342); SCH §11.3 p.325 (pdf 343)

### Penrose singularity theorem

*theorem · mention* · also: trapped surface theorem

Penrose's 1965 result that, given a trapped surface and reasonable energy and causality conditions, spacetime contains incomplete null geodesics; together with related results it shows trapped surfaces sit inside event horizons and that black-hole formation does not require symmetry.

**How introduced:** Mentioned in connection with trapped surfaces and Penrose's share of the 2020 Nobel Prize.

**Prerequisites:** locally trapped surface, curvature singularity

**Notes:** The book's summary mixes the theorem's content (singularities) with its consequence for horizons; a tutor should keep them separate.

**Where:** SCH §11.3 p.325 (pdf 343); SCH §11.3 p.327 (pdf 345)

### black hole uniqueness theorem

*theorem · core* · also: no-hair theorem, Kerr uniqueness

Every stationary, asymptotically flat, uncharged vacuum black hole is a Kerr black hole, characterized completely by its mass M and angular momentum J (plus charge Q, and in principle magnetic monopole charge, if electromagnetic fields are allowed).

**How introduced:** Top item in the list of general properties, followed by an extended reflection on how 1e60 particles' history collapses to two numbers and by Chandrasekhar's reaction in §11.4.

**Prerequisites:** Kerr metric, asymptotic flatness

### charged black holes

### black hole stability

### Hawking area theorem

*theorem · core* · also: area theorem, second law of black-hole mechanics

Classically, assuming positive local energy density, the total area of all event horizons never decreases in any process. Consequently, black holes can merge but a hole cannot split in two.

**How introduced:** Item five in the theorems list, related qualitatively to horizon growth during infall; quantified later via the Kerr area formula and turned into the thermodynamic second law in §11.5.

### black hole thermodynamics

*principle · core* · also: laws of black-hole mechanics

The correspondence in which a black hole behaves as a thermodynamic body with temperature T_H = hbar/(8 pi k M) and entropy S = kA/(4 hbar) (geometrized units). Changes in mass obey dE = T_H dS, and the area theorem plays the role of the second law.

### cosmic censorship conjecture

Penrose's conjecture that singularities visible to distant observers (naked singularities) cannot develop from regular initial data in asymptotically flat spacetimes, so singularities stay hidden behind horizons. ### Kerr metric

*solution-or-model · core* · also: Kerr solution, rotating black hole metric

The stationary, axisymmetric vacuum solution describing a rotating black hole of mass M and angular momentum J = aM. In Boyer-Lindquist coordinates its only off-diagonal component is g_t phi, it depends on r and theta through Delta = r^2 - 2Mr + a^2 and rho^2 = r^2 + a^2 cos^2 theta, and it reduces to Schwarzschild when a = 0 (full line element in Eq. 11.71).

**How introduced:** Stated without derivation after the uniqueness theorem as the one black hole astrophysics needs; its features are extracted by the same conserved-quantity methods used for Schwarzschild.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild metric, black hole uniqueness theorem

$$
ds^2 = -\frac{\Delta - a^2\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2}dt^2 - \frac{4Mar\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2}dt\,d\phi + \frac{(r^2+a^2)^2 - a^2\Delta\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2}\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2 + \frac{\rho^2}{\Delta}dr^2 + \rho^2 d\theta^2
$$

$$
\Delta = r^2 - 2Mr + a^2
$$

$$
\rho^2 = r^2 + a^2\cos^2\theta
$$

$$
g_{t\phi} = -2Mar\sin^2\theta/\rho^2
$$

**Notes:** The bibliography notes a hidden third constant of motion (Carter constant) and separability of wave equations.

**Where:** SCH p.328 (pdf 346); SCH p.329 (pdf 347)

### Boyer-Lindquist coordinates

*convention · introduced*

The standard (t, r, theta, phi) coordinates for Kerr, in which t and phi are the symmetry coordinates and r, theta are chosen orthogonal to them and to each other, leaving g_t phi as the only off-diagonal metric component. Surfaces of constant t and r are not metric two-spheres, so r is not an areal radius.

**How introduced:** Discussed right after the Kerr line element, with a general argument about how much freedom stationary axisymmetric metrics leave in r and theta.

### Kerr parameter

The ratio a = J/M, which has units of length in geometrized units. A horizon exists only for a <= M; a/M is the dimensionless spin.

### frame dragging

*phenomenon · core* · also: dragging of inertial frames, omega(r, theta)

The co-rotation imposed by a rotating mass on nearby freely moving matter and inertial frames. In a metric with g_t phi nonzero, a particle with zero angular momentum p_phi still has angular velocity dphi/dt = omega = -g_t phi/g_phi phi, which for Kerr has the sign of a and falls off as r^-3.

**How introduced:** Derived by raising the index of a zero-angular-momentum four-momentum: g^{phi t} mixes in p_t. Given explicitly for Kerr after inverting the t-phi block.

**Prerequisites:** Kerr metric, conserved momentum components from metric symmetries, index raising

$$
\omega = g^{\phi t}/g^{tt} = -g_{t\phi}/g_{\phi\phi}
$$

$$
\omega = \frac{2Mra}{(r^2+a^2)^2 - a^2\Delta\sin^2\theta}
$$

### gravitomagnetism

The set of gravitational effects produced by moving or spinning masses, analogous to magnetism produced by moving charges; frame dragging and gyroscope precession are its main manifestations.

### Lense-Thirring effect

### ergosphere

*definition · core* · also: static limit, stationary limit surface, r_0

The surface where g_tt = 0, which for Kerr is at r_0 = M + (M^2 - a^2 cos^2 theta)^{1/2}. Inside it (the ergoregion) no particle or photon can remain at fixed r, theta, phi or move against the rotation, and conserved energies of some physical orbits can be negative. It lies outside the horizon except at the poles.

**How introduced:** Found by examining photons launched tangentially: at g_tt = 0 one root of dphi/dt is zero, so even light cannot counter-rotate.

**Prerequisites:** frame dragging, Kerr metric

$$
r_0 = M + \sqrt{M^2 - a^2\cos^2\theta}
$$

**Notes:** The book uses 'ergosphere' for the bounding surface and 'ergoregion' for the region; usage in the literature varies. Exercise 11.30 proves the static-limit property.

**Where:** SCH p.330 (pdf 348); SCH p.331 (pdf 349)

### ergoregion

*definition · developed* · also: ergotoroid

A region where g_tt > 0, so the time-translation direction is spacelike and all matter is forced to co-rotate. In Kerr it lies between the horizon and the ergosphere; some very compact, rapidly rotating stars could have toroidal ergoregions without any horizon.

**How introduced:** Generalization after the Kerr ergosphere, citing rotating-star models; later shown to host the Penrose process and a stellar instability.

**Prerequisites:** ergosphere

**Notes:** The name is explained through the Greek root for energy (work).

**Where:** SCH p.331 (pdf 349); SCH p.336 (pdf 354)

### Kerr horizon

*definition · core* · also: outer horizon r_+

The event horizon of a Kerr black hole, at r_+ = M + (M^2 - a^2)^{1/2}, where Delta = 0 and g_rr diverges. Radial turning points for equatorial photons disappear inside it.

**How introduced:** Contrasted with Schwarzschild, where g_tt = 0 and g_rr infinite coincide; in Kerr they separate into ergosphere and horizon. Its horizon status is asserted, with only a partial (equatorial) justification.

### factored effective potentials

*technique · developed* · also: V_+ and V_- for Kerr equatorial photons

For equatorial Kerr photon motion the radial equation contains a cross term in EJ, so it is written as (dr/dlambda)^2 proportional to (E - V_+)(E - V_-). Motion is allowed where E > V_+ or E < V_-, and the two curves meet at the horizon at omega_+ J.

### zero-angular-momentum observer

*definition · developed* · also: ZAMO, locally non-rotating observer

An accelerated observer at fixed r and theta who has zero angular momentum and therefore circulates at the frame-dragging angular velocity omega; four-velocity U = A(1, 0, 0, omega) with A^2 = g_phi phi/(-D). A ZAMO measures a particle's energy as A(E - omega J).

A^2 = g_{\phi\phi}/(-D)

E_{ZAMO} = A(E - \omega J)

### negative-energy orbits

*phenomenon · core* · also: negative conserved energy, forward-in-time condition E > omega J

Future-directed geodesics whose conserved energy E = -p_t (energy at infinity) is negative. A geodesic is physical if a local forward-moving observer measures positive energy, which for a ZAMO requires E > omega J. For counter-rotating orbits inside an ergoregion this allows E < 0.

**How introduced:** Built from the observation that reversing a curve's direction flips every measured energy, so the forward direction must be judged locally; then read off from the counter-rotating potentials (Figure 11.16).

**Prerequisites:** zero-angular-momentum observer, factored effective potentials, ergosphere

E > \omega J
$$

**Notes:** Reused in §11.5 for photons inside the Schwarzschild horizon, where E is spatial.

### Penrose process

*phenomenon · core* · also: energy extraction from a rotating black hole

A mechanism for extracting rotational energy from a Kerr black hole. A body entering the ergoregion splits so that one fragment follows a negative-energy, negative-angular-momentum orbit into the hole while the other escapes with more energy than the original body. The hole's mass and spin both decrease.

**How introduced:** Presented as a thought experiment (Penrose 1969) following the discovery of negative-energy orbits in the ergoregion.

**Prerequisites:** negative-energy orbits, ergoregion

**Notes:** Exercise 11.35 proves the angular-momentum decrease. The book states it works for any ergoregion, not only Kerr.

**Where:** SCH p.335 (pdf 353); SCH p.336 (pdf 354)

### Blandford-Znajek mechanism

### intermediate-mass black hole

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: IMBH

A black hole in the range of roughly 1e2-1e4 solar masses, between stellar and supermassive holes. Candidates include some bright X-ray sources, globular-cluster centres such as Omega Centauri, and the ~150 solar-mass remnant of a LIGO-Virgo merger.

**How introduced:** Short survey, with possible origins in Population III stars and prospects for LISA.

**Prerequisites:** supermassive black hole

**Where:** SCH §11.4 p.343 (pdf 361)

### quasi-normal modes

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: black-hole ringdown, QNMs

Damped characteristic oscillations of a perturbed black hole, radiated as gravitational waves. Their frequencies and damping times depend only on M and a, and they decay within a few cycles, much faster than neutron-star modes.

**How introduced:** In the discussion of dynamical black holes: formation or merger leaves asymmetries that ring down to Kerr, and matching the Kerr spectrum would confirm the remnant's identity.

**Prerequisites:** Kerr metric, black hole stability

**Where:** SCH §11.4 p.343 (pdf 361)

### numerical relativity

*technique · mention*

The solution of Einstein's equations by computer, evolving the metric as a dynamical field on a grid. It became able to simulate binary black-hole orbits and mergers stably in 2005-2006 and now supplies waveforms for gravitational-wave searches.

**How introduced:** Historical survey of challenges (horizons crossing grids, coordinate and curvature singularities) and breakthroughs, plus the Mosaic-browser anecdote in the bibliography.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein field equations

**Where:** SCH §11.4 p.343 (pdf 361); SCH §11.4 p.344 (pdf 362); SCH §11.6 p.351 (pdf 369)

### gravitational recoil kick

*phenomenon · mention* · also: merger kick, black-hole recoil

The recoil velocity of a merged black hole caused by asymmetric emission of gravitational-wave linear momentum. Because velocity carries no mass scale, it depends only on the mass ratio and the spin magnitudes and orientations; typical values are hundreds of km/s.

**How introduced:** Highlighted as a striking outcome of merger simulations, able to eject holes from clusters or galaxies.

**Prerequisites:** numerical relativity

**Notes:** The book's claim of kicks exceeding 10% of c appears overstated; simulations give maxima of roughly 5000 km/s (about 1.7% of c).

**Where:** SCH §11.4 p.344 (pdf 362); SCH §11.4 p.342 (pdf 360)

### vacuum fluctuations

*principle · introduced* · also: virtual pairs, energy-time uncertainty

In quantum field theory the vacuum continually produces short-lived virtual particle pairs that borrow an energy Delta E for a time of order hbar/(2 Delta E). Energy conservation is violated only on these short timescales.

**How introduced:** The single quantum ingredient of the heuristic Hawking argument, applied near a horizon where spacetime is locally flat.

**Prerequisites:** Heisenberg uncertainty principle

$$
\Delta E\,\Delta t \ge \hbar/2
$$

**Notes:** A pedagogical picture, not a rigorous description of QFT in curved spacetime.

**Where:** SCH §11.5 p.345 (pdf 363); SCH §11.5 p.346 (pdf 364)

### Hawking radiation

*phenomenon · core* · also: Hawking effect, black-hole evaporation radiation

The quantum emission of thermal radiation by a black hole. Near the horizon, vacuum-fluctuation pairs can separate: a negative-energy partner crosses the horizon, where negative E is an allowed spatial momentum, and a positive-energy quantum escapes. The result is a black-body spectrum at T_H = hbar/(8 pi k M) and a decrease of the hole's mass.

**How introduced:** Plausibility argument: the negative-energy member must reach the horizon within the fluctuation time; equating that time to a freely falling frame's infall proper time gives an escaping energy hbar/8M, which is then compared with Hawking's exact thermal result.

**Prerequisites:** vacuum fluctuations, negative-energy orbits, interchange of timelike and spacelike coordinates inside the horizon, proper time of radial infall, gravitational redshift

$$
E \approx \hbar/8M
$$

**Notes:** The book contrasts it with the Penrose process: Hawking's negative-energy quanta are inside the horizon, not in the ergoregion, so there is no runaway.

**Where:** SCH §11.5 p.345 (pdf 363); SCH §11.5 p.346 (pdf 364); SCH §11.5 p.347 (pdf 365); SCH §11.5 p.348 (pdf 366)

### Hawking temperature

*physical-quantity · core* · also: T_H

The temperature of the thermal radiation from a Schwarzschild black hole, T_H = hbar/(8 pi k_B M) in geometrized units (hbar c^3/(8 pi G M k_B) in SI), about 6e-8 K for one solar mass. It is inversely proportional to mass.

**How introduced:** Quoted from Hawking's full calculation after the heuristic estimate; the mean black-body photon energy 2.701 kT is 0.860 times the heuristic hbar/8M.

**Prerequisites:** Hawking radiation

$$
T_H = \hbar/(8\pi k_B M)
$$

**Where:** SCH §11.5 p.347 (pdf 365); SCH §11.5 p.349 (pdf 367)

### black hole evaporation

*phenomenon · developed* · also: evaporation lifetime, tau ~ M^3

The gradual loss of a black hole's mass to Hawking radiation. Since luminosity scales as A T^4 ~ M^2 M^-4 = M^-2, dM/dt ~ -M^-2 and the lifetime scales as M^3; smaller holes are hotter and die faster, ending in a burst of gamma rays.

**How introduced:** Scaling argument from the Stefan-Boltzmann law with the horizon as emitting surface, calibrated to 1e12 kg lasting about 1e10 yr.

**Prerequisites:** Hawking temperature, Kerr horizon area

$$
dM/dt \sim -M^{-2}
$$

$$
\tau \sim M^3
$$

$$
\tau/10^{10}\,\mathrm{yr} = (M/10^{12}\,\mathrm{kg})^3
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 11.43 asks for a derivation of the calibration and for the mass below which e+e- pairs are emitted copiously.

**Where:** SCH §11.5 p.348 (pdf 366); SCH §11.5 p.349 (pdf 367)

### primordial black holes

*solution-or-model · mention*

Hypothetical black holes formed in the very early Universe, not from stars, possibly with small masses such as 1e12 kg, so that they would be evaporating today.

**How introduced:** Used to illustrate the observable final-second gamma-ray burst of evaporation; no such events have been seen. Also mentioned as possible MACHOs.

**Prerequisites:** black hole evaporation

**Where:** SCH §11.5 p.349 (pdf 367); SCH p.314 (pdf 332)

### Planck mass

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: M_Pl

The mass built from hbar, G and c alone, (hbar c/G)^{1/2} = 2.2e-8 kg, equal to hbar^{1/2} = 1.6e-35 m in geometrized units. It is the scale at which a Hawking quantum's energy is comparable to the hole's mass, so the fixed-background approximation fails and quantum gravity is needed.

**How introduced:** Stated as the validity limit of all Hawking derivations (E << M).

**Prerequisites:** Hawking radiation, geometrized units

$$
M_{Pl} = \hbar^{1/2} \approx 1.6\times10^{-35}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 2.2\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{kg}
$$

**Notes:** Printed with h instead of hbar.

**Where:** SCH §11.5 p.349 (pdf 367)

### Bekenstein-Hawking entropy

*physical-quantity · core* · also: black-hole entropy, S = kA/4 hbar

The entropy assigned to a black hole, S = k A/(4 hbar) in geometrized units (k c^3 A/(4 G hbar) in SI), proportional to horizon area. It makes dM = T_H dS hold and turns the area theorem into a second law; its enormous value reflects the information hidden by the horizon.

**How introduced:** Obtained by rearranging dM = dA/(32 pi M) so that the prefactor is the Hawking temperature; first mentioned in §11.3 as explaining the information loss.

**Prerequisites:** black hole thermodynamics, Hawking temperature, Hawking area theorem

$$
S = kA/4\hbar
$$

**Where:** SCH §11.5 p.349 (pdf 367); SCH §11.5 p.350 (pdf 368); SCH p.328 (pdf 346)

### black hole information problem

*principle · mention* · also: information paradox

The open question of whether, and how, information about matter that formed or fell into a black hole is carried away by Hawking radiation. Perfectly thermal radiation would carry almost none, but subtle correlations might.

**How introduced:** Raised at the end of §11.5 as a question the Hawking effect provokes, with a suggestion that correlations could originate in near-horizon fluctuations affected by the collapsing matter.

**Prerequisites:** Hawking radiation, Bekenstein-Hawking entropy

**Where:** SCH §11.5 p.350 (pdf 368)

### Carter constant

*physical-quantity · mention* · also: third constant of Kerr geodesic motion, separability in Kerr

A conserved quantity for Kerr geodesics associated with theta motion, not following from an obvious symmetry. Together with energy and angular momentum (and the mass) it makes Kerr orbits integrable; wave equations on Kerr likewise separate (Teukolsky).

**How introduced:** Mentioned only in the bibliography, as a surprising feature given Kerr's reduced symmetry.

**Prerequisites:** Kerr metric, conserved momentum components from metric symmetries

**Notes:** Not named in the book.

**Where:** SCH §11.6 p.351 (pdf 369)

### Painleve-Gullstrand coordinates

*convention · introduced* · also: rain coordinates, river model coordinates

Schwarzschild coordinates using the proper time of observers falling from rest at infinity: T = t - f(r) with df/dr = -(2M/r)^{1/2}/(1 - 2M/r). The metric becomes -(1 - 2M/r)dT^2 + 2(2M/r)^{1/2}dT dr + dr^2 + r^2 dOmega^2, regular at 2M and with flat spatial slices.

**How introduced:** Exercise 11.23, historically the first horizon-regular form (1921-22), with a light-cone sketch showing finite crossing time and a null horizon.

**Prerequisites:** coordinate singularity, light cones in Schwarzschild coordinates

$$
ds^2 = -(1-2M/r)dT^2 + 2\sqrt{2M/r}\,dT\,dr + dr^2 + r^2 d\Omega^2
$$

**Where:** SCH p.356 (pdf 374); SCH p.357 (pdf 375)

### Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates

*convention · introduced* · also: ingoing Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates, advanced time v

Coordinates (v, r, theta, phi) with v = t + r*, where r* is the tortoise coordinate. Ingoing radial light rays are v = const, and the metric -(1 - 2M/r)dv^2 + 2 dv dr + r^2 dOmega^2 is regular across the future horizon.

**How introduced:** Exercise 11.24, including a T* = v - r diagram to visualize tilted light cones.

**Prerequisites:** tortoise coordinate, coordinate singularity

$$
v = t + r^*
$$

$$
ds^2 = -(1-2M/r)dv^2 + 2\,dv\,dr + r^2 d\Omega^2
$$

**Notes:** Printed Eq. 11.127 contains a spurious + dr^2 term.

**Where:** SCH p.357 (pdf 375)

### tortoise coordinate

*convention · introduced* · also: r*, Regge-Wheeler coordinate

The radial coordinate r* defined by dr*/dr = (1 - 2M/r)^{-1}, i.e. r* = r + 2M ln|r/2M - 1|, which pushes r = 2M to r* = -infinity and makes radial null lines t +/- r* = const.

**How introduced:** Defined in Exercise 11.24 as the step toward Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates, with a remark that it recalls Kruskal's construction.

**Prerequisites:** light cones in Schwarzschild coordinates

$$
dr^*/dr = (1-2M/r)^{-1}
$$

**Where:** SCH p.357 (pdf 375)

### tidal disruption radius

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: tidal radius R_T

The distance from a black hole at which its tidal acceleration across a star (or a body) exceeds the self-gravity or strength holding it together, so it is torn apart. For a star of density rho around a hole of mass M_H it scales as R_T ~ (M_H/rho)^{1/3}, so large holes swallow stars whole.

**How introduced:** Exercises 11.25 and 11.28 use geodesic deviation to estimate survival near the horizon and the hole mass for which R_T = 100 M_H.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic deviation, Riemann tensor

$$
R_T \sim (M_H/\rho)^{1/3}
$$

**Notes:** The scaling is our own summary of what the exercise asks for.

**Where:** SCH p.357 (pdf 375); SCH p.358 (pdf 376)

## Key equations

### (11.1) Schwarzschild line element · central · SCH §11.1 p.296 (pdf 314)

$$
ds^2 = -\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)dt^2 + \left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}dr^2 + r^2 d\Omega^2
$$

Vacuum exterior of a spherical mass, and the geometry of a non-rotating black hole; the starting point of the chapter.

**Symbols:** M: mass in geometrized units; r: areal radius; dOmega^2 = dtheta^2 + sin^2 theta dphi^2.

### (11.2) Newtonian marginal escape · derivation-step · SCH §11.1 p.297 (pdf 315)

$$
\tfrac12 v^2 = \frac{GM}{R}
$$

Kinetic energy per unit mass that just balances gravitational binding at radius R.

**Symbols:** v: launch speed; R: stellar radius.

### (11.3) Dark-star radius · supporting · SCH §11.1 p.297 (pdf 315)

$$
R = \frac{2GM}{c^2}
$$

Radius at which the Newtonian escape speed equals c; numerically the same as the GR horizon radius.

### (11.4) Mean density of a dark star · supporting · SCH §11.1 p.297 (pdf 315)

$$
\bar\rho = \frac{M}{\tfrac43\pi R^3} = \frac{3c^6}{32\pi G^3 M^2}
$$

Required density falls as M^-2, so supermassive holes form at low density (below water for 1e9 solar masses).

### (11.5) Conserved energy · central · SCH §11.1 p.298 (pdf 316)

$$
\text{particle: } \tilde E := -p_0/m; \qquad \text{photon: } E = -p_0
$$

Energy at infinity conserved because the metric is static.

**Symbols:** p_0: covariant time component of four-momentum; m: rest mass.

### (11.6) Conserved angular momentum · central · SCH §11.1 p.298 (pdf 316)

$$
\text{particle: } \tilde J := p_\phi/m; \qquad \text{photon: } J = p_\phi
$$

Angular momentum conserved because the metric does not depend on phi.

### (11.7) Contravariant momentum of a particle · derivation-step · SCH §11.1 p.298 (pdf 316)

$$
p^0 = m\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}\tilde E,\quad p^r = m\frac{dr}{d\tau},\quad p^\phi = \frac{m\tilde J}{r^2}
$$

Raised components in the equatorial plane expressed through the constants of motion.

**Symbols:** tau: proper time.

### (11.8) Contravariant momentum of a photon · derivation-step · SCH §11.1 p.299 (pdf 317)

$$
p^0 = \left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}E,\quad p^r = \frac{dr}{d\lambda},\quad p^\phi = \frac{d\phi}{d\lambda} = \frac{J}{r^2}
$$

Same for photons; the p^r relation fixes the affine parameter lambda.

**Symbols:** lambda: affine parameter.

### (11.9) Particle normalization · derivation-step · SCH §11.1 p.299 (pdf 317)

$$
-m^2\tilde E^2\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1} + m^2\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\right)^2 + \frac{m^2\tilde J^2}{r^2} = -m^2
$$

p.p = -m^2 written out in terms of E-tilde, J-tilde and dr/dtau.

### (11.10) Photon normalization · derivation-step · SCH §11.1 p.299 (pdf 317)

$$
-E^2\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1} + \left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{dr}{d\lambda}\right)^2 + \frac{J^2}{r^2} = 0
$$

Null condition p.p = 0 written out.

### (11.11) Radial equation for particles · central · SCH §11.1 p.299 (pdf 317)

$$
\left(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\right)^2 = \tilde E^2 - \left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\left(1+\frac{\tilde J^2}{r^2}\right)
$$

One-dimensional energy equation for timelike Schwarzschild geodesics.

### (11.12) Radial equation for photons · central · SCH §11.1 p.299 (pdf 317)

$$
\left(\frac{dr}{d\lambda}\right)^2 = E^2 - \left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\frac{J^2}{r^2}
$$

One-dimensional energy equation for null geodesics.

### (11.13) Particle effective potential · central · SCH §11.1 p.299 (pdf 317)

$$
\tilde V^2(r) = \left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\left(1+\frac{\tilde J^2}{r^2}\right)
$$

Squared potential bounding the radial motion of massive particles; expanded, it contains -2M/r, J^2/r^2 and the relativistic -2MJ^2/r^3 term.

### (11.14) Photon effective potential · central · SCH §11.1 p.299 (pdf 317)

$$
V^2(r) = \left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\frac{J^2}{r^2}
$$

Squared potential for light; a single peak at 3M.

### (11.15) Radial acceleration of particles · supporting · SCH §11.1 p.301 (pdf 319)

$$
\frac{d^2r}{d\tau^2} = -\frac12\frac{d}{dr}\tilde V^2(r)
$$

Relativistic analogue of ma = -grad phi; circular orbits sit at extrema, stable at minima.

### (11.16) Radial acceleration of photons · derivation-step · SCH §11.1 p.301 (pdf 319)

$$
\frac{d^2r}{d\lambda^2} = -\frac12\frac{d}{dr}V^2(r)
$$

Same for light.

### (11.17) Radii of circular particle orbits · central · SCH §11.1 p.301 (pdf 319)

$$
r = \frac{\tilde J^2}{2M}\left[1 \pm \left(1 - \frac{12M^2}{\tilde J^2}\right)^{1/2}\right]
$$

Outer root is the stable orbit and inner root the unstable one; both exist only for J^2 > 12M^2.

### (11.18) Photon circular orbit · central · SCH §11.1 p.301 (pdf 319)

$$
r = 3M
$$

Unique unstable circular light orbit, independent of E and J.

### (11.19) Innermost stable circular orbit · central · SCH §11.1 p.301 (pdf 319)

$$
r_{\min} = 6M
$$

No stable circular orbit of a massive particle exists inside 6M.

### (11.20) Angular momentum of a circular orbit · supporting · SCH p.302 (pdf 320)

$$
\tilde J^2 = \frac{Mr}{1 - 3M/r}
$$

Specific angular momentum needed for a circular orbit at r; diverges at the photon orbit 3M.

### (11.21) Energy of a circular orbit · supporting · SCH p.302 (pdf 320)

$$
\tilde E^2 = \frac{(1 - 2M/r)^2}{1 - 3M/r}
$$

Specific energy of a circular orbit; at 6M it gives E-tilde = (8/9)^{1/2}, i.e. about 5.7% binding.

### (11.22) Angular velocity per proper time · derivation-step · SCH p.302 (pdf 320)

$$
\frac{d\phi}{d\tau} = U^\phi = g^{\phi\phi}\tilde J = \frac{\tilde J}{r^2}
$$

Azimuthal rate from the conserved angular momentum.

### (11.23) Time dilation factor on a geodesic · derivation-step · SCH p.303 (pdf 321)

$$
\frac{dt}{d\tau} = U^0 = g^{00}(-\tilde E) = \frac{\tilde E}{1 - 2M/r}
$$

Rate of coordinate time per proper time along the orbit.

### (11.24) Circular-orbit angular velocity · supporting · SCH p.303 (pdf 321)

$$
\frac{dt}{d\phi} = \left(\frac{r^3}{M}\right)^{1/2}
$$

Coordinate angular velocity (M/r^3)^{1/2}, identical to Newton's.

### (11.25) Orbital period · supporting · SCH p.303 (pdf 321)

$$
P = 2\pi\left(\frac{r^3}{M}\right)^{1/2}
$$

Period of a circular orbit in coordinate time, as measured from far away.

### (11.26) Orbit shape equation · derivation-step · SCH p.305 (pdf 323)

$$
\left(\frac{dr}{d\phi}\right)^2 = \frac{\tilde E^2 - (1-2M/r)(1+\tilde J^2/r^2)}{\tilde J^2/r^4}
$$

Radial equation divided by (dphi/dtau)^2 gives the shape r(phi).

### (11.28) Orbit equation in u = 1/r · supporting · SCH p.305 (pdf 323)

$$
\left(\frac{du}{d\phi}\right)^2 = \frac{\tilde E^2}{\tilde J^2} - (1 - 2Mu)\left(\frac{1}{\tilde J^2} + u^2\right)
$$

A cubic polynomial in u; the 2Mu^3 term is the relativistic correction.

**Symbols:** u := 1/r (Eq. 11.27).

### (11.29) Newtonian orbit equation · derivation-step · SCH p.305 (pdf 323)

$$
\left(\frac{du}{d\phi}\right)^2 = \frac{\tilde E^2}{\tilde J^2} - \frac{1}{\tilde J^2}(1 - 2Mu) - u^2
$$

Eq. 11.28 without the u^3 term.

### (11.31) Newtonian oscillator form · derivation-step · SCH p.305 (pdf 323)

$$
\left(\frac{dy}{d\phi}\right)^2 = \frac{\tilde E^2 - 1}{\tilde J^2} + \frac{M^2}{\tilde J^4} - y^2
$$

With y = u - M/J^2 (Eq. 11.30) the Newtonian orbit is a harmonic oscillator of unit frequency in phi.

**Symbols:** y: deviation of 1/r from the Newtonian circular value.

### (11.33) Newtonian ellipse · supporting · SCH p.305 (pdf 323)

$$
\frac{1}{r} = \frac{M}{\tilde J^2} + \left[\frac{\tilde E^2 + M^2/\tilde J^2 - 1}{\tilde J^2}\right]^{1/2}\cos(\phi + B)
$$

Conic-section orbit; closed because the period in phi is exactly 2 pi (y is given by Eq. 11.32).

**Symbols:** B: orientation constant.

### (11.34) Relativistic near-circular orbit equation · derivation-step · SCH p.305 (pdf 323)

$$
\left(\frac{dy}{d\phi}\right)^2 = \frac{\tilde E^2 + M^2/\tilde J^2 - 1}{\tilde J^2} + \frac{2M^4}{\tilde J^6} + \frac{6M^3}{\tilde J^4}y - \left(1 - \frac{6M^2}{\tilde J^2}\right)y^2
$$

Keeping the u^3 term and dropping y^3 gives an oscillator with shifted centre and reduced frequency. Printed as 6M^3 y/J^2, a misprint (dimensionally inconsistent); the coefficient given here, 6M^3/J^4, follows from expanding 2Mu^3 with u = y + M/J^2.

### (11.35)-(11.36) Precessing orbit solution · supporting · SCH p.306 (pdf 324)

$$
y = y_0 + A\cos(k\phi + B),\quad k = \left(1 - \frac{6M^2}{\tilde J^2}\right)^{1/2},\quad y_0 = \frac{3M^3}{k^2\tilde J^4}
$$

Radial oscillation with angular frequency k < 1 about a shifted mean; A is fixed by the constant terms. The page shows y_0 = 3M^3/(k^2 J^2); the corrected J^4 is used here, consistent with Eq. 11.34.

**Symbols:** A = k^{-1}[(E^2 + M^2/J^2 - 1)/J^2 + 2M^4/J^6 - y_0^2]^{1/2}... (see note on its printed form).

### (11.37) Azimuth between successive perihelia · central · SCH p.306 (pdf 324)

$$
\Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi}{k} = 2\pi\left(1 - \frac{6M^2}{\tilde J^2}\right)^{-1/2}
$$

Orbit returns to the same radius only after more than a full turn.

### (11.39) Perihelion advance per orbit · central · SCH p.306 (pdf 324)

$$
\delta\phi = 6\pi M^2/\tilde J^2
$$

Leading-order advance for nearly Newtonian orbits (from expanding Eq. 11.37 into Eq. 11.38).

### (11.40) Perihelion advance in terms of radius · central · SCH p.306 (pdf 324)

$$
\delta\phi \approx 6\pi\frac{M}{r}
$$

Using J^2 ~ Mr; in SI, 6 pi GM/(c^2 r) per orbit (for eccentric orbits r is replaced by the semi-latus rectum a(1-e^2)).

### (11.41)-(11.42) Mercury's relativistic perihelion shift · supporting · SCH p.306 (pdf 324)

$$
(\delta\phi)_{\rm Mercury} = 4.99\times10^{-7}\ \text{rad/orbit} = 0.43''/\text{yr} = 43''/\text{century}
$$

The classic anomalous precession explained by GR in 1915.

**Symbols:** r = 5.55e7 km, M_sun = 1.47 km, period 0.24 yr.

### (11.43) Weak-field Newtonian metric · supporting · SCH p.308 (pdf 326)

$$
ds^2 = -(1 + 2\phi)dt^2 + (1 - 2\phi)(dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2)
$$

Spacetime metric of a Newtonian source to first order in the potential (from §8.4).

**Symbols:** phi: Newtonian potential, -M/r outside a sphere.

### (11.44) Weak-field Schwarzschild · supporting · SCH p.308 (pdf 326)

$$
ds^2 \approx -\left(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\right)dt^2 + \left(1 + \frac{2M}{r}\right)dr^2 + r^2 d\Omega^2
$$

First order in M/r; g_00 and g_rr deviate from flat by equal amounts.

### (11.45)-(11.46) Isotropic form and radial shift · supporting · SCH p.308 (pdf 326)

$$
d\ell^2 = \left(1 + \frac{2M}{\bar r}\right)(d\bar r^2 + \bar r^2 d\Omega^2), \qquad \bar r = r - M
$$

Relabelling the radius makes the Schwarzschild spatial metric conformally flat to first order, matching Eq. 11.43.

**Symbols:** r-bar: isotropic radius.

### (11.47) Photon orbit equation · supporting · SCH p.309 (pdf 327)

$$
\frac{d\phi}{dr} = \pm\frac{1}{r^2}\left[\frac{1}{b^2} - \frac{1}{r^2}\left(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\right)\right]^{-1/2}
$$

Shape of a light ray, depending only on b.

**Symbols:** b := J/E (Eq. 11.48).

### (11.49) Photon orbit in u · derivation-step · SCH p.309 (pdf 327)

$$
\frac{d\phi}{du} = \left(\frac{1}{b^2} - u^2 + 2Mu^3\right)^{-1/2}
$$

Incoming ray with J > 0; the 2Mu^3 term produces bending.

### (11.51) Undeflected straight line · derivation-step · SCH p.310 (pdf 328)

$$
r\sin(\phi - \phi_0) = b
$$

Solution with M = 0: a straight line at distance b from the origin.

### (11.52)-(11.53) Substitution removing the cubic term · derivation-step · SCH p.310 (pdf 328)

$$
y := u(1 - Mu),\qquad \frac{d\phi}{dy} = \frac{1 + 2My}{(b^{-2} - y^2)^{1/2}} + O(M^2u^2)
$$

To first order in M the orbit integral becomes elementary.

### (11.54) Integrated ray · derivation-step · SCH p.310 (pdf 328)

$$
\phi = \phi_0 + \frac{2M}{b} + \arcsin(by) - 2M\left(\frac{1}{b^2} - y^2\right)^{1/2}
$$

Azimuth along the ray; closest approach at y = 1/b.

### (11.55) Light deflection angle · central · SCH p.310 (pdf 328)

$$
\Delta\phi = \frac{4M}{b}
$$

Total bending of a ray passing a mass M at impact parameter b; twice the Newtonian corpuscular value.

### (11.56) Solar limb deflection · supporting · SCH p.311 (pdf 329)

$$
(\Delta\phi)_{\odot,\max} = 8.45\times10^{-6}\ \text{rad} = 1.74''
$$

Maximum deflection by the Sun, with b = R_sun = 6.96e5 km.

### (11.57) Jupiter limb deflection · supporting · SCH p.311 (pdf 329)

$$
(\Delta\phi)_{\rm Jupiter,\max} = 6.42\times10^{-8}\ \text{rad} = 0.013''
$$

Small but measured by Hipparcos and routinely corrected by Gaia.

**Symbols:** M_J = 1.12e-3 km, R_J = 6.98e4 km.

### (11.58)-(11.59) Radial infall · supporting · SCH §11.2 p.316 (pdf 334)

$$
\left(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\right)^2 = \tilde E^2 - 1 + \frac{2M}{r},\qquad d\tau = -\frac{dr}{(\tilde E^2 - 1 + 2M/r)^{1/2}}
$$

Radial geodesic with J = 0; the integrand is finite at r = 2M.

### (11.60) Proper infall time from rest at infinity · central · SCH §11.2 p.316 (pdf 334)

$$
\Delta\tau = \frac{4M}{3}\left[\left(\frac{r}{2M}\right)^{3/2}\right]_{2M}^{R}
$$

Finite proper time to fall from R to the horizon for E-tilde = 1.

### (11.61) Coordinate time along infall · supporting · SCH §11.2 p.317 (pdf 335)

$$
dt = \frac{\tilde E\,d\tau}{1 - 2M/r} = -\frac{\tilde E\,dr}{(1 - 2M/r)(\tilde E^2 - 1 + 2M/r)^{1/2}}
$$

Coordinate time picks up a divergent factor near the horizon.

### (11.62) Log divergence of coordinate time · central · SCH §11.2 p.317 (pdf 335)

$$
dt = -\frac{(\varepsilon + 2M)^{3/2}\,d\varepsilon}{(2M)^{1/2}\,\varepsilon},\qquad \varepsilon := r - 2M
$$

Integrates to about -2M ln epsilon, so t -> infinity as the horizon is approached.

### (11.63) Metric just inside the horizon · central · SCH §11.2 p.317 (pdf 335)

$$
ds^2 = \frac{\varepsilon}{2M - \varepsilon}dt^2 - \frac{2M - \varepsilon}{\varepsilon}d\varepsilon^2 + (2M - \varepsilon)^2 d\Omega^2,\qquad \varepsilon := 2M - r
$$

Signs show t is spacelike and r (epsilon) timelike inside.

### (11.64) Radial null lines in Schwarzschild coordinates · supporting · SCH p.318 (pdf 336)

$$
\frac{dt}{dr} = \pm\left(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}
$$

Light-cone slopes in a t-r diagram, tending to infinity at 2M. The printed equation lacks the inverse power (verified on the page render), but the prose, which says the slope diverges at 2M, requires it.

### (11.65) Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates outside · central · SCH §11.2 p.319 (pdf 337)

$$
u = \left(\frac{r}{2M} - 1\right)^{1/2}e^{r/4M}\cosh\frac{t}{4M},\qquad v = \left(\frac{r}{2M} - 1\right)^{1/2}e^{r/4M}\sinh\frac{t}{4M}\quad (r > 2M)
$$

Coordinates regular across the horizon, defined here for the exterior.

### (11.66) Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates inside · supporting · SCH §11.2 p.319 (pdf 337)

$$
u = \left(1 - \frac{r}{2M}\right)^{1/2}e^{r/4M}\sinh\frac{t}{4M},\qquad v = \left(1 - \frac{r}{2M}\right)^{1/2}e^{r/4M}\cosh\frac{t}{4M}\quad (r < 2M)
$$

Interior patch; hyperbolic functions swap roles because t is spacelike there.

### (11.67) Schwarzschild metric in Kruskal coordinates · central · SCH §11.2 p.320 (pdf 338)

$$
ds^2 = -\frac{32M^3}{r}e^{-r/2M}(dv^2 - du^2) + r^2 d\Omega^2
$$

No singularity at r = 2M; only r = 0 is singular; the (u, v) part is conformally flat.

### (11.68) Implicit radius · central · SCH §11.2 p.320 (pdf 338)

$$
\left(\frac{r}{2M} - 1\right)e^{r/2M} = u^2 - v^2
$$

Curves of constant r are hyperbolae; r = 2M is u = +/- v, and r = 0 is v^2 - u^2 = 1.

### (11.69) Radial null lines in Kruskal coordinates · central · SCH §11.2 p.320 (pdf 338)

$$
dv = \pm du
$$

Light cones are at 45 degrees everywhere, as in special relativity.

### (11.70) Kerr spin parameter · supporting · SCH p.328 (pdf 346)

$$
a := J/M
$$

Angular momentum per unit mass, a length in geometrized units.

### (11.71) Kerr metric (Boyer-Lindquist) · central · SCH p.328 (pdf 346)

$$
ds^2 = -\frac{\Delta - a^2\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2}dt^2 - 2a\frac{2Mr\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2}dt\,d\phi + \frac{(r^2 + a^2)^2 - a^2\Delta\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2}\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2 + \frac{\rho^2}{\Delta}dr^2 + \rho^2 d\theta^2
$$

Unique stationary vacuum black hole with mass M and spin J = aM.

**Symbols:** Delta and rho^2 defined in Eq. 11.72.

### (11.72) Kerr auxiliary functions · supporting · SCH p.328 (pdf 346)

$$
\Delta := r^2 - 2Mr + a^2,\qquad \rho^2 := r^2 + a^2\cos^2\theta
$$

Delta = 0 locates the horizons; rho^2 = 0 is the ring singularity (not discussed).

### (11.73) Off-diagonal Kerr component · supporting · SCH p.329 (pdf 347)

$$
g_{t\phi} = -a\frac{2Mr\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2}
$$

Half the dt dphi coefficient; the source of frame dragging.

### (11.74)-(11.75) Raised momenta with a cross term · derivation-step · SCH p.329 (pdf 347)

$$
p^\phi = g^{\phi\phi}p_\phi + g^{\phi t}p_t,\qquad p^t = g^{tt}p_t + g^{t\phi}p_\phi
$$

Conserved p_t and p_phi mix when raised, so p_phi = 0 still gives p^phi nonzero.

### (11.77) Frame-dragging angular velocity · central · SCH p.329 (pdf 347)

$$
\frac{d\phi}{dt} = \frac{p^\phi}{p^t} = \frac{g^{\phi t}}{g^{tt}} := \omega(r, \theta)
$$

Angular velocity acquired by a particle with zero angular momentum.

### (11.78) Tangential photon angular velocities · supporting · SCH p.330 (pdf 348)

$$
\frac{d\phi}{dt} = -\frac{g_{t\phi}}{g_{\phi\phi}} \pm \left[\left(\frac{g_{t\phi}}{g_{\phi\phi}}\right)^2 - \frac{g_{tt}}{g_{\phi\phi}}\right]^{1/2}
$$

Two roots for light sent along +/- phi at fixed r; their average is omega.

### (11.79) Photon motion at the static limit · supporting · SCH p.330 (pdf 348)

$$
g_{tt} = 0 \Rightarrow \frac{d\phi}{dt} = 0 \ \text{or}\ \frac{d\phi}{dt} = -\frac{2g_{t\phi}}{g_{\phi\phi}}
$$

The backward photon cannot move against the rotation at all. The page prints the second root without the minus sign; the minus follows from Eq. 11.78, and the root equals 2 omega, so it has the sign of a.

### (11.80) Ergosphere radius · central · SCH p.331 (pdf 349)

$$
r_0 := r_{\rm ergosphere} = M + \sqrt{M^2 - a^2\cos^2\theta}
$$

Where g_tt = 0; inside, everything must co-rotate.

### (11.81) Kerr horizon radius · central · SCH p.331 (pdf 349)

$$
r_+ := r_{\rm horizon} = M + \sqrt{M^2 - a^2}
$$

Where Delta = 0; touches the ergosphere at the poles.

### (11.82) Induced metric on constant t, r · derivation-step · SCH p.331 (pdf 349)

$$
dl^2 = \frac{(r^2 + a^2)^2 - a^2\Delta}{\rho^2}\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2 + \rho^2 d\theta^2
$$

Two-geometry of a Boyer-Lindquist r = const surface at fixed time (the export shows dt^2 on the left; the printed page has dl^2).

### (11.84)-(11.85) Kerr surface and horizon area · central · SCH p.332 (pdf 350)

$$
A(r) = 4\pi\sqrt{(r^2 + a^2)^2 - a^2\Delta},\qquad A_{\rm horizon} = 4\pi(r_+^2 + a^2)
$$

Area from the determinant of the induced metric (Eq. 11.83); on the horizon Delta = 0.

### (11.86)-(11.87) Inverse of the stationary axisymmetric metric · derivation-step · SCH p.332 (pdf 350)

$$
g^{rr} = \Delta\rho^{-2},\quad g^{\theta\theta} = \rho^{-2},\quad \begin{pmatrix} g^{tt} & g^{t\phi} \\ g^{t\phi} & g^{\phi\phi}\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{D}\begin{pmatrix} g_{\phi\phi} & -g_{t\phi} \\ -g_{t\phi} & g_{tt}\end{pmatrix},\quad D = g_{tt}g_{\phi\phi} - g_{t\phi}^2
$$

Only the t-phi block needs a genuine inversion.

### (11.88) omega from covariant components · supporting · SCH p.332 (pdf 350)

$$
\omega = \frac{g^{\phi t}}{g^{tt}} = -\frac{g_{t\phi}}{g_{\phi\phi}}
$$

Frame-dragging rate is the midpoint of the two tangential photon rates.

### (11.89) Kerr inverse components · derivation-step · SCH p.332 (pdf 350)

$$
D = -\Delta\sin^2\theta,\quad g^{tt} = -\frac{(r^2+a^2)^2 - a^2\Delta\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2\Delta},\quad g^{t\phi} = -a\frac{2Mr}{\rho^2\Delta},\quad g^{\phi\phi} = \frac{\Delta - a^2\sin^2\theta}{\rho^2\Delta\sin^2\theta}
$$

Needed for momenta and potentials in Kerr.

### (11.90) Kerr frame-dragging rate · central · SCH p.333 (pdf 351)

$$
\omega = \frac{2Mra}{(r^2 + a^2)^2 - a^2\Delta\sin^2\theta}
$$

Same sign as a; about 2J/r^3 at large r.

### (11.91) Equatorial photon radial equation (general form) · supporting · SCH p.333 (pdf 351)

$$
\left(\frac{dr}{d\lambda}\right)^2 = g^{rr}\left[(-g^{tt})E^2 + 2g^{t\phi}EJ - g^{\phi\phi}J^2\right] = g^{rr}(-g^{tt})\left[E^2 - 2\omega EJ + \frac{g^{\phi\phi}}{g^{tt}}J^2\right]
$$

Null condition with p_t = -E and p_phi = J; an EJ cross term appears.

### (11.92) Equatorial Kerr photon equation · derivation-step · SCH p.333 (pdf 351)

$$
\left(\frac{dr}{d\lambda}\right)^2 = \frac{(r^2+a^2)^2 - a^2\Delta}{r^4}\left[E^2 - \frac{4Mra}{(r^2+a^2)^2 - a^2\Delta}EJ - \frac{r^2 - 2Mr}{(r^2+a^2)^2 - a^2\Delta}J^2\right]
$$

Explicit form at theta = pi/2; reduces to Eq. 11.12 at a = 0.

### (11.93) Factored photon equation · supporting · SCH p.333 (pdf 351)

$$
\left(\frac{dr}{d\lambda}\right)^2 = \frac{(r^2+a^2)^2 - a^2\Delta}{r^4}(E - V_+)(E - V_-)
$$

Motion allowed where E is above V_+ or below V_-.

### (11.94)-(11.95) Kerr equatorial photon potentials · central · SCH p.333 (pdf 351)

$$
V_\pm(r) = \left[\omega \pm \left(\omega^2 - \frac{g^{\phi\phi}}{g^{tt}}\right)^{1/2}\right]J = \frac{2Mra \pm r^2\Delta^{1/2}}{(r^2+a^2)^2 - a^2\Delta}J
$$

Analogue of the square root of Eq. 11.14; complex inside Delta < 0, so there are no turning points there.

### (11.96) Energy measured by a ZAMO · supporting · SCH p.335 (pdf 353)

$$
E_{\rm ZAMO} = -\vec p\cdot\vec U = -(p_0U^0 + p_\phi U^\phi) = A(E - \omega J)
$$

Local energy seen by the zero-angular-momentum observer with U = (A, 0, 0, omega A), A^2 = g_phi phi/(-D).

### (11.97) Forward-in-time condition · central · SCH p.335 (pdf 353)

$$
E > \omega J
$$

Selects physical trajectories (E > V_+); allows E < 0 for counter-rotating orbits inside the ergosphere.

### (11.98) Interior observer four-velocity · derivation-step · SCH §11.5 p.346 (pdf 364)

$$
U^r = -\left(\frac{2M}{r} - 1\right)^{1/2},\qquad r < 2M
$$

Radial velocity of an observer momentarily at U^t = 0 inside the horizon; negative because forward time means decreasing r.

### (11.99) Measured energy of an interior radial photon · supporting · SCH §11.5 p.346 (pdf 364)

$$
-\vec p\cdot\vec U = -p^r U^r g_{rr} = -\left(\frac{2M}{r} - 1\right)^{-1/2}p^r
$$

Positive for any ingoing photon regardless of the sign of E: E is a spatial momentum inside.

### (11.100)-(11.101) Infall time from just outside the horizon · supporting · SCH §11.5 p.347 (pdf 365)

$$
\Delta\tau = -\int_{2M+\varepsilon}^{2M}\left(\frac{2M}{r} - \frac{2M}{2M+\varepsilon}\right)^{-1/2}dr \approx 2(2M\varepsilon)^{1/2}
$$

Proper time for a frame released from rest at 2M + epsilon to reach 2M.

**Symbols:** epsilon << M.

### (11.102) Local energy of the escaping quantum · derivation-step · SCH §11.5 p.347 (pdf 365)

$$
\mathcal E = \tfrac14\hbar(2M\varepsilon)^{-1/2}
$$

From setting Delta tau = hbar/(2 E-local).

**Symbols:** script E: energy in the freely falling frame.

### (11.103)-(11.104) Heuristic Hawking energy at infinity · central · SCH §11.5 p.347 (pdf 365)

\mathcal E = U_0 g^{00}E \;\Rightarrow\; E = \mathcal E\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2M}\right)^{1/2} = \frac{\hbar}{8M}

Redshifted energy is independent of epsilon: a characteristic energy set by M alone.

**Symbols:** -U_0 = E-tilde ~ (epsilon/2M)^{1/2}; g^00 evaluated at 2M + epsilon.

### (11.105) Hawking temperature · central · SCH §11.5 p.347 (pdf 365)

T_H = \frac{\hbar}{8\pi k_B M}

Black-body temperature of a Schwarzschild hole (SI: hbar c^3/(8 pi G M k_B)).

**Symbols:** k_B: Boltzmann constant; M in length units.

### (11.106) Mean black-body photon energy · supporting · SCH §11.5 p.347 (pdf 365)

\langle E\rangle = 2.701\,k_BT = 0.860\,\hbar/8M
$$

The exact thermal mean is 86% of the heuristic value.

### (11.107)-(11.108) Evaporation scaling · central · SCH §11.5 p.348 (pdf 366)

$$
\frac{dM}{dt} \sim -M^{-2} \;\Rightarrow\; \tau \sim M^3
$$

Luminosity ~ A T^4 ~ M^-2 gives a lifetime growing as the cube of the mass.

### (11.109) Lifetime calibration · supporting · SCH §11.5 p.348 (pdf 366)

\frac{\tau}{10^{10}\,\mathrm{yr}} = \left(\frac{M}{10^{12}\,\mathrm{kg}}\right)^3

A 1e12 kg hole lives roughly the age of the Universe (order of magnitude).

M \approx 10^6\,\mathrm{kg} \sim 10^{23}\,\mathrm{J}

Final-second output of any evaporating hole, emitted mainly as gamma rays at about 1e11 K.

### (11.111) Planck mass · supporting · SCH §11.5 p.349 (pdf 367)

M_{\rm Pl} = \hbar^{1/2} = 1.6\times10^{-35}\,\mathrm{m} = 2.2\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{kg}

Mass below which the fixed-background Hawking calculation fails.

**Symbols:** Printed with h; the numbers correspond to hbar.

### (11.112) Area theorem · central · SCH §11.5 p.349 (pdf 367)

\frac{dA}{dt} \ge 0
$$

Classical horizon area never decreases.

## Worked examples

### Example · intro · SCH §11.1 p.297 (pdf 315)

**Problem:** Find the Newtonian radius at which a star of mass M traps corpuscular light, and the mean density at that radius.

**Method:** Energy balance at the escape threshold with v = c, then mass divided by sphere volume.

**Key insight:** The radius equals the GR horizon radius, and the density falls as M^-2, so large masses become black holes without extreme densities.

**Result:** R = 2GM/c^2 (about 3 km per solar mass); rho = 3c^6/(32 pi G^3 M^2), below water density for 1e9 solar masses (about 20 kg/m^3 by our estimate).

**Concepts:** Newtonian dark star, mean density at the Schwarzschild radius

### Example · standard · SCH §11.1 p.301 (pdf 319)

**Problem:** Locate circular geodesics of massive particles and photons in Schwarzschild and determine their stability and the innermost stable circular orbit.

**Method:** Differentiate the squared effective potentials, solve the quadratic for particles, identify maxima and minima, and find where the two roots merge.

**Key insight:** Circular orbits sit at extrema of V^2; the discriminant 1 - 12M^2/J^2 controls existence, and at its zero the stable orbit reaches r = 6M. Photons have only r = 3M.

**Result:** r = (J^2/2M)[1 +/- (1 - 12M^2/J^2)^{1/2}], r_ISCO = 6M, r_photon = 3M.

**Concepts:** stable and unstable circular orbits, innermost stable circular orbit, photon sphere

### Example · standard · SCH p.302 (pdf 320)

**Problem:** Compute the energy, angular momentum and coordinate-time period of a circular orbit at radius r.

**Method:** Invert Eq. 11.17 for J^2, set E^2 = V^2, and divide dt/dtau = E/(1 - 2M/r) by dphi/dtau = J/r^2.

**Key insight:** The observed period is exactly Kepler's, P = 2 pi (r^3/M)^{1/2}, because t is proper time far away; relativity hides in E and J, not the period.

**Result:** J^2 = Mr/(1 - 3M/r), E^2 = (1 - 2M/r)^2/(1 - 3M/r), P = 2 pi (r^3/M)^{1/2}.

**Concepts:** orbital period of circular Schwarzschild orbits

### Example · standard · SCH p.305 (pdf 323)

**Problem:** Recover the Newtonian ellipse from the u = 1/r orbit equation.

**Method:** Drop the u^3 term, shift to y = u - M/J^2, recognise a unit-frequency harmonic oscillator in phi and solve.

**Key insight:** A radial oscillation frequency exactly equal to the orbital frequency is what makes Newtonian orbits close.

**Result:** 1/r = M/J^2 + [(E^2 + M^2/J^2 - 1)/J^2]^{1/2} cos(phi + B), a conic.

**Concepts:** perihelion precession

### Example · challenging · SCH p.305 (pdf 323)

**Problem:** Derive the relativistic perihelion advance for a nearly circular orbit and evaluate it for Mercury.

**Method:** Keep the u^3 term, expand to second order in y, complete the square to get y = y_0 + A cos(k phi + B), compute 2 pi/k, expand for J >> M, and use J^2 ~ Mr. Plug in r = 5.55e7 km, M = 1.47 km, orbital period 0.24 yr.

**Key insight:** The relativistic term lowers the radial frequency to k = (1 - 6M^2/J^2)^{1/2}, so each radial cycle takes slightly more than 2 pi of azimuth.

**Result:** delta phi = 6 pi M^2/J^2 ~ 6 pi M/r = 4.99e-7 rad per orbit = 43 arcsec per century for Mercury.

**Concepts:** perihelion precession, post-Newtonian approximation

### Example · intro · SCH p.307 (pdf 325)

**Problem:** Estimate the periastron advance of the Hulse-Taylor binary pulsar.

**Method:** Apply 6 pi M/r with M = 1.4 solar masses (2.07 km) and mean separation 1.2e9 m, then convert to degrees per year. The text does not give the orbital period; the known value of about 7.75 h is needed to reproduce the per-year figure.

**Key insight:** A crude test-particle formula already gets within a factor of two; eccentricity and comparable masses supply the rest.

**Result:** About 3.3e-5 rad per orbit, or 2.1 degrees per year, versus 4.2 degrees per year observed.

**Concepts:** binary pulsar timing, perihelion precession

### Example · standard · SCH p.308 (pdf 326)

**Problem:** Show that the weak-field Schwarzschild metric agrees with the Newtonian weak-field metric of §8.4 despite its different-looking spatial part.

**Method:** Expand g_rr to first order, compare with the isotropic form, and substitute r-bar = r - M, expanding both the dr^2 and r^2 dOmega^2 terms to first order in M/r.

**Key insight:** The discrepancy is a choice of radial coordinate, not physics; both metrics have identical g_00, which alone governs Newtonian dynamics.

**Result:** dl^2 = (1 + 2M/r-bar)(dr-bar^2 + r-bar^2 dOmega^2) to first order.

**Concepts:** isotropic coordinates, post-Newtonian approximation

### Example · challenging · SCH p.309 (pdf 327)

**Problem:** Compute the total deflection of a light ray passing a mass M with impact parameter b, to first order in M/b.

**Method:** Form dphi/du, substitute y = u(1 - Mu) to cancel the cubic term, integrate in closed form, find the angle to closest approach (y = 1/b) and double it by symmetry.

**Key insight:** Each half of the path sweeps pi/2 + 2M/b, so bending is 4M/b; a clever substitution turns a nonelementary integral into an arcsine.

**Result:** Delta phi = 4M/b; 1.74 arcsec at the solar limb, 0.013 arcsec at Jupiter's limb.

**Concepts:** gravitational deflection of light, impact parameter

### Example · intro · SCH p.314 (pdf 332)

**Problem:** Check the angular scale of the EHT ring around the M87 black hole.

**Method:** Convert 6M for 2e9 solar masses into km and divide by the distance of about 16 Mpc.

**Key insight:** Horizon-scale structure at cosmological distances subtends less than 1e-10 rad, requiring an Earth-sized interferometer.

**Result:** Below 1e-10 rad (about 20 microarcseconds); roughly 3.6e-11 rad by our estimate with the book's mass.

**Concepts:** black hole shadow, innermost stable circular orbit

### Example · standard · SCH §11.2 p.316 (pdf 334)

**Problem:** Find the proper time for a radially falling particle to reach r = 2M from radius R.

**Method:** Set J = 0 in the radial equation, separate variables, and integrate; treat the bound, marginal (E = 1) and unbound cases.

**Key insight:** The integrand is finite at 2M and beyond, so the horizon is reached and crossed in finite proper time for any energy.

**Result:** For E = 1: Delta tau = (4M/3)[(R/2M)^{3/2} - 1].

**Concepts:** proper time of radial infall

### Example · standard · SCH §11.2 p.317 (pdf 335)

**Problem:** Find how much Schwarzschild coordinate time elapses during the same infall.

**Method:** Multiply dtau by dt/dtau = E/(1 - 2M/r), set E = 1, write r = 2M + epsilon and examine the small-epsilon behaviour.

**Key insight:** dt ~ -2M d epsilon/epsilon diverges logarithmically; the factor responsible is g^00, independent of E, so t, not the geometry, is singular.

**Result:** t -> infinity as r -> 2M, for all E.

**Concepts:** proper time of radial infall, coordinate singularity

### Example · intro · SCH §11.2 p.317 (pdf 335)

**Problem:** Determine the causal character of the coordinates just inside r = 2M.

**Method:** Substitute epsilon = 2M - r into the metric and inspect the signs of g_tt and g_epsilon epsilon.

**Key insight:** Inside, t is spacelike and r timelike, so moving forward in time means decreasing r; nothing, not even light, can hold its radius or move out.

**Result:** ds^2 = [epsilon/(2M - epsilon)]dt^2 - [(2M - epsilon)/epsilon]d epsilon^2 + (2M - epsilon)^2 dOmega^2.

**Concepts:** interchange of timelike and spacelike coordinates inside the horizon, Schwarzschild horizon

### Example · standard · SCH p.330 (pdf 348)

**Problem:** Find the angular velocities of photons launched tangentially at fixed r in the equator of a stationary axisymmetric metric, and interpret the surface g_tt = 0.

**Method:** Set ds^2 = 0 with only dt and dphi nonzero and solve the quadratic for dphi/dt; then set g_tt = 0.

**Key insight:** At g_tt = 0 the counter-rotating photon has zero angular velocity, so nothing can stay static or move against the rotation: the static limit.

**Result:** dphi/dt = -g_t phi/g_phi phi +/- [(g_t phi/g_phi phi)^2 - g_tt/g_phi phi]^{1/2}; at g_tt = 0 the roots are 0 and -2g_t phi/g_phi phi; for Kerr r_0 = M + (M^2 - a^2 cos^2 theta)^{1/2}.

**Concepts:** ergosphere, frame dragging

### Example · standard · SCH p.331 (pdf 349)

**Problem:** Compute the area of the Kerr horizon.

**Method:** Restrict the metric to dt = dr = 0, take the square root of the 2D determinant (rho^2 cancels), note theta-independence, integrate over the unit sphere, and set Delta = 0.

**Key insight:** Although the horizon is not a round sphere, its area formula is simple: A = 4 pi (r_+^2 + a^2).

**Result:** A = 4 pi (r_+^2 + a^2), reducing to 16 pi M^2 at a = 0.

**Concepts:** Kerr horizon area, Kerr horizon

### Example · standard · SCH p.332 (pdf 350)

**Problem:** Invert the Kerr metric and obtain the frame-dragging angular velocity explicitly.

**Method:** Invert the diagonal r and theta parts directly and the t-phi block with the 2x2 formula; form omega = g^{phi t}/g^{tt} and simplify using Delta and rho^2.

**Key insight:** omega = -g_t phi/g_phi phi is positive for a > 0 everywhere outside and falls off as 2J/r^3.

**Result:** omega = 2Mra/[(r^2 + a^2)^2 - a^2 Delta sin^2 theta], D = -Delta sin^2 theta.

**Concepts:** frame dragging, Kerr metric

### Example · challenging · SCH p.333 (pdf 351)

**Problem:** Reduce equatorial Kerr photon motion to a potential description and decide which trajectories are physical.

**Method:** Impose p.p = 0 with conserved E and J, factor the quadratic in E into (E - V_+)(E - V_-), and compute the energy measured by a ZAMO, A(E - omega J), requiring it to be positive.

**Key insight:** Because V_+ and V_- straddle omega J, positivity for the ZAMO selects E > V_+; for counter-rotating photons V_+ < 0 inside the ergosphere, so forward-moving photons can have E < 0 there.

**Result:** V_+/- = [2Mra +/- r^2 Delta^{1/2}]J/[(r^2 + a^2)^2 - a^2 Delta]; physical condition E > omega J.

**Concepts:** factored effective potentials, zero-angular-momentum observer, negative-energy orbits

### Example · challenging · SCH §11.5 p.346 (pdf 364)

**Problem:** Estimate the energy at infinity of Hawking quanta using only the uncertainty principle.

**Method:** Show that inside the horizon an ingoing photon may have either sign of E; compute the proper time for a frame released at 2M + epsilon to reach 2M; equate it to hbar/(2 E-local); redshift the local energy to infinity with g^00 and U_0.

**Key insight:** The epsilon dependence cancels between the short infall time and the large redshift, leaving an energy set only by M.

**Result:** E = hbar/8M, versus the exact black-body mean 0.860 hbar/8M for T_H = hbar/(8 pi k M).

**Concepts:** Hawking radiation, vacuum fluctuations, Hawking temperature

### Example · intro · SCH §11.5 p.348 (pdf 366)

**Problem:** Derive how a black hole's evaporation lifetime scales with mass and what it emits in its final second.

**Method:** Luminosity ~ A T^4 with A ~ M^2 and T ~ M^-1 gives dM/dt ~ -M^-2; integrate; calibrate with 1e12 kg <-> 1e10 yr and set tau = 1 s.

**Key insight:** Hotter small holes evaporate explosively; stellar holes (T ~ 1e-7 K) are effectively eternal.

**Result:** tau ~ M^3; final second releases about 1e6 kg ~ 1e23 J at about 1e11 K, mostly gamma rays.

**Concepts:** black hole evaporation, primordial black holes

### Example · intro · SCH §11.5 p.349 (pdf 367)

**Problem:** Show that black-hole area changes obey a thermodynamic first law and identify the entropy.

**Method:** Differentiate A = 16 pi M^2 and regroup factors so that dM = (hbar/8 pi k M) d(kA/4 hbar), recognising T_H.

**Key insight:** Once T_H is known, energy conservation forces S = kA/(4 hbar), and the area theorem becomes the second law.

**Result:** dE = T_H dS with S = kA/(4 hbar).

**Concepts:** Bekenstein-Hawking entropy, black hole thermodynamics, Hawking area theorem


## Analogies and intuitions

### Newtonian dark star of Michell and Laplace: light as a projectile that cannot exceed escape velocity. → Schwarzschild radius · strong · SCH §11.1 p.296 (pdf 314)

Gives the correct horizon radius from school physics and makes black holes feel unexotic, then serves as a foil to show where relativity differs.

**Where it breaks down:** In the dark star light leaves the surface and falls back, and nearby observers could see it; in GR light from inside never gets out even a little. The numerical agreement of 2GM/c^2 is a coincidence of the chosen coordinates.

**App idea:** Split-screen: a Newtonian corpuscle rising and falling back from a dark star next to a GR light cone tipping inward at the horizon, with a local inertial observer riding along to show the photon can never be at rest.

### Relativistic radial equation as a particle rolling in a one-dimensional potential (the analogue of ma = -grad phi). → effective potential · strong · SCH §11.1 p.301 (pdf 319)

Lets learners import intuition about turning points, stability at minima and instability at maxima.

**Where it breaks down:** The 'energy' is E^2 not E, the parameter is proper time or affine parameter, and the potential depends on J; the angular motion continues independently.

**App idea:** Bead-on-a-wire overlay on the potential plot that moves in sync with the orbit animation.

### A slowly rotating ellipse as the picture of a nearly Newtonian relativistic orbit. → perihelion precession · useful · SCH p.303 (pdf 321)

Frames the small effect as an accumulating rotation of the pericenter, which is why a tiny per-orbit shift was measurable for Mercury.

**Where it breaks down:** In strong fields the orbit is a rosette, not a gently turning ellipse (Figure 11.4).

### Slow walkers feel only the curvature of time; fast light feels time and space equally. → Newtonian corpuscular deflection · strong · SCH p.312 (pdf 330)

Explains why a Newtonian corpuscle bends by half the GR amount and why light bending measures spatial curvature.

**Where it breaks down:** Heuristic; the precise statement requires computing null geodesics in the PPN framework (gamma parameter).

**App idea:** Toggle that switches off g_rr's deviation in a ray tracer, halving the bending, and switches off g_00 for slow orbits with negligible effect on planets.

### Gravitating bodies as lenses. → gravitational lensing · useful · SCH p.312 (pdf 330)

Organizes multiple images, magnification and focusing under a familiar optical idea.

**Where it breaks down:** Gravitational lenses have no single focal length (deflection falls as 1/b), produce odd numbers of images for smooth lenses, and are achromatic.

**App idea:** Compare a glass-lens focal point with a point-mass lens's focal line on the same ray diagram.

### Poles of spherical coordinates on a flat map of the globe. → coordinate singularity · strong · SCH §11.2 p.315 (pdf 333)

Shows that coordinates can squash a single point into a whole line and that invariant measurements expose it; then applied to r = 2M and the t = infinity line.

**Where it breaks down:** Riemannian distances vanish only for identical points, whereas in Lorentzian geometry null separation does not imply identity, so the test is subtler.

**App idea:** Globe-unwrapping morph (see Figure 11.10 redesign).

### The horizon at sea: a ship on the horizon sees nothing special about its surroundings. → Schwarzschild horizon · useful · SCH §11.2 p.318 (pdf 336)

Conveys that a horizon is relational (about who can signal whom) rather than a local physical marker.

**Where it breaks down:** An Earth horizon can be recrossed and depends on the observer's height; the black-hole horizon is absolute and one-way.

### Invariant hyperbolae and radial lines in a special-relativistic spacetime diagram. → Kruskal diagram · useful · SCH §11.2 p.321 (pdf 339)

Explains why constant-r curves are hyperbolae and constant-t lines are straight rays from the origin that are Lorentz-orthogonal to them.

**Where it breaks down:** In Kruskal the hyperbolae also exist inside the horizon as spacelike curves and r = 0 is a singular boundary; the analogy (the Rindler picture) is only hinted at via Exercise 5.21.

**App idea:** Morph from a Minkowski diagram with accelerated observers (Rindler) to the Kruskal diagram near its origin.

### Gravitomagnetism: a spinning mass acts on moving masses as a spinning charge acts on moving charges. → frame dragging · useful · SCH p.330 (pdf 348)

Connects g_t phi effects to magnetism; Lense-Thirring gyroscope precession is likened to spin precession of an electron orbiting in a magnetic field.

**Where it breaks down:** Signs and factors differ (like masses attract; gravitomagnetic factors of 2 or 4); gravity is a spin-2 field, so the analogy is only linear and approximate.

**App idea:** Side-by-side field-line sketch: magnetic dipole of a spinning charged sphere and the gravitomagnetic field of a spinning mass, with test gyroscopes precessing.

### A ZAMO needs rockets to hover, just as we need our legs to stand at fixed radius on Earth. → zero-angular-momentum observer · useful · SCH p.335 (pdf 353)

Makes non-geodesic observers feel ordinary and legitimate as energy-measuring clocks.

**Where it breaks down:** Inside the ergoregion no amount of thrust allows a truly static observer; ZAMOs must co-rotate, and none exist inside the horizon.

### Horizon area behaving like entropy, and a hole's mass change like heat input at temperature T_H. → black hole thermodynamics · strong · SCH §11.3 p.326 (pdf 344)

Motivates the thermodynamic interpretation from the non-decreasing area theorem, then completed by Hawking's temperature.

**Where it breaks down:** The microscopic degrees of freedom behind the entropy are not explained; evaporation decreases area, so the generalized second law needs the radiation's entropy too.

**App idea:** A two-column ledger animation for merging or evaporating holes tracking area, entropy of hole plus radiation, and energy.

### A feedback leak that grows exponentially: the more energy leaks out, the deeper the trapped negative energy, the faster it leaks. → ergoregion instability · useful · SCH p.337 (pdf 355)

Explains the instability without mode calculations.

**Where it breaks down:** Growth times can be extremely long; viscosity or horizons can suppress it; purely qualitative.

### Quantum fuzziness might soften the singularity, letting the wave function continue. → curvature singularity · weak · SCH §11.3 p.327 (pdf 345)

Presents the hope that quantum gravity cures GR's breakdown inside horizons.

**Where it breaks down:** Speculative; no accepted theory yet.


## Misconceptions addressed

### A black hole is just a Newtonian dark star: light leaves the surface, rises, stops and falls back. · SCH §11.1 p.297 (pdf 315)

**Correction:** A local inertial observer beside the would-be turning point would see light at rest, which relativity forbids. In GR light emitted inside the horizon never moves outward at all, and the 'surface' is empty space, not a star.

**Why tempting:** The two pictures give the same radius, and escape velocity is familiar.

### Black holes require enormous densities to form. · SCH §11.1 p.297 (pdf 315)

**Correction:** The mean density inside the horizon scales as M^-2; a 1e9 solar-mass hole forms before reaching the density of water.

**Why tempting:** Stellar-mass holes and neutron stars involve nuclear densities, and 'collapse to a point' suggests extremes.

### More energetic photons orbit at a different radius, or bend differently. · SCH §11.1 p.302 (pdf 320)

**Correction:** Photon trajectories depend only on b = J/E; the circular photon orbit is always at 3M.

**Why tempting:** For massive particles orbits do depend on energy.

### The period P = 2 pi (r^3/M)^{1/2} is the proper time an orbiting clock records. · SCH p.303 (pdf 321)

**Correction:** It is coordinate time, equal to the time measured by a distant static observer; the orbiting clock records less (Exercise 11.8).

**Why tempting:** The formula is identical to Newton's, so it looks like 'the' period.

### Schwarzschild's weak-field spatial metric differs from the Newtonian limit, so the solution fails the Newtonian test. · SCH p.308 (pdf 326)

**Correction:** The difference is a radial relabelling; with r-bar = r - M the spatial metric takes the isotropic Newtonian form.

**Why tempting:** Metric components are compared at face value in different coordinates.

### GR's triumph in 1919 was predicting that light bends at all. · SCH p.311 (pdf 329)

**Correction:** Newtonian corpuscular reasoning (Cavendish, Soldner, and Einstein in 1908) already predicted bending of 2M/b; GR's success was the correct value 4M/b, which requires curved space.

**Why tempting:** Popular accounts present light bending as purely Einsteinian.

### Gravity's Newtonian effects come from the curvature of space. · SCH p.309 (pdf 327)

**Correction:** Slow motion is governed by g_00, the 'curvature of time' (equivalently gravitational redshift); spatial curvature enters only at post-Newtonian order or for fast particles like light.

**Why tempting:** Rubber-sheet pictures show curved space pulling objects.

### At r = 2M something physically violent happens: infinite forces tear matter apart. · SCH §11.2 p.317 (pdf 335)

**Correction:** Curvature components in the infaller's frame are finite at 2M; a large enough hole's horizon can be crossed unharmed. Only r = 0 has infinite tidal force.

**Why tempting:** The metric component g_rr blows up there.

### Since distant observers never see an object cross the horizon, it never actually falls in. · SCH §11.2 p.321 (pdf 339)

**Correction:** The object crosses in finite proper time; only its signals are delayed without bound. The distant observer's infinite wait reflects the coordinate t, as the Kruskal diagram shows.

**Why tempting:** Infinite coordinate time and the frozen, reddening image seem to imply the crossing never happens.

### A falling astronaut would notice when crossing the horizon. · SCH §11.2 p.318 (pdf 336)

**Correction:** Nothing local marks the horizon; it is defined by global signalling. The astronaut only discovers later that escape is impossible.

**Why tempting:** The horizon is imagined as a physical membrane.

### The singularity r = 0 is a point at the centre of space. · SCH §11.2 p.321 (pdf 339)

**Correction:** In the Kruskal diagram r = 0 is a spacelike hyperbola, a moment in the future of all interior events rather than a place one could avoid.

**Why tempting:** In flat-space polar coordinates r = 0 is a point.

### The full Kruskal diagram, with its second universe and past region, describes black holes formed from collapsing stars. · SCH §11.2 p.322 (pdf 340)

**Correction:** For a real collapse the region inside the stellar surface is replaced by the star's interior; regions III and IV are absent.

**Why tempting:** The maximal extension is often shown as 'the' Schwarzschild spacetime.

### The horizon's location can be found from a snapshot of the spacetime at one moment. · SCH §11.3 p.324 (pdf 342)

**Correction:** The event horizon depends on the entire future: gas falling in later changes where it was earlier. Only locally trapped surfaces are defined instant by instant.

**Why tempting:** In static Schwarzschild the horizon sits at a fixed radius.

### A black hole's exterior carries a detailed imprint of what formed it. · SCH p.328 (pdf 346)

**Correction:** A stationary uncharged hole is fully fixed by M and J; all other structure is radiated away or hidden.

**Why tempting:** All other astrophysical objects retain individuality.

### The Kerr ergosphere is the black hole's horizon. · SCH p.331 (pdf 349)

**Correction:** The ergosphere (g_tt = 0) lies outside the horizon (Delta = 0) except at the poles; things inside the ergosphere can still escape, but cannot remain static.

**Why tempting:** In Schwarzschild the static limit and horizon coincide.

### With enough counter-rotating angular momentum a particle can resist frame dragging inside the ergosphere. · SCH p.331 (pdf 349)

**Correction:** Even a counter-rotating photon has dphi/dt >= 0 there, so any particle must co-rotate regardless of its angular momentum.

**Why tempting:** Angular momentum seems like a free parameter one can always increase.

### Positive conserved energy E is the criterion for a physical, forward-in-time trajectory. · SCH p.334 (pdf 352)

**Correction:** E is energy relative to infinity; forwardness must be judged by a local observer. Near a Kerr hole the condition is E > omega J, which allows E < 0 inside the ergoregion and excludes some E > 0 solutions below V_-.

**Why tempting:** In flat spacetime positive energy for one forward observer implies it for all.

### The negative-energy photons of Hawking radiation are the same as those of the Penrose process. · SCH §11.5 p.348 (pdf 366)

**Correction:** Penrose-process negative-energy orbits lie outside the horizon inside an ergoregion and need rotation; Hawking's negative-energy partners lie inside the horizon and occur even for Schwarzschild.

**Why tempting:** Both invoke negative energy near a black hole.

### Hawking radiation creates energy from nothing, or needs the emitted photon to come from inside the horizon. · SCH §11.5 p.348 (pdf 366)

**Correction:** Each escaping quantum's partner carries negative energy into the hole, lowering its mass; the escaping quantum originates just outside.

**Why tempting:** Radiation from a 'black' object seems to violate both conservation and causality.

### Bigger black holes are hotter and evaporate faster, like bigger fires. · SCH §11.5 p.348 (pdf 366)

**Correction:** T_H scales as 1/M and lifetime as M^3; stellar holes are colder than the cosmic background and effectively eternal.

**Why tempting:** Everyday hot bodies radiate more when bigger.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Schwarzschild r measures distance from the centre. · SCH §11.2 p.320 (pdf 338)

**Correction:** r is defined by the area (or circumference) of the symmetry sphere through an event; proper radial distance differs, and inside the horizon r is a time.

**Why tempting:** The symbol r and flat-space habits.

### Hawking radiation is a runaway instability, like the ergoregion instability of a rapidly rotating star. · SCH §11.5 p.348 (pdf 366)

**Correction:** The negative-energy partner is already inside the horizon and cannot stimulate further emission outside, so the result is steady thermal radiation whose rate is set by M.

**Why tempting:** Both effects pair a negative-energy wave with outgoing positive energy, and the chapter discusses them only a few pages apart.


## Thought experiments

### Observer beside a turning photon · SCH §11.1 p.297 (pdf 315)

**Setup:** Imagine light from a Newtonian dark star rising until it stops and reverses, and place a freely falling inertial observer at rest next to that turning point.

**Lesson:** The observer would measure the photon at rest, contradicting local special relativity, so GR trapping must work differently: the light never makes progress outward.

**App idea:** Freeze-frame animation that zooms from the global trajectory into a local inertial frame at the apex.

### Dropping probes toward r = 2M · SCH §11.2 p.316 (pdf 334)

**Setup:** Release particles radially from finite radius and compare the time on their own clocks with Schwarzschild coordinate time until they reach 2M.

**Lesson:** Proper time stays finite while coordinate time diverges, so the singularity is in the coordinates.

**App idea:** Two-clock race: infaller's wristwatch versus a distant observer's clock with received-signal markers.

### SOS from inside the horizon · SCH §11.2 p.318 (pdf 336)

**Setup:** A particle that has crossed r = 2M tries to send a photon to a friend hovering just outside.

**Lesson:** Every future-directed signal inside must decrease r, so no message ever emerges; this is what makes r = 2M a horizon and distinguishes it from the dark star.

**App idea:** On the interactive Kruskal diagram, let learners try to aim a light pulse from region II into region I.

### Radio pulses from a falling clock · SCH §11.2 p.321 (pdf 339)

**Setup:** An infalling object emits a pulse at every tick of its clock until it crosses the horizon.

**Lesson:** Only finitely many pulses are sent, but they arrive spread over infinite distant time, so the distant observer sees time slowing to a stop and infinite redshift.

**App idea:** Kruskal diagram with 45-degree pulse lines from the infaller to a distant observer, and a histogram of reception times.

### Astronaut crossing the horizon · SCH §11.2 p.318 (pdf 336)

**Setup:** A person falls through the Schwarzschild horizon, compared with a ship sailing over the horizon seen from shore.

**Lesson:** There is no local warning at a horizon; its significance is global and causal.

### Photons from the centre of a collapsing star · SCH §11.3 p.323 (pdf 341)

**Setup:** During spherical collapse, send light rays outward from the centre at successively later times.

**Lesson:** Early rays escape, later ones are delayed, and one marginal ray hovers forever at 2M: its history is the horizon, which exists and grows before the matter falls inside 2M.

**App idea:** Collapse-and-horizon builder (Figure 11.13 redesign).

### Late gas falling onto a settled black hole · SCH §11.3 p.324 (pdf 342)

**Setup:** Long after a hole of mass M_0 has settled, a spherical shell of gas falls in and raises its mass to M_1. Trace backward the light rays that end up hovering at 2M_1.

**Lesson:** Those rays were already slowly moving outward before the gas arrived, while rays at 2M_0 were doomed; the event horizon is teleological and requires knowledge of the whole future.

**App idea:** Toggle 'add shell at time T' and watch the horizon curve re-draw backward in time on an Eddington-Finkelstein diagram.

### Tangential photons at the static limit · SCH p.330 (pdf 348)

**Setup:** At some r in the equatorial plane of Kerr, emit two photons tangentially, one with the rotation and one against it.

**Lesson:** Where g_tt = 0 the backward photon does not move in phi at all, so nothing can stay static or counter-rotate: the ergosphere.

**App idea:** Top-down Kerr equatorial view with two light fronts from an emitter as it is moved inward past r_0.

### Penrose process · SCH p.335 (pdf 353)

**Setup:** Drop an unstable particle into a Kerr ergosphere and let it split so that one fragment follows a negative-energy orbit into the hole while the other escapes.

**Lesson:** The escaping fragment carries more energy than the original; the extra comes from the hole's rotational energy, which decreases along with its angular momentum.

**App idea:** Energy-bookkeeping animation with sliders for split point and fragment directions, flagging splits that violate E > omega J, and a running tally of M and J of the hole.

### Seed wave in a stellar ergoregion · SCH p.336 (pdf 354)

**Setup:** A small gravitational wave gets onto a negative-energy path within a rotating star's ergotoroid and partly leaks outside.

**Lesson:** Leakage deepens the trapped negative energy, a positive feedback that makes the wave grow until the star spins down; black holes avoid this because the wave can cross the horizon.

### Virtual photon pair straddling the horizon · SCH §11.5 p.346 (pdf 364)

**Setup:** A vacuum fluctuation just outside the horizon produces photons of energy +E and -E, which must normally recombine within hbar/2E.

**Lesson:** If the negative-energy photon crosses the horizon in time, it becomes a legitimate particle (E is a spatial momentum inside), freeing its partner to escape as Hawking radiation with energy about hbar/8M.

**App idea:** Pair-production animation near the horizon with a countdown clock (fluctuation time) versus the infall time of the local frame; slider for epsilon shows the escaping energy stays fixed.

### Can a black hole split in two? · SCH §11.3 p.326 (pdf 344)

**Setup:** Ask whether one Kerr hole could spontaneously fission into two smaller holes with the same total mass and angular momentum.

**Lesson:** The total area would decrease, violating the area theorem, so it cannot happen (Exercise 11.29).


## Applications and observations

- **Mercury's perihelion advance** (observation, SCH p.306 (pdf 324)): The total observed precession is dominated by planetary perturbations; the residual matches 6 pi M/r per orbit. Key numbers: Total ~5600 arcsec/century; GR residual 43 arcsec/century; 4.99e-7 rad/orbit; r = 5.55e7 km; period 0.24 yr.
- **Hulse-Taylor binary pulsar PSR B1913+16** (astrophysical-system, SCH p.307 (pdf 325)): Periastron advance and the gravitational-redshift/transverse-Doppler timing term, with the mass function, give both neutron-star masses and the inclination. Key numbers: Periastron separation ~1.1 solar radii; mean separation 1.2e9 m; crude estimate 2.1 deg/yr; observed 4.2261 +/- 0.0007 deg/yr.
- **Double pulsar PSR J0737-3039** (astrophysical-system, SCH p.307 (pdf 325)): Both members are observed as pulsars, making it the best binary-pulsar laboratory for post-Newtonian tests and possibly gravitomagnetic effects. Key numbers: Pericenter shift about 17 deg/yr.
- **Star S2 orbiting Sgr A*** (observation, SCH p.304 (pdf 322)): Relativistic peribothon shift of S2 measured by the GRAVITY collaboration (the book's text misnames it). Key numbers: About 13 arcminutes per orbit, measured to about 20% (Abuter et al. 2020).
- **Solar and Jovian light deflection; astrometric satellites** (observation, SCH p.311 (pdf 329)): Deflection at the limb of the Sun and of Jupiter; Hipparcos detected Jupiter's effect and Gaia must correct for it; stars far from the Sun show partial deflection depending on the observer's position. Key numbers: Sun: 8.45e-6 rad = 1.74 arcsec (M = 1.47 km, R = 6.96e5 km). Jupiter: 6.42e-8 rad = 0.013 arcsec (M = 1.12e-3 km, R = 6.98e4 km).
- **Cluster lensing and dark matter** (observation, SCH p.313 (pdf 331)): HST image of cluster 0024+1654 shows multiple arc images of one background galaxy; lens mass models reveal smooth dark matter far exceeding stellar mass; magnification helps study the early Universe. Key numbers: Cluster mass can be ten or more times its stellar mass.
- **Microlensing searches for MACHOs** (observation, SCH p.314 (pdf 332)): Transient brightenings of Galactic stars suggest possible dark compact objects (primordial holes or brown dwarfs); results inconclusive. Key numbers: Lens masses of order 0.5 solar masses.
- **Event Horizon Telescope image of M87*** (detection-event, SCH p.314 (pdf 332)): Millimetre VLBI across Earth resolves a lensed emission ring around a dark centre. Key numbers: Book: 2e9 solar masses at ~16 Mpc; ring inner edge inside 6M; angle < 1e-10 rad. (EHT 2019 itself inferred about 6.5e9 solar masses.)
- **Frame dragging around Earth** (experiment, SCH p.330 (pdf 348)): Laser-ranged geodesy satellites and Gravity Probe B's gyroscopes measured Earth's gravitomagnetic effects. Key numbers: Satellites: agreement with GR at the 0.7% level (Ciufolini et al. 2016); GP-B launched 2004, ~20% accuracy (Everitt et al. 2011).
- **Stellar-mass black holes in X-ray binaries** (astrophysical-system, SCH §11.4 p.338 (pdf 356)): Accretion disks heated above 1e6 K; black holes distinguished from neutron stars by absence of steady pulsations and by dynamical mass above about 5 solar masses; no isolated stellar-mass hole identified. Key numbers: Masses 6-20 solar masses; neutron-star maximum at most ~3 solar masses; Cyg X-1 used in Exercise 11.8 with M = 14 solar masses.
- **Compact-object mass distribution and the mass gap** (data-analysis, SCH §11.4 p.340 (pdf 358)): Electromagnetic neutron stars below ~2 solar masses, X-ray black holes from ~5, LIGO-Virgo black holes heavier; GW170817 remnant in the gap; selection effects explained. Key numbers: Figure as of end of 2018; GW170817 remnant ~2.7 solar masses.
- **Sagittarius A*** (astrophysical-system, SCH §11.4 p.340 (pdf 358)): Infrared astrometry and spectroscopy of S-stars give Keplerian orbits with a common dark focus; GRAVITY interferometry confines the mass to about ISCO size. Key numbers: M = 4.15e6 solar masses; distance ~8.3 kpc; 0.1 arcsec ~ 1e4 horizon radii; stars that close move faster than ~0.01c.
- **Supermassive black-hole growth by mergers** (astrophysical-system, SCH §11.4 p.342 (pdf 360)): Double-nucleus galaxies such as NGC 6240, an apparently ejected black hole, and LISA prospects for merger detection. Key numbers: SMBH masses 1e6-1e10 solar masses; ejection at ~1% of c (Komossa et al. 2008); LISA band for 1e4-1e7 solar-mass mergers.
- **Intermediate-mass black holes** (observation, SCH §11.4 p.343 (pdf 361)): Candidates in globular clusters and bright X-ray sources; a LIGO-Virgo merger remnant in this range. Key numbers: Omega Centauri ~4e4 solar masses; merger remnant ~150 solar masses; range ~100-1e4.
- **Merger recoil kicks from numerical relativity** (numerical-estimate, SCH §11.4 p.344 (pdf 362)): Asymmetric gravitational-wave emission gives the remnant a recoil set by dimensionless parameters only. Key numbers: Typically a few hundred km/s; book claims some exceed 10% of c (literature maxima ~5000 km/s, about 1.7% of c).
- **Hawking evaporation timescales and temperatures** (numerical-estimate, SCH §11.5 p.348 (pdf 366)): Stellar holes are far colder than any environment; primordial 1e12 kg holes would be ending now with a gamma-ray flash; none seen. Key numbers: Solar-mass hole T ~ 1e-7 K; tau/1e10 yr = (M/1e12 kg)^3; last second ~1e6 kg ~ 1e23 J, ~0.1% solar luminosity, T ~ 1e11 K; Planck mass 2.2e-8 kg.
- **Density needed to form a 1e9 solar-mass black hole** (numerical-estimate, SCH §11.1 p.297 (pdf 315)): Newtonian estimate from Eq. 11.4 (Exercise 11.1); relevant to quasar black holes. Key numbers: Our estimate: about 20 kg/m^3, far below water's 1000 kg/m^3.

## Historical notes

- **John Michell, Pierre-Simon Laplace (late 1700s):** Independently speculated about stars so compact that their escape velocity exceeds the speed of light, making them dark. — Anticipated the horizon radius formula; used as the chapter's foil. (SCH §11.1 p.296 (pdf 314))
- **Albert Einstein (1915):** Calculated the relativistic perihelion shift of Mercury and found it matched the 43 arcsec per century left unexplained by planetary perturbations (out of about 5600 arcsec per century observed); he reported heart palpitations from excitement. — First observational support for GR. (SCH p.303 (pdf 321))
- **Henry Cavendish, Johann Georg von Soldner, Albert Einstein (1784, 1801, 1908):** Derived the Newtonian corpuscular light deflection 2M/b; Einstein rederived it in 1908, unaware of the earlier work. — Shows that the full GR value 4M/b, not the existence of bending, was the real test. (SCH p.311 (pdf 329))
- **Arthur Eddington (1919):** British eclipse expeditions confirmed the GR light deflection, making Einstein world famous and easing post-war scientific tensions between Britain and Germany. — First confirmed prediction made before observation. (SCH p.309 (pdf 327))
- **Martin Kruskal, George Szekeres (1960):** Found coordinates that cover the Schwarzschild geometry smoothly across r = 2M, settling the nature of the surface. — Arrived just before quasars, pulsars and X-ray sources made black holes astrophysically relevant. (SCH §11.2 p.319 (pdf 337))
- **Paul Painleve, Allvar Gullstrand, Arthur Eddington, David Finkelstein, Roger Penrose (1921-1960):** Earlier horizon-regular coordinate systems (Painleve 1921, Gullstrand 1922; Eddington 1924, Finkelstein 1958, cast in modern form by Penrose around 1960), not at first recognized as Schwarzschild in disguise. — Explored in Exercises 11.23-11.24 as simpler alternatives to Kruskal. (SCH p.356 (pdf 374))
- **Roy Kerr (1963):** Discovered the rotating black-hole solution. — Later proven to be the unique stationary vacuum black hole. (SCH p.328 (pdf 346))

## Notation and conventions

- **Units:** Geometrized units G = c = 1 throughout; masses quoted in km (1 solar mass = 1.47 km); SI occasionally for astrophysical numbers. — In §11.5 hbar and k are kept explicit while G = c = 1, so hbar has units of length squared and M_Pl = hbar^{1/2} is a length. (SCH p.306 (pdf 324))
- **Metric signature:** (-, +, +, +); timelike intervals have ds^2 < 0. (SCH §11.1 p.296 (pdf 314))
- **Tilde quantities:** E-tilde, J-tilde, V-tilde denote per-unit-rest-mass quantities for massive particles; untilded E, J, V are used for photons. From Eq. 11.13 to 11.17 untilded symbols stand for both. — One slip writes L-tilde for J-tilde (printed p.316). (SCH §11.1 p.298 (pdf 316))
- **Squared effective potential:** The potential is written V^2(r) so that (dr/dtau)^2 = E^2 - V^2; plots show V^2, not V. — Differs from texts that use V_eff with (1/2)(dr/dtau)^2 + V_eff = const. (SCH §11.1 p.299 (pdf 317))
- **u and y:** u := 1/r in orbit equations; y is a deviation variable (u - M/J^2 for orbits, u(1 - Mu) for light), redefined in each derivation. — u later means a Kruskal coordinate; context matters. (SCH p.305 (pdf 323))
- **Impact parameter:** b := J/E for photons; b = J/(E^2 - m^2)^{1/2} in general (Exercise 11.2). (SCH p.309 (pdf 327))
- **Apsis nomenclature:** Peri- (closest) and ap- (farthest) combined with -helion (Sun), -astron (star), -gee (Earth), -bothon (black hole); generic periapsis/apoapsis ('perapsis/apapsis' in the text). — Pericenter is used interchangeably. (SCH p.303 (pdf 321))
- **epsilon near the horizon:** epsilon := r - 2M outside (Eq. 11.62, §11.5) but epsilon := 2M - r inside (Eq. 11.63). — Sign convention changes between subsections. (SCH §11.2 p.317 (pdf 335))
- **Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates:** u is spacelike (horizontal axis) and v timelike (vertical axis); regions I (exterior), II (black-hole interior), III, IV. — Many texts use (T, X) or (U, V) null coordinates instead; here u, v are not null. (SCH §11.2 p.319 (pdf 337))
- **Kerr notation:** a := J/M; Delta := r^2 - 2Mr + a^2; rho^2 := r^2 + a^2 cos^2 theta; Boyer-Lindquist (t, r, theta, phi); r_0 ergosphere; r_+ horizon; omega frame-dragging rate with omega_+ its horizon value; D := g_tt g_phi phi - g_t phi^2. (SCH p.328 (pdf 346))
- **Energy symbols in §11.5:** E is the conserved energy at infinity (-p_0); script E is the energy measured in a local freely falling frame. (SCH §11.5 p.347 (pdf 365))
- **Exercise section reference:** The text cites exercises as '§11.6', although §11.6 is titled Bibliography and exercises follow it unnumbered. (SCH §11.1 p.302 (pdf 320))
- **Ergosphere versus ergoregion:** Ergosphere: the bounding surface g_tt = 0 of a Kerr hole (also 'static limit'); ergoregion: the region inside such a boundary; ergotoroid: a toroidal boundary in stars. (SCH p.331 (pdf 349))

- **11.10** (standard): Numerically integrate the orbit equation for given E-tilde and J-tilde to measure the perihelion advance and compare with the analytic formula; for a slightly higher energy, find the proper time to reach the horizon. — *Turns the perturbative derivation into a computational experiment and a natural app demo.* Skills: numerical integration, effective potential, perihelion precession
- **11.17** (challenging): Solve the first-order ray equation explicitly and compute the deflection that a static observer at finite distance from the Sun measures, then find how far from the Sun a 2 milliarcsecond astrometric accuracy can detect it. — *Connects coordinate results to what a Gaia-like instrument observes, a step many texts skip.* Skills: null geodesics, observer measurements with the metric, astrometry
- **11.18** (challenging): Model pulse arrival times from a satellite in circular orbit around a Schwarzschild mass, including relativistic travel-time corrections, as a simplified binary-pulsar timing problem. — *Lengthy multi-part derivation that previews Shapiro-delay style timing analysis.* Skills: photon travel time, perturbation theory, pulsar timing
- **11.21** (challenging): Compute all Schwarzschild Christoffel symbols and Riemann components, transform to an orthonormal frame, show they fall off as r^-3 and stay finite at 2M, and show the curvature invariant diverges at r = 0. — *Supplies the proof, used in the text, that r = 2M is regular and r = 0 is a true singularity.* Skills: Christoffel symbols, Riemann tensor, orthonormal frames, curvature invariants
- **11.22** (standard): For a radial infall with E-tilde = 0.95, compute proper times from 3M to 2M and from 2M to 0, the four-velocity just outside the horizon, and the total redshift of a photon sent outward there, including Doppler. — *Concrete numbers for the horizon-crossing story, and a reminder that the emitter's motion matters for redshift.* Skills: radial geodesics, four-velocity components, gravitational plus Doppler redshift
- **11.23-11.24** (standard): Transform Schwarzschild to Painleve-Gullstrand and to ingoing Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates, sketch the tilted light cones, and argue from the diagrams that the horizon is null, crossable inward in finite time, and impassable outward. — *Gives simpler horizon-regular charts than Kruskal, ideal for visualization (note the printed EF metric has a spurious dr^2 term).* Skills: coordinate transformations, light-cone sketching, causal structure
- **11.29 and 11.37** (standard): Use the Kerr area formula to show two holes have less total area than one hole with their combined mass and angular momentum, and use the area theorem to bound the energy released when two Schwarzschild holes merge. — *Shows the area theorem's predictive power: no bifurcation and up to about 29% of mass radiated for equal-mass merger (our result).* Skills: area theorem, Kerr horizon area, energy bounds
- **11.38** (intro): Estimate the Sun's angular momentum as a uniform sphere, convert to geometrized units, and compare a/M for a hypothetical collapse; then ask the same for an electron. — *Shows ordinary stars have a/M much greater than 1, so collapse must shed angular momentum.* Skills: unit conversion, Kerr parameter, estimation
- **11.43** (standard): Derive the evaporation lifetime calibration from T_H and the Stefan-Boltzmann law, and estimate how small a hole must be before its Hawking temperature allows copious electron-positron emission. — *Links the heuristic scaling to real numbers and to particle-physics thresholds.* Skills: Hawking temperature, black-body luminosity, particle thresholds

## Teaching gems

### Kill the Newtonian dark star with one local observer: if trapped light rose and fell back, an inertial observer at its apex would see light standing still. · SCH §11.1 p.297 (pdf 315)

**Why it works:** Uses only the equivalence principle and the constancy of c to show that GR trapping is qualitatively different, before any geometry is introduced.

**App idea:** Animated zoom from the Newtonian trajectory to the local frame at the apex with a 'speed of light here' meter reading zero, flagged as impossible.

### Treat particles and photons with one effective-potential diagram, labelling energy lines to name every orbit type. · SCH §11.1 p.299 (pdf 317)

**Why it works:** Recycles central-force intuition, gives a qualitative map before any integration, and makes ISCO and the photon orbit visible features rather than formulas.

**App idea:** Effective-potential explorer with draggable energy line linked to live orbit rendering (Figure 11.1 redesign).

### Explain the factor of two in light bending by what each kind of traveller 'feels': slow planets sense only g_00, light senses g_00 and g_rr equally. · SCH p.312 (pdf 330)

**Why it works:** Turns an arcane coefficient into a statement that light bending measures spatial curvature, and ties back to the claim that Newtonian gravity is curvature of time.

**App idea:** Ray tracer with separate toggles for the time-curvature and space-curvature parts of the metric, showing each contributes 2M/b.

### Diagnose r = 2M by invariant questions, with the pole of spherical coordinates as the model case. · SCH §11.2 p.315 (pdf 333)

**Why it works:** Gives a transferable method (compute proper times, curvature, circumferences) rather than a one-off answer.

**App idea:** Checklist-style 'singularity detective' activity: learners run proper-time, curvature and circumference probes on both the sphere pole and r = 2M.

### First show the closing light cones in Schwarzschild coordinates, then 'fix the picture' with Kruskal coordinates and read the diagram through a numbered list of remarks. · SCH §11.2 p.319 (pdf 337)

**Why it works:** Motivates new coordinates as a visualization need and gives a guided reading of a dense diagram.

**App idea:** Chart-morph spacetime diagram (Figure 11.11 redesign) with each Kruskal remark as a highlight step.

### Late-arriving gas reveals the horizon's teleology: rays that seemed to hover at 2M_0 were doomed, and the real horizon was already growing. · SCH §11.3 p.324 (pdf 342)

**Why it works:** Makes an abstract global definition concrete and memorable with a simple spherical scenario.

**App idea:** Horizon re-tracing animation triggered by adding a shell at a chosen future time.

### Negative energy is defined relative to a local forward-moving observer, not to infinity; the same idea powers both the Penrose process (via a ZAMO) and Hawking radiation (via an infalling observer inside the horizon). · SCH p.334 (pdf 352)

**Why it works:** Unifies two exotic effects under one careful concept and removes the paradox of 'negative energy particles'.

**App idea:** Observer-dependent energy widget: pick an observer's four-velocity and a photon momentum, and see the sign of -p.U, with regions of the Kerr and interior-Schwarzschild spacetimes shaded where E < 0 is allowed.

### Estimate Hawking's energy with the uncertainty principle: infall time 2(2M epsilon)^{1/2} sets the fluctuation energy, and redshift cancels the epsilon dependence to give hbar/8M. · SCH §11.5 p.347 (pdf 365)

**Why it works:** A one-page calculation with a striking cancellation that lands within 14% of the exact thermal mean, conveying why T ~ 1/M.

**App idea:** Slider for epsilon showing local energy rising and redshift factor falling, their product staying pinned at hbar/8M.

### Rearranging dM = dA/(32 pi M) so the prefactor is T_H makes S = kA/4 hbar pop out. · SCH §11.5 p.349 (pdf 367)

**Why it works:** Shows the entropy formula follows from the first law once the temperature is known.

**App idea:** Step-through algebra card that color-codes the T_H factor and the dS factor.

### ISCO at 6M depends on nothing about the orbiting body; it is a property of the geometry. · SCH §11.1 p.301 (pdf 319)

**Why it works:** Reinforces the equivalence principle and makes 6M a natural length unit for accretion disks and EHT images.

**App idea:** Drop test particles of different masses and compositions into the orbit explorer; all lose stability at the same radius.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Printed Eq. 11.34 has the linear term 6M^3 y/J^2 and Eq. 11.36 has y_0 = 3M^3/(k^2 J^2); a direct expansion gives 6M^3 y/J^4 and y_0 = 3M^3/(k^2 J^4). The dimensions of the printed forms are inconsistent.** — Students reproducing Exercise 11.14 will fail to match the text and may doubt their algebra. *Suggestion:* Present the corrected forms and teach a dimensional check (y has units of 1/length, J of length).
- **Printed Eq. 11.64 gives dt/dr = +/-(1 - 2M/r) without the inverse power, while the text correctly says the slope diverges at 2M.** — Contradiction between equation and prose; students sketching cones will get them opening up instead of closing. *Suggestion:* Use dt/dr = +/-(1 - 2M/r)^{-1} and derive it in one line from ds^2 = 0.
- **The isotropic-coordinate paragraph says 1 + 2M/r equals 1 - 2M/r-bar and that r^2 becomes r-bar^2(1 + 2M/r); to first order the correct statements are 1 + 2M/r ~ 1 + 2M/r-bar and r^2 ~ r-bar^2(1 + 2M/r-bar).** — A sign error in the key step of showing Schwarzschild's far field matches the Newtonian limit. *Suggestion:* Redo the expansion explicitly with r = r-bar + M, dr = dr-bar.
- **Eq. 11.79 prints the second root as 2g_t phi/g_phi phi; from Eq. 11.78 it is -2g_t phi/g_phi phi (positive for a > 0, since g_t phi < 0). Eq. 11.78 also has a g_tau phi typo.** — Sign confusion about which way the forward photon moves. *Suggestion:* Show that the two roots average to omega = -g_t phi/g_phi phi.
- **Exercise 11.24's Eq. 11.127 includes an extra + dr^2; ingoing Eddington-Finkelstein is -(1 - 2M/r)dv^2 + 2 dv dr + r^2 dOmega^2.** — Students cannot derive the printed metric and will mis-sketch the light cones. *Suggestion:* Give the correct metric and note the printed error; also show that radial ingoing light rays are v = const.
- **Numerical and naming slips: 'GALAXY team' should be the GRAVITY collaboration; Exercise 11.42 gives 0.1 arcsec at 8.3 kpc as 1.25e10 km, but it is about 1.25e11 km (which is what makes the stated ~1e4 horizon radii correct); Eq. 11.111 prints h for hbar; 'L-tilde' appears for J-tilde; 'Kruskel' in Exercise 11.27; a reference to dr/dlambda in 'Eq. 11.22' should be the photon radial equation.** — Minor but confusing when checking numbers or searching the literature. *Suggestion:* Correct silently in app content, and use the 0.1 arcsec calculation as a units exercise.
- **Dated or questionable astrophysical numbers: M87* mass given as 2e9 solar masses (EHT 2019 found about 6.5e9); recoil kicks 'exceeding 10% of c' (simulations give at most ~5000 km/s, under 2% of c); the mass-gap figure is as of 2018 (later events such as GW190814 fall in or near the gap); statements marked 'mid-2021' on dark matter and quasar jets; Sgr A* EHT image (2022) absent; the claim that ringdown has yet to be measured is outdated.** — Learners may quote superseded values. *Suggestion:* Keep the vault's observational numbers in an updatable data layer with dates and sources.
- **The Hawking section refers to gamma-ray bursts 'mentioned earlier in this chapter', but they do not appear in this chapter.** — A dangling reference that can confuse. *Suggestion:* Briefly introduce gamma-ray bursts where they are used, or point to ch10.
- **The Kruskal transformation and metric are stated without derivation, the construction via null coordinates and the tortoise coordinate is hidden in later exercises, and no Penrose (conformal) diagram is given; regions III and IV (white hole, second exterior, Einstein-Rosen bridge) are dismissed.** — The coordinates look like magic, and learners meeting wormhole or white-hole language elsewhere lack context. *Suggestion:* Teach Eddington-Finkelstein first (Exercise 11.24), build Kruskal from null coordinates u = t - r*, v = t + r* with exponentials, then compactify to a Penrose diagram.
- **The heuristic Hawking argument mixes locally measured and conserved energies and uses a fluctuation time hbar/2E with arbitrary O(1) factors; its numerical closeness to 0.860 is partly luck. The lifetime calibration (1e12 kg for 1e10 yr) is only an order of magnitude and depends on the number of particle species emitted.** — Students may think the argument is a derivation or over-trust the numbers. *Suggestion:* Frame it as a scaling argument; emphasize that the epsilon-independence and the 1/M dependence are the robust content; quote modern lifetime estimates (holes of ~5e11 kg ending now) with caveats.
- **Horizon status of r_+ in Kerr is asserted, not shown; the inner horizon, ring singularity, extremal limit a = M, and off-equatorial orbits are not discussed.** — An incomplete picture of Kerr structure; confusion when meeting r_- or a = M elsewhere. *Suggestion:* Add a visual of Kerr's surfaces (ergosphere, outer and inner horizons, ring singularity) as a function of a/M.
- **The notation shifts: u means 1/r and then a Kruskal coordinate; v is a Kruskal coordinate and then advanced time; y is redefined in each derivation; epsilon changes sign between subsections; E and J lose their tildes for part of the orbit discussion.** — Easy to import a formula with the wrong meaning. *Suggestion:* Use distinct symbols in app materials (for example U, V for Kruskal and w for 1/r).
- **The perihelion formula 6 pi M/r is derived for nearly circular orbits; the eccentric version 6 pi M/[a(1 - e^2)] is not given, though it matters for Hulse-Taylor and S2.** — Crude estimates (like 2.1 degrees per year vs 4.2 degrees per year) look like failures of GR instead of the approximation. *Suggestion:* State the semi-latus rectum form and recompute the Hulse-Taylor case including the two-body total mass.
- **The effective potential is squared (V^2), and plots show V^2; the photon potential's maximum position is 3M but the critical impact parameter 3 sqrt(3) M, which sets the shadow size, is never given.** — Learners connecting the EHT image to 3M may get the ring size wrong. *Suggestion:* Derive b_crit = 3 sqrt(3) M from V^2 at 3M and connect to the EHT ring diameter.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'If a star were squeezed until its escape speed reached c, what would happen to light leaving its surface?' Then contrast the Newtonian answer with the local-inertial-observer argument.
- Before any Schwarzschild orbit, check that the learner can explain why t- and phi-independence give conserved p_0 and p_phi; if not, detour to conserved momenta (SCH ch7).
- Teach the effective potential diagram first qualitatively (energy line, turning points, extrema) and only then compute 6M and 3M. Ask: 'Why does a minimum mean a stable orbit?'
- A good check: 'Does the circular photon orbit radius depend on the photon's color?' (No; only b matters.) Then: 'Does the ISCO depend on the orbiting object's mass?' (No.)
- For perihelion precession, emphasize the mechanism (the radial frequency is less than the orbital frequency) before the algebra; warn about the J^2 versus J^4 misprint if the learner follows the book's derivation.
- When learners ask 'why is light bent twice as much as Newton says?', use the slow-versus-fast argument: planets feel g_00, light feels g_00 and g_rr equally.
- For r = 2M, follow a fixed sequence: sphere-pole analogy, proper infall time (finite), coordinate time (infinite), curvature finite, interior signature flip, then the pictures (closing cones, Kruskal). Do not jump to Kruskal before the learner accepts that the problem is coordinate-induced.
- Common learner question: 'Does an infalling object ever cross, since I never see it cross?' Answer with the Kruskal diagram and the finite number of pulses emitted before crossing.
- Check understanding of the interior: ask the learner to explain why a rocket inside the horizon cannot hover, in terms of which coordinate is timelike.
- For event horizons, pose the late-gas thought experiment and ask where the horizon was just before the gas arrived; this reliably exposes the snapshot misconception.
- In Kerr, start from the fact that g_t phi is nonzero and let the learner discover that p_phi = 0 does not mean dphi/dt = 0. Then ask what happens to a counter-rotating photon where g_tt = 0.
- Distinguish ergosphere and horizon explicitly with a diagram and ask which one can be crossed outward.
- For negative energy, ask 'negative relative to whom?' and have the learner compute a ZAMO's measured energy A(E - omega J) before discussing the Penrose process.
- Present the Hawking derivation as a scaling argument; highlight that the epsilon cancellation gives E ~ hbar/M, then ask the learner to predict whether bigger holes are hotter or colder, and what that implies for lifetimes.
- When numbers come up (M87* mass, kick velocities, mass-gap objects), flag that the book's values date from 2018-2021 and check the vault's updated observational data.
- Suggested sequence for a short session: dark star -> effective potential -> ISCO/photon orbit -> light bending factor 2 -> horizon as coordinate singularity -> Kruskal picture -> Kerr ergosphere -> Hawking scaling; longer sessions add precession algebra, teleological horizons and the Penrose process.
