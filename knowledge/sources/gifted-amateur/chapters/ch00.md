# GA ch00 · Overture

> A jargon-setting prelude that moves from the Newtonian principle of relativity and Einstein's light-speed postulate to light cones, flat versus curved spacetime, the metric as a field and a word-equation form of Einstein's equation, then refreshes Newtonian gravity (field, potential, Gauss's law, Poisson's equation, escape speed, Mercury) as the weak-field baseline, and closes with the reader profile, a map of the six Parts and the rules for geometrized units.

**Pages:** printed 1–10 · pdf 18–27 · **Difficulty:** math 2/5, conceptual 2/5, novice-friendliness 4/5

Mostly verbal survey plus standard first-year Newtonian gravity. The one mathematically dense passage is the flux/divergence-theorem/delta-function route to Poisson's equation, which moves quickly and hides a step (from eqn 9 to eqn 12). Relativistic ideas (covariance, light cones, metric field, curvature) are named with pictures but no formulas, so the reader gains vocabulary rather than working knowledge; some glosses are loose (classical field theory, energy density as the sole source).

## Role in the book

Chapter 0 is the orientation layer for the whole book. It names the vocabulary that Part I (special relativity, vectors, the metric) and Part II (curvature, the field equation) will make precise: inertial frames, boosts, covariance, light cones, events, line elements, curvature as non-parallelism, and the metric field. It also fixes the Newtonian results (g = -grad Phi, Poisson's equation, Phi/c^2 as a strength gauge) that Chapter 6 and Chapter 13 use when demanding that general relativity reduce to Newton in the weak-field limit, and it sets up the unit conventions (c = 1 from Chapter 2, G = c = 1 from Part III) that every later chapter relies on. Nothing here is derived relativistically; its job is motivation, a shared dictionary, and a Newtonian launch pad.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can state the principle of relativity and explain why it rules out an absolute rest frame in Newtonian physics.
- Reader can define an inertial frame and a boost, and classify rotations, translations, reflections and boosts as symmetry transformations.
- Reader can state Einstein's second postulate (invariance of light speed) and explain why the resulting theory is called 'special'.
- Reader can explain what it means for a theory to be covariant, and why special relativity requires Lorentz (not Galilean) boosts.
- Reader can sketch a light cone on a spacetime diagram and identify which events can influence, or be influenced by, an observer.
- Reader can contrast flat spacetime (all light cones aligned) with curved spacetime (cones that cannot all be aligned) at a picture level.
- Reader can describe the metric as a field that returns, at each event, the rule for computing intervals between neighbouring events.
- Reader can explain how non-parallelism of initially parallel lines (or relative drift of nearby free test particles) diagnoses curvature.
- Reader can write the Einstein equation schematically as geometry on one side and matter-energy on the other.
- Reader can state the point-mass field g = -GM r_hat/r^2 and potential Phi = -GM/r, and derive Gauss's law for gravity and Poisson's equation using the divergence theorem and a Dirac delta source.
- Reader can use surface gravity, orbital period and the Cavendish value of G to estimate the masses and mean densities of the Earth and Sun.
- Reader can derive the Newtonian escape speed and find the radius at which it equals c, for a given density or mass.
- Reader can use |Phi|/c^2 as an order-of-magnitude gauge of how relativistic a gravitational field is, and cite GPS and Mercury as cases where small effects still matter.
- Reader can convert any quantity between SI and geometrized (G = c = 1) units using the factor c^m (G/c^2)^p, and restore constants in G = 8 pi T.

## Teaching approach

An informal, question-titled survey ('What is relativity?', 'What is a metric?') that front-loads vocabulary with everyday pictures (a juggler, trains in a station, parade soldiers) and postpones every relativistic derivation. The only real calculations are Newtonian: the authors deliberately rebuild the vector-field, potential and Gauss/Poisson description of gravity, plus back-of-envelope astronomy, so that the reader has a concrete weak-field target and a sense of scale before curvature arrives. Formal Example boxes are used as mini-stories rather than exercises, and margin notes carry biographies, cautions and pointers to later chapters. Example boxes can be long: Example 0.3 grows from weighing the Earth into weighing the Sun and a Newtonian dark-star estimate before it closes.

**Style:** survey-overview, conversational-informal, physical-motivation-first, analogy-driven, historical-narrative, example-driven, thought-experiment

**Narrative arc**

1. Hook: general relativity underlies cosmology, black holes and gravitational waves, yet rests on simple principles.
2. Relativity before Einstein: inertial frames and the principle of relativity as a symmetry; juggling and train examples.
3. Add the invariant light speed postulate; explain 'special', covariance and Lorentz covariance.
4. Picture causality with light cones; a lattice of aligned cones means flat spacetime, disordered cones hint at curvature.
5. Motivating puzzle: Newton described gravity but gave no mechanism; Einstein's answer is spacetime curvature sourced by matter.
6. Define a field as a position-to-amplitude machine, single out the metric field, and write Einstein's equation as curvature = matter energy.
7. Explain the metric via events and line elements; use Euclid's parallel postulate to define curvature operationally.
8. Reset the Newtonian baseline: force, field, potential, Cavendish, masses and densities of Earth and Sun, dark-star escape speed.
9. Formalize Newton with flux, divergence theorem, delta functions, Gauss's law and Poisson's equation.
10. Signal where Newton fails: |Phi| comparable to c^2, yet GPS and Mercury show measurable effects far below that.
11. Meta-level: reader profile, map of Parts I-VI, advice to read at least 13 chapters, skip-boxes.
12. Practical tooling: geometrized units, general conversion rule and table, restoring constants in the field equation.

**Signature moves**

- Section titles phrased as naive questions a newcomer would ask, each answered in a page or two.
- Principles set off in shaded boxes (principle of relativity, invariant light speed) and Wheeler's slogan in its own box.
- Four-case juggler ladder (at rest, constant velocity, constant acceleration, jolting) that previews both special relativity and the equivalence principle in one example.
- Visual shorthand for curvature: arrays of light cones that are neatly aligned versus tipped every which way.
- Word equation with bracketed phrases instead of symbols for Einstein's equation, deferring tensors.
- Newtonian gravity rewritten in field language (vector field, scalar potential, flux, Poisson) as a bridge to a field theory of gravity.
- Order-of-magnitude astronomy with modern numbers (weighing the Earth, the Sun's density, the dark-star radius) to build scale intuition.
- Dimensionless ratio |Phi|/c^2 introduced as the natural gauge of relativistic gravity, immediately undercut by Mercury and GPS examples.
- A single algebraic rule c^m (G/c^2)^p plus a table for unit conversion, demonstrated on the Einstein equation.

## Section by section

### Chapter opening — p.1 (pdf 18)

Opens with a Carlyle epigraph praising Newtonian gravity as complete, lists what general relativity explains (cosmic structure, black holes, light bending, gravitational waves, cosmic origins), credits Einstein, and promises that despite its reputation the theory rests on easy-to-grasp principles; this chapter will map the route and supply jargon.

**Concepts:** general relativity

**Key moves**
- Set an ironic frame: the epigraph's confidence in Newton is paid off later by the Mercury anomaly.
- Reassure: difficulty is mathematical, principles are simple.

### §0.1 What is relativity? — p.1 (pdf 18)

Defines inertial frames through Newton's first law, states the principle of relativity and concludes there is no absolute rest frame. Example 0.1 uses a juggler to show that Newtonian reasoning works on a stationary floor, on a uniformly moving train, and even under steady acceleration (which mimics extra gravity), but not under jerky acceleration. The principle is recast as a symmetry under rotations, translations and boosts; Example 0.2 uses the illusion of motion between adjacent trains. Einstein's 1905 invariant light-speed postulate is added, explaining the name 'special' relativity, the idea of covariance and Lorentz covariance. Light cones on spacetime diagrams encode causality; an aligned lattice of cones characterizes flat spacetime.

**Concepts:** inertial reference frame, principle of relativity, rest frame, boost, principle of invariant light speed, special relativity, covariance, Lorentz covariance, Lorentz transformation, spacetime diagram, light cone, flat spacetime, equivalence principle

**Key moves**
- Inertial frame defined operationally: the frames where the first law holds.
- No preferred rest frame follows from the principle of relativity.
- Juggler ladder distinguishes uniform motion, uniform acceleration (equivalent to a gravitational field) and time-varying acceleration.
- Relativity reframed as invariance of physics under coordinate transformations including boosts.
- Second postulate added; 'special' because gravity and non-inertial frames are excluded.
- Covariance glossed as equations keeping their form with no new terms after transformation.
- Speed limit c turns into forward and backward light cones; identical orientation of all cones is the mark of flat spacetime.

### §0.2 What is general relativity? — p.3 (pdf 20)

Recounts Newton's inverse-square gravity and its success, then the philosophical gap: no mechanism for how influence crosses empty space, which Newton himself admitted. Einstein's resolution is that gravity is the curvature of spacetime determined by matter, summarized by Wheeler's two-line slogan. A field is defined as a map from spacetime points to amplitudes (scalar, vector, tensor); GR is a classical field theory whose uniqueness is that matter-field energy couples to the metric field, the geometry itself. Einstein's equation is stated in words (curvature = matter energy density), and the section closes on Einstein's struggle with unfamiliar geometry, Grossmann's help and Cartan's later reformulation, motivating the book's early mathematical chapters.

**Concepts:** Newton's law of universal gravitation, action at a distance, spacetime curvature as gravity, field, classical field theory, matter fields, metric field, Einstein field equation, gravitational constant G

**Key moves**
- Contrast 'how gravity behaves' (Newton) with 'what gravity is' (missing mechanism).
- Replace a transmitted force by geometry that matter shapes and that guides motion.
- Field as a machine from position to amplitude; the metric is the special field.
- Einstein equation split into a geometric left side and a physical right side.
- Historical note that the mathematical barrier, not the physics, is the source of the theory's reputation.

### §0.3 What is a metric? — p.5 (pdf 22)

The metric field is described as the local rulebook for distances and angles between events in the unified entity spacetime, expressed through a line element for nearby events. Euclid's unprovable parallel postulate and the later discovery of consistent non-Euclidean geometries give an operational test for curvature: initially parallel lines converge or diverge. Physically, relative motion of two nearby neutral test particles launched in parallel signals gravity. The metric varies from point to point, the Einstein equation is a differential equation for it, and curvature comes from how the metric changes.

**Concepts:** metric field, spacetime, event, line element, parallel postulate, curvature, geodesic deviation, coordinate independence

**Key moves**
- Metric as distance-and-angle rules, local to each event.
- Line element as the practical way to write a metric, enabling thought experiments with assumed geometries.
- Parallel postulate failure equals curvature; convergence or divergence measures it.
- Translate geometric non-parallelism into relative motion of free test particles.
- Metric as a field: insert an event, get back the local distance rule; Einstein's equation governs its variation.

### §0.4 What are we building on? — p.6 (pdf 23)

Because GR must reproduce Newton for weak fields and slow motion, the section rebuilds Newtonian gravity in field language: force law, field g = F/m, conservative field and potential Phi = -GM/r. Example 0.3 weighs the Earth using g, R and Cavendish's G and gets its density; the same logic with the Earth's orbit gives the Sun's mass and density, and Fig. 6 shows how the Earth-Sun distance was first estimated from the half-Moon angle. The Sun estimate and the following escape-speed argument are still set inside the Example 0.3 box, in small type across the page break. Equating kinetic energy to potential depth gives escape speed and the radius R = c/sqrt(8 pi G rho) at which light could not escape, deemed implausible for ordinary densities. Flux through a sphere plus the divergence theorem and a Dirac delta give div g = -4 pi G M delta, generalized by linearity to div g = -4 pi G rho and Poisson's equation. Phi/c^2 near unity signals Newton's breakdown, but Example 0.4 notes GPS time dilation and Mercury's 43 arcsec per century perihelion excess as measurable relativistic effects in weak fields.

**Concepts:** Newtonian limit, Newton's law of universal gravitation, gravitational field, conservative force field, gravitational potential, shell theorem, Cavendish experiment, astronomical unit, escape velocity, Newtonian dark star, divergence theorem, Dirac delta function, Gauss's law for gravity, superposition in Newtonian gravity, Poisson's equation for gravity, relativistic strength parameter Phi/c^2, gravitational time dilation, perihelion precession of Mercury, neutron star

**Key moves**
- Force per unit mass defines a vector field; conservative, so it is minus the gradient of a scalar potential.
- Invert g = GM/R^2 to weigh the Earth once G is measured; divide by volume for density.
- Circular-orbit balance v^2 = GM/R with v = 2 pi R/tau gives the Sun's mass from one year and one AU.
- Escape speed grows linearly with radius at fixed density; setting it to c yields a radius larger than an AU for solar-system densities.
- Flux of g through a sphere is -4 pi G M independent of radius; the divergence theorem converts it to a local law with a delta-function source.
- Linearity turns the point-mass result into an integral over density and Poisson's equation.
- Dimensions of Phi are velocity squared, so Phi/c^2 is the natural dimensionless measure of relativistic gravity.
- Counterpoint: GR effects are measurable even when Phi/c^2 is tiny (GPS clocks, Mercury's orbit).

### §0.5 Who is this book for? — p.8 (pdf 25)

Describes the intended reader (curious, undergraduate physics background, not aiming to be a professional relativist), acknowledges two reader types (physics-first and mathematics-first), recommends that beginners work through at least Chapters 1-13, and lays out the six Parts: flat-spacetime geometry and the metric; curvature and the field equation; cosmology; stars, black holes and orbits; formal geometry; field theory and quantum links. A margin box explains that curved-arrow boxes flag optional detours.

**Key moves**
- Offer two reading paths and a minimum core.
- Announce the spiral: formal geometry in Part V revisits tools met informally in Parts I-II.

### §0.6 Units in this book — p.9 (pdf 26)

SI units are used at first, then geometrized units with G = c = 1 are phased in (c = 1 from Chapter 2, G = 1 from Part III), with frequent translation back to 'real-world' units. Example 0.5 shows that c converts time to length and G/c^2 converts mass to length; the general rule maps L^n T^m M^p to L^(n+m+p) with factor c^m (G/c^2)^p, tabulated for common quantities. Example 0.6 restores constants in G = 8 pi T to get G = (8 pi G/c^4) T.

**Concepts:** geometrized units, dimensional conversion factor, Einstein tensor, energy-momentum tensor, Einstein field equation

**Key moves**
- Identify c (L/T) and G/c^2 (L/M) as the two conversion keys.
- Generalize to a one-line exponent rule and a table.
- Check both sides of the field equation have dimension L^-2 in geometrized units, then insert G/c^4 on the matter side.

### Exercises — p.10 (pdf 27)

Three Newtonian estimation problems: the escape-speed route to the Schwarzschild radius, surface gravity and escape speed for Earth, Sun and a neutron star, and head-to-foot tidal forces on a person on Earth and at the horizon radius of stellar-mass and supermassive black holes.

**Concepts:** escape velocity, Schwarzschild radius, tidal force, neutron star

**Key moves**
- Push Newtonian formulas into regimes where Phi/c^2 is no longer small, to motivate GR.

## Concepts

### general relativity

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: general theory of relativity, GR

Einstein's classical field theory of gravitation in which gravity is the curvature of four-dimensional spacetime, the curvature being determined by the energy and momentum of matter and fields.

**How introduced:** Announced through its scope (cosmology, black holes, gravitational waves) and contrasted with Newtonian gravity's lack of mechanism.

**Prerequisites:** special relativity, metric field, curvature

**Where:** GA p.1 (pdf 18); GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21)

### inertial reference frame

*definition · introduced* · also: inertial frame

A non-accelerating frame of reference, i.e. one in which a body subject to no force moves in a straight line at constant speed.

**How introduced:** Defined via Newton's first law; illustrated by juggling on a fixed floor or a smoothly moving train.

**Where:** GA §0.1 p.1 (pdf 18); GA §0.1 p.2 (pdf 19)

### principle of relativity

*principle · introduced* · also: Galilean relativity

The laws of physics take the same form in every inertial reference frame, so no experiment can single out a state of absolute rest.

**How introduced:** Stated in a box as a consequence of Newton's first law holding in all inertial frames, then reinterpreted as a symmetry principle.

**Prerequisites:** inertial reference frame

**Notes:** Pre-Einsteinian; accepted by Galileo and Newton.

**Where:** GA §0.1 p.1 (pdf 18); GA §0.1 p.2 (pdf 19)

### rest frame

*definition · mention*

The frame of reference in which a given particle is at rest.

**How introduced:** Margin note used to sharpen the claim that Newtonian physics has no absolute rest frame.

**Prerequisites:** inertial reference frame

**Where:** GA §0.1 p.1 (pdf 18)

### boost

*operation · introduced* · also: velocity boost

A change of coordinates to a frame moving with constant velocity relative to the original one.

**How introduced:** Listed alongside rotations and translations as a transformation under which physics is unchanged; Example 0.2 (the neighbouring-train illusion) makes it tangible.

**Prerequisites:** inertial reference frame, principle of relativity

**Where:** GA §0.1 p.2 (pdf 19)

### principle of invariant light speed

*principle · introduced* · also: second postulate of special relativity, light postulate

In every inertial frame light travels through vacuum at the same speed c, regardless of how the source moves.

**How introduced:** Added as Einstein's 1905 postulate on top of the principle of relativity, with consequences deferred to Chapter 1.

**Prerequisites:** principle of relativity

**Where:** GA §0.1 p.2 (pdf 19)

### special relativity

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: special theory of relativity, SR

The theory built on the principle of relativity and the invariance of light speed, restricted to inertial frames and neglecting gravity; its spacetime is flat.

**How introduced:** Named 'special' because it covers only the special case of inertial frames without gravity.

**Prerequisites:** principle of relativity, principle of invariant light speed

**Where:** GA §0.1 p.3 (pdf 20)

### covariance

*definition · introduced* · also: covariant theory, form invariance

A theory is covariant under a group of coordinate changes when its equations look identical in the new coordinates: rewriting them generates no extra terms.

**How introduced:** Glossed in the text as transforming "sensibly"; a margin note then makes that precise as unchanged equation form.

**Prerequisites:** boost

**Notes:** Distinct from invariance of individual quantities; the chapter does not draw this distinction.

**Where:** GA §0.1 p.3 (pdf 20)

### Lorentz transformation

*operation · mention*

The linear coordinate transformation between inertial frames that keeps the speed of light the same in both; replaces the Galilean boost.

**How introduced:** Named only, as the correct way to perform boosts consistent with the light postulate; derived in Chapter 1.

**Prerequisites:** boost, principle of invariant light speed

**Where:** GA §0.1 p.3 (pdf 20)

### Lorentz covariance

*definition · introduced*

The property that the equations of a theory keep their form under Lorentz transformations (together with translations, rotations and reflections).

**How introduced:** Presented as the symmetry content of special relativity.

**Prerequisites:** covariance, Lorentz transformation

**Where:** GA §0.1 p.3 (pdf 20)

### spacetime diagram

*technique · introduced*

A plot with time on the vertical axis and one or two spatial coordinates horizontal, in which events are points and histories are world lines.

**How introduced:** Introduced to draw the speed-of-light limit as a cone (Fig. 2).

**Where:** GA §0.1 p.3 (pdf 20)

### light cone

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: forward light cone, backward light cone, future and past light cone

The set of events reachable by light signals to or from a given event; its interior and surface bound all events that event can influence (future cone) or be influenced by (past cone).

**How introduced:** Drawn as a double cone on a spacetime diagram; illustrated with a thrown stone or torch beam (future) and starlight or an assassin's bullet (past).

**Prerequisites:** spacetime diagram, principle of invariant light speed

$$
x^2+y^2+z^2 = c^2 t^2
$$

**Where:** GA §0.1 p.3 (pdf 20)

### flat spacetime

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: Minkowski spacetime

Spacetime without curvature, the arena of special relativity, in which coordinates can be chosen so that every event's light cone has the same orientation and opening angle.

**How introduced:** Pictured as a regular lattice of identical upright cones, like soldiers on parade (Fig. 3a).

**Prerequisites:** light cone, special relativity

**Where:** GA §0.1 p.3 (pdf 20)

### curved spacetime

*phenomenon · introduced*

Spacetime whose geometry cannot be made globally flat by any choice of coordinates; nearby free particles accelerate relative to one another and light cones cannot all be aligned.

**How introduced:** Pictured as light cones tipped at irregular angles, like soldiers who have been drinking (Fig. 3b); linked to gravity in §0.2.

**Prerequisites:** flat spacetime, curvature

**Where:** GA §0.1 p.3 (pdf 20); GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21); GA §0.3 p.5 (pdf 22)

### equivalence principle

*principle · mention*

Locally, the effects of a uniform gravitational field cannot be distinguished from those of uniform acceleration of the reference frame.

**How introduced:** Foreshadowed in Example 0.1: a juggler in a steadily accelerating train can compensate as if gravity were a bit stronger.

**Prerequisites:** inertial reference frame

**Notes:** Developed in GA ch6.

**Where:** GA §0.1 p.2 (pdf 19)

### Newton's law of universal gravitation

*law · developed* · also: inverse-square law of gravity

Two point masses M and m separated by r attract along the line joining them with force of magnitude GMm/r^2.

**How introduced:** Given first as the historic unification of falling apples and the orbiting Moon (§0.2), then in vector form eqn 2 (§0.4).

$$
F = GMm/r^2
$$

$$
\vec F = -\frac{GMm}{r^2}\hat{\vec r}
$$

**Where:** GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21); GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23)

### gravitational constant G

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: Newton's constant

The constant of proportionality in Newton's law of gravitation, about 6.674 x 10^-11 N m^2 kg^-2.

**How introduced:** Value given in a margin note with attribution to Cavendish's 1798 torsion-balance measurement.

**Prerequisites:** Newton's law of universal gravitation

$$
G = 6.6741\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N\,kg^{-2}\,m^2}
$$

**Where:** GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21); GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23)

### action at a distance

*historical-idea · introduced* · also: instantaneous action at a distance

The Newtonian picture in which a mass influences another across empty space with no intervening mechanism or propagation.

**How introduced:** Presented as the flaw in Newton's theory that troubled seventeenth-century mechanists and Newton himself (margin note quoting his 1717 Opticks preface).

**Prerequisites:** Newton's law of universal gravitation

**Where:** GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21)

### spacetime curvature as gravity

*principle · introduced* · also: geometric view of gravity, Wheeler's slogan

Gravity is not a force propagating through spacetime but the curvature of spacetime itself: matter-energy determines the curvature, and curvature determines how free bodies move.

**How introduced:** Offered as Einstein's answer to Newton's missing mechanism, compressed into Wheeler's two-line summary and pointed at Fig. 3(b).

**Prerequisites:** action at a distance, curved spacetime

**Where:** GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21)

### field

*definition · introduced*

A rule assigning to every point of spacetime a quantity (scalar, vector or tensor) giving the amplitude of something at that point.

**How introduced:** Described as a machine that eats a spacetime position and outputs an amplitude; examples are the electromagnetic, particle and fluid fields.

**Where:** GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21)

### classical field theory

*definition · mention*

A field theory in which fields are ordinary (non-quantized) functions on spacetime; general relativity is one.

**How introduced:** Margin note, which glosses 'classical' as not compatible with quantum mechanics and points ahead to Chapter 49.

**Prerequisites:** field

**Notes:** The book's gloss conflates 'not quantized' with 'incompatible with quantum mechanics'; see gaps.

**Where:** GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21)

### matter fields

*definition · mention*

All non-gravitational fields, including those of massive particles and fluids and massless fields such as electromagnetism, whose energy acts as the source of gravity.

**How introduced:** Margin note defining the term used on the right-hand side of the word equation.

**Prerequisites:** field

**Where:** GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21)

### metric field

*mathematical-object · core* · also: metric, metric tensor field

A field on spacetime that at each event supplies the rule for computing intervals (distances, times) and angles between neighbouring events; its variation from place to place encodes curvature and hence gravity.

**How introduced:** Singled out in §0.2 as the special field to which matter energy couples, then explained in §0.3 as a local rulebook that returns a distance formula when fed a position.

**Prerequisites:** field, spacetime, event

**Notes:** Made precise as g_{mu nu} in GA ch5.

**Where:** GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21); GA §0.3 p.5 (pdf 22)

### Einstein field equation

*law · introduced* · also: Einstein equation, field equation

The equation relating spacetime curvature (built from the metric and its derivatives) to the energy-momentum content of matter fields; in geometrized units G = 8 pi T.

**How introduced:** Written first as a word equation, curvature on the left equal to matter energy density on the right, then in §0.6 with tensor symbols for unit conversion.

**Prerequisites:** metric field, curvature, matter fields

$$
(\text{curvature}) = (\text{energy density of matter})
$$

$$
\mathbf G = 8\pi \mathbf T
$$

$$
\mathbf G = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\mathbf T
$$

**Where:** GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21); GA §0.3 p.5 (pdf 22); GA §0.6 p.10 (pdf 27)

### spacetime

*definition · introduced*

The single four-dimensional continuum formed by space and time together, whose points are events.

**How introduced:** Introduced as a consequence of the metric linking space and time inseparably; margin note warns that coordinates, including t, lose intrinsic meaning.

**Where:** GA §0.3 p.5 (pdf 22)

### event

*definition · introduced*

A point of spacetime: a particular place at a particular instant.

**How introduced:** Named as the spacetime analogue of a point between which the metric gives distances.

**Prerequisites:** spacetime

**Where:** GA §0.3 p.5 (pdf 22)

### line element

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: interval, ds^2

An expression for the squared interval between two infinitesimally separated events in terms of coordinate differentials and the metric components.

**How introduced:** Presented as the practical way of writing down a metric, allowing one to posit a geometry and explore its consequences.

**Prerequisites:** metric field, event

**Notes:** No explicit formula is given in this chapter.

**Where:** GA §0.3 p.5 (pdf 22)

### parallel postulate

*historical-idea · introduced* · also: Euclid's fifth postulate

Euclid's assumption that lines which start parallel remain parallel indefinitely; it holds in flat geometry but fails in curved spaces.

**How introduced:** Historical route: Euclid could not prove it, and later geometers found consistent geometries without it.

**Where:** GA §0.3 p.5 (pdf 22)

### curvature

*definition · introduced* · also: intrinsic curvature

The departure of a geometry from flatness, detected by the convergence or divergence of initially parallel straight lines (geodesics) and quantified by how the metric varies.

**How introduced:** Defined operationally from failure of the parallel postulate, then linked to the Einstein equation as a differential equation for the metric.

**Prerequisites:** parallel postulate, metric field

**Notes:** Made precise with the Riemann tensor in GA ch11.

**Where:** GA §0.3 p.5 (pdf 22)

### geodesic deviation

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: relative acceleration of test particles, tidal convergence

The relative acceleration of neighbouring freely falling test particles whose paths start parallel; it vanishes in flat spacetime and measures curvature (tidal gravity).

**How introduced:** Physical restatement of the parallel-lines test: two small uncharged test particles started parallel at the same speed drift relative to each other only if gravity is present.

**Prerequisites:** curvature

**Notes:** Strictly, a uniform field produces no relative motion; only tidal (non-uniform) gravity does.

**Where:** GA §0.3 p.5 (pdf 22)

### coordinate independence

*principle · mention* · also: general covariance (informal)

Coordinates are arbitrary labels of events with no intrinsic physical meaning; physical content lies in geometric quantities such as intervals.

**How introduced:** Margin note stating that treating spacetime as one entity demotes coordinates, including the time label t.

**Prerequisites:** spacetime

**Where:** GA §0.3 p.5 (pdf 22)

### Newtonian limit

*principle · introduced* · also: weak-field slow-motion limit, correspondence with Newtonian gravity

General relativity must reproduce Newtonian gravity when fields are weak and speeds are much smaller than c.

**How introduced:** Used as the justification for reviewing Newtonian gravity in §0.4 (margin note 15).

**Prerequisites:** general relativity, Newton's law of universal gravitation

**Where:** GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23)

### gravitational field

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: gravitational field vector, g-field

The vector field g(r) giving gravitational force per unit test mass at each position; for a point mass it points radially inward with magnitude GM/r^2.

**How introduced:** Obtained by dividing the force law by m, drawn as inward arrows on a circle (Fig. 4).

**Prerequisites:** Newton's law of universal gravitation, field

$$
\vec F = m\vec g
$$

$$
\vec g = -\frac{GM}{r^2}\hat{\vec r}
$$

**Where:** GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23)

### conservative force field

*definition · introduced*

A force field for which the work done around any closed path is zero, so it can be written as minus the gradient of a scalar potential.

**How introduced:** Illustrated by the work to roll a ball uphill equalling the energy released when it rolls back down.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational field

**Where:** GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23)

### gravitational potential

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: Newtonian potential, Phi

The scalar field Phi(r) with g = -grad Phi; for a point mass Phi = -GM/r, and it has dimensions of velocity squared.

**How introduced:** Introduced from the conservative nature of g with the conventional minus sign; plotted as a -1/r well (Fig. 5).

**Prerequisites:** conservative force field, gravitational field

$$
\vec g = -\vec\nabla\Phi
$$

$$
\Phi = -GM/r
$$

**Where:** GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23); GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

### shell theorem

*theorem · mention* · also: Newton's shell theorem, spherical-mass exterior result

Outside a spherically symmetric mass distribution, the field and potential are identical to those of a point mass with the same total mass at the centre.

**How introduced:** Stated as a consequence of Gauss's theorem, to justify treating Earth and Sun as point masses.

**Prerequisites:** Gauss's law for gravity

**Where:** GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23)

### Cavendish experiment

*experiment-or-observation · introduced* · also: weighing the world, torsion-balance measurement of G

Cavendish's 1798 torsion-balance measurement of the tiny attraction between lead spheres, which fixes G and so allows the Earth's mass to be deduced from surface gravity.

**How introduced:** Example 0.3 turns it into a calculation of Earth's mass and density.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational constant G

$$
M_\oplus = gR_\oplus^2/G
$$

**Where:** GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21); GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23)

### astronomical unit

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: A.U., Earth-Sun distance

The mean Earth-Sun distance, about 1.496 x 10^11 m.

**How introduced:** Introduced in the Sun-mass estimate inside the Example 0.3 box, with the half-Moon angle method (Fig. 6) and transit-of-Venus parallax as historical routes.

$$
R_{ES} = 1.496\times10^{11}\ \mathrm m
$$

**Where:** GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

### escape velocity

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: escape speed

The minimum launch speed from the surface of a body for a projectile to reach infinity, found by equating kinetic energy to the depth of the potential well: sqrt(2GM/R).

**How introduced:** Worked out in a couple of lines at the end of the long Example 0.3 box (the dark-star remark), then revisited in Exercises 0.1-0.2.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational potential

$$
v_{esc}=\sqrt{2GM/R}=R\sqrt{8\pi G\rho}
$$

**Where:** GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24); GA p.10 (pdf 27)

### Newtonian dark star

*historical-idea · introduced* · also: Michell-Laplace dark star, Newtonian black hole

A hypothetical body so compact that its Newtonian escape speed exceeds c, so light cannot leave it; for uniform density this needs R >= c/sqrt(8 pi G rho), equivalently R <= 2GM/c^2 at fixed mass.

**How introduced:** Used to explain why, with solar-system densities, black holes looked implausible in the nineteenth century: the required radius exceeds an AU.

**Prerequisites:** escape velocity

$$
R = \frac{c}{\sqrt{8\pi G\rho}}
$$

**Notes:** The book does not name Michell or Laplace here; GA ch25 credits both in its opening margin note.

**Where:** GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

### divergence theorem

*theorem · developed* · also: Gauss's divergence theorem

The flux of a vector field out of a closed surface equals the volume integral of the field's divergence over the enclosed region.

**How introduced:** Quoted from vector calculus to turn the sphere-flux result into a local differential law.

$$
\int_S \vec g\cdot d\vec S = \int_V \vec\nabla\cdot\vec g\, dV
$$

**Where:** GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

### Dirac delta function

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: delta function

A distribution concentrated at the origin with unit integral, defined by integrating against a test function to give the function's value at the origin; the three-dimensional version is the product of three one-dimensional deltas.

**How introduced:** Margin note used to represent a point mass as a density so that Gauss's law can be written locally.

$$
\int d^dx\,\delta^{(d)}(\vec x)=1
$$

$$
\int d^dx\, f(\vec x)\delta^{(d)}(\vec x)=f(0)
$$

**Where:** GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24); GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

### Gauss's law for gravity

*law · developed* · also: Gauss' theorem for gravitational fields

The flux of the gravitational field through any closed surface equals -4 pi G times the enclosed mass; locally, div g = -4 pi G rho.

**How introduced:** Computed first for a sphere around a point mass (radius drops out), then converted and generalized in margin note 19.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational field, divergence theorem, Dirac delta function

$$
\oint_S \vec g\cdot d\vec S = -4\pi G M_{enc}
$$

$$
\vec\nabla\cdot\vec g = -4\pi G\rho
$$

**Where:** GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24); GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

### superposition in Newtonian gravity

*principle · introduced* · also: linearity of Newtonian gravity

Because Newton's equations are linear in mass, the field or potential of any distribution is the sum (integral) of point-mass contributions.

**How introduced:** Invoked to pass from a point mass to an arbitrary density via eqn 13.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational potential

$$
\Phi(\vec r)=-\int\frac{G\rho(\vec r')}{|\vec r-\vec r'|}d^3r'
$$

**Notes:** Contrast with GR, whose field equation is nonlinear (not stated here).

**Where:** GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

### Poisson's equation for gravity

*law · core* · also: Newtonian field equation

The Laplacian of the gravitational potential equals 4 pi G times the mass density; it is the Newtonian analogue of Einstein's field equation.

**How introduced:** Obtained by substituting g = -grad Phi into div g = -4 pi G rho, and compared with Poisson's equation in electrostatics.

**Prerequisites:** Gauss's law for gravity, gravitational potential

$$
\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho
$$

**Notes:** Used again in GA ch6 and ch13 as the target the Einstein equation must reduce to.

**Where:** GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

### relativistic strength parameter Phi/c^2

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: compactness, gravitational potential in units of c^2

The dimensionless ratio |Phi|/c^2 comparing gravitational potential energy per unit mass to rest energy per unit mass; when it approaches order unity Newtonian gravity fails badly.

**How introduced:** Motivated by noting Phi has dimensions of velocity squared, then tempered by examples where GR effects matter even when the ratio is tiny.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational potential

$$
m|\Phi| \sim mc^2 \iff |\Phi|/c^2\sim 1
$$

**Where:** GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

### gravitational time dilation

*phenomenon · mention*

Clocks deeper in a gravitational potential run slow relative to clocks higher up.

**How introduced:** Mentioned in Example 0.4 as measurable at the Earth's surface and needed for GPS accuracy.

**Prerequisites:** relativistic strength parameter Phi/c^2

**Notes:** Treated in GA ch6 §6.5.

**Where:** GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

### perihelion precession of Mercury

*experiment-or-observation · introduced* · also: anomalous precession of Mercury

The slow rotation of Mercury's orbital ellipse, about 575 arcsec per century, of which about 532 is explained by Newtonian perturbations from other planets; the remaining 43 arcsec per century is accounted for by general relativity.

**How introduced:** Example 0.4 uses it to show relativistic effects can matter at speeds well under a thousandth of c, and ties it ironically to the Carlyle epigraph.

**Prerequisites:** Newton's law of universal gravitation

**Notes:** Calculated in GA ch23; listed among triumphs in GA ch14.

**Where:** GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

### neutron star

*solution-or-model · mention*

A collapsed stellar remnant with nuclear-scale density, supported largely by neutron degeneracy pressure, typically about 1.4 solar masses in a radius of order 10 km.

**How introduced:** Margin note 17 says such dense compact objects could only be understood after quantum mechanics, undercutting the nineteenth-century density argument; used in Exercise 0.2.

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian dark star

**Where:** GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24); GA p.10 (pdf 27)

### Schwarzschild radius

*physical-quantity · mention*

The radius 2GM/c^2 associated with mass M; in GR it marks a non-rotating black hole's event horizon, and by coincidence it equals the Newtonian radius at which escape speed reaches c.

**How introduced:** Reached in Exercise 0.1 by setting the Newtonian escape speed equal to c.

**Prerequisites:** escape velocity

$$
r_s = 2GM/c^2
$$

**Where:** GA p.10 (pdf 27)

### tidal force

*phenomenon · mention* · also: tidal acceleration

The difference in gravitational force across an extended body due to the field's variation with position, roughly 2GMm h/r^3 along the radial direction for a body of length h.

**How introduced:** Only in Exercise 0.3 (head-to-feet difference on a human), pointing ahead to curvature.

**Prerequisites:** Newton's law of universal gravitation

$$
\Delta F \approx \frac{2GMm\,h}{r^3}
$$

**Where:** GA p.10 (pdf 27)

### geometrized units

*convention · core* · also: G = c = 1 units, natural relativistic units

A unit system in which G and c are set to 1, so time, mass and energy are all measured in lengths; equations lose explicit G and c factors.

**How introduced:** Motivated by simplification of equations, balanced against numerical confusion; phased in gradually with frequent return to SI ('real-world') units.

$$
G=c=1
$$

**Where:** GA §0.6 p.9 (pdf 26); GA §0.6 p.10 (pdf 27)

### dimensional conversion factor

*technique · developed* · also: unit conversion rule for geometrized units

A real-world quantity with dimensions L^n T^m M^p becomes a length power L^(n+m+p) in geometrized units after multiplication by c^m (G/c^2)^p.

**How introduced:** Built from the two keys c (L/T) and G/c^2 (L/M) in Example 0.5, then tabulated and applied to the field equation.

**Prerequisites:** geometrized units

$$
Q_{geo} = c^m (G/c^2)^p\, Q_{SI}
$$

**Where:** GA §0.6 p.9 (pdf 26); GA §0.6 p.10 (pdf 27)

### Einstein tensor

*mathematical-object · mention* · also: G tensor

The curvature tensor on the geometric side of the field equation, built from the Ricci tensor and scalar; it has dimensions of inverse length squared.

**How introduced:** Named only in Example 0.6, with a caution not to confuse the bold tensor G with the constant G.

**Prerequisites:** curvature

**Notes:** Defined in GA ch13.

**Where:** GA §0.6 p.10 (pdf 27)

### energy-momentum tensor

*mathematical-object · mention* · also: stress-energy tensor, T tensor

The tensor describing the density and flux of energy and momentum of matter fields; it sources curvature in Einstein's equation.

**How introduced:** Named only in Example 0.6 as the tensor with dimensions of energy density on the matter side; caution not to confuse bold T with temperature.

**Prerequisites:** matter fields

**Notes:** Defined in GA ch12.

**Where:** GA §0.6 p.10 (pdf 27)

## Key equations

### Light cone surface · supporting · GA §0.1 p.3 (pdf 20)

$$
\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2} = c|t|
$$

Events reachable by light signals from the origin; boundary between causally connected and disconnected events.

**Symbols:** x,y,z spatial coordinates; t time; c speed of light

### Newton's gravitation (magnitude) · supporting · GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21)

$$
F = \frac{GMm}{r^2}
$$

Magnitude of the attraction between two point masses.

**Symbols:** G gravitational constant; M,m masses; r separation

### (1) Einstein equation in words · central · GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21)

$$
\begin{pmatrix}\text{Curvature of}\\ \text{spacetime}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\text{Energy density}\\ \text{of matter fields}\end{pmatrix}
$$

Geometry on the left is determined by matter-energy on the right; the conceptual form of GR.

### (2) Newton's law in vector form · central · GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23)

$$
\vec F = -\frac{GMm}{r^2}\hat{\vec r}
$$

Force on m at r from M at the origin; minus sign expresses attraction.

**Symbols:** r_hat unit radial vector

### (3) Definition of gravitational field · supporting · GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23)

$$
\vec F = m\vec g
$$

Gravitational field is force per unit mass, independent of the test mass.

**Symbols:** g gravitational field

### (4) Point-mass field · central · GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23)

$$
\vec g(\vec r) = -\frac{GM\hat{\vec r}}{r^2}
$$

Inward-pointing inverse-square vector field around a point mass (Fig. 4).

### (5) Field from potential · central · GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23)

$$
\vec g = -\vec\nabla\Phi
$$

Conservative field written as minus the gradient of a scalar potential.

**Symbols:** Phi gravitational potential

### Point-mass potential · central · GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23)

$$
\Phi(\vec r) = -\frac{GM}{r}
$$

Potential well of a point mass (Fig. 5); also valid outside any spherical mass.

### (6) Weighing the Earth · supporting · GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23)

$$
M_\oplus = \frac{g R_\oplus^2}{G}
$$

Earth's mass from surface gravity, radius and G.

**Symbols:** g = 9.81 m s^-2; R_earth = 6.378e6 m

### Mean density · derivation-step · GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23)

$$
\bar\rho = \frac{M}{\tfrac43\pi R^3}
$$

Mean density of a sphere; gives 5.5e3 kg m^-3 for Earth and 1.4e3 kg m^-3 for the Sun.

### Circular orbit balance · derivation-step · GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

$$
\frac{GM_\odot M_\oplus}{R_{ES}^2} = \frac{M_\oplus v^2}{R_{ES}} \;\Rightarrow\; v^2 = \frac{GM_\odot}{R_{ES}},\quad \tau = \frac{2\pi R_{ES}}{v}
$$

Gravity supplies the centripetal force for Earth's nearly circular orbit.

**Symbols:** R_ES astronomical unit; v orbital speed; tau period

### Solar mass from Kepler's third law (rearranged, not printed) · derivation-step · GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

$$
M_\odot = \frac{4\pi^2 R_{ES}^3}{G\tau^2}
$$

Combining the two orbit relations; with tau = 1 yr gives 1.99e30 kg.

### Escape speed · supporting · GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

$$
\tfrac12 m v_{esc}^2 = \frac{GMm}{R}\;\Rightarrow\; v_{esc} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}} = R\sqrt{8\pi G\rho}
$$

Launch speed needed to climb out of the potential well; at fixed density it grows linearly with radius.

**Symbols:** rho mean density

### (7) Dark-star radius · supporting · GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

$$
R = \frac{c}{\sqrt{8\pi G\rho}}
$$

Radius at which Newtonian escape speed equals c for a body of density rho.

### (8) Flux through a sphere · derivation-step · GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

$$
\int_S \vec g\cdot d\vec S = -\frac{GM}{R^2}\cdot 4\pi R^2 = -4\pi GM
$$

Inverse-square fall-off cancels the area growth, so the flux is independent of radius.

**Symbols:** S sphere of radius R

### (9) Divergence theorem applied to g · derivation-step · GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

$$
\int_S \vec g\cdot d\vec S = \int_V \vec\nabla\cdot\vec g\, dV
$$

Surface flux equals volume integral of divergence.

**Symbols:** dV = d^3 r

### (10) Delta-function normalization · supporting · GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

$$
\int d^d x\,\delta^{(d)}(\vec x) = 1
$$

Unit total weight of the delta function.

**Symbols:** d dimension

### (11) Delta-function sifting property · supporting · GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

$$
\int d^d x\, f(\vec x)\,\delta^{(d)}(\vec x) = f(0)
$$

Defining property of the delta function.

### (12) Local Gauss law for a point mass · central · GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

$$
\vec\nabla\cdot\vec g = -4\pi GM\,\delta(\vec r)
$$

Divergence of g vanishes except at the point mass, where it is a delta spike of strength -4 pi G M.

**Symbols:** delta(r) = delta(x)delta(y)delta(z)

### (13) Potential of a mass distribution · central · GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

$$
\Phi(\vec r) = -\int \frac{G\rho(\vec r')}{|\vec r-\vec r'|}\, d^3 r'
$$

Superposition of point-mass potentials.

**Symbols:** rho(r') mass density

### Density as superposed point masses (margin note 19) · derivation-step · GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

$$
\rho(\vec r) \equiv \int \rho(\vec r')\,\delta(\vec r-\vec r')\, d^3 r'
$$

Any density can be viewed as a continuum of delta-function point masses.

### Gauss's theorem for gravity, integral form (margin note 19) · supporting · GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

$$
\int_S \vec g\cdot d\vec S = -4\pi G M,\qquad M = \int\rho(\vec r')\,d^3 r'
$$

Flux through any closed surface depends only on enclosed mass.

**Symbols:** M mass enclosed by S

### (14) Local Gauss law for gravity · central · GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

$$
\vec\nabla\cdot\vec g(\vec r) = -4\pi G\rho(\vec r)
$$

Mass density is the local source of gravitational field lines.

### (15) Poisson's equation for gravity · central · GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

$$
\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho
$$

Newtonian field equation: second derivatives of the potential set by mass density. GR must reduce to it.

### Electrostatic analogues (margin note 20) · supporting · GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

$$
\vec F = q\vec E,\quad \vec E = -\vec\nabla\phi,\quad \oint_S\vec E\cdot d\vec S = Q/\epsilon_0,\quad \vec\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho/\epsilon_0,\quad \nabla^2\phi = -\rho/\epsilon_0
$$

Same mathematical structure as Newtonian gravity with opposite sign (like charges repel).

**Symbols:** The printed note writes the potential as V in Poisson's equation but phi elsewhere.

### Newtonian breakdown criterion · supporting · GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

$$
m|\Phi| \sim mc^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |\Phi| \sim c^2
$$

When gravitational potential energy rivals rest energy, relativistic gravity becomes essential.

### Geometrized conversion rule · central · GA §0.6 p.10 (pdf 27)

$$
[Q]_{SI} = \mathsf{L}^n\mathsf{T}^m\mathsf{M}^p \;\longrightarrow\; [Q]_{geo} = \mathsf{L}^{n+m+p},\quad \text{factor } c^m\,(G/c^2)^p
$$

One rule for converting any quantity between SI and G = c = 1 units.

**Symbols:** L length, T time, M mass dimensions

### Einstein equation, geometrized units · central · GA §0.6 p.10 (pdf 27)

$$
\mathbf G = 8\pi\mathbf T
$$

Einstein tensor equals 8 pi times energy-momentum tensor when G = c = 1.

**Symbols:** bold G Einstein tensor; bold T energy-momentum tensor

### (16) Einstein equation, SI units · central · GA §0.6 p.10 (pdf 27)

$$
\mathbf G = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\mathbf T
$$

Restoring constants: curvature (L^-2) equals G/c^4 times energy density.

### Schwarzschild radius (Exercise 0.1 target) · supporting · GA p.10 (pdf 27)

$$
r = \frac{2GM}{c^2}
$$

Radius at which Newtonian escape speed equals c; coincides with the GR horizon radius.

## Worked examples

### Example 0.1 · intro · GA §0.1 p.2 (pdf 19)

**Problem:** Explain in which kinds of moving frame a juggler can still use simple Newtonian reasoning to catch balls.

**Method:** Qualitative case analysis over four frames: stationary floor, train at constant velocity, train at constant acceleration, jolting train.

**Key insight:** Uniform velocity changes nothing (principle of relativity, basis of SR); uniform acceleration is equivalent to an extra uniform gravitational field and can be compensated (seed of the equivalence principle); only time-varying acceleration spoils simple laws.

**Result:** Juggling works in inertial and uniformly accelerating frames, fails under jolts.

**Concepts:** inertial reference frame, principle of relativity, equivalence principle

### Example 0.2 · intro · GA §0.1 p.2 (pdf 19)

**Problem:** Illustrate everyday insensitivity of physics to boosts.

**Method:** Describe sitting in a stationary train while a neighbouring train pulls away, and the need to look at the platform to decide which train moved.

**Key insight:** Only relative motion is perceptible without an external reference, so the station frame and the boosted frame are physically on an equal footing.

**Concepts:** boost, principle of relativity

### Example 0.3 · intro · GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23)

**Problem:** Determine the Earth's mass and mean density from surface gravity, Earth's radius and the Cavendish value of G.

**Method:** Set mg = GM m/R^2 from eqn 4 to get M = gR^2/G, then divide by the sphere volume.

**Key insight:** Measuring G in the lab is what converts known accelerations into masses: 'weighing the world'.

**Result:** M_earth = 5.97e24 kg; mean density 5.5e3 kg m^-3, about 5.5 times water.

**Concepts:** Cavendish experiment, gravitational field, gravitational constant G

### Example 0.3 (continued) · intro · GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

**Problem:** Estimate the Sun's mass and mean density from the length of the year and the astronomical unit.

**Method:** Treat Earth's orbit as circular, equate gravitational and centripetal forces to get v^2 = GM/R, combine with tau = 2 pi R/v and tau = 1 yr, then divide by the Sun's volume using R_sun = 6.96e8 m. (Printed in small type inside the Example 0.3 box, which runs over from p. 6 to p. 7.)

**Key insight:** A planet's orbit weighs its star; the Earth's own mass cancels.

**Result:** M_sun = 1.99e30 kg; density about 1.4e3 kg m^-3; M_sun/M_earth = 3.33e5 and R_sun/R_earth = 1.09e2 (margin data box).

**Concepts:** astronomical unit, Newton's law of universal gravitation

### Example 0.3 (continued, dark star) · intro · GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

**Problem:** How large must a body of given mean density be for its Newtonian escape speed to reach c?

**Method:** Equate kinetic energy at launch to potential well depth GMm/R, write M = (4/3) pi rho R^3, and solve v_esc = c for R. (Still inside the Example 0.3 box; the box closes after the remark that eqn 7 seemed unattainable, and margin note 17 hangs off that remark.)

**Key insight:** At fixed density escape speed grows in proportion to radius, so trapping light needs either enormous size or enormous density; solar-system densities would require radii beyond an AU.

**Result:** R = c/sqrt(8 pi G rho). Own check: rho = 1000 kg m^-3 gives about 2.3e11 m (1.5 AU); rho = 5500 kg m^-3 gives about 1e11 m (0.66 AU).

**Concepts:** escape velocity, Newtonian dark star

### Example · standard · GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

**Problem:** Derive the local (differential) form of Newton's law of gravity from the point-mass field.

**Method:** Integrate g over a sphere of radius R (flux = -4 pi G M, independent of R); apply the divergence theorem; represent the point mass with a 3D Dirac delta to get div g = -4 pi G M delta(r); superpose via density to obtain div g = -4 pi G rho and substitute g = -grad Phi.

**Key insight:** Inverse-square fall-off is exactly what makes flux depend only on enclosed mass, allowing a local field equation with mass density as source.

**Result:** nabla^2 Phi = 4 pi G rho (eqn 15).

**Concepts:** Gauss's law for gravity, divergence theorem, Dirac delta function, Poisson's equation for gravity

### Example 0.4 · intro · GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

**Problem:** Give cases where relativistic gravity matters although |Phi| is far below c^2.

**Method:** Two short case studies: gravitational time dilation for GPS; the budget of Mercury's perihelion precession (575 total, 532 Newtonian perturbations, 43 unexplained arcsec per century).

**Key insight:** Small relativistic corrections become important when measurements are very precise (clocks) or when effects accumulate over many orbits (precession).

**Result:** Residual 43 arcsec per century explained by GR; Mercury's speed 47 km/s is below 0.0002c.

**Concepts:** relativistic strength parameter Phi/c^2, gravitational time dilation, perihelion precession of Mercury

### Example 0.5 · intro · GA §0.6 p.9 (pdf 26)

**Problem:** Find the factors that turn SI times and masses into lengths in geometrized units.

**Method:** Inside the box: c has dimensions L/T and G/c^2 has L/M, so multiply a time by c and a mass by G/c^2 to get lengths. Directly after the box, the main text generalizes: L^n T^m M^p becomes L^(n+m+p) with factor c^m (G/c^2)^p, and gives a table of eight quantities.

**Key insight:** Setting G = c = 1 is not new physics, only choosing units where c and G/c^2 are dimensionless conversion rates.

**Result:** Inside the box: time multiplied by c, mass by G/c^2. In the following table: velocity c^-1, energy, energy density and pressure G/c^4, mass density G/c^2.

**Concepts:** geometrized units, dimensional conversion factor

### Example 0.6 · intro · GA §0.6 p.10 (pdf 27)

**Problem:** Restore G and c in the geometrized field equation G = 8 pi T.

**Method:** Identify dimensions: the curvature side is L^-2 (factor 1), the matter side is an energy density (factor G/c^4); multiply each side by its factor.

**Key insight:** The coupling constant 8 pi G/c^4 is tiny, which is why enormous energy densities are needed for appreciable curvature.

**Result:** G = (8 pi G/c^4) T (eqn 16).

**Concepts:** Einstein field equation, geometrized units, Einstein tensor, energy-momentum tensor


## Analogies and intuitions

### A juggler on a floor, a smooth train, an accelerating train and a jolting train → inertial reference frame · strong · GA §0.1 p.2 (pdf 19)

Each setting tests whether ball trajectories remain predictable, sorting frames into inertial, uniformly accelerated (gravity-like) and hopeless.

**Where it breaks down:** Uniform acceleration is only locally equivalent to gravity; real gravitational fields vary in space (tides), which the juggler cannot remove.

**App idea:** The frame-switching juggling lab described under Fig. 1, with a tidal mode showing balls drifting apart in a non-uniform field.

### The neighbouring-train illusion in a station → boost · useful · GA §0.1 p.2 (pdf 19)

Shows that without an external reference one cannot tell which of two uniformly moving frames is 'really' moving.

**Where it breaks down:** The illusion occurs during the gentle start-up (a small acceleration) and relies on limited peripheral cues; strictly it illustrates relative velocity perception.

**App idea:** Split-window view from two trains with a hidden platform; the learner guesses which train moves, then reveals the platform.

### Light cones as soldiers on parade, orderly when sober and staggering when drunk → curved spacetime · useful · GA §0.1 p.3 (pdf 20)

Aligned cones depict flat spacetime; tipped, disorderly cones depict curvature.

**Where it breaks down:** Cone tilt in a drawing depends on the coordinates chosen; locally every cone can be straightened, and curvature is really the impossibility of doing so everywhere at once.

**App idea:** Light-cone field viewer with a 'sober up here' click that straightens cones locally but not globally.

### Newton's gravity as clockwork with no visible gear wheels → action at a distance · useful · GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21)

Seventeenth-century mechanical taste is invoked to explain why an influence with no mechanism felt unsatisfying.

**Where it breaks down:** GR does not supply a mechanical mechanism either; it replaces the question with geometry.

### A field as a machine that takes a position and returns an amplitude → field · strong · GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21)

Gives an input-output picture of scalar, vector and tensor fields, and later of the metric field returning a local distance rule.

**Where it breaks down:** Hides the requirement that the output transform correctly under coordinate changes.

**App idea:** Hover probe over a spacetime map: the tooltip displays the local value of a scalar, a vector arrow or a metric matrix.

### Rolling a ball uphill and letting it roll back → conservative force field · useful · GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23)

Energy spent lifting equals energy returned, so a potential exists.

**Where it breaks down:** Friction in real rolling obscures the idealization.

### Initially parallel lines that meet or spread → curvature · strong · GA §0.3 p.5 (pdf 22)

Failure of Euclid's parallel postulate is the test for curvature, and its physical version is relative drift of free test particles.

**Where it breaks down:** Needs the notion of 'straight' lines (geodesics) on a curved space, which comes later.

**App idea:** Two ants walking straight on plane, sphere and saddle surfaces, with their separation plotted versus distance travelled.

### Gravity as electrostatics with the sign flipped → Poisson's equation for gravity · strong · GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

Gauss's law and Poisson's equation are shown to have identical structure, with mass for charge and -4 pi G for 1/epsilon_0.

**Where it breaks down:** Mass has one sign so gravity always attracts; and GR gravity is nonlinear and tensorial, unlike Maxwell theory.

### Weighing the world → Cavendish experiment · useful · GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23)

Once G is measured in a lab, the Earth's mass follows from surface gravity, so a tabletop balance effectively weighs the planet.

**Where it breaks down:** Uses the shell theorem and ignores Earth's rotation and oblateness.


## Misconceptions addressed

### General relativity is only for specialists because its core ideas are intrinsically obscure. · GA p.1 (pdf 18)

**Correction:** The principles are simple; the historic barrier was unfamiliar geometry, which the book teaches first.

**Why tempting:** Reputation, heavy tensor notation, and the story that Einstein himself needed a decade and a mathematician friend.

### There is an absolute state of rest against which motion is measured in Newtonian physics. · GA §0.1 p.1 (pdf 18)

**Correction:** The principle of relativity makes all inertial frames equivalent, so motion is always relative to a chosen frame.

**Why tempting:** Everyday life treats the ground as 'at rest'.

### Any acceleration of your frame makes simple physics impossible. · GA §0.1 p.2 (pdf 19)

**Correction:** Constant acceleration acts like an extra uniform gravitational field and can be compensated; only time-varying acceleration causes trouble.

**Why tempting:** Standard teaching labels all accelerated frames 'non-inertial' and thus problematic.

### 'Special' relativity is called special because it is especially important or advanced. · GA §0.1 p.3 (pdf 20)

**Correction:** It is special in the sense of restricted: inertial frames only, no gravity.

**Why tempting:** The everyday meaning of 'special'.

### Gravity is a force that travels through space from one mass to another. · GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21)

**Correction:** In GR gravity is the curvature of spacetime produced by matter; free bodies simply follow that geometry.

**Why tempting:** Newtonian force language and school physics.

### Newtonian gravity is essentially perfect and needs no replacement. · GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

**Correction:** Mercury's unexplained 43 arcsec per century precession, and conceptual problems with action at a distance, required GR.

**Why tempting:** Newtonian gravity works superbly for almost all solar-system phenomena.

### Relativistic effects only matter for speeds near c or fields where |Phi| is close to c^2. · GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

**Correction:** GPS clocks and Mercury's orbit show measurable GR effects in weak fields at low speeds, because of precision or accumulation over time.

**Why tempting:** The criterion |Phi| ~ c^2 is presented as the breakdown scale.

### Objects dense enough to trap light cannot exist since stars resemble the Sun in density. · GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

**Correction:** Quantum-mechanically supported compact objects such as neutron stars reach nuclear densities; black holes need not be huge.

**Why tempting:** Nineteenth-century evidence came from solar-system bodies within a factor five of water's density.

### The bold G and T in G = 8 pi T are Newton's constant and temperature. · GA §0.6 p.10 (pdf 27)

**Correction:** They are the Einstein tensor and the energy-momentum tensor; Newton's constant is hidden by setting it to 1.

**Why tempting:** Identical letters with different fonts.

### Setting G = c = 1 changes the physics or loses information. · GA §0.6 p.9 (pdf 26)

**Correction:** It is only a choice of units; constants can always be restored uniquely by dimensional analysis.

**Why tempting:** Equations visibly lose factors and quantities like mass acquire units of length.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Tipped light cones in a diagram prove spacetime is curved. · GA §0.1 p.3 (pdf 20)

**Correction:** Cone tilt can be produced in flat spacetime by odd coordinates and removed locally in curved spacetime; curvature is the impossibility of aligning all cones at once, equivalently tidal relative acceleration.

**Why tempting:** Fig. 3 pairs tilted cones directly with the word 'curved'.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Any gravitational field, even a uniform one, makes neighbouring free particles move relative to each other. · GA §0.3 p.5 (pdf 22)

**Correction:** A uniform field accelerates both equally; only field gradients (tides) produce relative motion, which is what curvature measures.

**Why tempting:** The chapter states that relative motion of parallel test particles signals a gravitational field without saying 'non-uniform'.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Only mass (or energy density) is the source of gravity in GR. · GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21)

**Correction:** The full energy-momentum tensor, including pressure, momentum density and stresses, sources curvature.

**Why tempting:** The word equation lists only 'energy density of matter fields' and the Newtonian analogue has only rho.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### The juggler in four frames · GA §0.1 p.2 (pdf 19)

**Setup:** A juggler performs on a fixed floor, on a train at steady speed, on a steadily accelerating train and on a jerking train.

**Lesson:** Inertial frames are equivalent; steady acceleration mimics gravity; changing acceleration breaks simple laws.

**App idea:** Juggling lab with frame modes and a ground/platform view toggle.

### Which train is moving? · GA §0.1 p.2 (pdf 19)

**Setup:** Seated in a train at a platform, you see the adjacent train start to move.

**Lesson:** Uniform relative motion is undetectable without an external reference.

**App idea:** Two-window train view with the platform hidden until the learner commits to an answer.

### A crowd of observers each carrying a light cone · GA §0.1 p.3 (pdf 20)

**Setup:** Fill spacetime with many observers and draw each one's light cone.

**Lesson:** If all cones are identically aligned, spacetime is flat; disordered cones represent curvature and gravity.

**App idea:** Light-cone field viewer driven by selectable metrics.

### Two test particles launched in parallel · GA §0.3 p.5 (pdf 22)

**Setup:** Release two small neutral particles side by side with equal velocities along parallel paths.

**Lesson:** Any change in their separation reveals gravity as curvature, the physical form of the parallel-postulate test.

**App idea:** Drop two balls toward a planet and watch them converge as they fall radially inward; switch to a uniform field to see no convergence.

### How big must a star be to trap light? · GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

**Setup:** Take a body of fixed density and grow its radius until Newtonian escape speed hits c.

**Lesson:** Ordinary densities need radii beyond an AU, so dark stars seemed impossible until compact objects were understood.

**App idea:** Slider for density (water to nuclear) showing the critical radius compared with Earth's orbit and a neutron star.

### When does potential energy rival rest energy? · GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

**Setup:** Compare m|Phi| with mc^2 for various bodies.

**Lesson:** |Phi|/c^2 near one signals strong gravity, but precision and accumulated effects reveal GR far earlier.

**App idea:** Log-scale ladder of |Phi|/c^2 from Earth (7e-10) through Sun (2e-6), white dwarf and neutron star (0.2) to a black-hole horizon (0.5).


## Applications and observations

- **Mass and density of the Earth** (numerical-estimate, GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23)): Surface gravity, Earth's radius and Cavendish's G give the Earth's mass and mean density. Key numbers: g = 9.81 m s^-2; R = 6.378e6 m; G = 6.6741e-11; M = 5.97e24 kg; density 5.5e3 kg m^-3.
- **Mass and density of the Sun** (numerical-estimate, GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)): Continuing inside the Example 0.3 box: circular-orbit balance with one AU and one year gives the Sun's mass, and its radius gives a density just above water's. A shaded margin data box collects the Earth and Sun figures. Key numbers: AU = 1.496e11 m; M_sun = 1.99e30 kg; R_sun = 6.96e8 m; density 1.4e3 kg m^-3; M_sun/M_earth = 3.33e5; R_sun/R_earth = 1.09e2.
- **Half-Moon method for the astronomical unit** (observation, GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)): At exact half-Moon the Sun-Moon-Earth angle is a right angle; measuring the Moon-Sun angle from Earth gives the ratio of distances (Fig. 6). Key numbers: Angle 89.853 degrees; Earth-Moon distance 0.00257 AU (own check: cos 89.853 deg = 0.00257).
- **Newtonian dark-star radius** (numerical-estimate, GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)): Radius at which escape speed equals c for a given density; for solar-system densities it exceeds an AU, so black holes seemed implausible. Key numbers: R = c/sqrt(8 pi G rho). Own estimates: about 1.5 AU for water density, about 0.66 AU for Earth's density.
- **Gravitational time dilation in GPS** (technology, GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)): Clocks at different heights in Earth's field tick at measurably different rates, which satellite navigation must correct. Key numbers: Own estimate: Earth-surface |Phi|/c^2 is about 7e-10; the net relativistic GPS clock correction is about 38 microseconds per day (external figure, not in the chapter).
- **Anomalous perihelion precession of Mercury** (observation, GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)): Mercury's orbit precesses by about 575 arcsec per century; Newtonian planetary perturbations account for about 532; the remaining 43 was explained by GR. Key numbers: Orbital speed 47 km/s (below 0.0002c); 575, 532 and 43 arcsec per century. Own check: 6 pi GM/(c^2 a (1-e^2)) per orbit gives 43 arcsec per century.
- **Surface gravity and escape speed of Earth, Sun and a neutron star (Exercise 0.2)** (numerical-estimate, GA p.10 (pdf 27)): Newtonian estimates across compactness regimes. Key numbers: Own estimates (consistent with GA Appendix E): Earth 9.8 m s^-2, 11.2 km/s; Sun 274 m s^-2, 618 km/s; 1.4 M_sun, 10 km neutron star 1.9e12 m s^-2, 1.9e8 m/s (0.64c), |Phi|/c^2 about 0.21.
- **Tidal stretching of a human near black holes (Exercise 0.3)** (numerical-estimate, GA p.10 (pdf 27)): Head-to-foot tidal acceleration difference 2GMh/r^3 for h = 1.8 m. Key numbers: Own estimates: Earth surface 5.5e-6 m s^-2; at r_s = 8.9 km of a 3 M_sun hole about 2e9 m s^-2 (lethal); at r_s = 3.0e9 m of a 1e6 M_sun hole about 0.019 m s^-2 (harmless). Tidal acceleration at the horizon scales as 1/M^2.

## Historical notes

- **Galileo Galilei, Isaac Newton:** Accepted the principle that physical laws do not distinguish between uniformly moving frames. — Relativity predates Einstein; his innovation was adding the light-speed postulate. (GA §0.1 p.2 (pdf 19))
- **Albert Einstein (1905):** Added the invariance of light speed to the principle of relativity, founding special relativity. — First step toward the geometric picture of spacetime. (GA §0.1 p.2 (pdf 19))
- **Isaac Newton (1686):** Principia presents universal gravitation, unifying the fall of an apple with the Moon's orbit. — Launched the first quantitative theory of gravity, later the weak-field limit of GR. (The book dates it 1686, when it was presented; publication was 1687.) (GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21))
- **Isaac Newton (1717):** In the preface to the Opticks, Newton declined to treat gravity as an essential property of matter because he did not know its cause. — Shows Newton recognized the missing mechanism that GR later addresses. (GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21))
- **Henry Cavendish (1798):** Torsion-balance measurement of the attraction between lead spheres, determining G and 'weighing the world'. — Made absolute masses of Earth and Sun calculable. (GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23))
- **Thomas Carlyle (1836):** Wrote that the theory of gravitation was essentially perfect, citing Lagrange and Laplace on solar-system stability (chapter epigraph). — Used ironically: the Mercury discrepancy proved significant. (GA p.1 (pdf 18))
- **Marcel Grossmann, Albert Einstein:** Grossmann helped Einstein learn the geometry needed for GR; Einstein then worked roughly a further decade to complete the theory. — Explains why mathematical preparation is front-loaded in the book. (GA §0.2 p.5 (pdf 22))
- **Élie Cartan:** Developed modern geometric techniques (differential forms, moving frames) that reshaped how GR is presented. — Foreshadows Part V's Cartan methods. (GA §0.2 p.5 (pdf 22))
- **Euclid (c. 300 BC):** Could not prove the parallel postulate; eighteenth- and nineteenth-century geometers built consistent geometries without it. — Supplies the operational definition of curvature used throughout the book. (GA §0.3 p.5 (pdf 22))
- **History:** Greek astronomers estimated the Earth-Sun distance from the angle between the Sun and a half-Moon; seventeenth-century and later transit-of-Venus observations refined it. — Illustrates how astronomical scale was bootstrapped geometrically. (The book does not name Aristarchus.) (GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24))
- **John Archibald Wheeler:** Coined the two-line summary that spacetime governs motion of matter and matter governs curvature of spacetime. — The book's slogan for the geometric view of gravity. (GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21))

## Notation and conventions

- **Three-vectors:** Ordinary spatial vectors carry an arrow (F, g, r); unit vectors carry a hat over the arrow. — Only three-vectors occur in this chapter; four-vector notation arrives with Part I. (GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23))
- **Sign of the gravitational potential:** g = -grad Phi with Phi = -GM/r negative and vanishing at infinity; Poisson's equation reads nabla^2 Phi = +4 pi G rho. (GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23))
- **Astronomical symbols:** Circle-plus denotes the Earth and circle-dot the Sun (M_earth, R_sun, etc.); R_ES is the Earth-Sun distance. (GA §0.4 p.6 (pdf 23))
- **Dirac delta in d dimensions:** delta(r) means delta(x)delta(y)delta(z), also written delta^(3)(x); integrals written with the measure first, d^d x. (GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24))
- **Dimension symbols:** Sans-serif L, T and M denote the dimensions of length, time and mass. — Do not confuse sans-serif M (dimension) with a mass M. (GA §0.6 p.9 (pdf 26))
- **Geometrized units:** G = c = 1, introduced gradually: SI at first, c = 1 from Chapter 2, G = 1 as well from Part III; SI is called 'real-world' units. — Appendix B summarizes electromagnetic units and notation. (GA §0.6 p.9 (pdf 26))
- **Conversion factor rule:** A quantity with SI dimensions L^n T^m M^p is multiplied by c^m (G/c^2)^p to express it in geometrized length units; to go back, insert these factors into geometrized equations. (GA §0.6 p.10 (pdf 27))
- **Bold tensors:** Bold upright G and T denote the Einstein and energy-momentum tensors, distinct from italic G (Newton's constant) and T (temperature). (GA §0.6 p.10 (pdf 27))
- **Spacetime diagrams:** Time runs up the page; spatial axes are horizontal. — c is not yet set to 1 in this chapter, so the cone is sqrt(x^2+y^2+z^2) = c|t|. (GA §0.1 p.3 (pdf 20))
- **Equation numbering:** Chapter 0 equations are numbered (1)-(16) without a chapter prefix; later chapters use (N.M). — Eqns (10) and (11) live in a margin note between (9) and (12). (GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24))
- **Skip boxes:** A double-ruled margin box opened by a small curved arrow marks advice on optional material and reading paths; the first one (p. 9) says the book need not be read in order. (GA §0.5 p.9 (pdf 26))

## Teaching gems

### Grade reference frames with one juggler: rest, constant velocity, constant acceleration, jolts. Two of the book's pillars (relativity principle and equivalence principle) fall out of the same scene. · GA §0.1 p.2 (pdf 19)

**Why it works:** A single embodied scenario carries the distinctions, and the surprising third case (steady acceleration is fine) plants the equivalence principle early.

**App idea:** Juggling lab with a motion-mode dial, ground/platform camera toggle, live fictitious-force arrows and a hidden 'is there gravity or acceleration?' quiz.

### Encode flat versus curved spacetime as a field of light cones that are either parade-ground neat or tipsy. · GA §0.1 p.3 (pdf 20)

**Why it works:** Causal structure is the most physical content of the metric, and the contrast is instantly readable before any tensor appears.

**App idea:** Metric-driven light-cone lattice in 3D with a 'local inertial frame' tool that straightens one cone at a time, showing curvature as the failure to straighten them all.

### Frame GR as the answer to a question Newton could not answer: not how gravity acts but what it is. · GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21)

**Why it works:** Gives learners a motive for geometry rather than presenting it as arbitrary sophistication.

**App idea:** Short narrated timeline card: apple and Moon, Newton's admitted ignorance, Einstein's geometric resolution, with Wheeler's slogan animated as two arrows between 'matter' and 'geometry'.

### Write Einstein's equation first as a word equation, curvature = matter energy. · GA §0.2 p.4 (pdf 21)

**Why it works:** Learners can hold the logical structure of the theory in mind long before they can compute either side.

**App idea:** Progressive-disclosure equation widget: tap each bracket to reveal successive layers (word, Newtonian nabla^2 Phi = 4 pi G rho, tensor G = 8 pi T, components).

### Rebuild Newtonian gravity in field language, ending at Poisson's equation, as the explicit target GR must reproduce. · GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

**Why it works:** Turns review into preparation: the learner knows exactly which equation the relativistic theory must collapse to.

**App idea:** Gauss-surface sandbox where flux stays fixed as the surface deforms, linked to a side panel that shows div g and nabla^2 Phi as colour maps of the mass density.

### Use Phi/c^2 as a one-number relativity meter, then immediately show two cases (GPS, Mercury) where GR matters even when the meter reads almost zero. · GA §0.4 p.8 (pdf 25)

**Why it works:** Gives a scale and simultaneously prevents the oversimplification that small means negligible.

**App idea:** Log slider of astrophysical objects showing |Phi|/c^2 with badges for measured GR effects (GPS clocks, Mercury precession, light bending, neutron-star redshift, horizon).

### One exponent rule c^m (G/c^2)^p handles every conversion to geometrized units. · GA §0.6 p.10 (pdf 27)

**Why it works:** Replaces a memorized table with a derivable recipe, removing the fear of G = c = 1.

**App idea:** Units converter with presets (1 solar mass = 1.48 km, 1 second = 3e8 m) and a 'restore constants' mode for any geometrized formula.

### Ask how big a star of ordinary density would need to be to trap light, and discover it would engulf the Earth's orbit. · GA §0.4 p.7 (pdf 24)

**Why it works:** Connects escape speed, density and history in one calculation and explains why black holes were long dismissed.

**App idea:** Density slider from water to nuclear matter, drawing the critical radius against the Solar System and a neutron star to scale.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Margin note 9 glosses 'classical' as 'not compatible with quantum mechanics'.** — Conflates 'not quantized' with 'inconsistent with quantum theory', which can confuse learners about semiclassical gravity and QFT in curved spacetime. *Suggestion:* Explain 'classical' as describing fields by ordinary functions without quantization; separately note that a full quantum theory of gravity is still open.
- **The word equation puts only 'energy density' on the matter side.** — Learners may later be surprised that pressure and momentum flux gravitate (e.g. in cosmology with rho + 3p). *Suggestion:* Say 'energy, momentum and stress of matter' and flag that pressure counts too.
- **The statement that relative motion of parallel-launched test particles must be due to a gravitational field omits that only non-uniform (tidal) fields cause relative motion.** — Blurs the equivalence-principle point that uniform gravity is locally removable, and misidentifies what curvature measures. *Suggestion:* Pair with the falling-elevator picture: two balls dropped side by side converge because the field points toward a centre, not because gravity exists per se.
- **Fig. 3(b)'s tilted cones are presented as the signature of curvature without noting coordinate dependence.** — May think cone tilt is itself physical, and fail to see why local flatness holds everywhere. *Suggestion:* Show that a coordinate change can tip cones in flat spacetime and straighten any single cone in curved spacetime; curvature is the global obstruction.
- **The step from the flux result (8)-(9) to div g = -4 pi GM delta(r) (12) is asserted without showing why the divergence vanishes away from the origin yet integrates to -4 pi GM.** — Delta-function reasoning feels like sleight of hand to readers new to distributions. *Suggestion:* Compute div(r_hat/r^2) = 0 for r != 0 explicitly, then note the flux through any sphere is fixed, so all the divergence is concentrated at the origin.
- **Margin note 20 writes Poisson's equation with V while the rest of the note uses phi for the electrostatic potential.** — Minor notational confusion. *Suggestion:* Use one symbol consistently (phi).
- **The body text spells Grossmann as 'Grossman' while the margin note has 'Grossmann'; the epigraph source is printed 'Sartor Restartus' (correct title Sartor Resartus); Principia is dated 1686 (published 1687).** — Trivial, but may propagate into citations. *Suggestion:* Use Grossmann, Sartor Resartus and 1687 (presented 1686) in app text.
- **The claim that the dark-star radius exceeds an AU holds for water-like densities but is closer to 0.7 AU for Earth-like density.** — A learner checking numbers may think they erred. *Suggestion:* Quote the range: roughly 0.7-1.5 AU for densities between five times water and water.
- **Mercury's 575 arcsec per century total is not stated to be relative to an inertial (non-precessing) frame; the raw observed value in equatorial coordinates is about 5600 arcsec per century because of precession of the equinoxes.** — Learners looking up data may find conflicting totals. *Suggestion:* Specify the reference frame when quoting the budget.
- **Why GR effects matter at low speed for Mercury and GPS is asserted but not explained.** — Leaves the |Phi|/c^2 criterion and the examples in apparent tension. *Suggestion:* Explain accumulation: about 5e-7 rad per orbit adds to 43 arcsec after roughly 415 orbits per century; for GPS, 4e-10 fractional rate differences mean kilometres of error per day.
- **Exercise 0.3 asks for a tidal force without giving the person's mass, and the Appendix E answer for the million-solar-mass case appears to be about three times too small.** — Confusion over units (force vs acceleration) and mismatch with the answer key. *Suggestion:* Ask for tidal acceleration difference (or assume 70 kg) and give 2h/r_s = 1.2e-9 as the fractional difference.
- **Covariance is defined only loosely, and the chapter never distinguishes Galilean from Lorentz boosts explicitly, nor covariance from invariance.** — Terms may be memorized without content until Chapter 1. *Suggestion:* Show a one-line example: Newton's second law keeps its form under Galilean boosts but Maxwell's wave equation does not, which forces Lorentz boosts.

## Tutor notes

- Opening question: 'If you were juggling on a train, when would it get hard?' Let the learner discover that steady speed and even steady acceleration are fine, and only jolts are hard; then name the principle of relativity and hint at the equivalence principle.
- Second opening: 'Newton told us how strongly gravity pulls. Did he tell us what gravity is?' Use it to motivate geometry.
- Order of explanation for a novice: inertial frames and relativity principle, light-speed postulate and light cones, flat versus curved as cone alignment, metric as local distance rule, curvature as non-parallelism and tides, word equation, then Newtonian gravity as the limit.
- For learners with strong physics backgrounds, skim §0.1-0.3 and spend time on the delta-function derivation of Poisson's equation and on geometrized unit conversions, which they will need immediately.
- Check for understanding: ask why a uniformly accelerating juggler can still juggle and what goes wrong in a real, non-uniform gravitational field (answer: tidal drift).
- Check: 'Is a single tilted light cone evidence of curvature?' Expected answer: no, only the impossibility of aligning all cones (equivalently, tides) is.
- Check: have the learner convert the Sun's mass to metres (about 1.48 km) and one second to metres (about 3e8 m) using c^m (G/c^2)^p.
- Check: ask the learner to estimate |Phi|/c^2 at Earth's surface (7e-10) and a neutron star (0.2), and to explain why Mercury's orbit still shows GR effects.
- Common learner question: 'Why is it called special relativity?' Answer: restricted to inertial frames without gravity, not because it is more important.
- Common learner question: 'Doesn't the Schwarzschild radius come from Newton?' Answer: the number 2GM/c^2 coincides, but the Newtonian dark star lets light rise and fall back, whereas a GR horizon traps all outgoing light-like paths; use Exercise 0.1 as a hook, not as a derivation.
- Common learner question: 'What does it mean that the metric is a field?' Use the machine picture: feed in an event, receive the rule for local lengths and times; show a hover probe if available.
- Watch for the misreading that energy density alone sources gravity, and that the bold G is Newton's constant.
- Avoid quoting the epigraph or Wheeler's slogan verbatim at length; paraphrase: geometry guides matter's motion, and matter shapes geometry.
