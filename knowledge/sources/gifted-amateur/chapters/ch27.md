# GA ch27 · Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates

> Finishes the coordinate-singularity programme begun with Rindler and Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates: exponentiating the Schwarzschild null coordinates yields the Kruskal metric, whose 45-degree light cones make horizons, infall, signal delays, the four-region maximal extension (wormhole, white hole), and the Kruskal Penrose diagram with its trapped surfaces all readable by eye, with the Minkowski-versus-accelerating-frame analogy explaining why the construction is natural.

**Pages:** printed 280–288 · pdf 297–305 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 4/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

The algebra is short and largely a repeat of the Rindler calculation, but the conceptual load is high: implicit coordinates, t as a hyperbolic angle, regions not covered by the original chart, time-reversed universes, dynamical wormholes and conformal compactification all arrive within nine pages. Diagrams carry most of the argument, which helps visual learners but means OCR-quality figure labels matter. Several printed slips (du du in 27.4, dx^2 in 27.11, 'timelike slice' captions, region II labels in Fig. 27.6b, 'I' and I'') can confuse a careful reader.

## Role in the book

Third of the black-hole geometry chapters in Part IV. Chapter 25 introduced the tortoise coordinate and the infalling astronaut, Chapter 26 set up the problem (closed trapped surfaces, the Rindler toy model tamed by exponentiated null coordinates, and Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates that only half-fix r = 2M). This chapter applies the Rindler recipe to Schwarzschild to get one global chart, then uses it to settle causal questions (what can escape, what can be seen, whether the extension is traversable) and to redraw the black hole on a Penrose diagram, reusing the conformal toolkit of Chapter 19. It closes the geometric treatment of Schwarzschild and sets up Chapter 28, where the Rindler picture of the near-horizon region and exponential redshift feed the Unruh and Hawking radiation arguments.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can carry out the chain Schwarzschild -> tortoise/null coordinates (U, V) -> exponentiated null coordinates (u, v) -> Kruskal (T, X) and obtain the Kruskal line element.
- Reader can explain why the Kruskal metric is regular at r = 2M and why this proves the Schwarzschild radius is only a coordinate singularity.
- Reader can use the implicit relations (1 - r/2M) e^{r/2M} = T^2 - X^2 and T/X = tanh(t/4M) to locate constant-r hyperbolae, constant-t rays, the horizons X = +/-T and the singularities T^2 - X^2 = 1.
- Reader can identify regions I, II, I', II' of the Kruskal diagram and say which part the original Schwarzschild chart (outside and inside) covers.
- Reader can draw an infalling timelike world line and a hovering observer's hyperbola, and explain from 45-degree light rays why received pulses thin out and why pulses from inside the horizon never arrive.
- Reader can state the Rindler analogy: Kruskal coordinates are to Schwarzschild coordinates as inertial Minkowski coordinates are to accelerating (Rindler) coordinates.
- Reader can derive the Rindler metric from the hyperbolic transformation T = x sinh t, X = x cosh t and read x as the inverse proper acceleration.
- Reader can describe the maximal Kruskal extension as two asymptotically flat exteriors joined by a (non-traversable) Einstein-Rosen bridge, with a black-hole region II and a white-hole region II'.
- Reader can argue, both from the Kruskal diagram and from a sequence of spacelike slices, that no causal signal can pass from region I to region I'.
- Reader can explain why the Schwarzschild geometry is static only in regions I and I' and why slices through II and II' evolve (pinch-off of the throat).
- Reader can sketch and label the Penrose diagram of the Kruskal extension (i+, i-, i0, scri+/-, horizons, spacelike singularities) and contrast it with Robertson-Walker diagrams.
- Reader can use constant-r and constant-t lines on the Penrose diagram to show that light fronts emitted inside the horizon have shrinking area (trapped surfaces).
- Reader can show that a uniformly accelerating observer sees a plane wave exponentially redshifted as Omega e^{-g tau}, linking horizon redshift to acceleration.

## Teaching approach

A short, picture-centred chapter. The algebra is dispatched quickly by explicitly re-running the recipe the reader already used on Rindler space in Chapter 26, so the new content is almost entirely interpretive: a sequence of Kruskal diagrams (full grid, constant r, constant t, infall, signals) trains the eye to read causal structure off 45-degree light rays. When the coordinates still feel artificial, the authors invert the reader's intuition with Rindler's analogy, making Kruskal the 'natural' inertial-like chart and Schwarzschild the awkward accelerated one. The extension to the whole plane is then interpreted physically (second universe, wormhole, white hole) and immediately tamed by causal arguments and a flip-book of embedding diagrams showing the throat pinching off. The chapter ends by recycling the Penrose-diagram toolkit of Chapter 19 and closing the loop to the trapped-surface motivation of Chapter 26. Exercises push the Rindler connection toward exponential redshift, preparing Hawking radiation.

**Style:** diagram-driven, analogy-driven, example-driven, spiral-revisit, thought-experiment, conversational-informal

**Narrative arc**

1. Recall the unfinished business: Eddington-Finkelstein charts each fix only one family of radial light rays at r = 2M; the metric was left as a function times dU dV.
2. Example 27.1: use r* = (V - U)/2 to factor the prefactor into a regular function of r, an exponential in V - U, and the flat light-cone form.
3. Copy the Rindler move: exponentiate the null coordinates to absorb the exponential, then form timelike and spacelike combinations T and X to get the Kruskal metric.
4. Check: the conformal factor 32M^3 e^{-r/2M}/r is finite at r = 2M, so the Schwarzschild radius is a coordinate artefact; all radial light rays are at 45 degrees.
5. Tour the Kruskal diagram: constant-r hyperbolae, horizons as X = +/-T, two r = 0 singularity hyperbolae, straight constant-t lines, and which quadrants Schwarzschild coordinates cover.
6. Application (Example 27.2): infalling astronaut and a hovering observer exchanging light pulses, read directly off the diagram.
7. Raise the puzzle of the uncovered half-plane (regions I' and II') and name the Kruskal extension.
8. Build intuition (Example 27.3): hyperbolic coordinates in flat space give the Rindler metric, whose structure mirrors Schwarzschild; state the Minkowski:accelerating = Kruskal:Schwarzschild analogy.
9. Interpret the extension: region I' as a second asymptotically flat universe, region II' as a white hole, the T = 0 slice as a wormhole throat of radius 2M.
10. Test traversability: no causal curve joins I and I'; Example 27.4 re-tells this dynamically as a sequence of slices where the throat opens and pinches off too quickly.
11. Compactify: the Penrose diagram of the extension, with spacelike singularities on its edges, asymptotic infinities, and a comment that the visible past singularity is an artefact of the idealized extension.
12. Close the loop (Example 27.5): read constant-r lines on the Penrose diagram to show outgoing and ingoing fronts both shrink inside the horizon, justifying the trapped surfaces of Chapter 26.
13. Exercises: verify the metric and coordinate relations, Rindler observers and their acceleration, and exponential redshift for uniform acceleration.

**Signature moves**

- Explicitly reuse the Rindler three-step recipe (null coordinates, exponentiate, rotate to T and X) so the Schwarzschild computation feels like a repeat, not a trick.
- Factorize a singular-looking metric into (regular function) x (exponential of null coordinates) x (flat light-cone metric) to show exactly which piece the coordinate change must absorb.
- Replace algebraic causal arguments with visual ones: once every radial light ray is at 45 degrees, questions like 'can a signal reach region I prime?' are answered by looking.
- Analogy table (eqn 27.12) reversing the reader's sense of which coordinates are natural: Kruskal behaves like inertial coordinates, Schwarzschild like a rocket's accelerating frame.
- Flip-book of embedding diagrams paired with horizontal slices on the Kruskal diagram to animate a wormhole opening and pinching shut.
- Reading a Penrose diagram with a coordinate grid by 'moving up to the next side-to-side line' to decide whether wavefront areas grow or shrink.
- Exercises that tie a geometric feature (horizon) to a kinematic effect (exponential redshift of an accelerated observer), seeding the next chapter.

## Section by section

### Chapter opening — p.280 (pdf 297)

After a Thomas Hardy epigraph on the mortality of stars, the authors state the goal inherited from Chapter 26: a single chart that is continuous across the horizon and still intuitive. Margin notes give short biographies of Martin Kruskal and George Szekeres.

**Concepts:** Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates, coordinate singularity

**Key moves**
- Frame the chapter as the answer to the open problem of Chapter 26

### §27.1 Enter the Kruskal metric — p.280 (pdf 297)

Explains why Eddington-Finkelstein charts are unsatisfactory (each smooths only ingoing or only outgoing rays at r_S), restarts from ds^2 = -(1 - 2M/r) dU dV, rewrites the prefactor via the tortoise relation, exponentiates the null coordinates exactly as for Rindler space, and forms T and X to obtain the Kruskal metric (27.6), regular at r = 2M. The inverse relations are given in a margin note.

**Concepts:** Eddington-Finkelstein null coordinates, tortoise coordinate, exponential rescaling of null coordinates, Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates, Kruskal-Szekeres line element, coordinate singularity, conformally flat radial sector

**Key moves**
- Substitute r* = (V - U)/2 to write 1 - 2M/r = (2M/r) e^{-r/2M} e^{(V-U)/4M}.
- Define u = -e^{-U/4M}, v = e^{V/4M}, so du dv carries the factor e^{(V-U)/4M}/16M^2 that cancels the exponential.
- Result ds^2 = -(32M^3 e^{-r/2M}/r) du dv, then T = (u + v)/2, X = (v - u)/2 gives a conformal factor times -dT^2 + dX^2.
- Observe the prefactor is finite and non-zero at r = 2M: the horizon singularity is gone.

### Reading the Kruskal diagram and the infalling astronaut — p.282 (pdf 299)

A guided tour of the X-T plane: constant-r curves are hyperbolae, r = 2M becomes the crossed lines X = +/-T dividing the plane into four regions, r = 0 is a pair of spacelike hyperbolae T^2 - X^2 = 1 (past and future), constant-t surfaces are straight lines through the origin, and Schwarzschild coordinates cover only the half-plane T > -X (region I outside, region II inside). Example 27.2 then shows an astronaut crossing into region II and a hovering observer on a hyperbola receiving ever sparser pulses, while pulses sent from inside end on the singularity.

**Concepts:** Kruskal diagram, Kruskal regions I, II, I', II', constant-r hyperbolae, constant-t lines in Kruskal coordinates, event horizon, curvature singularity at r = 0, static (hovering) observer, infalling observer and signal delay

**Key moves**
- Each point of the diagram stands for a 2-sphere of area 4 pi r^2.
- r = 2M gives X^2 - T^2 = 0, so the horizon is a pair of null lines.
- t is a hyperbolic angle: t = +infinity on X = T, t = -infinity on X = -T, and t = 0 on T = 0 outside but on X = 0 inside.
- Causal questions reduce to checking whether a path can stay within 45 degrees of vertical.

### Rindler analogy (Example 27.3) — p.283 (pdf 300)

Motivates the still-strange coordinates using Wolfgang Rindler's analogy. Hyperbolic coordinates T = x sinh t, X = x cosh t in flat space describe a family of uniformly accelerated observers with proper acceleration 1/x, and the Minkowski metric becomes the Rindler metric; swapping sinh and cosh covers the future and past wedges. A further substitution produces a line element shaped like Schwarzschild, giving the correspondence Minkowski:Kruskal as accelerating:Schwarzschild. Margin notes discuss rigid acceleration (the back of a rocket must accelerate harder) and why constant-t lines are straight.

**Concepts:** Rindler coordinates, uniformly accelerated observer, Born-rigid acceleration, Rindler-Kruskal analogy, Kruskal extension

**Key moves**
- Recall that X^2 - T^2 = x^2 is the world line of constant proper acceleration 1/x.
- Apply the hyperbolic change of variables and compute -dT^2 + dX^2 = -x^2 dt^2 + dx^2 in the right wedge.
- Use T = x cosh t, X = x sinh t in the top and bottom wedges, where the roles of the time and space coordinates are exchanged.
- Substitute x^2 = 2r - 1 (with a sign flip inside) to make one formula resemble the Schwarzschild metric in all wedges.
- Conclude that the Kruskal chart is the 'inertial' one and the Schwarzschild chart the accelerated one.

### §27.2 Wormholes — p.284 (pdf 301)

Takes the whole Kruskal plane seriously. Region I' is asymptotically flat at large negative X, so it is another exterior universe; region II' is reached only by past-directed curves from I', making its past singularity a white hole, and the primed side runs with t reversed. The T = 0 slice is an embedding-diagram wormhole with a throat of radius 2M joining the two universes. Nevertheless no timelike or null curve joins I and I'. Example 27.4 shows the spacelike slices are not static beyond the horizons: moving the slice up in T, the two sheets are separate, meet at a pinch, open into a throat of maximum radius 2M at T = 0, and pinch off again before any signal could cross.

**Concepts:** Kruskal extension, Einstein-Rosen bridge, white hole, time reversal of the primed universe, non-traversability of the Schwarzschild wormhole, embedding diagram, static region of Schwarzschild, dynamical evolution of the wormhole throat

**Key moves**
- Asymptotic flatness at X -> -infinity identifies region I' as a second exterior.
- Past-directed world lines fall from I' into II' just as future-directed ones fall from I into II, so II' is a time-reversed black hole.
- Light cones at 45 degrees show no causal curve from I to I'.
- Stack embedding diagrams for horizontal slices at increasing T to show the throat opening and closing faster than light could cross.

### §27.3 Another Penrose diagram — p.285 (pdf 302)

Presents the conformal diagram of the Kruskal extension: the two exteriors, the black- and white-hole regions, horizontal spacelike singularities at the top and bottom edges, and the infinities i+, i-, i0 and scri+/- that exist because each exterior is asymptotically flat. Unlike Robertson-Walker models, not every world line ends on a singularity. The past singularity is visible from region I (called naked here) and is blamed on the idealized extension. A grid of constant-r and constant-t lines is added, and Example 27.5 uses it to show that inside the horizon both outgoing and ingoing light fronts move to smaller r, i.e. trapped surfaces.

**Concepts:** Penrose diagram, conformal infinity, spacelike singularity as boundary of spacetime, naked singularity, trapped surface, Kruskal regions I, II, I', II'

**Key moves**
- Compactify so that singularities and infinities sit at the finite edges of the diagram.
- Contrast with Robertson-Walker Penrose diagrams where all world lines meet the singularity.
- Rotate the role of the grid lines: inside the horizon, 'side to side' lines are constant r, so the next future moment has smaller r.
- Outgoing and ingoing fronts both shrink in region II: closed trapped surfaces, confirming the Chapter 26 motivation.

### Chapter summary and exercises — p.287 (pdf 304)

The summary lists three points: Kruskal coordinates remove the r_S singularity and invite an extension, their relation to Schwarzschild parallels inertial versus accelerating Minkowski coordinates, and the conformal picture places the singularity at the edge of spacetime. Five exercises verify the coordinate algebra, treat Rindler observers and their covariant acceleration, and derive exponential redshift for a uniformly accelerating observer.

**Concepts:** Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates, Rindler coordinates, exponential redshift of accelerated observer, relativistic Doppler shift

**Key moves**
- Exercises connect the Rindler picture to exponential redshift, preparing Hawking and Unruh radiation in Chapter 28.

## Concepts

### Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates

*definition · core* · also: Kruskal coordinates, (T, X) coordinates, Kruskal-Szekeres chart

Coordinates (T, X, theta, phi) on the Schwarzschild geometry defined implicitly by (1 - r/2M) e^{r/2M} = T^2 - X^2 and T/X = tanh(t/4M) in the exterior, in which the radial part of the metric is a positive function of r times the flat form -dT^2 + dX^2; they are regular across r = 2M and extend to cover the entire maximally extended spacetime.

**How introduced:** Presented as the completion of the Chapter 26 programme: take Eddington-Finkelstein null coordinates, exponentiate them as in the Rindler toy model, then form sum and difference. Interpreted afterwards through a Kruskal diagram and Rindler's analogy.

**Prerequisites:** Eddington-Finkelstein null coordinates, tortoise coordinate, exponential rescaling of null coordinates, Rindler coordinates

$$
u=-e^{-U/4M},\; v=e^{V/4M}
$$

$$
T=\tfrac{1}{2}(u+v),\; X=\tfrac{1}{2}(v-u)
$$

$$
T^2-X^2=\left(1-\frac{r}{2M}\right)e^{r/2M}
$$

$$
\frac{T}{X}=\tanh\frac{t}{4M}\ \text{(region I)}
$$

**Notes:** The book's T and X are dimensionless (r = 0 sits at T^2 - X^2 = 1). Other texts normalize differently or use the null pair (u, v) directly as 'Kruskal coordinates'.

**Where:** GA p.280 (pdf 297); GA §27.1 p.280 (pdf 297); GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298); GA p.287 (pdf 304)

### Kruskal-Szekeres line element

*mathematical-object · core* · also: Kruskal metric

The Schwarzschild metric written in Kruskal coordinates, ds^2 = (32M^3/r) e^{-r/2M} (-dT^2 + dX^2) + r^2 dOmega^2, with r = r(T, X) given implicitly; its coefficients are finite and non-degenerate at r = 2M and blow up only at r = 0.

**How introduced:** Obtained in Example 27.1 and the following text by successive substitutions starting from -(1 - 2M/r) dU dV; checked by noting that nothing goes wrong at r = 2M.

**Prerequisites:** Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates, Schwarzschild metric, coordinate singularity

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2=\frac{32M^3e^{-r/2M}}{r}(-\mathrm{d}T^2+\mathrm{d}X^2)+r^2\mathrm{d}\Omega^2
$$

**Notes:** Asymptotically the conformal factor does not approach 1 in these coordinates; the book's remark that the metric becomes flat as X -> infinity refers to the geometry, not to the component form.

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298)

### coordinate singularity

*definition · revisited* · also: removable singularity, horizon singularity of Schwarzschild coordinates

A place where metric components in a particular chart diverge or become degenerate although curvature invariants stay finite, so that a different choice of coordinates removes the problem; r = 2M in Schwarzschild coordinates is the standard example.

**How introduced:** Revisited: the Kruskal metric is regular at r = 2M, which the authors present as the definitive demonstration of the claim made in Chapters 25 and 26. A margin note quotes Synge's remark that the singularity would never have been suspected had these coordinates been used first.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild metric, Kruskal-Szekeres line element

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298); GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299); GA p.287 (pdf 304)

### curvature singularity at r = 0

*phenomenon · developed* · also: physical singularity, Schwarzschild singularity, future singularity, past singularity

The genuine singularity of the Schwarzschild geometry, where tidal curvature diverges; in Kruskal coordinates it is the pair of spacelike hyperbolae T^2 - X^2 = 1, one to the future (bounding region II) and one to the past (bounding region II').

**How introduced:** Shown as thick lines with the excluded region shaded in every Kruskal diagram; discussed as an 'edge' of spacetime when the Penrose diagram is drawn.

**Prerequisites:** Kruskal diagram, coordinate singularity

$$
T^2-X^2=1\quad (r=0)
$$

**Notes:** Because it is spacelike, the future singularity is a moment in the infaller's future, not a point at the centre of space; the book implies this through the diagrams without saying so directly.

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298); GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299); GA §27.3 p.286 (pdf 303)

### tortoise coordinate

*definition · revisited* · also: r*, Regge-Wheeler coordinate

The radial coordinate r* = r + 2M ln|r/2M - 1| for which radial null geodesics of Schwarzschild satisfy t = +/- r* + const; it tends to minus infinity at the horizon.

**How introduced:** Used, not re-derived: its expression in terms of the null coordinates, r* = (V - U)/2, is the key substitution of Example 27.1.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild metric

$$
r^*=r+2M\ln\left|\frac{r}{2M}-1\right|=\frac{V-U}{2}
$$

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.280 (pdf 297)

### Eddington-Finkelstein null coordinates

*definition · revisited* · also: U = t - r*, V = t + r*, retarded and advanced time

Null coordinates U = t - r* (constant along outgoing radial light rays) and V = t + r* (constant along ingoing ones), in which the radial Schwarzschild metric takes the form -(1 - 2M/r) dU dV.

**How introduced:** Taken over from Chapter 26 as the starting point; the chapter opens by pointing out that charts using only one of them leave the other family of rays badly behaved at r_S.

**Prerequisites:** tortoise coordinate, null (light-cone) coordinates

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2=-\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\mathrm{d}U\,\mathrm{d}V+r^2\mathrm{d}\Omega^2
$$

**Notes:** Capital U, V here are the logarithmic null coordinates, while in the Rindler Example 26.3 capital letters denoted the exponentiated ones: the letter roles are swapped between chapters.

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.280 (pdf 297)

### null (light-cone) coordinates

*technique · revisited* · also: light-cone coordinates

Coordinates labelled by families of null curves (in 2D, u = t - x and v = t + x), in which a flat metric reads -du dv; they turn radial light rays into coordinate lines and make light-cone structure explicit.

**How introduced:** Assumed from Chapters 19 and 26 and used as the scaffolding of the construction.

$$
-\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v=-\mathrm{d}T^2+\mathrm{d}X^2
$$

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.280 (pdf 297); GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298)

### exponential rescaling of null coordinates

*technique · developed* · also: Rindler trick, exponentiating the null coordinates

Replacing null coordinates by monotonic exponentials of themselves, e.g. u = -e^{-U/4M}, v = e^{V/4M}, so that a metric of the form (regular function) x e^{(V-U)/4M} dU dV becomes (regular function) x du dv, and the finite ranges of u and v can then be extended past the old coordinate boundary.

**How introduced:** Presented as the same step already used for Rindler space; its necessity is made visible by first factorizing the metric into a regular r-dependent piece, an exponential of null coordinates, and the flat light-cone metric.

**Prerequisites:** null (light-cone) coordinates, Eddington-Finkelstein null coordinates

$$
u=-e^{-U/4M},\quad v=e^{V/4M}
$$

$$
\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v=\frac{1}{16M^2}e^{(V-U)/4M}\mathrm{d}U\,\mathrm{d}V
$$

**Notes:** The rate 1/4M is fixed by the requirement that the exponential cancels exactly; it equals the surface gravity of the hole (not named in this chapter).

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.280 (pdf 297); GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298)

### conformally flat radial sector

*identity · developed* · also: 45-degree light cones, conformal factor times Minkowski

In Kruskal coordinates the (T, X) part of the metric is a positive function times -dT^2 + dX^2, so radial null rays satisfy dT = +/- dX everywhere and all light cones in the diagram have Minkowski shape.

**How introduced:** Stated right after the metric: by construction every radial null geodesic is a 45-degree line, not just one family as with Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates; future cones point to increasing T.

**Prerequisites:** Kruskal-Szekeres line element, light cone

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2=0\ \Rightarrow\ \mathrm{d}T=\pm\mathrm{d}X
$$

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298); GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299)

### Kruskal diagram

*mathematical-object · core* · also: X-T plane diagram, Kruskal spacetime diagram

A spacetime diagram of the Schwarzschild geometry in the (X, T) plane with angles suppressed, so each point is a 2-sphere; light rays are at 45 degrees, horizons are the diagonals, the r = 0 singularities are hyperbolae, and constant-r and constant-t curves form a hyperbolic grid.

**How introduced:** Introduced through Figs 27.1-27.3 and then used as a working tool for infall, signalling, the extension and traversability.

**Prerequisites:** Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates, conformally flat radial sector

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298); GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299); GA §27.2 p.284 (pdf 301)

### Kruskal regions I, II, I', II'

*definition · core* · also: four quadrants of the Kruskal diagram

The four regions into which the horizons X = +/-T divide the Kruskal plane: I (X > |T|) is our exterior, II (T > |X|) the black-hole interior ending at the future singularity, I' (X < -|T|) a second exterior, and II' (T < -|X|) the white-hole interior beginning at the past singularity.

**How introduced:** Labelled on Fig. 27.1; the text notes that ordinary Schwarzschild coordinates cover I (r > 2M) and II (r < 2M), i.e. the half-plane T > -X, and that a collapsing star only passes through I and II.

**Prerequisites:** Kruskal diagram, event horizon

$$
\text{I: }X>|T|,\ \text{II: }T>|X|,\ \text{I}': X<-|T|,\ \text{II}': T<-|X|
$$

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298); GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299); GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300); GA §27.2 p.284 (pdf 301); GA §27.3 p.286 (pdf 303)

### constant-r hyperbolae

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: lines of constant r

Curves of fixed Schwarzschild radius in the Kruskal plane, X^2 - T^2 = (r/2M - 1) e^{r/2M}: timelike hyperbolae opening left and right for r > 2M, the null diagonals for r = 2M, and spacelike hyperbolae opening up and down for r < 2M, reaching T^2 - X^2 = 1 at r = 0.

**How introduced:** Fig. 27.2 plots examples; the first bullet of the diagram tour reads off the horizon and singularity as special members.

**Prerequisites:** Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates

$$
X^2-T^2=\left(\frac{r}{2M}-1\right)e^{r/2M}
$$

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298); GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299)

### constant-t lines in Kruskal coordinates

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: t as hyperbolic angle, lines of constant Schwarzschild time

Surfaces of fixed Schwarzschild time map to straight lines through the origin of the Kruskal plane: T/X = tanh(t/4M) outside the horizon and X/T = tanh(t/4M) inside, with t -> +infinity on X = T and t -> -infinity on X = -T; t behaves like a boost angle.

**How introduced:** Fig. 27.3 and the third bullet of the diagram tour; margin note 7 gives the analogous statement in Rindler coordinates.

**Prerequisites:** Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates, Rindler coordinates

$$
\frac{T}{X}=\tanh\frac{t}{4M}\ (\text{I}),\quad \frac{X}{T}=\tanh\frac{t}{4M}\ (\text{II})
$$

**Notes:** Inside the horizon constant-t lines are timelike and t is a spatial coordinate; the whole horizon line X = T corresponds to the single value t = +infinity, which is why Schwarzschild time cannot label events on it.

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298); GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299); GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300)

### event horizon

*definition · developed* · also: Schwarzschild horizon, r = 2M surface

The null surface r = 2M separating events that can send signals to the distant exterior from those that cannot; in Kruskal coordinates it is the pair of 45-degree lines X = +/-T, the future horizon (X = T, T > 0) bounding the black hole and the past horizon (X = -T, T < 0 on the region I side) bounding the white hole.

**How introduced:** Appears as the r = 2M hyperbola that degenerates into straight lines X = +/-T cutting the plane into four regions.

**Prerequisites:** constant-r hyperbolae, conformally flat radial sector

$$
r=2M\iff X=\pm T
$$

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299); GA §27.3 p.286 (pdf 303)

### static (hovering) observer

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: observer at fixed r, accelerated Schwarzschild observer

An observer who keeps a fixed radius r > 2M, which requires continuous thrust; in the Kruskal diagram the world line is a constant-r hyperbola in region I, directly analogous to a uniformly accelerating rocket in Minkowski space.

**How introduced:** Example 27.2 places such an observer on a hyperbola receiving pulses; Example 27.3 explains why this is the natural analogue of a Rindler observer.

**Prerequisites:** constant-r hyperbolae, uniformly accelerated observer

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299); GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300)

### infalling observer and signal delay

*phenomenon · developed* · also: death of an astronaut revisited, infinite delay of signals near the horizon

A radially infalling body crosses r = 2M at finite proper time; pulses it emits at regular intervals reach a hovering observer at ever longer coordinate-time intervals, the arrival time diverging for emission approaching the horizon, while pulses emitted inside travel at 45 degrees into the future singularity.

**How introduced:** Example 27.2 with Figs 27.4 and 27.5: the result that previously needed integrals is read off a single diagram.

**Prerequisites:** Kruskal diagram, event horizon, static (hovering) observer

**Notes:** Links back to the exponential redshift derived in Chapter 26 (time constant 4M).

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299)

### Rindler coordinates

*definition · revisited* · also: accelerating Minkowski coordinates, Rindler metric, Rindler wedge

Coordinates (t, x, y, z) on flat spacetime defined in the right wedge by T = x sinh t, X = x cosh t, in which the metric reads -x^2 dt^2 + dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2; curves of constant x are uniformly accelerated world lines with proper acceleration 1/x and lines of constant t are straight rays through the origin. In the top and bottom wedges T = x cosh t, X = x sinh t gives x^2 dt^2 - dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2.

**How introduced:** Example 27.3, credited to Wolfgang Rindler, as a flat-space model whose coordinate behaviour mimics Schwarzschild; builds on the Rindler toy model of Chapter 26.

**Prerequisites:** uniformly accelerated observer, null (light-cone) coordinates

$$
T=x\sinh t,\ X=x\cosh t
$$

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2=-x^2\mathrm{d}t^2+\mathrm{d}x^2+\mathrm{d}y^2+\mathrm{d}z^2
$$

**Notes:** The time coordinate t here is a dimensionless boost parameter, unrelated to Schwarzschild t except through the analogy (t_Schwarzschild/4M plays its role).

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300); GA p.287 (pdf 304); GA p.288 (pdf 305)

### uniformly accelerated observer

*solution-or-model · revisited* · also: hyperbolic motion, constant proper acceleration

A world line in flat spacetime with constant proper acceleration g, X^2 - T^2 = g^{-2}, parametrized by proper time as T = g^{-1} sinh(g tau), X = g^{-1} cosh(g tau); it asymptotes to the null lines X = +/-T, which act as horizons for that observer.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapter 2 in Example 27.3 and used in Exercises 27.3 and 27.5.

$$
X^2-T^2=g^{-2}
$$

$$
T(\tau)=\frac{1}{g}\sinh g\tau,\ X(\tau)=\frac{1}{g}\cosh g\tau
$$

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300); GA p.287 (pdf 304); GA p.288 (pdf 305)

### Born-rigid acceleration

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: rigid rocket, differential acceleration of a rigid body

For an extended body to accelerate without its proper length changing, each point must follow its own hyperbola X^2 - T^2 = x^2 with proper acceleration 1/x, so the rear (smaller x) accelerates harder than the front; seen in the inertial frame the body length-contracts progressively.

**How introduced:** Margin note 6 and the horizontal bars in Fig. 27.6(a) joining different hyperbolae at equal T.

**Prerequisites:** uniformly accelerated observer, Rindler coordinates

$$
a=1/x
$$

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300)

### Rindler-Kruskal analogy

*principle · core* · also: Minkowski : accelerating :: Kruskal : Schwarzschild

The correspondence in which inertial Minkowski coordinates play the role of Kruskal coordinates and accelerating (Rindler) coordinates play the role of Schwarzschild coordinates: in both pairs the second chart covers only a wedge, has a coordinate singularity on the null boundary, and its time coordinate is a hyperbolic angle; a hovering Schwarzschild observer corresponds to a rocket.

**How introduced:** Built in Example 27.3 by transforming Minkowski space into hyperbolic coordinates, rewriting the result in a Schwarzschild-like form, and summarized as the analogy (27.12); used to argue Kruskal coordinates are the natural ones.

**Prerequisites:** Rindler coordinates, Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates, static (hovering) observer

$$
\text{Minkowski}\leftrightarrow\text{Kruskal},\quad\text{Rindler}\leftrightarrow\text{Schwarzschild}
$$

**Notes:** Precisely, the near-horizon region of Schwarzschild is Rindler space with proper distance rho = sqrt(8M(r - 2M)) and Rindler time t/4M; the book's global substitution x^2 = 2r - 1 is only a loose resemblance.

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300); GA §27.1 p.284 (pdf 301); GA p.287 (pdf 304)

### Kruskal extension

*solution-or-model · core* · also: maximal analytic extension of Schwarzschild, maximally extended Schwarzschild spacetime, eternal black hole

The spacetime obtained by letting Kruskal coordinates range over the whole region T^2 - X^2 < 1, adding to the Schwarzschild exterior and interior (I, II) a second exterior I' and a white-hole interior II'; it is the vacuum solution with no star, in which every geodesic either reaches infinity or a singularity.

**How introduced:** Raised as a puzzle after Example 27.2 (half the plane is unused), then interpreted physically in Section 27.2 and drawn as a Penrose diagram in Section 27.3; Fig. 27.1's caption stresses that a real collapsing star only uses regions I and II.

**Prerequisites:** Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates, Kruskal regions I, II, I', II'

**Notes:** The authors call the visible past singularity a likely artefact of this artificial extension.

**Where:** GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300); GA §27.2 p.284 (pdf 301); GA §27.3 p.286 (pdf 303); GA p.287 (pdf 304)

### Einstein-Rosen bridge

*phenomenon · developed* · also: Schwarzschild wormhole, wormhole throat, throat

The geometry of a spacelike slice of the Kruskal extension through both exteriors: two asymptotically flat sheets joined by a neck whose minimum areal radius is 2M on the T = 0 slice.

**How introduced:** Section 27.2 and Fig. 27.7 via an embedding diagram of the T = 0 slice.

**Prerequisites:** Kruskal extension, embedding diagram

$$
r_{\min}=2M\ \text{on}\ T=0
$$

**Notes:** The book does not use the name Einstein-Rosen bridge; it calls it a wormhole with a throat.

**Where:** GA §27.2 p.284 (pdf 301); GA §27.2 p.285 (pdf 302)

### white hole

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: time-reversed black hole, past singularity region

The region II' of the Kruskal extension: bounded to the past by the r = 0 singularity, from which everything in it must emerge, and able only to send matter and light out across the past horizon; the time reverse of the black-hole interior.

**How introduced:** Identified by noting that past-directed curves fall from I' into II' just as future-directed ones fall from I into II.

**Prerequisites:** Kruskal extension, Kruskal regions I, II, I', II'

**Where:** GA §27.2 p.284 (pdf 301); GA §27.3 p.286 (pdf 303)

### time reversal of the primed universe

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: reversed Schwarzschild time in region I'

In the second exterior I', the Schwarzschild time t increases toward the past as measured by the Kruskal T direction, so the primed side is a time-reversed copy of the unprimed side.

**How introduced:** Asserted in Section 27.2 when interpreting region II' as reached by past-directed world lines from I'.

**Prerequisites:** constant-t lines in Kruskal coordinates, Kruskal extension

**Where:** GA §27.2 p.284 (pdf 301)

### non-traversability of the Schwarzschild wormhole

*theorem · developed* · also: no travel between universes

No timelike or null curve connects exterior region I to exterior region I' in the Kruskal extension; any causal curve crossing a horizon ends on the future singularity.

**How introduced:** Answered first by inspection of 45-degree light cones on Fig. 27.1, then dynamically in Example 27.4 using a sequence of slices where the throat closes before a signal can cross.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein-Rosen bridge, conformally flat radial sector, Kruskal regions I, II, I', II'

**Where:** GA §27.2 p.284 (pdf 301); GA §27.2 p.285 (pdf 302)

### dynamical evolution of the wormhole throat

*phenomenon · developed* · also: pinch-off of the Einstein-Rosen bridge

Following spacelike slices through both exteriors in order of increasing T, the geometry changes: early slices hit the past singularity and the two sheets are disconnected, then they touch at a pinch, open into a throat reaching radius 2M at T = 0, shrink, pinch again at the future singularity and separate.

**How introduced:** Example 27.4 with the flip-book of embedding diagrams in Fig. 27.8; margin note 8 cautions that the choice of slicing is arbitrary.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein-Rosen bridge, static region of Schwarzschild, embedding diagram

**Where:** GA §27.2 p.285 (pdf 302)

### static region of Schwarzschild

*definition · introduced* · also: staticity only outside the horizon

Schwarzschild time translations t -> t + delta t leave the geometry unchanged and are timelike only in the exterior regions I and I'; inside the horizons the same shift is a spatial displacement, so there is no preferred static time and spatial slices through II or II' genuinely evolve.

**How introduced:** Opening argument of Example 27.4, motivating why slices of the extension need not look the same at different T.

**Prerequisites:** constant-t lines in Kruskal coordinates, event horizon

**Notes:** In modern language: the Killing vector d/dt is timelike in I and I', null on the horizons and spacelike in II and II'. The book does not use Killing-vector language here.

**Where:** GA §27.2 p.285 (pdf 302)

### embedding diagram

*technique · mention* · also: spatial slice embedded in R^3

A picture of a 2D slice (equatorial, fixed time) of a curved space drawn as a surface in flat 3D space so that distances within the surface are correct; used to show throats and funnels.

**How introduced:** Used without re-derivation for the T = 0 wormhole slice (Fig. 27.7) and for the sequence of slices in Fig. 27.8.

**Where:** GA §27.2 p.284 (pdf 301); GA §27.2 p.285 (pdf 302)

### Penrose diagram

*technique · revisited* · also: conformal diagram, Carter-Penrose diagram

A compactified spacetime diagram obtained by a conformal rescaling that brings infinities to finite distance while keeping radial light rays at 45 degrees, so the whole causal structure fits on the page.

**How introduced:** Section 27.3 reuses the Chapter 19 construction for the Kruskal extension, contrasting it with the Robertson-Walker diagrams.

**Prerequisites:** conformally flat radial sector, conformal infinity

**Where:** GA §27.3 p.285 (pdf 302); GA §27.3 p.286 (pdf 303); GA §27.3 p.287 (pdf 304)

### conformal infinity

*definition · revisited* · also: i+, i-, i0, future and past null infinity scri+/-

The boundary points and surfaces added in a Penrose diagram: future and past timelike infinity i+ and i- where timelike geodesics begin and end, spatial infinity i0, and future and past null infinity scri+ and scri- where light rays end and begin.

**How introduced:** Named on Fig. 27.9 for both exteriors; their presence is attributed to asymptotic flatness, so each exterior corner looks like half of the Minkowski diagram.

**Prerequisites:** Penrose diagram

**Where:** GA §27.3 p.286 (pdf 303)

### spacelike singularity as boundary of spacetime

*principle · developed* · also: singularity at the edge of spacetime

In a conformal diagram a curvature singularity is not an interior point but a boundary segment; for Schwarzschild both r = 0 singularities are spacelike horizontal edges at the top and bottom.

**How introduced:** Emphasized in Section 27.3 and in the chapter summary, contrasting with Robertson-Walker spacetimes where every world line meets the singularity.

**Prerequisites:** Penrose diagram, curvature singularity at r = 0

**Where:** GA §27.3 p.286 (pdf 303); GA p.287 (pdf 304)

### naked singularity

*definition · mention* · also: visible singularity

A singularity from which causal signals can reach distant observers, i.e. one not hidden behind an event horizon, so that it could influence the future of the exterior.

**How introduced:** Mentioned in Section 27.3: the past (white-hole) singularity can send light into region I, which the authors call naked and attribute to the idealized extension rather than realistic collapse.

**Prerequisites:** Penrose diagram, white hole

**Notes:** Many authors reserve 'naked' for singularities forming in the future of regular initial data (e.g. in some collapse models) and simply call the Kruskal past singularity a white hole.

**Where:** GA §27.3 p.286 (pdf 303)

### trapped surface

*definition · revisited* · also: closed trapped surface

A closed spacelike 2-surface (here a sphere of constant r) for which both the outgoing and the ingoing families of orthogonal future-directed light rays have decreasing area; in Schwarzschild every sphere with r < 2M is trapped.

**How introduced:** Example 27.5 revisits the Chapter 26 motivation, using the constant-r grid of the Penrose diagram to show that inside the horizon both wavefronts move to smaller r.

**Prerequisites:** Penrose diagram, Kruskal regions I, II, I', II'

**Where:** GA §27.3 p.286 (pdf 303); GA §27.3 p.287 (pdf 304)

### relativistic Doppler shift

*law · mention* · also: longitudinal Doppler factor

An observer receding at speed v along a light wave's direction measures frequency Omega' = Omega sqrt((1 - v)/(1 + v)), obtainable by evaluating the wave phase Omega(T - X) along the observer's proper-time parametrized world line.

**How introduced:** Exercise 27.5(a)-(b) as a warm-up for the accelerated observer.

$$
\Omega'=\Omega\left(\frac{1-v}{1+v}\right)^{1/2}
$$

**Where:** GA p.288 (pdf 305)

### exponential redshift of accelerated observer

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: horizon redshift for uniform acceleration

A uniformly accelerated observer receiving a light wave that chases it measures a frequency falling as Omega e^{-g tau}, because the wave phase along the hyperbola decays exponentially; this mirrors the exponential fading of signals from near a black-hole horizon.

**How introduced:** Exercise 27.5(c), explicitly framed as linking the redshift near horizons with the view of Schwarzschild coordinates as accelerating coordinates.

**Prerequisites:** uniformly accelerated observer, relativistic Doppler shift, Rindler-Kruskal analogy

$$
\omega(\tau)=\Omega e^{-g\tau}
$$

**Notes:** This is the classical kernel of the Unruh and Hawking effects treated in Chapter 28.

**Where:** GA p.288 (pdf 305)

## Key equations

### (27.1) Schwarzschild metric in null coordinates · supporting · GA §27.1 p.280 (pdf 297)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2=-\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\mathrm{d}U\,\mathrm{d}V+r^2\mathrm{d}\Omega^2,\qquad \mathrm{d}\Omega^2=\mathrm{d}\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi^2
$$

Starting point carried over from Chapter 26: the radial part is a function of r times the flat light-cone form, but that function vanishes at r = 2M.

**Symbols:** U = t - r*, V = t + r*; r defined implicitly through r*

### Tortoise coordinate in terms of null coordinates · derivation-step · GA §27.1 p.280 (pdf 297)

$$
r^*=r+2M\ln\left|\frac{r}{2M}-1\right|=\frac{V-U}{2}
$$

The substitution that converts the troublesome factor 1 - 2M/r into an exponential of V - U.

**Symbols:** r* tortoise coordinate; U, V null coordinates

### (27.2) Factorized two-dimensional metric · central · GA §27.1 p.280 (pdf 297)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2=-\left(\frac{2M}{r}e^{-r/2M}\right)e^{(V-U)/4M}\,\mathrm{d}U\,\mathrm{d}V
$$

The radial metric split into a factor regular at r = 2M, an exponential of the null coordinates, and the flat light-cone metric; the exponential is what the next substitution must remove.

**Symbols:** Follows from 1 - 2M/r = (2M/r)(r/2M - 1) and r/2M - 1 = e^{r*/2M} e^{-r/2M} outside the horizon

### (27.3) Exponentiated (Kruskal) null coordinates · central · GA §27.1 p.280 (pdf 297)

$$
u=-e^{-U/4M},\qquad v=e^{V/4M}
$$

Change of null coordinates that absorbs the exponential factor; u < 0 and v > 0 in region I, and the ranges can be extended beyond.

**Symbols:** u, v Kruskal null coordinates

### Product of the new null differentials (implicit step) · derivation-step · GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298)

$$
\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v=\frac{1}{16M^2}\,e^{(V-U)/4M}\,\mathrm{d}U\,\mathrm{d}V
$$

The Jacobian of (27.3) exactly reproduces the exponential in (27.2), leaving 16M^2 times the regular factor.

### (27.4) Metric in Kruskal null coordinates · central · GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2=-\frac{32M^3e^{-r/2M}}{r}\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v
$$

Radial Schwarzschild metric as a regular conformal factor times -du dv; nothing special happens at r = 2M.

**Symbols:** Printed in the book as du du; it must be du dv.

### (27.5) Kruskal time and space coordinates · supporting · GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298)

$$
T=\tfrac{1}{2}(u+v),\qquad X=\tfrac{1}{2}(v-u)
$$

Converts null coordinates into a timelike and a spacelike one, so that -du dv = -dT^2 + dX^2.

### (27.6) Kruskal metric · central · GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2=\frac{32M^3e^{-r/2M}}{r}\left(-\mathrm{d}T^2+\mathrm{d}X^2\right)+r^2\left(\mathrm{d}\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi^2\right)
$$

The Schwarzschild geometry in a chart regular across the horizon: conformally flat in the T-X plane, with singular behaviour only at r = 0.

**Symbols:** r = r(T, X) implicit via (27.13)

### (27.13) Kruskal-Schwarzschild coordinate relations (also margin note 4) · central · GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298)

$$
T^2-X^2=\left(1-\frac{r}{2M}\right)e^{r/2M},\qquad \frac{t}{4M}=\tanh^{-1}\!\left(\frac{T}{X}\right)
$$

Implicit map between Schwarzschild (t, r) and Kruskal (T, X): r labels hyperbolae and t labels rays through the origin. The t relation as written holds in region I.

**Symbols:** Given in margin note 4 on p.281 and set as Exercise 27.2 on p.287

### Constant-t lines inside the horizon · supporting · GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298)

$$
\frac{X}{T}=\tanh\frac{t}{4M}\quad(\text{region II})
$$

In the black-hole interior the roles of T and X in the t relation are exchanged, so constant-t lines are steep (timelike) rays.

### Horizon and singularity loci · supporting · GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299)

$$
r=2M:\ X^2-T^2=0\ \Rightarrow X=\pm T;\qquad r=0:\ X^2-T^2=-1
$$

Special members of the constant-r family: the horizon is a pair of null lines and the singularity a pair of spacelike hyperbolae.

### (27.7) Hyperbolic (Rindler) coordinates, left and right wedges · central · GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300)

$$
T=x\sinh t,\quad X=x\cosh t,\quad Y=y,\quad Z=z
$$

Flat-space coordinates adapted to a family of uniformly accelerating observers; curves of constant x are hyperbolae X^2 - T^2 = x^2.

**Symbols:** (T, X, Y, Z) inertial; (t, x, y, z) accelerating

### Proper acceleration of a hyperbolic world line (Example 27.3 and margin note 6) · supporting · GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300)

$$
X^2-T^2=x^2\ \Rightarrow\ a=\frac{1}{x}
$$

Each constant-x curve is a world line of constant proper acceleration 1/x; smaller x means harder acceleration.

### (27.8) Hyperbolic coordinates, top and bottom wedges · supporting · GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300)

$$
T=x\cosh t,\quad X=x\sinh t,\quad Y=y,\quad Z=z,\qquad X^2-T^2=-x^2
$$

Covers the future and past wedges, where T and X swap roles, just as t and r swap character inside a black hole.

### (27.9) Rindler metric · central · GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2=-x^2\mathrm{d}t^2+\mathrm{d}x^2+\mathrm{d}y^2+\mathrm{d}z^2
$$

Minkowski space seen in accelerating coordinates; the metric degenerates at x = 0, the null boundary of the wedge, as Schwarzschild does at r = 2M.

### (27.10) Metric in the top/bottom wedges · supporting · GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2=x^2\mathrm{d}t^2-\mathrm{d}x^2+\mathrm{d}y^2+\mathrm{d}z^2
$$

In the future and past wedges x is timelike and t spacelike.

### Constant-t lines are straight (margin note 7) · derivation-step · GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300)

$$
\frac{X}{T}=\coth t
$$

Rindler time is a hyperbolic angle, so its level sets are rays through the origin, like constant Schwarzschild t in Kruskal coordinates.

### (27.11) Schwarzschild-like form of the Rindler metric · supporting · GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2=-(2r-1)\,\mathrm{d}t^2+(2r-1)^{-1}\mathrm{d}r^2+\mathrm{d}y^2+\mathrm{d}z^2
$$

After x^2 = 2r - 1 (right/left wedges) or -x^2 = 2r - 1 (top/bottom), a single expression covers all wedges and mimics the structure -f dt^2 + f^{-1} dr^2 with f changing sign at r = 1/2.

**Symbols:** The book prints dx^2 in the second term; after the substitution it must be dr^2 (since dx^2 = dr^2/(2r - 1)).

### (27.12) Analogy table · central · GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300)

$$
\begin{pmatrix}\text{Minkowski coords}\\ \text{Kruskal coords}\end{pmatrix}\longleftrightarrow\begin{pmatrix}\text{Accelerating Minkowski coords}\\ \text{Schwarzschild coords}\end{pmatrix}
$$

Kruskal coordinates stand to Schwarzschild coordinates as inertial coordinates stand to a rocket's accelerating coordinates.

### (27.14) Proper-time parametrization of uniform acceleration · supporting · GA p.287 (pdf 304)

$$
T(\tau)=\frac{1}{g}\sinh g\tau,\quad X(\tau)=\frac{1}{g}\cosh g\tau,\qquad X^2-T^2=g^{-2}
$$

World line of an observer with constant proper acceleration g; reused as (27.16) in Exercise 27.5.

**Symbols:** g proper acceleration; tau proper time

### Inertial observer and plane wave (Exercise 27.5a) · derivation-step · GA p.288 (pdf 305)

$$
T(\tau)=\gamma\tau,\quad X(\tau)=\gamma v\tau,\qquad A=e^{i\Omega(T-X)}
$$

Set-up for reading off the observed frequency as the proper-time derivative of the wave phase along the world line.

### (27.15) Relativistic Doppler shift · supporting · GA p.288 (pdf 305)

$$
\Omega'=\Omega\left(\frac{1-v}{1+v}\right)^{1/2}
$$

Frequency seen by an observer receding along the wave's direction at speed v.

### (27.17) Exponential redshift for uniform acceleration · central · GA p.288 (pdf 305)

$$
\omega(\tau)=\Omega\,e^{-g\tau}
$$

Along the hyperbola T - X = -e^{-g tau}/g, so the observed frequency decays exponentially in proper time: the flat-space counterpart of the fading of signals from near a horizon.

## Worked examples

### Example 27.1 · standard · GA §27.1 p.280 (pdf 297)

**Problem:** Starting from the radial Schwarzschild metric in null coordinates, rewrite the factor 1 - 2M/r using r* = (V - U)/2, then remove the remaining exponential with a further coordinate change.

**Method:** Exponentiate the tortoise relation to get r/2M - 1 = e^{-r/2M} e^{(V-U)/4M}, substitute into 1 - 2M/r, then define u = -e^{-U/4M}, v = e^{V/4M} so du dv contains the same exponential; the text continues with T and X on the next page.

**Key insight:** The metric factors into a regular function of r, an exponential in the null coordinates, and a flat light-cone metric; the coordinate singularity lives entirely in the exponential, which a change of null coordinates can absorb, exactly as for Rindler space.

**Result:** ds^2 = -(2M/r) e^{-r/2M} e^{(V-U)/4M} dU dV, then ds^2 = -(32M^3 e^{-r/2M}/r) du dv.

**Concepts:** exponential rescaling of null coordinates, tortoise coordinate, Eddington-Finkelstein null coordinates, Kruskal-Szekeres line element

### Example · standard · GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298)

**Problem:** Complete the construction of the Kruskal metric and relate old and new coordinates.

**Method:** Set T = (u + v)/2, X = (v - u)/2 so that -du dv = -dT^2 + dX^2, restore the angular part, and from uv = -e^{r*/2M} and -v/u = e^{t/2M} obtain T^2 - X^2 = (1 - r/2M) e^{r/2M} and T/X = tanh(t/4M).

**Key insight:** Finite, non-zero conformal factor at r = 2M proves the horizon singularity is a coordinate effect; conformal flatness in T-X puts every radial light ray at 45 degrees.

**Result:** Kruskal metric (27.6) with relations in margin note 4 / (27.13).

**Concepts:** Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates, Kruskal-Szekeres line element, coordinate singularity, conformally flat radial sector

### Example · intro · GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299)

**Problem:** Map Schwarzschild structures onto the Kruskal plane: where are r = 2M, r = 0, constant r, constant t, and which region does the original chart cover?

**Method:** Read the implicit relations: fixed r fixes X^2 - T^2 (hyperbolae), r = 2M gives X = +/-T, r = 0 gives T^2 - X^2 = 1; fixed t fixes a ratio T/X or X/T (rays); check signs to place r > 2M in X > |T| and r < 2M in T > |X|.

**Key insight:** Schwarzschild (t, r) is a hyperbolic-polar chart on half of the plane (T > -X); t is an angle running from -infinity to infinity within each quadrant.

**Result:** Four regions; Schwarzschild coordinates cover I and II only.

**Concepts:** Kruskal diagram, Kruskal regions I, II, I', II', constant-r hyperbolae, constant-t lines in Kruskal coordinates

### Example 27.2 · intro · GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299)

**Problem:** Describe an astronaut falling into the hole on a Kruskal diagram, and the light pulses the astronaut sends to an observer staying at fixed radius.

**Method:** Draw the astronaut's world line always steeper than 45 degrees from region I across X = T into region II; draw the hovering observer as a constant-r hyperbola; join emission events to the hyperbola with 45-degree lines.

**Key insight:** Because the observer's hyperbola asymptotes to the horizon, successive 45-degree rays hit it at ever later times, and any ray leaving the astronaut inside region II can never reach X > T.

**Result:** Pulses arrive increasingly rarely, with arrival time diverging for emission near the horizon; pulses from inside end at the singularity together with the astronaut.

**Concepts:** infalling observer and signal delay, static (hovering) observer, event horizon, conformally flat radial sector

### Example 27.3 · standard · GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300)

**Problem:** Explain why Kruskal coordinates are natural by comparing with uniformly accelerated coordinates in flat spacetime.

**Method:** Write T = x sinh t, X = x cosh t (and the sinh/cosh swap for the top and bottom wedges), compute the transformed metric -x^2 dt^2 + dx^2 (or its sign-swapped version), substitute x^2 = +/-(2r - 1) to obtain a single Schwarzschild-like form, and tabulate the correspondence.

**Key insight:** Schwarzschild coordinates behave like the coordinates of a rocket crew: they cover only a wedge, have a degenerate time component at the wedge boundary, and use a hyperbolic angle as time. Kruskal coordinates play the inertial role.

**Result:** Rindler metric (27.9)-(27.10), Schwarzschild-like form (27.11), analogy (27.12).

**Concepts:** Rindler coordinates, uniformly accelerated observer, Rindler-Kruskal analogy, Born-rigid acceleration

### Example 27.4 · standard · GA §27.2 p.285 (pdf 302)

**Problem:** Given that the wormhole slice at T = 0 appears to connect two universes, follow how spacelike slices of the extension change with T and decide whether anything can pass through.

**Method:** Argue that time translation is a symmetry only in regions I and I', so slices through II and II' need not be identical; take horizontal slices at several T and draw each as an embedding diagram.

**Key insight:** The connection between universes is transient: it forms from a pinch at the past singularity, opens to radius 2M at T = 0 and pinches off at the future singularity faster than any causal signal can cross.

**Result:** No traveller or signal gets from I to I'; it ends at r = 0.

**Concepts:** dynamical evolution of the wormhole throat, non-traversability of the Schwarzschild wormhole, static region of Schwarzschild, embedding diagram

### Example 27.5 · intro · GA §27.3 p.287 (pdf 304)

**Problem:** Use the Penrose diagram with constant-r and constant-t lines to compare how a spherical light flash spreads outside and inside the horizon.

**Method:** Emit at a point P in region I and step up to the next future 'side-to-side' grid line, noting whether outgoing and ingoing fronts are at larger or smaller r; repeat at a point Q in region II, where side-to-side lines are constant r.

**Key insight:** Inside the horizon the future direction is the direction of decreasing r, so both fronts shrink: the spheres are trapped, which is the mechanism signalling the singularity from Chapter 26.

**Result:** Region I: outgoing front grows, ingoing shrinks. Region II: both shrink; closed trapped surfaces.

**Concepts:** trapped surface, Penrose diagram, Kruskal regions I, II, I', II'


## Analogies and intuitions

### Inertial versus rocket coordinates in flat space: Minkowski coordinates are to Rindler coordinates as Kruskal coordinates are to Schwarzschild coordinates. → Rindler-Kruskal analogy · strong · GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300)

Rebuilds the reader's intuition about which chart is natural: the Rindler wedge has a degenerate metric on its null boundary, a hyperbolic-angle time and a mirror wedge, exactly like the Schwarzschild exterior, so the awkwardness of Schwarzschild coordinates is attributed to their 'accelerated' character.

**Where it breaks down:** Minkowski space is flat everywhere and has no singularity; the Schwarzschild interior ends at r = 0 and is curved. The book's global substitution x^2 = 2r - 1 only mimics the shape of the metric; the accurate statement is that Schwarzschild near its horizon is approximately Rindler.

**App idea:** Side-by-side flat-space rocket and black-hole diagrams with synchronized cursors; a zoom slider on the black-hole side magnifies the neighbourhood of the horizon until the grid becomes indistinguishable from the Rindler one.

### A hovering observer outside a black hole is like a passenger in a constantly thrusting rocket. → static (hovering) observer · useful · GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299)

Both world lines are hyperbolae on the respective inertial-like diagram; the observer in Example 27.2 is explicitly kept in place by a rocket.

**Where it breaks down:** The hovering observer's required acceleration grows without bound near r = 2M and depends on r in a way the Rindler model reproduces only close to the horizon.

**App idea:** Thrust meter: drag the hovering radius toward 2M and watch the required proper acceleration climb while the hyperbola hugs the horizon.

### A rigid rocket: its tail must push harder than its nose. → Born-rigid acceleration · useful · GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300)

Horizontal bars between hyperbolae at equal T in Fig. 27.6(a) and margin note 6 show nested hyperbolae with accelerations 1/x.

**Where it breaks down:** Only a side remark here; not connected explicitly to the gravitational redshift between the top and bottom of a hovering tower, which it naturally explains.

### A white hole is a black hole run backward in time. → white hole · useful · GA §27.2 p.284 (pdf 301)

Region II' is defined by symmetry with II: past-directed curves fall into it from I' as future-directed ones fall from I into II, so everything in II' comes out of the past singularity.

**Where it breaks down:** Time-reversal symmetry of the vacuum solution does not imply white holes form in nature; realistic collapse removes regions I' and II'.

**App idea:** Play/reverse toggle on the Kruskal diagram that reflects T -> -T, turning an infall animation into an emission from the white hole.

### The throat as a tube joining two sheets of paper. → Einstein-Rosen bridge · useful · GA §27.2 p.284 (pdf 301)

Fig. 27.7's embedding picture gives a concrete spatial image of the two universes joined at radius 2M.

**Where it breaks down:** It shows a single slice and tempts the reader to think one could walk through; Example 27.4 is needed to show the tube exists only fleetingly and cannot be crossed.

### A bridge that collapses before a runner can cross it. → non-traversability of the Schwarzschild wormhole · strong · GA §27.2 p.285 (pdf 302)

Example 27.4 narrates the throat opening and pinching shut too fast for any signal to reach the far side.

**Where it breaks down:** The 'speed' of pinching depends on the slicing, which is arbitrary (margin note 8); the invariant statement is the causal one read from light cones.

**App idea:** Race animation: a light pulse travels along the embedding surface as the throat closes; learners adjust the launch time and always lose.

### The singularity as the edge of the map rather than a place on it. → spacelike singularity as boundary of spacetime · useful · GA §27.3 p.286 (pdf 303)

The Penrose diagram places both r = 0 singularities on the boundary, alongside the infinities, reinforcing the Chapter 19 view.

**Where it breaks down:** Boundary language is intuitive but the precise notion of a singular boundary is subtle and not developed.

### Synge's remark: had spherical symmetry been approached with good coordinates, no one would have suspected a singularity at the horizon. → coordinate singularity · weak · GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299)

Encouragement in margin note 5 to push through the unfamiliar coordinates by stressing that they are the simplest description.

**Where it breaks down:** Rhetorical rather than explanatory.


## Misconceptions addressed

### The Schwarzschild radius is a real singularity where spacetime breaks down. · GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298)

**Correction:** In Kruskal coordinates all metric components are finite and regular at r = 2M; only r = 0 is singular.

**Why tempting:** Schwarzschild metric components diverge there and infinite coordinate times appear in calculations.

### Kruskal coordinates are an artificial mathematical trick with no physical naturalness. · GA §27.1 p.284 (pdf 301)

**Correction:** They are the analogue of inertial coordinates; Schwarzschild coordinates are the awkward, accelerated-frame description.

**Why tempting:** The definition is implicit and exponential and seems pulled from nowhere.

### Each point of the Kruskal diagram is a single event. · GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298)

**Correction:** The angular coordinates are suppressed, so each point represents a whole 2-sphere of area 4 pi r^2.

**Why tempting:** Two-dimensional spacetime diagrams usually show events.

### The wormhole in the Kruskal extension lets a traveller reach another universe. · GA §27.2 p.284 (pdf 301)

**Correction:** No timelike or null curve connects I and I'; the throat pinches off before any signal can cross, and every crossing path ends at r = 0.

**Why tempting:** The T = 0 embedding picture looks like an open tunnel.

### The Schwarzschild geometry is static everywhere, so its spatial slices never change. · GA §27.2 p.285 (pdf 302)

**Correction:** Time-translation symmetry is timelike only in regions I and I'; inside the horizons t is spacelike and slices genuinely evolve.

**Why tempting:** The metric components do not depend on t, suggesting a static solution throughout.

### A black hole formed by a collapsing star comes with a second universe and a white hole. · GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298)

**Correction:** Only regions I and II are relevant to a collapsing star; the star's interior replaces the rest, and the visible past singularity is attributed to the idealized extension.

**Why tempting:** The vacuum Kruskal diagram is shown as 'the' black-hole spacetime.

### The future singularity r = 0 is a point at the centre of the black hole that one could avoid by steering sideways. · GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299)

**Correction:** In the Kruskal and Penrose diagrams r = 0 is a spacelike line lying in the future of every event in region II; it is a moment, not a place, so no steering avoids it.

**Why tempting:** Everyday language of a 'central point', and r = 0 being the origin in flat space.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Light emitted anywhere just outside the horizon never reaches a distant observer. · GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299)

**Correction:** Every pulse emitted outside r = 2M eventually arrives, though the arrival time grows without bound as the emission point approaches the horizon; only light emitted on or inside the horizon never arrives.

**Why tempting:** The book's phrasing that pulses just above the horizon arrive at t -> infinity can be read as 'never'.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The hyperbolae in the top wedge of the Rindler picture are world lines of accelerating observers. · GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300)

**Correction:** Curves X^2 - T^2 = -x^2 are spacelike; no observer can follow them. They are the analogues of constant-r surfaces inside a black hole.

**Why tempting:** The text refers to 'accelerating world lines in regions I and II', and the curves look like rotated copies of the region I hyperbolae.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Schwarzschild time t measures the passage of time everywhere, so t = infinity on the horizon means the infaller never crosses. · GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299)

**Correction:** t is a hyperbolic angle that labels the whole horizon line with a single value t = infinity; the infaller crosses at finite proper time and finite Kruskal T.

**Why tempting:** Coordinate time is what distant observers use, and it diverges along the infall.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Because a black hole is a region where r < 2M, a hovering observer could stay at fixed r inside it with enough thrust. · GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299)

**Correction:** Constant-r curves inside the horizon are spacelike hyperbolae; following one would require moving faster than light.

**Why tempting:** Outside, fixed r is achievable with thrust, and the diagram tour lists constant-r curves in all regions alike.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### The astronaut's farewell pulses · GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299)

**Setup:** An astronaut falls radially into a Schwarzschild black hole, sending regular light pulses to a colleague who hovers at fixed radius with rockets.

**Lesson:** Received intervals stretch without limit as the astronaut approaches the horizon; pulses emitted inside never arrive; all of this is visible from 45-degree rays on the Kruskal diagram.

**App idea:** Animated pulse ladder with receiver clock plot (see Fig. 27.5 redesign).

### Coordinates inside an accelerating rocket · GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300)

**Setup:** A rocket accelerates uniformly through empty space; its crew labels events with coordinates adapted to their motion (hyperbolic Rindler coordinates).

**Lesson:** The crew's chart covers only a wedge, has a horizon and a degenerate metric on it, just as Schwarzschild coordinates do; inertial coordinates remove the problem, as Kruskal coordinates do.

**App idea:** Rocket crew view versus inertial view of the same set of events, with a toggle to show the Rindler horizon.

### Trying to cross the wormhole · GA §27.2 p.284 (pdf 301)

**Setup:** A traveller in universe I heads for the throat hoping to emerge in universe I'.

**Lesson:** The light-cone structure forbids it; seen as a sequence of slices, the throat closes before the traveller reaches its middle, and the traveller meets r = 0.

**App idea:** Wormhole flip-book race (see Fig. 27.8 redesign).

### A flash inside and outside the horizon · GA §27.3 p.287 (pdf 304)

**Setup:** A spherical light flash is set off at an event outside the horizon and another inside, and the areas of the outgoing and ingoing fronts are tracked.

**Lesson:** Outside, the outgoing front grows; inside, both fronts shrink because the future points toward smaller r: trapped surfaces.

**App idea:** Click-to-flash on the Penrose diagram with area readouts of both fronts.

### Chasing a light wave with constant acceleration · GA p.288 (pdf 305)

**Setup:** An observer with constant proper acceleration g moves along a hyperbola while a plane light wave comes up from behind.

**Lesson:** The measured frequency decays as e^{-g tau}: acceleration alone yields the exponential redshift characteristic of horizons.

**App idea:** Wave crests drawn at 45 degrees on a Minkowski diagram crossing a hyperbolic world line, with a live frequency readout on a log scale.

## Notation and conventions

- **Units:** Geometric units G = c = 1; mass M has dimensions of length and the Schwarzschild radius is r_S = 2M. — Fig. 27.9 labels the horizon as r = 2m (lowercase). (GA §27.1 p.280 (pdf 297))
- **Metric signature:** (-, +, +, +): timelike intervals have ds^2 < 0; the Kruskal metric has -dT^2 + dX^2. (GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298))
- **Angular part:** dOmega^2 = dtheta^2 + sin^2 theta dphi^2; dropped from intermediate steps and restored at the end. — Margin note 3. (GA §27.1 p.280 (pdf 297))
- **Null coordinate letters:** Capital U = t - r*, V = t + r* are Eddington-Finkelstein null coordinates; lowercase u = -e^{-U/4M}, v = e^{V/4M} are the exponentiated Kruskal null coordinates. — Reversed from Chapter 26's Rindler example, where lowercase u, v were logarithmic and capital U, V exponentiated. (GA §27.1 p.280 (pdf 297))
- **Kruskal coordinates:** T = (u + v)/2 timelike, X = (v - u)/2 spacelike, both dimensionless; r = 0 at T^2 - X^2 = 1. — Other texts sometimes rescale by 4M or use different letters (e.g. U, V or u, v for Kruskal null coordinates). (GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298))
- **Region labels:** I (our exterior), II (black-hole interior), I' (second exterior), II' (white-hole interior). — Other books often use I, II, III, IV in varying order. (GA §27.1 p.281 (pdf 298))
- **Diagram conventions:** Kruskal and Penrose diagrams suppress the 2-sphere; radial light rays at 45 degrees; future is up; singularities drawn as thick, jagged, hatched or double lines with the region beyond shaded. (GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299))
- **Rindler coordinates in Example 27.3:** (t, x, y, z) denote accelerating coordinates and (T, X, Y, Z) inertial ones; x is the inverse proper acceleration. — The letters t and x clash with Schwarzschild t and the Kruskal X; the tutor should disambiguate. (GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300))
- **Conformal infinities:** i+ future timelike, i- past timelike, i0 spatial infinity; scri+ and scri- future and past null infinity. — Same symbols as Chapter 19. (GA §27.3 p.286 (pdf 303))
- **Acceleration and redshift in exercises:** g denotes proper acceleration, tau proper time, Omega the wave's angular frequency in the inertial frame; acceleration vector a = nabla_u u. (GA p.288 (pdf 305))

## Teaching gems

### Present the Schwarzschild calculation as a replay of the Rindler calculation: same three moves (null coordinates, exponentiate, sum and difference), different function. · GA §27.1 p.280 (pdf 297)

**Why it works:** Learners who survived the flat-space toy model recognise each step, so the new difficulty is only bookkeeping and the method becomes a transferable recipe.

**App idea:** Two-column step-through derivation: Rindler on the left, Schwarzschild on the right, advancing together with matching steps highlighted.

### Factorize the metric into a regular piece, an exponential of null coordinates, and the flat light-cone form before choosing the new coordinates. · GA §27.1 p.280 (pdf 297)

**Why it works:** It shows precisely what the coordinate change must accomplish, turning an apparently magical substitution into an obvious one.

**App idea:** Colour-coded equation where the learner drags candidate substitutions onto the exponential factor and sees which choice cancels it.

### Flip the intuition: Kruskal coordinates are the inertial ones and Schwarzschild the accelerated ones (analogy table 27.12). · GA §27.1 p.283 (pdf 300)

**Why it works:** Replaces 'weird coordinates' with a familiar special-relativity picture and explains why Schwarzschild t freezes at the horizon.

**App idea:** Synchronized rocket-in-flat-space and hovering-near-black-hole diagrams with a horizon-zoom slider.

### Once every radial light ray is at 45 degrees, answer causal questions by eye: can a path from I reach I'? can a pulse from II escape? · GA §27.2 p.284 (pdf 301)

**Why it works:** Learners check claims themselves with a ruler instead of trusting integrals, which builds durable causal intuition.

**App idea:** Causal-connectivity puzzle: pick two events; the app shades the future light cone of the first and asks whether the second is reachable before revealing the answer.

### Tell the wormhole story as a flip-book of slices so non-traversability is seen as the throat closing too fast. · GA §27.2 p.285 (pdf 302)

**Why it works:** Converts an abstract causal theorem into a vivid dynamic image, while margin note 8 keeps the slicing honest.

**App idea:** Scrubbable T slider linking a Kruskal slice line to a morphing 3D embedding with a racing light pulse.

### Read trapped surfaces off a grid on the Penrose diagram by stepping to the next future side-to-side line and asking whether r went up or down. · GA §27.3 p.287 (pdf 304)

**Why it works:** A simple, repeatable visual test that makes 'the future points to smaller r inside' concrete.

**App idea:** Click-a-flash tool on the gridded Penrose diagram displaying the areas of the outgoing and ingoing spheres one step later.

### Hovering observer as a hyperbola receiving a ladder of 45-degree pulses. · GA §27.1 p.282 (pdf 299)

**Why it works:** The crowding of rungs near the horizon makes infinite delay and redshift visually self-evident.

**App idea:** Animated pulse ladder with live plot of reception interval versus emission proper time.

### Derive exponential redshift from a plane wave's phase along an accelerated hyperbola (Exercise 27.5). · GA p.288 (pdf 305)

## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Printed eqn 27.4 reads 'du du' instead of 'du dv'.** — A careful reader may think the metric is degenerate or that u alone appears. *Suggestion:* Show the corrected form -(32M^3 e^{-r/2M}/r) du dv and include the Jacobian step du dv = e^{(V-U)/4M} dU dV/16M^2.
- **Eqn 27.11 keeps dx^2 after substituting x^2 = 2r - 1; the correct term is (2r - 1)^{-1} dr^2. The resemblance to Schwarzschild is also only schematic (a linear function 2r - 1 vs 1 - 2M/r).** — Learners may try to verify the substitution and fail, or conclude that flat space literally is Schwarzschild. *Suggestion:* State the corrected metric and supplement with the precise near-horizon statement: with rho = sqrt(8M(r - 2M)), the Schwarzschild t-r metric is approximately -rho^2 (dt/4M)^2 + drho^2, i.e. Rindler with time t/4M.
- **Fig. 27.6(b) labels the region II hyperbolae 't = 1, 2, 3' and its caption says region II shows constant t, while eqn 27.8 makes those curves constant x; the text also calls them accelerating world lines, though they are spacelike.** — Confuses which coordinate is timelike inside the wedge and suggests observers can move along spacelike curves. *Suggestion:* Label them constant x (timelike coordinate in that wedge) and describe them as analogues of constant-r surfaces inside the horizon, not as world lines.
- **Captions of Figs 27.7 and 27.8 call the slices 'timelike', but they are spacelike hypersurfaces (the text says so).** — Undermines the distinction between spatial slices and world lines just when the wormhole argument depends on it. *Suggestion:* Refer to them consistently as spacelike slices at fixed T.
- **Typo: the primed universe is described as 'regions I' and I''' where I' and II' is meant.** — Minor, but region labels are the backbone of the discussion. *Suggestion:* Use I' and II'.
- **Sign handling across regions is glossed: u = -e^{-U/4M}, v = e^{V/4M} and t/4M = artanh(T/X) are valid in region I; inside the horizon the absolute value in r* flips a sign (u > 0), and regions I', II' require further sign choices.** — Learners attempting to verify region II or I' relations get sign contradictions. *Suggestion:* Tabulate the signs of u and v in each quadrant (I: u<0<v; II: u,v>0; I': v<0<u; II': u,v<0) and derive the relations separately in each.
- **Null-coordinate letters swap roles between Chapter 26 (lowercase logarithmic, capital exponentiated) and Chapter 27 (capital logarithmic, lowercase exponentiated); Example 27.3 also reuses t and x for Rindler coordinates.** — Easy to substitute the wrong definition when combining results from the two chapters. *Suggestion:* In the app, use distinct symbols (e.g. U_EF, V_EF for Eddington-Finkelstein; U_K, V_K for Kruskal; eta, rho for Rindler).
- **The throat is described as formed by 'the two singular r = 0 lines', but the narrowest sphere of the T = 0 slice is the r = 2M bifurcation sphere at the origin of the diagram.** — Learners may think the wormhole throat is at the singularity. *Suggestion:* Point to the origin X = T = 0 as the throat (a 2-sphere of radius 2M) and explain that the singularities cut off slices at larger |T|.
- **Calling the past singularity 'naked' is non-standard; most texts reserve the term for singularities not hidden by horizons that arise in the future of regular data, and simply call this a white hole.** — Learners may carry an imprecise definition into later reading on cosmic censorship. *Suggestion:* Define naked singularity carefully and note that the white-hole singularity is visible but belongs to an idealized eternal solution.
- **Example 27.2 says pulses emitted just above the horizon reach the observer only as t -> infinity, which blurs 'arbitrarily late' and 'never'.** — Reinforces the misconception that nothing outside the horizon can be seen. *Suggestion:* Stress that each pulse from r > 2M arrives at finite t; only the limit of emission at the horizon has infinite delay.
- **No explicit mention that realistic collapse replaces regions I' and II' by the stellar interior; only the Fig. 27.1 caption hints at it.** — Learners may believe observed black holes contain wormholes and white holes. *Suggestion:* Juxtapose the Kruskal Penrose diagram with the collapse diagram of Chapter 25 (Fig. 25.11) and shade the part of the extension that survives.
- **The statement that the metric is static only in regions I and I' is made without Killing-vector language or a demonstration of why t becomes spacelike inside.** — Leaves 'increments in t are increments in space' as an assertion. *Suggestion:* Show g_tt = -(1 - 2M/r) changes sign at r = 2M and draw the vector d/dt as a boost-like field on the Kruskal diagram (timelike in I and I', spacelike in II and II').
- **Exercise 27.5's preamble says the exponential horizon redshift was seen 'in this chapter', but it was derived in Chapter 26.** — Learners may search this chapter in vain. *Suggestion:* Point to Chapter 26 eqn 26.42.

## Tutor notes

- Opening question: 'Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates let things fall in smoothly. What goes wrong for light trying to come out, and why would we want one chart that handles both?' Then recall the Rindler toy model before touching Schwarzschild.
- Order of explanation: (1) Rindler recap and its three-step recipe; (2) factorized Schwarzschild metric; (3) exponentiate and form T, X; (4) the regular conformal factor; (5) diagram tour; (6) infall and pulses; (7) Rindler analogy; (8) extension, wormhole and white hole; (9) non-traversability; (10) Penrose diagram and trapped surfaces.
- Check for understanding: ask the learner to place on a blank Kruskal diagram the horizon, r = 0, a hovering observer, and the line t = 0 inside and outside. Common error: drawing r = 0 as a point or constant t inside as horizontal.
- Check: 'Why must T/X = tanh(t/4M) hit the diagonal only as t -> infinity?' A correct answer mentions that tanh never reaches 1, so Schwarzschild time cannot label the horizon.
- When a learner asks 'is the other universe real?', separate the mathematics (the vacuum solution naturally extends) from physics (collapse removes I' and II'; the extension is an idealization), and mention that astrophysical black holes are expected to follow the collapse diagram of Chapter 25.
- When asked 'can I go through the wormhole?', first let them try with 45-degree lines on the diagram; then show the slice flip-book. Emphasize the causal argument is slicing-independent.
- Correct the book's slips explicitly when they appear: du dv in 27.4, dr^2 in 27.11, spacelike (not timelike) slices, region II curves in Fig. 27.6(b) are constant x and not world lines.
- For advanced learners, give the sharper form of the analogy: near r = 2M, with proper distance rho = sqrt(8M(r - 2M)), the metric becomes -rho^2 d(t/4M)^2 + drho^2. So 1/4M is the surface gravity and the Kruskal exponent is not arbitrary.
- For beginners, stay with pictures: light at 45 degrees, hyperbolae of constant r, rays of constant t. Skip the implicit inverse relations until they can read the diagram.
- Common learner question: 'Why is T called time if the infaller's clock is proper time?' Answer: T is a coordinate chosen to make light cones simple; no observer's clock equals T in general, just as Minkowski t is only one observer's time.
- Common learner question: 'If t is spacelike inside, what is time inside the black hole?' Answer: r decreases toward the future; the constant-r hyperbolae in region II are successive moments ending at r = 0.
- Bridge to Chapter 28: after Exercise 27.5, ask what a thermometer on the accelerating rocket might read, and why the same logic could make a black hole glow.
