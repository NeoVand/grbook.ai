# GA ch04 · Linear slot machines

> Introduces 1-forms as the dual partners of vectors (down-index objects pictured as stacks of surfaces), defines tensors as linear machines that eat vectors and 1-forms and return numbers, works out how components and bases transform so that contractions stay invariant, and cashes the formalism in by building the energy-momentum tensor of dust and reading its meaning by feeding an observer's 4-velocity into its slots.

**Pages:** printed 43–55 · pdf 60–72 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

Algebra is simple (sums, matrix inverses, chain rule) but index bookkeeping is dense, especially the primed-index Jacobians in §4.3 and the outer-product evaluations in §4.4. Conceptually the hardest idea is that 1-forms are objects in their own right, not lowered vectors, which the component-first route partly obscures. The slot-machine and plane-stack pictures help a lot. §4.5 moves quickly from gamma-counting to flux interpretation and slot probing with shifting symbols (u, v, p as pressure), and there are several printed index slips (eqns 4.46, 4.51 components, the line before 4.29, Exercise 4.3).

## Role in the book

Chapter 4 closes the flat-spacetime toolkit of Part I. Chapter 2 gave 4-vectors, the Minkowski dot product and observer-measured components; Chapter 3 gave coordinate transformations of basis vectors. This chapter adds the second kind of first-rank object (the 1-form), the general tensor with valence (m,n), index raising and lowering, and the tensor transformation law, so that Chapter 5 can generalize the metric and Part II can talk about covariant derivatives, Riemann curvature and the field equations. It also plants the energy-momentum tensor early as the future right-hand side of Einstein's equation, to be revisited in Chapter 12, and deliberately leans on the metric even though 1-forms do not need one; the metric-free, rigorous treatment of forms is postponed to Part V (Chapters 31-32).

## Learning objectives

- Reader can explain why a vector at a point on a curved space lives in the tangent space at that point rather than in the space itself, using the Oxford-to-Durham example.
- Reader can lower and raise indices with the Minkowski metric and its inverse, and state that X_0 = -X^0 while spatial components are unchanged.
- Reader can describe up and down components geometrically in a non-orthogonal basis: up components from the parallelogram rule, down components from dot products with the basis vectors.
- Reader can define a 1-form as a linear map from vectors to real numbers, expand it in basis 1-forms, and picture it as a stack of evenly spaced surfaces whose density measures its size.
- Reader can use the duality relation <omega^nu, e_mu> = delta^nu_mu to evaluate a contraction as sigma_mu X^mu and to extract components by inserting basis objects into slots.
- Reader can interpret physical quantities as 1-forms: wave phase difference as surfaces of constant phase pierced, E = -p(u) and n = -J(u) for an observer with 4-velocity u.
- Reader can show that partially filling the metric, eta(v, ·), produces the 1-form with components v_mu, and connect this to index lowering.
- Reader can write and justify the transformation laws for vector components, 1-form components, basis vectors and basis 1-forms, and prove that X_mu Y^mu is invariant.
- Reader can define a tensor of valence (m,n) as a multilinear slot machine, build tensors with the outer product, extract components, and apply the tensor transformation law one factor per index.
- Reader can argue that energy density picks up two Lorentz factors under a boost and hence belongs to a second-rank tensor.
- Reader can construct T = J ⊗ p for dust, read off T^00, T^0i and T^ij as energy density, momentum density and stress, write the rest-frame matrices for dust and a perfect fluid, and interpret T(u,u), T(u,a) and T(u, ·) for an observer.
- Reader can state why a tensor equation true in one frame is true in all, and why a scalar can be evaluated in whichever frame is most convenient.

## Teaching approach

An informal, picture-rich chapter that motivates a second first-rank object by asking what vectors must be paired with so that combining them yields a coordinate-invariant number. The authors first reach 1-forms through components (the metric absorbed into a vector gives down components), then recast everything in a single governing metaphor: tensors as slot machines with vector and 1-form slots and a crank that outputs a number. Geometry (tangent planes, stacks of surfaces pierced by arrows, parallel versus perpendicular projections) is paired with explicit index calculations in boxed examples, and physics examples (de Broglie phase, observer-measured energy and number density) keep the abstractions grounded. The chapter closes with a payoff: the energy-momentum tensor of dust, interrogated by inserting an observer's velocity into its slots.

**Style:** analogy-driven, geometric-first, example-driven, conversational-informal, physical-motivation-first, spiral-revisit

**Narrative arc**

1. Motivating puzzle: following a straight arrow due North from Oxford leaves you above Durham, so vectors on a curved space must live in a flat tangent space at a point.
2. Motivating question: to form invariant numbers from vectors we need partner objects whose components transform oppositely; the bra-ket pairing of quantum mechanics is offered as a familiar precedent, and 1-forms are pictured as stacks of planes.
3. Component route (§4.1): absorb the metric into one vector of the dot product to define down components, then define the inverse metric and raising, and show geometrically what up and down components are in an oblique basis.
4. Reframe (§4.2): the metric becomes a two-slot machine eta( , ); valence (m,n) is introduced; vectors and 1-forms become one-slot machines whose mutual filling is the contraction <sigma, X>.
5. Core rule: the duality relation <omega^nu, e_mu> = delta^nu_mu turns contractions into sigma_mu X^mu and lets basis objects extract components.
6. Geometric and physical interpretation: contraction counts surfaces pierced, illustrated by wave phase; momentum and current 1-forms fed an observer's 4-velocity give minus energy and minus number density.
7. Bridge back to the metric: filling one slot of eta maps a vector to its 1-form, the geometric version of index lowering.
8. Consistency check (§4.3): require invariance of X_mu Y^mu to derive how down components transform, then show basis 1-forms transform like vector components; summarize all four laws.
9. Generalization (§4.4): arbitrary (m,n) tensors as slot machines, the outer product as slot-order bookkeeping, component extraction, the tensor transformation law, raising and lowering on tensors, and two corollaries on frame independence.
10. Payoff (§4.5): a gamma-squared argument shows energy density is part of a second-rank tensor; the energy-momentum tensor is built as J ⊗ p, its components interpreted as fluxes of 4-momentum, specialized to dust and a perfect fluid, and finally probed slot by slot with an observer's velocity.

**Signature moves**

- The single overarching 'linear slot machine' metaphor, with cartoon machines that have labelled slots, a crank and a spout that emits a number.
- Valence bookkeeping as an accounting rule: add the (m,n) of everything inserted and the machine, and a scalar results only when the totals match.
- Introducing a new object twice: first as a component trick (absorbing eta into Y), then as a coordinate-free geometric object, with a margin warning that the first route is not the fundamental one.
- Physical quantities defined by what an observer measures when their 4-velocity is inserted into a slot, with explicit minus signs from the metric signature.
- Deriving a transformation law by demanding that a scalar stays a scalar, rather than postulating it.
- Using index position itself as a mnemonic for transformation behaviour (down-index components move with down-index basis vectors).
- Spoiler margin notes that point forward to where each object will matter (Ricci scalar, Einstein equation, Chapter 12).
- Explicit preference for the slot-machine definition of a tensor over the coordinate-transformation definition, justified by the 'death by indices' remark.

## Section by section

### Chapter opening: vectors in tangent spaces and the need for 1-forms — p.43 (pdf 60)

Opens with the Oxford-Durham picture: an arrow tangent to the Earth at one city ends in the air above the other, showing that vectors belong to a flat tangent space attached at a point and that journeys on curved spaces are paths, not vectors. Recalls that vectors are coordinate-free objects expanded as X = X^mu e_mu. Introduces 1-forms (from Cartan's differential forms) with basis 1-forms omega^mu and down-index components, notated with a tilde. Explains the need: pairing two objects into an invariant number requires one whose components transform oppositely, just as bras pair with kets. Pictures a 1-form as evenly spaced planes whose density gives its magnitude, and announces the umbrella idea that tensors are linear slot machines producing scalars.

**Concepts:** tangent space, manifold, 4-vector, 1-form, basis 1-forms, p-form (differential form), dual space, stacked-surfaces picture of a 1-form, tensor

**Key moves**
- A tangent arrow from Oxford overshoots the curved Earth, so vectors must be local objects living in the tangent space at a point.
- Distinguish a path through a space from a vector between endpoints.
- Motivate 1-forms by invariance: combining with vectors must give a scalar, so components must transform contragrediently.
- Offer the bra-ket pairing as a precedent for objects living in a dual space.
- Give the geometric picture of a 1-form as a family of parallel planes, with spatial frequency as magnitude.

### §4.1 Dot products and down vectors — p.44 (pdf 61)

Starts from the Minkowski dot product eta_{mu nu} X^mu Y^nu and defines down components Y_mu = eta_{mu nu} Y^nu, so the dot product becomes a single up-down sum. Example 4.1 shows X·e_lambda equals X_lambda and, for diagonal Minkowski components, only the time component flips sign. The inverse metric eta^{mu nu} is defined and used to raise indices. Example 4.2 uses a non-orthogonal 2D basis to show that up components come from the parallelogram decomposition while down components come from dot products with the basis vectors, related by the inverse of a non-diagonal metric. The section ends by announcing that down components belong to a new object, the 1-form X̃ = X_mu omega^mu, while a margin note cautions that 1-forms do not actually require a metric.

**Concepts:** scalar product, Minkowski metric, index lowering, inverse metric, index raising, contravariant and covariant components, Kronecker delta, 1-form, Einstein summation convention

**Key moves**
- Absorb eta_{mu nu} Y^nu into a new object Y_mu so that X·Y = X^mu Y_mu.
- Show X·e_lambda = X_lambda: a down component is a dot product with a basis vector.
- Evaluate the lambda = 2 case explicitly to see that in Minkowski components X_i = X^i and X_0 = -X^0.
- Define eta^{mu nu} by eta_{mu nu} eta^{nu lambda} = delta and write the dot product with two down components.
- In an oblique basis contrast parallelogram (up) and projection (down) components and connect them via eta^{ij}.
- Announce the 1-form X̃ = X_mu omega^mu as the home of down components.

### §4.2 Vectors and 1-forms — p.46 (pdf 63)

Recasts the metric as a two-slot linear machine eta( , ) that outputs the dot product, calls it a (0,2) tensor and introduces valence (m,n). Vectors become (1,0) and 1-forms (0,1) tensors; a scalar results when the valences of machine plus inputs balance. The contraction <sigma, X> = sigma(X) = X(sigma) is defined, and with the duality rule <omega^nu, e_mu> = delta^nu_mu it reduces to sigma_mu X^mu. Example 4.3 extracts components by inserting basis objects. Example 4.4 uses de Broglie waves: the phase difference between two points is the contraction of the wave's 1-form (its surfaces of constant phase) with the separation vector, i.e. the number of surfaces pierced. Example 4.5 identifies the momentum 1-form (components from the Lagrangian) and the particle-current 1-form, which give -E and -n when fed an observer's 4-velocity, checked in the rest frame. The section closes by showing that filling one slot of the metric turns a vector into its 1-form.

**Concepts:** metric tensor, tensor, valence of a tensor, multilinearity, contraction of a 1-form with a vector, dual basis relation, component extraction by inserting basis objects, stacked-surfaces picture of a 1-form, wavevector 1-form, de Broglie relation, momentum 1-form, canonical momentum, observer-measured energy, particle current 4-vector, metric as a map from vectors to 1-forms, dual space

**Key moves**
- Write eta(X,Y) = eta_{mu nu} X^mu Y^nu and state bilinearity (4.17).
- Define valence and the balancing rule for producing a scalar.
- Treat vector and 1-form symmetrically: X(sigma) = sigma(X) = <sigma, X>, with a margin caution that this symmetry fails for complex bra-kets.
- Expand both objects in bases, use linearity, and apply <omega^nu, e_mu> = delta^nu_mu to get sigma_mu X^mu.
- Extract X^mu = X(omega^mu) and sigma_mu = sigma(e_mu).
- Interpret the contraction as counting planes pierced, via the phase of a matter wave.
- Define physical 1-forms by the observer measurement they encode: p(u) = -E, J(u) = -n; verify in the rest frame where u = e_0.
- Identify sigma·X = eta(sigma, X) = <sigmã, X> and conclude eta(sigma, ·) = sigmã.

### §4.3 Transformations — p.49 (pdf 66)

Demands that the contraction X_{alpha'} Y^{alpha'} be invariant. Given that up components transform with Lambda^{alpha'}_mu = ∂x^{alpha'}/∂x^mu, the down components must transform with the inverse matrix ∂x^nu/∂x^{beta'}, and the product of the two matrices collapses to a Kronecker delta. Down components therefore transform like basis vectors. Example 4.6 multiplies the duality relation by transformation matrices to show that basis 1-forms transform like vector components. A margin note writes eqn 4.30 in components and gives the wave 1-form components k_mu = (-omega, k). The four transformation rules are collected in eqn 4.37.

**Concepts:** coordinate transformation matrix, transformation law for vector components, transformation law for 1-form components, transformation of basis vectors and basis 1-forms, scalar invariant, Kronecker delta, dual basis relation

**Key moves**
- Posit X^{alpha'} = (∂x^{alpha'}/∂x^mu) X^mu and require X_{alpha'} Y^{alpha'} = X_mu Y^mu.
- Use Lambda^nu_{alpha'} Lambda^{alpha'}_mu = delta^nu_mu to infer X_{beta'} = (∂x^nu/∂x^{beta'}) X_nu.
- Observe that index position tracks transformation type: lower-index components and basis vectors transform alike, upper-index components and basis 1-forms transform alike.
- Derive omega^{sigma'} = Lambda^{sigma'}_beta omega^beta by transforming both sides of the duality relation.

### §4.4 Tensors — p.50 (pdf 67)

Defines a general tensor T as a linear machine with m 1-form slots and n vector slots, valence (m,n), and introduces the outer product ⊗ as a device that keeps track of slot order. Example 4.7 evaluates e_1 ⊗ e_2 on two 1-forms and the mixed tensor omega^2 ⊗ e_3 on a vector and a 1-form. Example 4.8 extracts components of a (3,1) tensor by inserting basis objects, expands the tensor in outer products of basis objects, and evaluates it on arbitrary inputs. The tensor transformation law is stated as one Jacobian factor per index, with the authors preferring the slot-machine definition. Example 4.9 writes the metric as eta_{mu nu} omega^mu ⊗ omega^nu, shows eta(v, ·) = v_nu omega^nu, and defines the (2,0) inverse metric. Indices on tensor components can be raised and lowered one at a time. Two corollaries close the section: tensor equality in one frame implies equality in all, and scalars have the same value in every frame. Margin notes introduce abstract index notation and warn against 'death by indices'.

**Concepts:** tensor, valence of a tensor, outer product, mixed tensor, component extraction by inserting basis objects, tensor transformation law, metric tensor, inverse metric, index lowering, index raising, frame independence of tensor equations, scalar invariant, index-free notation, abstract index notation

**Key moves**
- Generalize from one-slot and two-slot machines to an (m,n) slot machine.
- Use ⊗ so that each factor acts on its own slot, and evaluate products of basis objects on inputs via the duality rule.
- Components of a tensor are its values on basis 1-forms and basis vectors; the tensor is components times outer products of basis objects.
- Transform components with one ∂x'/∂x factor per up index and one ∂x/∂x' factor per down index.
- Partially filling the (0,2) metric maps vectors to 1-forms; the (2,0) version maps 1-forms to vectors.
- Argue frame independence by considering the difference tensor C = A - B, which vanishes identically.

### §4.5 Energy-momentum tensor — p.52 (pdf 69)

Payoff section. For dust at rest the energy density is n_0 m; boosted, each particle's energy gains a gamma and length contraction raises the number density by another gamma, so energy density scales as gamma squared, the signature of a second-rank tensor. Recalling that the particle current J = n_0 u carries number density and particle flux, the authors argue that the flux of the 4-vector momentum must be a rank-two object and define (for now) T = J̃ ⊗ p̃ with components T_{mu nu} = J_mu p_nu, symmetric and convertible to T^{mu nu}. Example 4.10 evaluates T^{mu nu} = n_0 m u^mu u^nu, identifies T^00 as energy density, T^0i as momentum density or energy flux, T^ij as stresses, interprets T^{mu nu} as flux of p^mu across surfaces of constant x^nu, and gives rest-frame matrices for dust and an isotropic fluid with pressure. Finally Example 4.11 feeds an observer's velocity u and a direction a into the slots of T = rho_0 ṽ ⊗ ṽ to read off energy density (T(u,u) = nE), momentum density along a, energy flux along a, and the momentum-density 1-form T(u, ·). Margin notes preview the Einstein equation and the sign changes when time indices are raised.

**Concepts:** dust, Lorentz factor, length contraction, second-rank tensor, particle current 4-vector, energy-momentum tensor, symmetric tensor, flux interpretation of the energy-momentum tensor, stress tensor, perfect fluid, observer-measured energy, Einstein field equations, index lowering

**Key moves**
- Count gammas: one from relativistic energy per particle, one from contraction of the volume, so energy density transforms with gamma squared.
- Since particle number is a scalar its flux is a vector; momentum is a vector so its flux must be rank two.
- Define T = J̃ ⊗ p̃ and write T^{mu nu} = n_0 m u^mu u^nu.
- Interpret each block of the matrix physically and via flux of p^mu across constant-x^nu surfaces.
- Specialize to dust at rest (only rho) and an isotropic fluid (diag(rho,p,p,p)).
- Use raising with eta to show T^{0i} = -T_{0i} while diagonal entries keep sign.
- Probe T with the observer's 4-velocity: T(u,u) = nE, T(u,a) = -n p(a), T(a,u) = -E J(a), T(u, ·) = -n p̃.

### Chapter summary and exercises — p.55 (pdf 72)

A three-point summary restates 1-forms as plane stacks paired with vectors, tensors as linear slot machines, and the energy-momentum tensor as a symmetric rank-two object describing 4-momentum flux with T^00 the energy density. Eight exercises follow on proving tensor character, the Kronecker delta, invariant traces, basis vectors and 1-forms in curvilinear coordinates, derivatives of the invariant norm, and the relativistic Doppler effect from the wavevector.

**Concepts:** 1-form, tensor, energy-momentum tensor, tensor transformation law, trace of a mixed tensor, Doppler effect

**Key moves**
- Condense the chapter into three take-home ideas.
- Practice the transformation law on outer products, Kronecker deltas and traces.
- Compute non-Cartesian basis vectors and basis 1-forms from coordinate relations.

## Concepts

### tangent space

*definition · introduced* · also: T_pM, tangent plane at a point

The flat vector space attached to a single point p of a manifold, containing all vectors (directions and rates of change) at that point; vectors at p belong to it rather than to the manifold itself.

**How introduced:** Through the Oxford-Durham picture: an arrow tangent to the Earth at Oxford points off the surface, so the natural home of that arrow is a flat plane touching the sphere at Oxford (R^2 touching S^2). Fig. 4.2 sketches a flat sheet T_pM touching a curved manifold at p.

**Prerequisites:** manifold, 4-vector

**Notes:** Only an intuitive picture here; the formal construction is in GA appC and Part V. The chapter itself then works in flat Minkowski spacetime, where the distinction is invisible.

**Where:** GA p.43 (pdf 60); GA p.44 (pdf 61)

### manifold

*mathematical-object · mention* · also: M, space of events

A space that looks locally like R^n and whose points (events, for spacetime) can be labelled by coordinates, but which may be curved globally.

**How introduced:** Named in the caption of Fig. 4.2 as the curved space whose points spacetime events occupy, with the caveat that the book usually avoids this vocabulary.

**Where:** GA p.44 (pdf 61)

### 4-vector

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: vector, (1,0) tensor, contravariant vector, up vector

A coordinate-independent geometric object with magnitude and direction, expanded in a basis as X = X^mu e_mu with upper-index components; in this chapter it is recast as a linear machine with one slot that accepts a 1-form.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapter 2 as an arrow, then reinterpreted as a (1,0) tensor X( ) whose slot is filled by a 1-form.

**Prerequisites:** basis vectors, Einstein summation convention

$$
X = X^\mu e_\mu
$$

$$
X(\tilde\sigma) = \langle \tilde\sigma, X \rangle
$$

**Notes:** Footnote 1 recalls that the squared length X^2 is agreed by all observers.

**Where:** GA p.43 (pdf 60); GA §4.2 p.46 (pdf 63)

### basis vectors

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: e_mu, coordinate basis vectors

A set of vectors e_mu, one per coordinate direction, in terms of which any vector is written as X^mu e_mu; their mutual dot products give the metric components.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapters 2-3; used with dot products to produce down components (X·e_lambda = X_lambda) and later paired with basis 1-forms through the duality relation.

**Prerequisites:** 4-vector

$$
e_\mu \cdot e_\nu = \eta_{\mu\nu}
$$

$$
e_{\beta'} = \Lambda^{\alpha}{}_{\beta'} e_\alpha
$$

**Where:** GA §4.1 p.45 (pdf 62); GA §4.3 p.49 (pdf 66)

### Einstein summation convention

*convention · revisited* · also: summation over repeated indices

An index appearing once up and once down in a term is summed over all its values (0 to 3 in spacetime) without writing the sum sign.

**How introduced:** Used throughout from eqn 4.1; the chapter emphasizes that invariant sums pair one upper and one lower index.

$$
X^\mu Y_\mu = \sum_{\mu=0}^{3} X^\mu Y_\mu
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 4.3 prints the upper limit of the sum as 4; for four spacetime dimensions it should be 3.

**Where:** GA §4.1 p.44 (pdf 61); GA p.55 (pdf 72)

### Minkowski metric

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: eta_{mu nu}, flat spacetime metric

The metric of flat spacetime, with components diag(-1, 1, 1, 1) in inertial coordinates, used to form the invariant dot product of vectors.

**How introduced:** Recalled from eqn 2.15 in a margin note, then used as the tool that lowers indices and as the prototype (0,2) tensor.

**Prerequisites:** scalar product

$$
\eta_{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(-1,1,1,1)
$$

**Where:** GA §4.1 p.45 (pdf 62)

### scalar product

*operation · revisited* · also: dot product, inner product of two vectors

The bilinear, coordinate-invariant number formed from two vectors with the metric, X·Y = eta_{mu nu} X^mu Y^nu.

**How introduced:** The chapter's starting point: a familiar way of making a scalar from two vectors, which is then rewritten with down components and finally as the output of the metric slot machine.

**Prerequisites:** Minkowski metric, 4-vector

$$
X\cdot Y = \eta_{\mu\nu} X^\mu Y^\nu = X^\mu Y_\mu = \eta^{\mu\nu} X_\mu Y_\nu
$$

**Where:** GA §4.1 p.44 (pdf 61); GA §4.2 p.46 (pdf 63)

### scalar invariant

*principle · developed* · also: Lorentz scalar, (0,0) tensor, invariant

A number built from tensors with all slots filled (all indices contracted); it takes the same value in every coordinate system, so it may be computed in whichever frame is easiest.

**How introduced:** Invoked at the outset as the reason 1-forms are needed, used as the constraint that derives the 1-form transformation law, and restated as a corollary at the end of §4.4 with the Ricci scalar as a forward example.

**Prerequisites:** contraction of a 1-form with a vector

$$
X_{\alpha'} Y^{\alpha'} = X_\mu Y^\mu
$$

**Where:** GA p.44 (pdf 61); GA §4.3 p.49 (pdf 66); GA §4.4 p.52 (pdf 69)

### index lowering

*operation · core* · also: lowering an index, flat map

Contracting an upper index with the metric to produce a lower-index component, Y_mu = g_{mu nu} Y^nu (here eta); with Minkowski components this flips the sign of the time component only.

**How introduced:** By absorbing eta_{mu nu} Y^nu in the dot product into a single down-index object; later reinterpreted as filling one slot of the metric tensor, and applied index by index to higher-rank tensors.

**Prerequisites:** Minkowski metric, Einstein summation convention

$$
Y_\mu = \eta_{\mu\nu} Y^\nu
$$

$$
X_0 = -X^0,\; X_i = X^i
$$

$$
S^{\mu\nu}\eta_{\nu\beta} = S^{\mu}{}_{\beta}
$$

**Where:** GA §4.1 p.44 (pdf 61); GA §4.1 p.45 (pdf 62); GA §4.4 p.51 (pdf 68); GA §4.5 p.53 (pdf 70)

### inverse metric

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: eta^{mu nu}, (2,0) metric

The matrix inverse of the metric components, eta^{mu nu} with eta_{mu nu} eta^{nu lambda} = delta_mu^lambda; as a (2,0) tensor it takes two 1-forms and maps a 1-form to a vector.

**How introduced:** Defined right after lowering so that the process can be reversed; for Minkowski components it is numerically the same matrix, a coincidence the margin note warns will not hold for most rank-two tensors.

**Prerequisites:** Minkowski metric, Kronecker delta

$$
\eta_{\mu\nu}\eta^{\nu\lambda} = \delta_\mu{}^\lambda
$$

$$
\eta(\,,\,) = \eta^{\mu\nu} e_\mu \otimes e_\nu
$$

**Where:** GA §4.1 p.45 (pdf 62); GA §4.4 p.51 (pdf 68)

### index raising

*operation · developed* · also: raising an index, sharp map

Contracting a lower index with the inverse metric to obtain the upper-index component, X^nu = eta^{mu nu} X_mu.

**How introduced:** As the mirror image of lowering once eta^{mu nu} is defined; used in Example 4.2 with a non-diagonal metric and in footnote 22 to relate T^{mu nu} and T_{mu nu}.

**Prerequisites:** inverse metric

$$
X^\nu = \eta^{\mu\nu} X_\mu
$$

$$
T^{\mu\nu} = \eta^{\alpha\mu}\eta^{\beta\nu} T_{\alpha\beta}

### flux interpretation of the energy-momentum tensor

*principle · developed* · also: T^{mu nu} as 4-momentum flux

Component T^{mu nu} gives the amount of the mu-th component of 4-momentum crossing a unit surface of constant x^nu; a surface of constant time is all of space, so crossing it measures density.

**How introduced:** Example 4.10, as a second reading of the dust components after the direct evaluation, organized into bullet points for the time-time, time-space and space-space blocks.

**Prerequisites:** energy-momentum tensor, particle current 4-vector

$$
T^{00} = \gamma n m
$$

$$
T^{0i} = T^{i0} = n\gamma m v^i
$$

$$
T^{ij} = n\gamma m v^i v^j
$$

**Where:** GA §4.5 p.53 (pdf 70)

### stress tensor

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: Cauchy stress, T^{ij}

The spatial block of the energy-momentum tensor: momentum flux, whose diagonal entries are pressures (normal stresses) and off-diagonal entries are shear stresses.

### perfect fluid

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: isotropic fluid in equilibrium, ideal fluid

Matter with energy density rho and isotropic pressure p and no shear or heat flow in its rest frame, where T = diag(rho, p, p, p).

**How introduced:** Second simple example in Example 4.10, deduced from isotropy (no momentum density, no off-diagonal stress); detailed treatment deferred to Chapter 12.

**Prerequisites:** energy-momentum tensor, stress tensor

$$

**Notes:** Footnote 24 warns that p here is pressure, not momentum.

**Where:** GA §4.5 p.53 (pdf 70)

### (4.1) Dot product with the metric · supporting · GA §4.1 p.44 (pdf 61)

$$
X\cdot Y = \eta_{\mu\nu} X^{\mu} Y^{\nu}

Starting point: the invariant product of two vectors uses the metric components.

**Symbols:** eta_{mu nu} Minkowski metric components; X^mu, Y^nu vector components

### (4.2) Lowering an index · central · GA §4.1 p.44 (pdf 61)

$$
Y_{\mu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} Y^{\nu}

Definition of down components by absorbing the metric into one vector.

**Symbols:** Y_mu down (1-form) components

### (4.3) · central · GA §4.1 p.45 (pdf 62)

$$
X\cdot Y = X^{\mu} Y_{\mu}
$$

The dot product becomes one sum over an upper and a lower index.

### (4.4) Down component as a dot product with a basis vector · supporting · GA §4.1 p.45 (pdf 62)

$$
X\cdot e_{\lambda} = X^{\mu} e_{\mu}\cdot e_{\lambda} = X^{\mu}\eta_{\mu\lambda} = X_{\lambda}

Projecting onto a basis vector yields the lowered component.

**Symbols:** e_lambda basis vector

### (4.5) Explicit sum for one lowered component · derivation-step · GA §4.1 p.45 (pdf 62)

$$
X_2 = X^{\mu}\eta_{\mu 2} = X^0\eta_{02} + X^1\eta_{12} + X^2\eta_{22} + X^3\eta_{32}
$$

Writing out the implied sum for lambda = 2 shows which metric entries survive; with diagonal eta only eta_22 remains.

### (4.6) · supporting · GA §4.1 p.45 (pdf 62)

$$
X_2 = X^2\eta_{22} = X^2,\qquad X_0 = -X^0
$$

With diagonal Minkowski components only the time component changes sign on lowering.

### (4.7) Inverse metric · central · GA §4.1 p.45 (pdf 62)

$$
\eta_{\mu\nu}\eta^{\nu\lambda} = \delta_{\mu}{}^{\lambda}
$$

Defines eta^{mu nu} as the matrix inverse of eta_{mu nu}.

**Symbols:** delta Kronecker delta

### (4.8) Raising an index · central · GA §4.1 p.45 (pdf 62)

$$
X^{\nu} = \eta^{\mu\nu} X_{\mu}
$$

The inverse metric restores up components from down components.

### (4.9) · supporting · GA §4.1 p.45 (pdf 62)

$$
X\cdot Y = \eta^{\mu\nu} X_{\mu} Y_{\nu}
$$

Dot product written purely with down components and the inverse metric.

### (4.10) Vector in a skewed basis · supporting · GA §4.1 p.45 (pdf 62)

$$
X = X^1 e_1 + X^2 e_2
$$

Up components are the coefficients of the parallelogram decomposition along non-orthogonal basis vectors (Fig. 4.4).

**Symbols:** e_1, e_2 non-orthogonal 2D basis vectors

### (4.11)-(4.13) Up and down components in an oblique basis · supporting · GA §4.1 p.45 (pdf 62)

$$
X_j = X\cdot e_j,\qquad X^i = \eta^{ij}(X\cdot e_j),\qquad X^1 = \eta^{11}X_1 + \eta^{12}X_2
$$

In a non-orthogonal basis down components are projections and up components are recovered with the non-diagonal inverse metric.

**Symbols:** eta_{ij} = e_i·e_j here not diagonal

### (4.14) 1-form expansion · central · GA §4.1 p.46 (pdf 63)

$$
\tilde X = X_{\mu}\,\omega^{\mu}
$$

Down components are the components of a 1-form in the basis of basis 1-forms.

**Symbols:** omega^mu basis 1-forms; tilde marks a 1-form

### (4.16) Metric slot machine · central · GA §4.2 p.46 (pdf 63)

$$
\eta(X,Y) = \eta_{\mu\nu}X^{\mu}Y^{\nu} = \eta_{00}X^0Y^0 + \eta_{11}X^1Y^1 + \eta_{22}X^2Y^2 + \eta_{33}X^3Y^3
$$

The metric as a two-slot machine returning the dot product (expanded for diagonal components).

### (4.17) Linearity of the metric · supporting · GA §4.2 p.46 (pdf 63)

$$
\eta(aX, bY) = ab\,\eta(X,Y),\qquad \eta(X+Y, Z) = \eta(X,Z) + \eta(Y,Z)
$$

The machine is linear in each slot.

**Symbols:** a, b scalars

### Linearity of the contraction (margin box) · supporting · GA §4.2 p.46 (pdf 63)

$$
\langle a\tilde\sigma, bX\rangle = ab\langle\tilde\sigma, X\rangle,\quad \langle\tilde\sigma, X+Y\rangle = \langle\tilde\sigma, X\rangle + \langle\tilde\sigma, Y\rangle,\quad \langle\tilde\sigma+\tilde\zeta, X\rangle = \langle\tilde\sigma, X\rangle + \langle\tilde\zeta, X\rangle
$$

Bilinearity of the pairing, used to expand both arguments in bases.

### (4.18) Contraction notation · central · GA §4.2 p.46 (pdf 63)

$$
\langle\tilde\sigma, X\rangle = X(\tilde\sigma) = \tilde\sigma(X)
$$

Inserting a vector into a 1-form or a 1-form into a vector gives the same number.

### (4.19) · derivation-step · GA §4.2 p.47 (pdf 64)

$$
\langle\tilde\sigma, X\rangle = \langle\sigma_\nu\omega^\nu, X^\mu e_\mu\rangle = \sigma_\nu X^\mu \langle\omega^\nu, e_\mu\rangle
$$

Linearity pulls components out, leaving pairings of basis objects.

### (4.20) Duality relation · central · GA §4.2 p.47 (pdf 64)

$$
\langle\omega^{\nu}, e_{\mu}\rangle = \delta^{\nu}{}_{\mu}
$$

Basis 1-forms and basis vectors are dual; the most important rule for tensor manipulation.

### (4.21) Contraction in components · central · GA §4.2 p.47 (pdf 64)

$$
\langle\tilde\sigma, X\rangle = \sigma_\nu X^\mu\delta^\nu{}_\mu = \sigma_\mu X^\mu
$$

A 1-form on a vector is the up-down sum of components: no metric needed.

### (4.22)-(4.23) Extracting components · central · GA §4.2 p.47 (pdf 64)

$$
X(\omega^\mu) = X^\mu,\qquad \tilde\sigma(e_\mu) = \sigma_\mu
$$

Feed basis 1-forms to vectors and basis vectors to 1-forms to read off components.

### (4.24) Plane-wave amplitude · supporting · GA §4.2 p.47 (pdf 64)

$$
\psi(x) = A e^{i\phi} = A e^{i k\cdot x} = A e^{i p\cdot x/\hbar}
$$

Phase of a matter wave; the phase difference between two events is k·x.

**Symbols:** k wavevector, p = hbar k momentum

### 4-dimensional phase difference · supporting · GA §4.2 p.47 (pdf 64)

$$
\Delta\phi = k\cdot x = \vec k\cdot\vec x - \omega t
$$

In Minkowski space the phase difference includes the time part with a minus sign.

**Symbols:** k = (omega, vec k)

### (4.25) Phase as surfaces pierced · central · GA §4.2 p.48 (pdf 65)

$$
\Delta\phi = \langle\tilde k, x\rangle = (\text{number of surfaces pierced})
$$

The wave 1-form's constant-phase surfaces counted along the separation vector give the phase difference.

### (4.26) Geometric meaning of contraction · central · GA §4.2 p.48 (pdf 65)

$$
\langle\tilde\sigma, X\rangle = (\text{number of planes of } \tilde\sigma \text{ pierced by } X)
$$

General geometric reading of the pairing.

### (4.27) Energy from the momentum 1-form · central · GA §4.2 p.48 (pdf 65)

$$
\tilde p(u) = -E
$$

Inserting an observer's 4-velocity into the momentum 1-form gives minus the energy that observer measures; component form p_mu u^mu = -E.

**Symbols:** u observer 4-velocity; E measured energy

### (4.28) Number density from the current 1-form · central · GA §4.2 p.48 (pdf 65)

$$
\tilde J(u) = -n
$$

Inserting an observer's 4-velocity into the particle current 1-form gives minus the measured number density; J_mu u^mu = -n.

**Symbols:** n number density measured by O_u

### (4.29) · supporting · GA §4.2 p.49 (pdf 66)

$$
\sigma\cdot X = \eta(\sigma, X) \equiv \langle\tilde\sigma, X\rangle
$$

Contracting a 1-form with a vector equals the dot product of the vector with the raised-index version of the 1-form.

**Symbols:** sigma = sigma^nu e_nu vector with raised components

### (4.30) Metric maps vectors to 1-forms · central · GA §4.2 p.49 (pdf 66)

$$
\eta(\sigma, \cdot\,) = \tilde\sigma, \qquad \eta_{\mu\nu}X^\mu = X_\nu
$$

Filling one slot of the metric yields the corresponding 1-form (index lowering, geometrically). The component version is in the margin.

### (4.31) Vector component transformation · central · GA §4.3 p.49 (pdf 66)

$$
X^{\alpha'} = \Lambda^{\alpha'}{}_{\mu} X^{\mu} = \frac{\partial x^{\alpha'}}{\partial x^{\mu}} X^{\mu}
$$

Up components transform with the forward Jacobian.

**Symbols:** primed indices refer to the new coordinates

### (4.32) 1-form component transformation · central · GA §4.3 p.49 (pdf 66)

$$
X_{\beta'} = \Lambda^{\nu}{}_{\beta'} X_{\nu} = \frac{\partial x^{\nu}}{\partial x^{\beta'}} X_{\nu}
$$

Down components must transform with the inverse Jacobian.

### (4.33) Invariance of the contraction · central · GA §4.3 p.49 (pdf 66)

$$
X_{\alpha'}Y^{\alpha'} = X_{\mu}\Lambda^{\mu}{}_{\alpha'}\Lambda^{\alpha'}{}_{\nu}Y^{\nu} = X_\mu\delta^\mu{}_\nu Y^\nu = X_\mu Y^\mu
$$

The two Jacobians cancel, so the up-down sum is a scalar.

### (4.34)-(4.35) · derivation-step · GA §4.3 p.49 (pdf 66)

$$
\langle\omega^\beta, \Lambda^\alpha{}_{\gamma'}e_\alpha\rangle = \Lambda^\beta{}_{\gamma'},\qquad \langle\Lambda^{\sigma'}{}_\beta\omega^\beta, \Lambda^\alpha{}_{\gamma'}e_\alpha\rangle = \Lambda^{\sigma'}{}_\beta\Lambda^\beta{}_{\gamma'} = \delta^{\sigma'}{}_{\gamma'}
$$

Multiplying the duality relation by the transformation matrices in turn.

### (4.36) Basis 1-form transformation · central · GA §4.3 p.49 (pdf 66)

$$
\omega^{\sigma'} = \Lambda^{\sigma'}{}_{\beta}\,\omega^{\beta}
$$

Basis 1-forms transform like vector components.

### (4.37) Summary of transformation rules · central · GA §4.3 p.50 (pdf 67)

$$
X^{\beta'} = \Lambda^{\beta'}{}_{\alpha}X^{\alpha},\quad \sigma_{\beta'} = \Lambda^{\alpha}{}_{\beta'}\sigma_{\alpha},\quad e_{\beta'} = \Lambda^{\alpha}{}_{\beta'}e_{\alpha},\quad \omega^{\beta'} = \Lambda^{\beta'}{}_{\alpha}\omega^{\alpha}
$$

The four rules: index position determines which Jacobian is used.

### (4.38) Abstract index contraction (margin) · supporting · GA §4.4 p.50 (pdf 67)

$$
T^{ab}{}_{c}\,\tilde Z_a \tilde Z_b A^c
$$

Roman indices label slots, not components, and do not imply summation.

**Symbols:** a, b, c abstract slot labels

### (4.39) Outer product of basis vectors on 1-forms · supporting · GA §4.4 p.50 (pdf 67)

$$
e_1\otimes e_2(\tilde\alpha,\tilde\beta) = e_1(\tilde\alpha)\,e_2(\tilde\beta) = \alpha_\lambda\delta^\lambda{}_1\,\beta_\rho\delta^\rho{}_2 = \alpha_1\beta_2
$$

Each factor of an outer product acts on its own slot.

### (4.40) Mixed outer product · supporting · GA §4.4 p.50 (pdf 67)

$$
\omega^2\otimes e_3(v,\tilde\alpha) = \omega^2(v)\,e_3(\tilde\alpha) = v^2\alpha_3
$$

A mixed tensor takes a vector in one slot and a 1-form in the other.

### (4.42) Components of a (3,1) tensor · central · GA §4.4 p.50 (pdf 67)

$$
S^{\mu\nu\lambda}{}_{\sigma} = S(\omega^\mu, \omega^\nu, \omega^\lambda, e_\sigma)
$$

Components are outputs on basis objects.

### (4.43) Tensor expanded in a basis · central · GA §4.4 p.51 (pdf 68)

$$
S(\,,\,,\,,\,) = S^{\mu\nu\lambda}{}_{\sigma}\,e_\mu(\,)\otimes e_\nu(\,)\otimes e_\lambda(\,)\otimes\omega^\sigma(\,)
$$

A tensor equals its components times outer products of basis vectors and basis 1-forms.

### (4.44) Evaluating a tensor on inputs · supporting · GA §4.4 p.51 (pdf 68)

$$
S(\tilde\zeta, \tilde\eta, \tilde\chi, u) = S^{\mu\nu\lambda}{}_{\sigma}\,\zeta_\mu\eta_\nu\chi_\lambda u^\sigma
$$

Output is the full contraction of components with input components.

### (4.45) Tensor transformation law · central · GA §4.4 p.51 (pdf 68)

$$
S^{\mu'\nu'\lambda'}{}_{\sigma'} = \frac{\partial x^{\mu'}}{\partial x^{\mu}}\frac{\partial x^{\nu'}}{\partial x^{\nu}}\frac{\partial x^{\lambda'}}{\partial x^{\lambda}}\frac{\partial x^{\sigma}}{\partial x^{\sigma'}}\,S^{\mu\nu\lambda}{}_{\sigma}
$$

One Jacobian factor per index, forward for up and inverse for down.

### (4.46) Metric in basis 1-forms · central · GA §4.4 p.51 (pdf 68)

$$
\eta(\,,\,) = \eta_{\mu\nu}\,\omega^{\mu}(\,)\otimes\omega^{\nu}(\,)
$$

The metric as a (0,2) tensor; eta(e_alpha, e_beta) = eta_{alpha beta}.

### (4.47) Partially filled metric · central · GA §4.4 p.51 (pdf 68)

$$
\eta(v, \,) = \eta_{\mu\nu}\,\omega^\mu(v)\,\omega^\nu(\,) = \eta_{\mu\nu}v^\mu\omega^\nu(\,) = v_\nu\,\omega^\nu(\,)
$$

Explicit check that one filled slot of the metric produces the 1-form with lowered components.

### (4.48) (2,0) metric · supporting · GA §4.4 p.51 (pdf 68)

$$
\eta(\,,\,) = \eta^{\mu\nu}\,e_\mu\otimes e_\nu
$$

Inverse metric as a tensor taking two 1-forms; maps 1-forms to vectors.

### (4.49) Raising and lowering tensor indices · supporting · GA §4.4 p.51 (pdf 68)

$$
S^{\mu\nu}\eta_{\nu\beta} = S^{\mu}{}_{\beta},\qquad T_{\mu\nu}\eta^{\mu\alpha} = T^{\alpha}{}_{\nu}
$$

The metric moves indices one at a time on any tensor.

## Worked examples

### Example 4.1 · intro · GA §4.1 p.45 (pdf 62)

**Problem:** Compute the dot product of a vector with a basis vector and evaluate a specific component in 3+1 Minkowski spacetime.

**Method:** Expand X in its basis, use e_mu·e_lambda = eta_{mu lambda}, recognize index lowering, then write out the lambda = 2 sum explicitly with diagonal eta.

**Key insight:** A down component is a projection onto a basis vector; with diagonal Minkowski components only the time component changes sign.

**Result:** X·e_lambda = X_lambda; X_i = X^i for spatial i and X_0 = -X^0.

**Concepts:** index lowering, Minkowski metric, basis vectors

### Example 4.2 · intro · GA §4.1 p.45 (pdf 62)

**Problem:** Understand geometrically what up and down components of a 2D vector are in a basis whose vectors are not orthogonal.

**Method:** Decompose X by the parallelogram rule to get X^1, X^2; take dot products with e_1, e_2 to get X_1, X_2; relate them with the non-diagonal inverse metric.

**Key insight:** Up and down components are genuinely different numbers unless the basis is orthonormal; the metric converts one set into the other.

**Result:** X_j = X·e_j and X^1 = eta^{11}X_1 + eta^{12}X_2.

**Concepts:** contravariant and covariant components, index raising, inverse metric

### Example 4.3 · intro · GA §4.2 p.47 (pdf 64)

**Problem:** Extract the components of a vector and of a 1-form using the basis objects.

**Method:** Insert a basis 1-form into the vector's slot and a basis vector into the 1-form's slot; apply linearity and the duality relation.

**Key insight:** Components are just outputs of the machine on basis inputs.

**Result:** X(omega^mu) = X^mu and sigma(e_mu) = sigma_mu.

**Concepts:** component extraction by inserting basis objects, dual basis relation

### Example 4.4 · standard · GA §4.2 p.47 (pdf 64)

**Problem:** Find the phase difference of a de Broglie wave between two events.

**Method:** First use the dot product of the wave 4-vector k = (omega, k) with the separation, then reinterpret using the wave's 1-form whose surfaces are constant-phase surfaces, counting how many the separation vector crosses.

**Key insight:** A wave is naturally a 1-form: its wavefronts are the stack of surfaces, and phase difference is a contraction requiring no metric.

**Result:** Delta phi = <k̃, x> = k_mu x^mu = (spatial k)·(spatial x) - omega t, read as the number of constant-phase surfaces pierced.

**Concepts:** wavevector 1-form, stacked-surfaces picture of a 1-form, de Broglie relation, contraction of a 1-form with a vector

### Example 4.5 · standard · GA §4.2 p.48 (pdf 65)

**Problem:** Identify physical quantities that are naturally 1-forms and what they output when an observer's 4-velocity is inserted.

**Method:** Take the momentum 1-form (components from the Lagrangian) and insert u; check in the rest frame where u = e_0 using the duality rule and a lowered time component. Repeat for a particle current 1-form with an observer counting particles in a moving permeable box.

**Key insight:** Feeding an observer's 4-velocity into a slot is the coordinate-free way to ask 'what does this observer measure?'; the minus signs come from the signature.

**Result:** p̃(u) = -E and J̃(u) = -n; component forms p_mu u^mu = -E, J_mu u^mu = -n.

**Concepts:** momentum 1-form, observer-measured energy, particle current 4-vector, 4-velocity

### Example 4.6 · standard · GA §4.3 p.49 (pdf 66)

**Problem:** Derive how basis 1-forms transform under a change of coordinates.

**Method:** Start from the duality relation, multiply by the matrix transforming basis vectors, then by the forward matrix on the 1-form side, and compare with the duality relation written in primed coordinates.

**Key insight:** Keeping the duality relation valid in every coordinate system forces basis 1-forms to transform like vector components.

**Result:** omega^{sigma'} = Lambda^{sigma'}_beta omega^beta.

**Concepts:** transformation of basis vectors and basis 1-forms, dual basis relation, coordinate transformation matrix

### Example 4.7 · intro · GA §4.4 p.50 (pdf 67)

**Problem:** Evaluate the outer products e_1 ⊗ e_2 on two 1-forms and omega^2 ⊗ e_3 on a vector and a 1-form.

**Method:** Split the outer product so each factor acts on its own slot, expand inputs in bases, and apply the duality relation.

**Key insight:** ⊗ is slot-order bookkeeping; each factor picks out one component of its own input.

**Result:** e_1 ⊗ e_2(alpha, beta) = alpha_1 beta_2; omega^2 ⊗ e_3(v, alpha) = v^2 alpha_3.

**Concepts:** outer product, mixed tensor, dual basis relation

### Example 4.8 · standard · GA §4.4 p.50 (pdf 67)

**Problem:** Find components of a (3,1) tensor, write it in a basis, and evaluate it on three 1-forms and a vector.

**Method:** Fill slots with basis objects to define components; expand the tensor as components times outer products of basis objects; insert general inputs and collapse Kronecker deltas.

**Key insight:** The full evaluation is simply a complete contraction of the tensor's components with its inputs' components.

**Result:** S(zeta, eta, chi, u) = S^{mu nu lambda}_sigma zeta_mu eta_nu chi_lambda u^sigma.

**Concepts:** tensor, component extraction by inserting basis objects, outer product

### Example 4.9 · standard · GA §4.4 p.51 (pdf 68)

**Problem:** Write the metric as a (0,2) tensor and show what happens when a vector fills one slot; define the (2,0) version.

**Method:** Expand eta in basis 1-forms, insert v into the first factor, use duality to get eta_{mu nu} v^mu, and lower the index.

**Key insight:** Index lowering is partial evaluation of the metric: the output is a 1-form.

**Result:** eta(v, ) = v_nu omega^nu; the (2,0) metric eta^{mu nu} e_mu ⊗ e_nu maps 1-forms to vectors.

**Concepts:** metric tensor, metric as a map from vectors to 1-forms, inverse metric

### Example 4.10 · standard · GA §4.5 p.53 (pdf 70)

**Problem:** Work out and interpret the components of the energy-momentum tensor of a moving dust cloud, then give rest-frame forms for dust and a perfect fluid.

**Method:** Write the upper-index tensor as current components times momentum components, each proportional to the cloud 4-velocity gamma(1, v); read off each block; reinterpret as flux of p^mu across constant-x^nu surfaces; impose rest and isotropy.

**Key insight:** The time-time, time-space and space-space blocks are energy density, momentum density/energy flux and stress, and each has a flux reading.

**Result:** T^00 = gamma n m, T^0i = n gamma m v^i, T^ij = n gamma m v^i v^j; dust at rest diag(rho,0,0,0); perfect fluid diag(rho,p,p,p).

**Concepts:** energy-momentum tensor, flux interpretation of the energy-momentum tensor, dust, perfect fluid, stress tensor

### Example 4.11 · challenging · GA §4.5 p.54 (pdf 71)

**Problem:** Use the slot-machine form T = rho_0 ṽ ⊗ ṽ of a particle swarm to extract what an observer with 4-velocity u measures.

**Method:** Insert u into both slots, then u and a unit direction a in each order, then u into one slot only, using p̃(u) = -E and J̃(u) = -n.

**Key insight:** Every physical reading of T is a choice of what to put in its slots; symmetry makes momentum density equal energy flux.

**Result:** T(u,u) = nE (energy density); T(u,a) = -n p̃(a); T(a,u) = -E J̃(a); T(u, ) = -n p̃ (4-momentum density 1-form).

**Concepts:** energy-momentum tensor, observer-measured energy, symmetric tensor, particle current 4-vector

### Embedded: invariance of the contraction (eqns 4.31-4.33) · intro · GA §4.3 p.49 (pdf 66)

**Problem:** Determine how down components must transform so that X_mu Y^mu is a scalar.

**Method:** Insert the vector transformation law, require the result to equal the unprimed contraction, and use the fact that a Jacobian times its inverse is the identity.

**Key insight:** The 1-form transformation law is forced by invariance, not assumed.

**Result:** X_{beta'} = (∂x^nu/∂x^{beta'}) X_nu.

**Concepts:** transformation law for 1-form components, scalar invariant

### Embedded: gamma-squared transformation of dust energy density · intro · GA §4.5 p.52 (pdf 69)

**Problem:** How does the energy density of a cloud of dust change when viewed from a frame in which it moves?

**Method:** Energy per particle grows from m to gamma m; volume along the motion contracts by gamma so number density grows by gamma.

**Key insight:** Two factors of gamma signal two tensor indices, pointing to a rank-two energy-momentum tensor.

**Result:** Energy density n_0 m becomes gamma^2 n_0 m.

**Concepts:** dust, Lorentz factor, length contraction, second-rank tensor


## Analogies and intuitions

### Tensors as slot machines: a box with labelled slots for vectors and 1-forms, a crank, and a spout that delivers a number → tensor · strong · GA §4.2 p.46 (pdf 63)

The organizing metaphor of the whole chapter (and its title). The metric is a two-vector-slot machine, vectors and 1-forms are one-slot machines, a general tensor has m 1-form slots and n vector slots, and partially filled machines are new, smaller machines.

**Where it breaks down:** The picture hides multilinearity (linearity must be stated separately), does not show slot order or symmetry, and suggests the tensor is a device acting on inputs rather than a geometric object with its own existence; it also says nothing about how the machine varies from point to point.

**App idea:** Drag-and-drop tensor machines whose leftover slots after partial filling reassemble into the correct smaller machine, with a running valence counter.

### Bras and kets in quantum mechanics → dual space · useful · GA p.44 (pdf 61)

Vectors are compared to kets and 1-forms to bras: they live in different spaces and combine to give a number, so learners with quantum mechanics recognize the dual pairing.

**Where it breaks down:** Bra-ket pairings are complex and conjugate-symmetric (footnote 7), and the Hilbert-space inner product also serves to identify bras with kets, which blurs the fact that a 1-form needs no metric.

### A 1-form as a stack of parallel sheets that an arrow skewers → stacked-surfaces picture of a 1-form · strong · GA p.44 (pdf 61)

Gives 1-forms a visual identity distinct from arrows; magnitude is sheet density and the contraction is the count of sheets crossed.

**Where it breaks down:** Only local and flat: general 1-form fields have curved, unevenly spaced surfaces (and non-exact ones do not come from surfaces at all); crossings must be counted with sign and can be fractional; Minkowski 1-forms with null or timelike character are hard to picture this way.

**App idea:** Adjustable plane stack with a draggable arrow and a signed crossing counter.

### Wavefronts of a matter wave as the sheets of a 1-form → wavevector 1-form · strong · GA §4.2 p.48 (pdf 65)

The surfaces of constant phase make the stacked-sheet picture concrete: counting wavefronts crossed between two events gives the phase difference.

**Where it breaks down:** Works for plane waves; for general waves the surfaces curve and the 1-form is the gradient of the phase, which the chapter does not mention.

**App idea:** Animated spacetime plane wave in (t, x): drag two events and count constant-phase lines crossed, compared with k·x - omega t.

### Observer carrying a small permeable box through a dust cloud and counting particles → particle current 4-vector · useful · GA §4.2 p.48 (pdf 65)

Grounds the statement that J̃(u) = -n is the density that particular observer measures.

**Where it breaks down:** Informal; it does not explain why the answer depends on the observer beyond implicit length contraction.

**App idea:** Boost slider for an observer moving through a lattice of dust; the box's particle count per volume rises as gamma.

### Learning electromagnetism only in components without vector notation → index-free notation · useful · GA §4.4 p.51 (pdf 68)

Footnote 17 justifies preferring coordinate-free tensor notation by comparison with how vector notation declutters Maxwell's equations.

**Where it breaks down:** Explicit component work is still unavoidable for calculation, as the chapter's own examples show.

### Parallelogram versus perpendicular-projection components on a skewed grid → contravariant and covariant components · strong · GA §4.1 p.45 (pdf 62)

Fig. 4.4 makes the abstract distinction between up and down components visible in two dimensions.

**Where it breaks down:** Assumes a Euclidean dot product in the picture and unit-length basis vectors for the perpendicular-foot reading; in Minkowski space the difference is instead a sign.

**App idea:** Draggable oblique basis showing both constructions and the metric matrix.

### Travelling on a tangent line from Oxford ends in mid-air above Durham → tangent space · strong · GA p.43 (pdf 60)

Motivates why vectors live in tangent spaces and why finite displacements on curved spaces are paths.

**Where it breaks down:** Relies on an embedding of the sphere in 3D, whereas spacetime's tangent spaces must be understood without any embedding.

**App idea:** Globe with tangent arrow and height-gap readout.


## Misconceptions addressed

### A vector can join two distant points of a curved space, like an arrow from one city to another on the Earth. · GA p.43 (pdf 60)

**Correction:** Vectors belong to the tangent space at a single point; a route between distant points on a curved surface is a path, and a tangent arrow leaves the surface.

**Why tempting:** In flat Euclidean space position vectors do join points, and school geometry trains that habit.

### 1-forms exist only because of the metric; they are just vectors with lowered indices. · GA §4.1 p.46 (pdf 63)

**Correction:** 1-forms are independent linear maps on vectors and exist without any metric; the metric merely provides a one-to-one correspondence between vectors and 1-forms.

**Why tempting:** The chapter itself first reaches down components by absorbing the metric into a vector, and in Minkowski components the two differ only by a sign.

### Raising or lowering indices never changes the numbers, since eta^{mu nu} and eta_{mu nu} are the same matrix. · GA §4.5 p.53 (pdf 70)

**Correction:** That equality is special to Minkowski components in inertial coordinates; for a general tensor, raising a time index flips the sign of mixed components (T^{0i} = -T_{0i}), and in a non-orthogonal basis up and down components differ substantially.

**Why tempting:** The metric and inverse metric look identical in the most common example.

### Up and down components of a vector are the same thing written differently. · GA §4.1 p.45 (pdf 62)

**Correction:** They are different numbers with different geometric meanings (coefficients along axes versus projections onto basis vectors), related by the metric.

**Why tempting:** In Cartesian Euclidean coordinates they coincide exactly.

### sigma(X) and X(sigma) must be different, as <phi|psi> and <psi|phi> are in quantum mechanics. · GA §4.2 p.46 (pdf 63)

**Correction:** In GR all quantities are real, so the book defines the vector acting on a 1-form to equal the 1-form acting on the vector.

**Why tempting:** Prior experience with complex bra-kets, where order matters by complex conjugation.

### A tensor is an array of numbers that transforms in a certain way. · GA §4.4 p.51 (pdf 68)

**Correction:** A tensor is a coordinate-independent multilinear machine; its components depend on the basis and the transformation law follows from that, which is why the authors prefer the slot definition.

**Why tempting:** Many physics courses define tensors by their transformation law and work only with component arrays.

### The p in the perfect-fluid matrix is momentum. · GA §4.5 p.53 (pdf 70)

**Correction:** There p is pressure; the clash of symbols is unfortunate and context decides.

**Why tempting:** The same chapter uses p for momentum and p̃ for the momentum 1-form.

### Energy density transforms like energy, with a single factor of gamma. · GA §4.5 p.52 (pdf 69)

**Correction:** Energy per particle gives one gamma and the contracted volume gives another, so energy density scales as gamma squared and is a component of a rank-two tensor, not a 4-vector.

**Why tempting:** Energy is the time component of a 4-vector, so a density of it seems to be one too.

### Letters in abstract index notation are summation indices. · GA §4.4 p.50 (pdf 67)

**Correction:** Abstract indices name slots; component indices use a different alphabet.

**Why tempting:** They look exactly like component indices.

### A larger 1-form has more widely spaced surfaces, like a longer arrow. · GA p.44 (pdf 61)

**Correction:** Magnitude goes with spatial frequency: the more tightly packed the surfaces, the larger the 1-form and the more surfaces a given vector crosses.

**Why tempting:** Size of an arrow is its length, so learners map 'size' onto spacing rather than density.

### The Kronecker delta with both indices down (or both up) is a tensor with identity components in every frame. · GA p.55 (pdf 72)

**Correction:** Only the mixed delta^alpha_beta keeps identity components under all transformations; converting indices with the metric turns the all-down version into the metric itself.

**Why tempting:** In Euclidean Cartesian work delta_{ij} is used freely as an identity tensor.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The number of surfaces pierced must be a whole, positive number. · GA §4.2 p.48 (pdf 65)

**Correction:** The contraction is a real number: partial crossings count fractionally, and crossing in the direction of decreasing surface labels counts negatively; an arrow lying within a surface gives zero.

**Why tempting:** The word 'number of planes' and the three-sheet cartoon suggest discrete counting.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Due North from Oxford on a straight line · GA p.43 (pdf 60)

**Setup:** Start at Oxford, point a straight arrow due North tangent to the ground and follow it for the 337 km to Durham.

**Lesson:** The arrow ends about 9 km above Durham: vectors are tangent objects at a point, and on a curved space finite journeys are paths.

**App idea:** Globe with a tangent-arrow length slider and height readout.

### Observer counting dust in a moving box · GA §4.2 p.48 (pdf 65)

**Setup:** An observer with 4-velocity u carries a small permeable box of known volume through a cloud of dust and counts the particles inside.

**Lesson:** The measured density depends on the observer and is obtained by feeding u into the particle current 1-form, J̃(u) = -n.

**App idea:** Boosted-box animation with a contracted lattice of dust and a live count.

### The same dust seen from two frames · GA §4.5 p.52 (pdf 69)

**Setup:** Dust particles of mass m are at rest with density n_0 in frame S; view them from a frame S' moving at speed v.

**Lesson:** Per-particle energy and number density each gain a gamma, so energy density needs a rank-two tensor.

**App idea:** Split screen of the dust box in S and S' with a velocity slider and bars for energy per particle, density and energy density.

### Counting wavefronts between two events · GA §4.2 p.48 (pdf 65)

**Setup:** A plane matter wave fills spacetime; pick two events and ask how much the phase differs.

**Lesson:** The answer is the number of constant-phase surfaces crossed by the separation vector: a contraction of the wave's 1-form.

**App idea:** (t,x) diagram with moving phase lines and two draggable events.


## Applications and observations

- **Oxford-Durham tangent-line gap** (numerical-estimate, GA p.43 (pdf 60)): Following a straight tangent line due North from Oxford for the Oxford-Durham distance leaves you about 9 km above the ground. Our check: the height of a tangent line after distance d is about d^2/(2R). Key numbers: d = 337 km; book quotes about 9 km; own estimate with R = 6371 km: 337^2/(2 x 6371) ≈ 8.9 km.
- **Phase of de Broglie matter waves** (observation, GA §4.2 p.47 (pdf 64)): Matter waves of wavevector k = p/hbar; phase difference between events is k·x - omega t, which is the contraction of the wave 1-form with the separation. Key numbers: |k| = 2 pi / lambda; p = hbar k.
- **Astrophysical dust** (astrophysical-system, GA §4.5 p.52 (pdf 69)): Dust in the Universe (grains from a few molecules to macroscopic size) at densities low enough for particles not to interact is the physical model behind the simplest energy-momentum tensor.
- **Perfect fluid energy-momentum tensor** (numerical-estimate, GA §4.5 p.53 (pdf 70)): Isotropic fluid in equilibrium with density rho and pressure p has rest-frame T = diag(rho, p, p, p); later used for stars and cosmology (our note, GA ch12 and Part III). Key numbers: Dust: diag(rho,0,0,0). Fluid: diag(rho,p,p,p).
- **Relativistic Doppler effect** (observation, GA p.55 (pdf 72)): Exercise 4.8 derives the Doppler shift by Lorentz-transforming k^mu = (omega, k); Appendix E gives omega' = gamma(omega - beta k^x).

## Historical notes

- **Élie Cartan:** Pioneered differential geometry based on differential forms, from which the term 1-form comes. — Explains the name and foreshadows the forms machinery of Part V (Cartan's method, GA ch36). (GA p.43 (pdf 60))
- **Louis de Broglie (1924):** Proposed that all particles have wave properties, with p = hbar k. — Provides the physical wave whose constant-phase surfaces illustrate a 1-form. (GA §4.2 p.47 (pdf 64))
- **Roger Penrose:** Invented abstract index notation, in which indices name tensor slots rather than components. — An alternative notation used by advanced texts such as Wald; the book mentions but does not adopt it. (GA §4.4 p.50 (pdf 67))
- **John Keats (1820):** Epigraph from 'Ode on a Grecian Urn' about a silent form that teases us out of thought. — A pun on '1-form'; sets the playful tone. (GA p.43 (pdf 60))

## Notation and conventions

- **Metric signature:** (-,+,+,+): eta_{mu nu} = diag(-1,1,1,1), so lowering the time index flips its sign (X_0 = -X^0, p_0 = -E). — Origin of the minus signs in p̃(u) = -E and J̃(u) = -n. (GA §4.1 p.45 (pdf 62))
- **Units:** c = 1 throughout; rest energy per particle written m (mc^2 when c is restored); gamma = (1 - v^2)^{-1/2}. (GA §4.5 p.52 (pdf 69))
- **Vectors versus 1-forms:** Bold letters (X) for vectors; bold with a tilde (sigmã) for 1-forms; basis 1-forms are bold omega^mu without a tilde; basis vectors bold e_mu. (GA p.43 (pdf 60))
- **Index placement:** Vector components and basis 1-forms carry upper indices; 1-form components and basis vectors carry lower indices; index position signals transformation behaviour. (GA §4.3 p.49 (pdf 66))
- **Valence (m,n):** m counts upper indices (1-form slots), n counts lower indices (vector slots); vectors (1,0), 1-forms (0,1), metric (0,2). — Some texts reverse the order or use 'type' or 'rank'. (GA §4.2 p.46 (pdf 63))
- **Empty slots and angle brackets:** Tensors written with empty slots, e.g. eta( , ), T( , ), p̃( ); the contraction of a 1-form and vector written <sigmã, X> with the 1-form first. (GA §4.2 p.46 (pdf 63))
- **Transformation matrices:** Primed indices denote the new coordinates; Lambda^{alpha'}_mu = ∂x^{alpha'}/∂x^mu and Lambda^mu_{alpha'} = ∂x^mu/∂x^{alpha'}, distinguished only by where the prime sits. (GA §4.3 p.49 (pdf 66))
- **Outer product:** ⊗ between tensors records slot order; each factor acts on its own slot. — Also (loosely) written between numbers once slots are filled in eqns 4.58-4.60. (GA §4.4 p.50 (pdf 67))
- **Abstract indices:** Not used in the book; if used, Roman letters label slots and Greek letters label components. (GA §4.4 p.50 (pdf 67))
- **Observer notation and densities:** O_u is the observer whose 4-velocity is u; n_0 is rest-frame number density, n = gamma n_0 the density in the measurement frame; rho_0 = m n_0. — u denotes the dust velocity in Example 4.10 but the observer's velocity in Example 4.11, where the dust velocity is v. (GA §4.5 p.54 (pdf 71))
- **Energy-momentum tensor names:** T( , ) with components T_{mu nu} or T^{mu nu}; also called the stress-energy tensor; p in the fluid matrix is pressure. (GA §4.5 p.53 (pdf 70))

### Point a straight arrow North from Oxford and watch it end up above Durham: vectors live in tangent spaces. · GA p.43 (pdf 60)

**Why it works:** One concrete, slightly comic image makes the abstract tangent-space idea unforgettable and explains why displacements are not vectors on curved spaces.

**App idea:** Globe demo with a tangent-arrow length slider and a live height gap d^2/(2R).

**Why it works:** Turns index contraction into a visual, countable rule that learners can apply before mastering index gymnastics.

**App idea:** Tensor-machine builder that shows the leftover valence after each insertion and generates the matching index expression.

**Why it works:** Gives 1-forms their own geometric identity and ties them to phase, something physically meaningful that needs no metric.

**App idea:** Plane-wave (t,x) diagram where dragging two events counts phase lines crossed and matches k_mu x^mu.

**Why it works:** Makes the difference between index positions visible and shows why the metric is needed to convert between them.

**App idea:** Draggable oblique basis with both constructions and live metric/inverse metric matrices.

## Gaps and pitfalls

- **The gradient of a scalar field, the most natural example of a 1-form (contour surfaces of a function), is never mentioned; the plane-stack picture is presented only for constant 1-forms.** — Learners may think 1-forms are exotic and miss that df is the prototype, making Part V and the chain-rule origin of transformation laws feel unmotivated. *Suggestion:* Introduce df with contour maps: level sets of a function are the sheets, and df(v) is the directional derivative; the Jacobian transformation law then follows from the chain rule.
- **Crossing counts need orientation and can be fractional, but the text speaks only of 'number of planes pierced'.** — Sign and non-integer contractions (including zero for arrows within a sheet) become confusing. *Suggestion:* Label the sheets with increasing values and define the contraction as the change in label along the arrow.
- **Printed index slips: the text before eqn 4.29 writes the contraction as sigma_mu X^nu; eqn 4.46 repeats mu in omega^mu ⊗ omega^mu; the component formula after 4.51 reads J_mu rho_nu instead of J_mu p_nu; Exercise 4.3 sums mu from 0 to 4.** — Careful readers checking index balance will be confused or lose trust in the rules just taught. *Suggestion:* Use the corrected forms sigma_mu X^mu, eta_{mu nu} omega^mu ⊗ omega^nu, T_{mu nu} = J_mu p_nu, and a sum from 0 to 3; turn the slips into 'spot the error' index-balance checks.
- **Eqn 4.48 states that eta_{mu nu} = eta^{mu nu} follows from the inverse relation, and §4.3 uses general Jacobians ∂x'/∂x while still calling the metric eta.** — Conflicts with Example 4.2's non-diagonal metric and with Exercise 4.4's curvilinear coordinates, where metric components are not diag(-1,1,1,1) and inverse metric components differ. *Suggestion:* State that the equality is special to orthonormal inertial bases; in general coordinates the metric is g_{mu nu} with a genuinely different inverse (bridge to GA ch5).
- **In Example 4.2 the claim that X_1 is the projection along e_1 is only a perpendicular foot when e_1 has unit length.** — Learners may misread down components as lengths in a basis with non-unit vectors. *Suggestion:* Show both unit and non-unit basis cases, displaying X_j = |X||e_j| cos theta.
- **The energy-momentum tensor is defined as J̃ ⊗ p̃ only for dust with a single velocity; symmetry is asserted without saying it relies on J and p being parallel.** — Learners may think T is always an outer product of two 1-forms and miss where pressure terms come from. *Suggestion:* Flag the definition as dust-specific, show how symmetry follows from J ∝ p, and preview T = (rho + p)u ⊗ u + p g from GA ch12.
- **Symbols shift within §4.5: u is the cloud velocity in Example 4.10 but the observer velocity in Example 4.11 (dust velocity v), ⊗ is written between numbers in eqns 4.58-4.60, p means both momentum and pressure, and T^{0i} is called mass flux.** — Easy to conflate observer and matter velocities, which is exactly the distinction the slot probing is meant to teach. *Suggestion:* Use distinct colours or symbols (U for matter, u_obs for observer), write plain products after filling slots, and say 'energy flux (= momentum density with c = 1)'.
- **The chapter's opening sends readers to Section 4.3 for linearity, but linearity is stated in §4.2 (eqn 4.17 and the margin box).** — Minor navigation confusion. *Suggestion:* Point to §4.2 and restate multilinearity when defining a general tensor in §4.4.
- **The component-first route (absorbing eta) comes before the metric-free definition, and the tangent-space picture is dropped immediately in favour of flat Minkowski space.** — Learners can form the misconception that 1-forms are merely lowered vectors and not see where tangent and dual spaces matter. *Suggestion:* In an app, lead with the linear-map definition and the plane picture, then add the metric as an optional bridge; revisit dual spaces at a point when curvature arrives.
- **Why canonical momentum p_mu = ∂L/∂ẋ^mu naturally has a lower index is asserted without argument.** — Seems arbitrary; learners miss a nice consistency check. *Suggestion:* Show that differentiating a scalar with respect to up components gives an object that transforms with the inverse Jacobian (connect to Exercise 4.6).

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'If you walk a straight line due North from one city, do you arrive at a city 300 km away?' Let the learner discover the gap before naming tangent spaces.
- Then ask: 'What kind of object, combined with a vector, always gives the same number for every observer?' Guide toward the need for a partner that transforms oppositely, and use bras and kets only if the learner knows quantum mechanics.
- Recommended order: linear-map definition of a 1-form and the sheet picture; duality relation and sigma_mu X^mu; the metric as a bridge (lowering/raising); transformation laws from invariance; general tensors as slot machines; energy-momentum tensor.
- Check understanding of the sheet picture with: 'Doubling a 1-form does what to its sheets?' (halves the spacing) and 'What contraction does an arrow lying inside a sheet give?' (zero).
- For Minkowski lowering, have the learner compute p_mu for p^mu = (E, p) and then p_mu u^mu in the rest frame to recover -E; this exposes the sign convention quickly.
- Use an oblique 2D basis (as in Fig. 4.4) to break the belief that up and down components differ only by signs; a quick numeric case with a 60-degree angle between unit basis vectors works well.
- When learners struggle with valence, have them count slots aloud: a (0,2) machine with one vector inserted is a (0,1) machine, i.e. a 1-form.
- Common question: 'Is a 1-form just a row vector?' Answer: in components it can be displayed that way, but it is a geometric object independent of basis, and it exists without a metric; row/column is a representation choice.
- Common question: 'Why the minus sign in E = -p̃(u)?' Answer: the signature (-,+,+,+) makes the lowered time component negative; with the opposite signature the sign flips.
- Before §4.5, rehearse length contraction; ask the learner to predict how the number density and energy density of a moving box of dust change before revealing gamma and gamma^2.
- When interpreting T, keep two velocities distinct: the matter's 4-velocity builds T, and the observer's 4-velocity goes into the slots. Ask 'Whose velocity is this?' at every step.
- Point out the printed slips (4.46, the component formula after 4.51, Exercise 4.3) as practice in checking that indices balance; this reinforces the chapter's main rule.
- Forward link for motivated learners: the energy-momentum tensor is the right-hand side of Einstein's equation; the metric generalizes to g_{mu nu} in the next chapter.
