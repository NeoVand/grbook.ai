# GA ch03 · Coordinates

> Argues that physics should ideally be coordinate-free, then concedes that coordinates are needed and uses plane polar coordinates to show how components and basis vectors transform, why the natural coordinate basis vector e_theta grows with r, how coordinate (holonomic) and normalized non-coordinate bases differ via loop closure and the Lie bracket, why the position vector must be retired, and how non-Euclidean spaces (Minkowski, the sphere) break Euclid's circle and triangle results.

**Pages:** printed 36–42 · pdf 53–59 · **Difficulty:** math 2/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 4/5

Computationally light: partial derivatives, 2 by 2 matrices and trigonometry. Conceptually the chapter packs several deep ideas (coordinate versus non-coordinate bases, Lie brackets, vectors as derivatives, why coordinates are not vectors) into a few pages, treating some loosely and deferring justification to Chapters 10 and 31. The Section 3.2 argument blurs flat curvilinear coordinates with curved spacetime, and the term non-Euclidean is used more broadly than in mathematics, which can confuse careful readers. The sphere results are stated without proof, but the exercises supply scaffolded proofs.

## Role in the book

Third chapter of Part I, sitting between Chapter 2 (vectors, components and Lorentz transformation laws in flat spacetime) and Chapters 4-5 (one-forms, tensors and the metric). It generalizes the Chapter 2 transformation rules from Lorentz boosts to arbitrary, nonlinear coordinate changes, which is the step that makes the machinery usable in curved spacetime. It plants several seeds that the book harvests later: basis vectors as derivative operators (Chapter 31), non-coordinate orthonormal frames (Chapter 10), velocity as a tangent to a world line (Chapter 7), derivatives of basis vectors that become connection coefficients (Chapters 7 and 9), and intrinsic curvature tests with circles and triangles (Chapters 11 and 30). It is short and light on formalism by design: a vocabulary chapter rather than a derivation chapter.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can explain why coordinate-free (geometric) statements of physical laws are preferred, using the example of Newton's second law acquiring centrifugal and Coriolis terms in a rotating frame.
- Reader can define Euclidean space through the metric components delta_mu_nu and use it to compute lengths and angles.
- Reader can build the Jacobian matrices between Cartesian and plane polar coordinates and use them to transform the components of an infinitesimal displacement.
- Reader can derive the coordinate basis vectors e_r and e_theta from the basis-vector transformation law and show that e_theta has length r.
- Reader can distinguish a coordinate (holonomic) basis from a non-coordinate (anholonomic) basis, both by the closed-loop walking picture and by computing the Lie bracket.
- Reader can identify coordinate basis vectors with partial-derivative operators via the chain rule along a parametrized path, and compute [e_hat_r, e_hat_theta] = -e_hat_theta / r.
- Reader can explain why the position vector x = x^mu e_mu is not a vector under general coordinate changes while infinitesimal displacements are, and state the slogan that coordinates are not vectors.
- Reader can state what makes a space non-Euclidean, recognise Minkowski spacetime as non-Euclidean yet flat, and write its line element in (-,+,+,+) form.
- Reader can quote and use the sphere results C = 2 pi r sinc(r/R) and angle sum = pi + A/R^2, and recover Euclid's results in the small-circle or small-triangle limit.
- Reader can extract a curvature K = 1/R^2 from the deficit of circle circumferences in the limit of small circles, and prove Girard's theorem by counting lune areas.
- Reader can transform the Minkowski line element into a uniformly rotating frame and interpret the new metric components.

## Teaching approach

A brisk, conversational vocabulary chapter. It opens by questioning whether coordinates are needed at all, grants that practical problems force them on us, and then works almost entirely inside one familiar example, plane polar coordinates, so that every new idea (transformation matrices, coordinate basis, non-coordinate basis, Lie bracket, vectors as derivatives) is met on known ground. Surprising outputs are embraced rather than hidden: the unnormalized e_theta is presented as a feature, and a loop-walking picture explains why. The chapter then clears away the position vector with a geographic counterexample and closes with non-Euclidean geometry stated (not proved) in example boxes, handing the proofs and a first curvature definition to guided exercises. Humor (Shakespeare, the Death Star) and margin slogans carry the informal voice.

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, components-first, example-driven, diagram-driven, conversational-informal, spiral-revisit

**Narrative arc**

1. Provocation: ask whether coordinates are even needed; a law like f = dp/dtau is frame-independent, while a rotating frame dresses it with pseudo-forces.
2. Concession: symmetries and frame-specific values drag us back to coordinates, so the authors list the vocabulary still to be defined: Euclidean and non-Euclidean space, Cartesian coordinates, and coordinate versus non-coordinate bases.
3. Model: Euclidean space defined by the identity metric delta_mu_nu, giving the usual scalar product, length and angle; restrict to the plane.
4. Two descriptions of the same plane: Cartesian (x, y) and polar (r, theta), with the familiar conversion formulas (Fig. 3.1).
5. Formalism reused: the Chapter 2 component law, now with a coordinate-dependent Jacobian, applied to displacement components (Example 3.1).
6. Formalism reused again: the basis-vector law gives e_r and e_theta (Example 3.2), which turn out not to be unit vectors (e_theta . e_theta = r^2).
7. Reinterpretation: the growth of e_theta is required for a coordinate (holonomic) basis; normalized vectors form a non-coordinate basis; a closed-loop walk distinguishes them (Figs. 3.2, 3.3).
8. Check via a new representation: tangent vectors as derivative operators along a path, e_mu = partial/partial x^mu, making the Lie bracket computable (Example 3.3).
9. Clearing debris: the position vector fails the general transformation law because nonlinear coordinate changes have coordinate-dependent coefficients; a New York to Tokyo arrow would leave the Earth's surface; keep coordinates as labels and infinitesimal displacements as vectors.
10. Application to geometry: non-Euclidean spaces, first Minkowski spacetime (non-Euclidean but flat), then the sphere where circle circumference and triangle angle sums deviate (Example 3.4).
11. Consolidation through exercises: inverse basis transformation, derivatives of polar basis vectors, curvature from shrinking circles, Girard's theorem by lune areas, and the Minkowski metric in a rotating frame.

**Signature moves**

- Open a technical chapter by doubting that its subject is needed, then justify it pragmatically.
- Apply a known formula mechanically, let it produce an unexpected result (a basis vector whose length is r), and reframe the surprise as a necessary property.
- Explain holonomic versus anholonomic bases by walking a four-step loop on a polar grid and checking whether it closes.
- Pattern-match the chain rule d/dlambda = (dx^mu/dlambda) partial_mu against X = X^mu e_mu to identify basis vectors with derivative operators, which turns the Lie bracket into a one-line calculation.
- Use everyday geography (an arrow from New York to Tokyo tunnelling through the Earth) to show that finite displacements do not live in a curved space.
- Condense a caution into a margin slogan: coordinates are not vectors.
- State striking non-Euclidean results in an example box and defer their proofs to scaffolded exercises that also introduce a limit definition of curvature.
- Lighten dense material with literary and pop-culture jokes and with a fair-credit historical footnote.

## Section by section

### Chapter opening: do we need coordinates? — p.36 (pdf 53)

After a Shakespeare epigraph about girdling the Earth, the authors contrast a coordinate-free vector law (f = dp/dtau) with its component version in a rotating frame, which acquires centrifugal and Coriolis terms although the physics is unchanged. They advocate geometric statements whenever possible but admit that particular problems, frame-specific values and symmetries require coordinates, and list the terms the chapter will define.

**Concepts:** coordinate-free formulation of physical laws, rotating reference frame

**Key moves**
- A vector equation relates arrows in spacetime and holds in any coordinates.
- Rewriting it in a rotating frame produces pseudo-force terms: coordinate artefacts, not new physics.
- Coordinates remain necessary for frame-specific values and for exploiting symmetry.

### §3.1 Coordinates in Euclidean space — p.36 (pdf 53)

Defines Euclidean space as the space whose scalar product uses the identity metric delta_mu_nu, so lengths and angles take their school forms, and specializes to the plane. The plane is labelled either by Cartesian (x, y) or plane polar (r, theta) coordinates. The Chapter 2 transformation law for components, now written with a Jacobian of partial derivatives, converts displacement components dx, dy into dr, dtheta (Example 3.1), and the opposite law for basis vectors produces e_r and e_theta in terms of e_x and e_y (Example 3.2). These differ from the textbook unit vectors: e_theta has squared length r^2.

**Concepts:** Euclidean space, Euclidean metric, Cartesian coordinates, plane polar coordinates, curvilinear coordinates, Jacobian matrix of a coordinate transformation, transformation law for vector components, transformation law for basis vectors, infinitesimal displacement vector

**Key moves**
- Scalar product X . Y = delta_mu_nu X^mu Y^nu defines length and angle.
- Reuse the Chapter 2 law with Lambda^{mu'}_nu = partial x^{mu'} / partial x^nu, which is now position dependent.
- Compute both Jacobians (derivatives of x, y with respect to r, theta, and the reverse) and assemble the displacement transformation matrix.
- Transform basis vectors with the inverse-direction Jacobian to get e_r = cos theta e_x + sin theta e_y and e_theta = -r sin theta e_x + r cos theta e_y.
- Compare with the normalized textbook vectors and compute e_r . e_r = 1, e_theta . e_theta = r^2.

### Coordinate versus non-coordinate bases and vectors as derivatives (end of 3.1) — p.38 (pdf 55)

Explains why the growing e_theta is desirable. The unhatted vectors are a coordinate (holonomic) basis; the normalized ones are a non-coordinate (anholonomic) basis. The loose test is to walk a closed loop of steps along the basis directions: with coordinate basis vectors the loop closes because the outer arc is automatically longer, while with normalized vectors equal arcs at different radii subtend different angles and the loop fails to close (Figs. 3.2, 3.3). Example 3.3 makes this precise by writing the derivative of a function along a path as a sum of components times partial derivatives, identifying e_mu with partial/partial x^mu, and computing that partial derivatives commute whereas the normalized pair has bracket -e_hat_theta/r. Detailed treatment is deferred to Chapters 10 and 31.

**Concepts:** coordinate basis, non-coordinate basis, orthonormal polar basis, Lie bracket, loop-closure test for a coordinate basis, directional derivative along a curve, tangent vectors as derivative operators

**Key moves**
- Norm growth of e_theta is a feature: steps along e_theta must cover more distance at larger r.
- Closed loop +e_r, +e_theta, -e_r, -e_theta returns to its start for coordinate basis vectors, not for normalized ones.
- Chain rule along x^mu(lambda): df/dlambda = (dx^mu/dlambda) partial f / partial x^mu, valid for any f, so the operator itself has the shape of a vector.
- Identify e_mu with partial_mu; coordinate basis vectors commute by equality of mixed partial derivatives.
- Apply the commutator of e_hat_r = partial_r and e_hat_theta = (1/r) partial_theta to a test function to get -e_hat_theta / r.

### §3.2 Farewell to the position vector — p.39 (pdf 56)

The position vector worked in special relativity because Lorentz transformations are linear, but in general relativity it is abandoned. If coordinates are related by x' = A x with coefficients that depend on position, the Jacobian is not A and the coordinates do not obey the vector transformation law; only linear homogeneous maps (Lorentz boosts, rotations) are safe. Geometrically, on a curved surface a finite displacement is not even in the space: the straight arrow from New York to Tokyo runs through the Earth, whereas a path of infinitesimal steps stays on the surface. Coordinates stay as event labels, infinitesimal displacements stay as vectors, and velocity will be rebuilt as a tangent to a world line in Chapter 7.

**Concepts:** position vector, coordinates are not vectors, linear homogeneous transformation, tangent vector, four-velocity as tangent to a world line

**Key moves**
- State the general vector law X^{mu'} = (partial x^{mu'} / partial x^nu) X^nu.
- Coordinates themselves transform through coefficients that depend on position, so partial x^{mu'} / partial x^nu differs from those coefficients and x^mu is not a vector.
- Special case: constant coefficients (Lorentz, rotations) rescue the position vector.
- Geographic picture: a finite chord leaves the surface; a chain of infinitesimal displacements does not.
- Reassure: velocity survives as the tangent to a world line; infinitesimal displacement still transforms correctly.

### §3.3 Non-Euclidean space — p.40 (pdf 57)

Defines non-Euclidean space negatively, as any space whose metric is not delta_mu_nu, and offers Minkowski spacetime with ds^2 = -dt^2 + dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2 as the first example, a (3+1)-dimensional space. Example 3.4 then shows Euclid's results failing on the surface of a sphere of radius R: a circle of surface radius r has circumference 2 pi r sinc(r/R), and by Girard's theorem the angles of a spherical triangle of area A sum to pi + A/R^2. Both reduce to the Euclidean values for small r or small A; proofs are assigned as Exercises 3.3 and 3.4.

**Concepts:** non-Euclidean space, Minkowski spacetime, flat space, great circle, spherical triangle, circumference of a circle on a sphere, Girard's theorem

**Key moves**
- Non-Euclidean is defined by the metric, not by curvature, so flat Minkowski spacetime qualifies.
- On a sphere a circle's circumference falls short of 2 pi r, by an amount controlled by r/R.
- Triangle angle sums exceed pi by the triangle's area divided by R^2.
- Euclid is recovered as a small-size limit, hinting that curvature is visible only on scales comparable to R.

### Chapter summary and exercises — p.41 (pdf 58)

The summary restates that Euclidean space may be described in several coordinate systems, that coordinate basis vectors obtained by transformation have vanishing Lie brackets unlike a non-coordinate basis, and that a non-Euclidean metric such as Minkowski's may still be flat. Five exercises follow: inverting the polar basis transformation, differentiating polar basis vectors, extracting curvature from small circles, proving Girard's theorem with lunes (Fig. 3.6), and writing the Minkowski metric in a rotating frame.

**Concepts:** Gaussian curvature, lune, rotating reference frame, derivatives of basis vectors

**Key moves**
- Curvature K = 1/R^2 is defined operationally as the small-circle limit of the circumference deficit.
- Girard's theorem follows from area bookkeeping: six lune-minus-triangle strips plus two triangles tile the sphere.
- A rotating frame gives a non-diagonal, position-dependent Minkowski metric, closing the loop with the chapter opening.

## Concepts

### coordinate-free formulation of physical laws

*principle · introduced* · also: geometric formulation, staying above the fray

Writing physical laws as relations between geometric objects (vectors, tensors) that do not refer to any coordinate system, so the statement holds unchanged in every frame; components appear only when a basis is chosen.

**How introduced:** Motivated by Newton's second law f = dp/dtau, which is a relation between two spacetime arrows; in a rotating frame its components pick up centrifugal and Coriolis terms although nothing physical has changed.

**Prerequisites:** vector, rotating reference frame

$$
\mathbf{f} = \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{p}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}
$$

**Notes:** The ideal the whole book pursues; Part V returns to it with forms and index-free geometry.

**Where:** GA p.36 (pdf 53)

### rotating reference frame

*definition · mention* · also: uniformly rotating frame

A frame whose spatial axes turn at angular speed Omega relative to an inertial frame; in it, laws written in components acquire pseudo-force terms, and the Minkowski metric picks up cross terms coupling dt' to dx' and dy' plus a g_tt that depends on distance from the rotation axis (none of the components depend on t').

**How introduced:** Used at the opening as the example of coordinates complicating a simple law, and returned to in Exercise 3.5, where the coordinate change to a rotating frame is applied to the Minkowski line element.

**Prerequisites:** coordinate transformation

$$
x = x'\cos\Omega t' - y'\sin\Omega t',\; y = x'\sin\Omega t' + y'\cos\Omega t'
$$

**Where:** GA p.36 (pdf 53); GA p.42 (pdf 59)

### Euclidean space

*definition · developed* · also: flat Euclidean space, Euclidean plane (n = 2)

An n-dimensional space of points in which the scalar product of two vectors is the plain sum of products of their components in Cartesian coordinates, i.e. the metric components are delta_mu_nu; lengths, angles and Euclid's theorems (angle sum pi, circumference 2 pi r) follow.

**How introduced:** Defined at the start of Section 3.1 via its scalar product, with length and angle derived from it, and tied to Euclid's Elements; the authors immediately specialise to the plane.

**Prerequisites:** scalar product, Euclidean metric

$$
\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{Y} = \sum_\mu X^\mu Y^\mu
$$

$$
|\mathbf{X}| = \sqrt{\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{X}}
$$

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.36 (pdf 53); GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54)

### Euclidean metric

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: Kronecker delta metric, delta_mu_nu, identity metric

The metric whose components in Cartesian coordinates form the identity matrix, delta_mu_nu, so X . Y = delta_mu_nu X^mu Y^nu and ds^2 = delta_mu_nu dx^mu dx^nu.

**How introduced:** Given in a margin note as the identity-matrix form of the metric in the scalar product, then used in Section 3.3 as the benchmark against which non-Euclidean spaces are defined.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein summation convention, scalar product

$$
\delta_{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(1,1,1)
$$

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = \delta_{\mu\nu}\,\mathrm{d}x^\mu\mathrm{d}x^\nu
$$

**Notes:** The components are delta_mu_nu only in Cartesian coordinates; in polar coordinates the same flat metric has components diag(1, r^2), a point the chapter implies through eqn 3.11 but does not state.

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.36 (pdf 53); GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57)

### Cartesian coordinates

*definition · introduced* · also: rectangular coordinates

Coordinates that label each point of Euclidean space by its signed distances along mutually perpendicular straight axes, so that the metric components are the identity.

**How introduced:** Introduced historically (Descartes, 1637) with Fig. 3.1(a), a grid with a point located by (x0, y0).

**Prerequisites:** Euclidean space

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54)

### plane polar coordinates

*definition · core* · also: polar coordinates, cylindrical polar coordinates (book's slip in Example 3.1)

Coordinates (r, theta) on the Euclidean plane giving the distance from the origin and the angle from a reference axis, related to Cartesian ones by x = r cos theta, y = r sin theta.

**How introduced:** Presented as the alternative description of the same plane in Fig. 3.1(b); then used as the single running example for every idea in Section 3.1.

**Prerequisites:** Cartesian coordinates

$$
x = r\cos\theta,\; y = r\sin\theta,\; r = (x^2+y^2)^{1/2},\; \tan\theta = y/x
$$

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54); GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55); GA §3.1 p.39 (pdf 56)

### curvilinear coordinates

*definition · mention*

Coordinates whose coordinate lines are not all straight and mutually orthogonal with uniform spacing, so the line element is not simply the sum of squared coordinate differentials (for polar coordinates ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2 dtheta^2, not dr^2 + dtheta^2).

**How introduced:** Named in a margin note that calls polar coordinates the first example and points out that Pythagoras fails in its naive form.

**Prerequisites:** plane polar coordinates

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = \mathrm{d}r^2 + r^2\mathrm{d}\theta^2
$$

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54)

### Jacobian matrix of a coordinate transformation

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: transformation matrix Lambda, Lambda^{mu'}_nu

The matrix of partial derivatives partial x^{mu'} / partial x^nu between two coordinate systems; it maps components of vectors at a point, depends on position for nonlinear transformations, and its inverse is partial x^nu / partial x^{mu'}.

**How introduced:** Carried over from the Lorentz matrix of Chapter 2 and computed explicitly for Cartesian to polar coordinates in Example 3.1, where its entries depend on r and theta.

**Prerequisites:** partial derivative, plane polar coordinates

$$
\Lambda^{\mu'}{}_{\nu} = \frac{\partial x^{\mu'}}{\partial x^\nu}
$$

$$
\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & \sin\theta\\ -\sin\theta/r & \cos\theta/r\end{pmatrix}
$$

**Notes:** The chapter never shows explicitly that the matrices built from eqns 3.4 and 3.5 are inverses; multiplying them is a worthwhile check.

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54); GA §3.2 p.39 (pdf 56)

### transformation law for vector components

*law · core* · also: contravariant transformation law, vector transformation rule

Under a change of coordinates the components of a vector at a point change by the Jacobian: X^{mu'} = (partial x^{mu'} / partial x^nu) X^nu. Any set of quantities that obeys this is a vector.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapter 2 (eqn 2.11), rewritten with partial derivatives in eqn 3.3, used on displacement components in Example 3.1 and elevated in Section 3.2 to the test that the position vector fails.

**Prerequisites:** Jacobian matrix of a coordinate transformation, vector

$$
X^{\mu'} = \frac{\partial x^{\mu'}}{\partial x^\nu}X^\nu
$$

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54); GA §3.2 p.39 (pdf 56)

### transformation law for basis vectors

*law · core*

Basis vectors transform with the inverse Jacobian, e_{mu'} = (partial x^nu / partial x^{mu'}) e_nu, so that the vector X = X^mu e_mu itself is unchanged by the coordinate change.

**How introduced:** Recalled from eqn 2.25 as the opposite of the component law (eqn 3.7) and used in Example 3.2 to generate the polar basis vectors.

**Prerequisites:** transformation law for vector components

$$
\mathbf{e}_{\mu'} = \frac{\partial x^\nu}{\partial x^{\mu'}}\mathbf{e}_\nu
$$

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54); GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

### infinitesimal displacement vector

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: dx, displacement vector d x = dx^mu e_mu

The vector dx = dx^mu e_mu joining a point to an infinitesimally nearby point; its components transform with the Jacobian and it lies in the space even when the space is curved.

**How introduced:** Used as the test case for the component transformation in Example 3.1, and in Section 3.2 as the well-behaved replacement for the finite position vector.

**Prerequisites:** transformation law for vector components

$$
\mathrm{d}x^{\alpha'} = \frac{\partial x^{\alpha'}}{\partial x^\mu}\mathrm{d}x^\mu
$$

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54); GA §3.2 p.40 (pdf 57)

### coordinate basis

*definition · core* · also: holonomic basis, natural basis, e_mu = partial/partial x^mu

A basis of vector fields obtained from a coordinate system, e_mu = partial/partial x^mu, each vector pointing along a coordinate line and sized so that one unit of it advances that coordinate by one; its members have vanishing Lie brackets.

**How introduced:** Arrived at by plugging polar coordinates into the basis-vector law, noticing e_theta has length r, and explaining this with a closed loop that only closes if the theta-step grows with r; then confirmed by writing e_mu as partial derivatives.

**Prerequisites:** transformation law for basis vectors, tangent vectors as derivative operators

$$
\mathbf{e}_r = \cos\theta\,\mathbf{e}_x + \sin\theta\,\mathbf{e}_y
$$

$$
\mathbf{e}_\theta = -r\sin\theta\,\mathbf{e}_x + r\cos\theta\,\mathbf{e}_y
$$

$$
[\mathbf{e}_r,\mathbf{e}_\theta] = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55); GA §3.1 p.39 (pdf 56); GA p.41 (pdf 58)

### non-coordinate basis

*definition · developed* · also: anholonomic basis, non-holonomic basis

A basis of vector fields that cannot be written as partial derivatives with respect to any coordinates; at least one Lie bracket among its members is non-zero, so stepping around a small loop along its vectors fails to close.

**How introduced:** Introduced by contrast with the unhatted polar vectors: the normalized textbook pair e_hat_r, e_hat_theta is shown to have a non-zero bracket and a non-closing loop.

**Prerequisites:** coordinate basis, Lie bracket

$$
[\hat{\mathbf{e}}_r,\hat{\mathbf{e}}_\theta] = -\frac{\hat{\mathbf{e}}_\theta}{r}
$$

**Notes:** Returns in Chapter 10 as orthonormal measurement frames (vielbeins).

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55); GA §3.1 p.39 (pdf 56); GA p.41 (pdf 58)

### orthonormal polar basis

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: normalized polar basis, unit vectors r-hat and theta-hat

The unit vectors e_hat_r = cos theta e_x + sin theta e_y and e_hat_theta = -sin theta e_x + cos theta e_y, orthogonal and of unit length at every point; as derivative operators e_hat_r = partial_r and e_hat_theta = (1/r) partial_theta.

**How introduced:** Quoted as what elementary treatments use, then shown to be a non-coordinate basis.

**Prerequisites:** plane polar coordinates, non-coordinate basis

$$
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_\theta = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}
$$

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55); GA §3.1 p.39 (pdf 56)

### metric components as basis-vector dot products

*identity · mention* · also: g_mu_nu = e_mu . e_nu

The metric components in a basis are the scalar products of the basis vectors; for the polar coordinate basis g_rr = 1, g_theta_theta = r^2, g_r_theta = 0.

**How introduced:** Implicit in eqn 3.11, where the norms of e_r and e_theta are computed; the general statement is made in Chapter 5.

**Prerequisites:** coordinate basis, Euclidean metric

$$
\mathbf{e}_\theta\cdot\mathbf{e}_\theta = r^2
$$

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

### Lie bracket

*operation · introduced* · also: commutator of vector fields, [X, Y]

For vector fields viewed as derivative operators, [X, Y] f = X(Y f) - Y(X f); the result is again a vector field, and for small steps it measures by how much a loop of flows along X, Y, -X, -Y fails to close.

**How introduced:** Named loosely during the loop discussion, then computed properly in Example 3.3 once basis vectors are partial derivatives; the non-zero bracket of the normalized polar vectors is derived on a test function.

**Prerequisites:** tangent vectors as derivative operators, partial derivative

$$
[\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}]f = \mathbf{X}(\mathbf{Y}f) - \mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{X}f)
$$

**Notes:** Formalized in Part V with Lie derivatives (Chapter 33).

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55); GA §3.1 p.39 (pdf 56)

### loop-closure test for a coordinate basis

*technique · introduced* · also: closed-loop walk, holonomy picture

Step along basis vector field A, then B, then back along A, then back along B; the loop closes (to second order in step size) for every pair exactly when the basis vector fields commute, i.e. when they form a coordinate basis.

**How introduced:** Offered as the intuitive meaning of holonomic, illustrated on a quarter-disk polar grid in Fig. 3.3 with loops ABCDE.

**Prerequisites:** coordinate basis, Lie bracket

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

### parametrized curve

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: path x^mu(lambda), path parameter lambda

A map from a parameter lambda (here in [0, 1]) to points x^mu(lambda) of the space, tracing a path.

**How introduced:** Set up in Example 3.3 so that a function can be evaluated and differentiated along the path.

$$
x^\mu(\lambda)
$$

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.39 (pdf 56)

### directional derivative along a curve

*operation · introduced* · also: derivative along a path, df/dlambda

The rate of change of a scalar function along a parametrized curve, obtained by the chain rule as the curve's tangent components times the partial derivatives of the function.

**How introduced:** Computed in Example 3.3 for an arbitrary function f(x^mu) on a path x^mu(lambda); stripping off f gives an operator.

**Prerequisites:** parametrized curve, partial derivative

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} = \frac{\mathrm{d}x^\mu}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^\mu}
$$

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.39 (pdf 56)

### tangent vectors as derivative operators

*definition · introduced* · also: vectors as derivations, e_mu identified with partial_mu

The modern definition of a tangent vector as a directional-derivative operator: the operator d/dlambda along a curve has components dx^mu/dlambda in the basis partial/partial x^mu, so basis vectors are identified with partial derivatives.

**How introduced:** Presented as a trick suggested by the resemblance between the chain-rule operator and X^mu e_mu; its full justification is postponed to Chapter 31.

**Prerequisites:** directional derivative along a curve, coordinate basis

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} = \frac{\mathrm{d}x^\mu}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}
$$

$$
\mathbf{e}_\mu \leftrightarrow \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}
$$

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.39 (pdf 56)

### position vector

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: displacement vector x = x^mu e_mu, radius vector

The object x = x^mu e_mu built from the coordinates of a point; it behaves as a vector only under linear homogeneous coordinate changes and is not a legitimate vector in general curved-space or curvilinear settings.

**How introduced:** Recalled from its successful use under Lorentz transformations in Chapter 2, then retired in Section 3.2 by showing that nonlinear coordinate changes spoil its transformation and by the New York to Tokyo picture.

**Prerequisites:** transformation law for vector components, linear homogeneous transformation

$$
x^{\mu'} = A^{\mu'}{}_\nu x^\nu
$$

**Where:** GA §3.2 p.39 (pdf 56); GA §3.2 p.40 (pdf 57)

### coordinates are not vectors

*principle · core* · also: farewell to the position vector

Coordinates are labels of points (events); the array x^mu does not transform by the Jacobian under general coordinate changes and so is not the component set of any vector, while infinitesimal coordinate differences dx^mu are.

**How introduced:** Condensed into a margin slogan after the position-vector argument of Section 3.2; footnote 4 points back to Example 3.1 as a concrete failure.

**Prerequisites:** position vector, infinitesimal displacement vector

**Where:** GA §3.2 p.39 (pdf 56); GA §3.2 p.40 (pdf 57)

### linear homogeneous transformation

*definition · introduced* · also: constant-coefficient coordinate change

A coordinate change x^{mu'} = A^{mu'}_nu x^nu with constant coefficients (no shift and no position dependence), such as Lorentz boosts and rotations; for these the Jacobian equals A and coordinates do transform like vector components.

**How introduced:** Named as the special case in which the position vector survives, explaining why special relativity could use it.

**Prerequisites:** Jacobian matrix of a coordinate transformation

$$
\frac{\partial x^{\mu'}}{\partial x^\nu} = A^{\mu'}{}_\nu\ \text{(constant)}
$$

**Where:** GA §3.2 p.39 (pdf 56); GA §3.2 p.40 (pdf 57)

### tangent vector

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: vector at a point, local vector

A vector attached to a single point of a space, representing an infinitesimal displacement or a rate of change along a curve through that point; on a curved space such vectors lie in the tangent space at the point rather than in the space itself.

**How introduced:** Implicit in the New York to Tokyo argument: finite arrows leave the Earth's surface, while chains of infinitesimal displacements stay on it.

**Prerequisites:** infinitesimal displacement vector

**Notes:** The chapter does not use the words tangent space; Chapter 31 and Appendix C supply them.

**Where:** GA §3.2 p.40 (pdf 57)

### four-velocity as tangent to a world line

*definition · mention* · also: velocity vector

The velocity vector of a particle defined geometrically as the tangent vector to its world line parametrized by proper time, rather than as the derivative of a position vector.

**How introduced:** Offered as reassurance that dropping the position vector does not destroy velocity; the construction is deferred to Chapter 7.

**Prerequisites:** tangent vector, parametrized curve

$$
u^\mu = \frac{\mathrm{d}x^\mu}{\mathrm{d}\tau}
$$

**Where:** GA §3.2 p.40 (pdf 57)

### non-Euclidean space

*definition · developed*

In this book's usage, any space whose metric is not the Euclidean delta_mu_nu (equivalently, whose line element cannot be brought to that form); this includes both indefinite flat metrics such as Minkowski's and curved spaces such as the sphere.

**How introduced:** Defined negatively at the start of Section 3.3 and illustrated first by Minkowski spacetime, then by the surface of a sphere where Euclid's theorems fail.

**Prerequisites:** Euclidean metric

**Notes:** In mathematics 'non-Euclidean geometry' usually means curved (spherical or hyperbolic) geometry; the book widens the term to signature as well.

**Where:** GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57); GA p.41 (pdf 58)

### Minkowski spacetime

*solution-or-model · revisited* · also: Minkowski space, (3+1)-dimensional spacetime

The flat spacetime of special relativity with line element ds^2 = -dt^2 + dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2 in inertial coordinates (c = 1): three space dimensions and one time dimension with an indefinite metric eta_mu_nu.

**How introduced:** Given as the first example of a non-Euclidean space and noted in the summary to be flat despite being non-Euclidean.

**Prerequisites:** Euclidean metric, non-Euclidean space

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = \eta_{\mu\nu}\mathrm{d}x^\mu\mathrm{d}x^\nu = -\mathrm{d}t^2+\mathrm{d}x^2+\mathrm{d}y^2+\mathrm{d}z^2
$$

**Where:** GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57); GA p.41 (pdf 58); GA p.42 (pdf 59)

### flat space

*definition · mention* · also: uncurved space

A space (or spacetime) with zero intrinsic curvature, so that coordinates exist in which the metric components are constant everywhere; flatness is independent of the signature and of how complicated the metric looks in a particular coordinate system.

**How introduced:** Asserted in the chapter summary (a non-Euclidean metric can still be flat, as Minkowski's is); no test for flatness is given until the Riemann tensor in Chapter 11.

**Prerequisites:** non-Euclidean space, Minkowski spacetime

**Where:** GA p.41 (pdf 58)

### great circle

*definition · mention*

The intersection of a sphere with a plane through its centre; great circles are the geodesics (locally shortest paths) on the sphere.

**How introduced:** Used to build the spherical triangle of Fig. 3.5 and the lune construction of Fig. 3.6.

**Where:** GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57); GA p.42 (pdf 59)

### spherical triangle

*definition · introduced*

A region on a sphere bounded by arcs of three great circles, with interior angles alpha_1, alpha_2, alpha_3 measured between the tangent directions of the arcs at each vertex.

**How introduced:** Drawn in Fig. 3.5 as the small cap near the top where three great circles cross.

**Prerequisites:** great circle

**Where:** GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57)

### circumference of a circle on a sphere

*theorem · developed* · also: circle circumference deficit

A circle whose radius r is measured along the surface of a sphere of radius R has circumference C = 2 pi R sin(r/R) = 2 pi r sinc(r/R), which is less than 2 pi r and tends to it as r/R goes to zero.

**How introduced:** Stated in Example 3.4 with the Death Star cartoon of Fig. 3.4 as an everyday failure of a Euclidean formula; its proof by elementary geometry is Exercise 3.3.

**Prerequisites:** Euclidean space, great circle

$$
C = 2\pi r\,\mathrm{sinc}\frac{r}{R}
$$

**Where:** GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57); GA p.41 (pdf 58)

### Girard's theorem

*theorem · developed* · also: spherical excess theorem, angle excess

The interior angles of a spherical triangle of area A on a sphere of radius R sum to pi + A/R^2; the excess over pi is proportional to area, and vanishes for triangles small compared with R^2.

**How introduced:** Stated in Example 3.4 with Fig. 3.5, credited in a footnote to Thomas Harriot, and proved in Exercise 3.4 by decomposing the sphere into lune strips (Fig. 3.6).

**Prerequisites:** spherical triangle, lune

$$
\sum_i \alpha_i = \pi + \frac{A}{R^2}
$$

**Where:** GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57); GA p.41 (pdf 58); GA p.42 (pdf 59)

### lune

*definition · mention* · also: spherical lune, digon

The region of a sphere between two great semicircles meeting at antipodal points with angle alpha; on a unit sphere its area is 2 alpha.

**How introduced:** Appears in Exercise 3.4 as the strips (lune minus triangle, area 2 alpha_1 - A) that tile the sphere outside two antipodal triangles.

**Prerequisites:** great circle

$$
A_{\text{lune}} = 2\alpha R^2
$$

**Notes:** The word lune is ours; the book calls them strips.

**Where:** GA p.41 (pdf 58); GA p.42 (pdf 59)

### Gaussian curvature

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: curvature K, intrinsic curvature of a surface

A number at each point of a surface measuring its intrinsic curvature; for a sphere of radius R it is K = 1/R^2, and it can be obtained from how much the circumference of small circles falls short of 2 pi r.

**How introduced:** Introduced only in Exercise 3.3, which rewrites the circle result using K = 1/R^2 and extracts K as a small-circle limit.

**Prerequisites:** circumference of a circle on a sphere

$$
K = \lim_{r\to 0}\frac{3}{\pi r^3}\left(2\pi r - C\right)
$$

**Notes:** Developed properly in Chapter 11 (Riemann tensor) and Chapter 30 (classical curvature).

**Where:** GA p.41 (pdf 58)

### derivatives of basis vectors

*identity · mention* · also: non-constant basis vectors

In curvilinear coordinates the basis vectors change from point to point; for plane polar coordinates the partial derivatives of e_r and e_theta are again combinations of e_r and e_theta with position-dependent coefficients.

**How introduced:** Exercise 3.2 has the reader compute them, foreshadowing connection coefficients.

**Prerequisites:** coordinate basis, plane polar coordinates

$$
\partial_\theta \mathbf{e}_r = \mathbf{e}_\theta/r,\; \partial_\theta\mathbf{e}_\theta = -r\,\mathbf{e}_r,\; \partial_r\mathbf{e}_r = 0,\; \partial_r\mathbf{e}_\theta = \mathbf{e}_\theta/r
$$

**Notes:** The coefficients are the Christoffel symbols of flat polar coordinates (Gamma^theta_{r theta} = 1/r, Gamma^r_{theta theta} = -r), met in Chapters 7 and 9.

**Where:** GA p.41 (pdf 58)

### Einstein summation convention

*convention · mention* · also: summation convention

A repeated index appearing once up and once down in a term is summed over its range, so X^mu e_mu means the sum over mu.

**How introduced:** Used without comment throughout; the margin note on the Euclidean metric invokes it explicitly.

$$
\delta_{\mu\nu}X^\mu Y^\nu
$$

**Where:** GA §3.1 p.36 (pdf 53)

## Key equations

### (3.1) Newton's second law as a vector relation · supporting · GA p.36 (pdf 53)

$$
\mathbf{f} = \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{p}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}
$$

A relation between spacetime vectors valid in every frame; its component form becomes messier in a rotating frame although the physics is the same.

**Symbols:** f four-force; p four-momentum; tau proper time

### Euclidean scalar product · central · GA §3.1 p.36 (pdf 53)

$$
\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{Y} = \delta_{\mu\nu}X^\mu Y^\nu = \sum_\mu X^\mu Y^\mu,\qquad \delta_{\mu\nu} = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
$$

Defines Euclidean space: the metric components in Cartesian coordinates are the identity.

**Symbols:** X, Y vectors; delta_mu_nu Euclidean metric components

### Length and angle from the scalar product · supporting · GA §3.1 p.36 (pdf 53)

$$
|\mathbf{X}| = \sqrt{\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{X}},\qquad \vartheta = \cos^{-1}\frac{\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{Y}}{|\mathbf{X}||\mathbf{Y}|}
$$

The metric alone fixes lengths and angles.

**Symbols:** vartheta angle between X and Y

### (3.2) Cartesian-polar relations · supporting · GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54)

$$
x = r\cos\theta,\quad y = r\sin\theta,\quad r = (x^2+y^2)^{1/2},\quad \tan\theta = y/x
$$

Connects the two labellings of the same point of the plane.

**Symbols:** r distance from origin; theta angle from the x axis

### (3.3) Component transformation law · central · GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54)

$$
X^{\mu'} = \Lambda^{\mu'}{}_{\nu}X^\nu = \left(\frac{\partial x^{\mu'}}{\partial x^\nu}\right)X^\nu
$$

Vector components at a point transform with the Jacobian of the coordinate change; this generalizes the Lorentz-matrix rule of Chapter 2.

**Symbols:** primed indices label new coordinates; Lambda Jacobian matrix

### (3.4) Partial derivatives of x and y with respect to polar coordinates · derivation-step · GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54)

$$
\left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial r}\right)_\theta = \cos\theta,\ \left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial r}\right)_\theta = \sin\theta,\ \left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial\theta}\right)_r = -r\sin\theta,\ \left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial\theta}\right)_r = r\cos\theta
$$

Entries of the Jacobian from polar to Cartesian; they later build the basis vectors.

**Symbols:** subscript on the bracket is the variable held fixed

### (3.5) Partial derivatives of r and theta with respect to Cartesian coordinates · derivation-step · GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54)

$$
\left(\frac{\partial r}{\partial x}\right)_y = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}},\ \left(\frac{\partial r}{\partial y}\right)_x = \frac{y}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}},\ \left(\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x}\right)_y = -\frac{y}{x^2+y^2},\ \left(\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial y}\right)_x = \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}
$$

Entries of the inverse Jacobian, from Cartesian to polar.

### (3.6) Displacement components in polar coordinates · supporting · GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54)

$$
\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}r\\ \mathrm{d}\theta\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}(\partial r/\partial x)_y & (\partial r/\partial y)_x\\ (\partial\theta/\partial x)_y & (\partial\theta/\partial y)_x\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}x\\ \mathrm{d}y\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & \sin\theta\\ -\frac{1}{r}\sin\theta & \frac{1}{r}\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}x\\ \mathrm{d}y\end{pmatrix}
$$

The component law applied to an infinitesimal displacement; the matrix depends on position.

### (3.7) Basis-vector transformation law · central · GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54)

$$
\mathbf{e}_{\mu'} = \Lambda^{\nu}{}_{\mu'}\mathbf{e}_\nu = \left(\frac{\partial x^\nu}{\partial x^{\mu'}}\right)\mathbf{e}_\nu
$$

Basis vectors transform with the inverse Jacobian, keeping X = X^mu e_mu invariant.

### (3.8) Polar coordinate basis vectors · central · GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

$$
\mathbf{e}_r = \cos\theta\,\mathbf{e}_x + \sin\theta\,\mathbf{e}_y,\qquad \mathbf{e}_\theta = -r\sin\theta\,\mathbf{e}_x + r\cos\theta\,\mathbf{e}_y
$$

Coordinate basis of plane polar coordinates in terms of the Cartesian basis; e_theta carries a factor r.

### (3.9) Basis transformation in matrix form · derivation-step · GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

$$
\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{e}_r & \mathbf{e}_\theta\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{e}_x & \mathbf{e}_y\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}(\partial x/\partial r)_\theta & (\partial x/\partial\theta)_r\\ (\partial y/\partial r)_\theta & (\partial y/\partial\theta)_r\end{pmatrix}
$$

Same result as 3.8, with basis vectors as a row multiplied on the right by the Jacobian.

### (3.10) Normalized polar basis vectors · supporting · GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

$$
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_r = \cos\theta\,\mathbf{e}_x + \sin\theta\,\mathbf{e}_y,\qquad \hat{\mathbf{e}}_\theta = -\sin\theta\,\mathbf{e}_x + \cos\theta\,\mathbf{e}_y
$$

The unit vectors of elementary treatments; they form a non-coordinate basis.

**Symbols:** hat marks unit-normalized basis vectors

### (3.11) Norms of the coordinate basis vectors · central · GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

$$
\mathbf{e}_r\cdot\mathbf{e}_r = 1,\qquad \mathbf{e}_\theta\cdot\mathbf{e}_\theta = r^2
$$

e_theta grows with distance from the origin; these are the metric components g_rr and g_theta_theta of the plane in polar coordinates.

### Polar line element (implied by 3.11, not printed in the chapter) · supporting · GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = \mathrm{d}r^2 + r^2\,\mathrm{d}\theta^2
$$

The flat metric of the plane in polar coordinates; equivalent to eqn 3.11 and explains the margin remark that s^2 is not r^2 + theta^2.

### Lie bracket of the coordinate basis · supporting · GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

$$
[\mathbf{e}_r,\mathbf{e}_\theta] = \mathbf{e}_r\mathbf{e}_\theta - \mathbf{e}_\theta\mathbf{e}_r = 0
$$

Coordinate basis vectors commute, so a four-step loop along them closes.

**Symbols:** products of vectors are meaningful only as successive derivative operators

### (3.12) Derivative of a function along a path · supporting · GA §3.1 p.39 (pdf 56)

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} = \frac{\mathrm{d}x^\mu}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^\mu}
$$

Chain rule along a parametrized path.

**Symbols:** f scalar function; x^mu(lambda) path; lambda in [0, 1]

### (3.13) Tangent operator along a path · central · GA §3.1 p.39 (pdf 56)

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} = \frac{\mathrm{d}x^\mu}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}
$$

Has the form components times basis vectors, motivating the identification of e_mu with partial derivatives.

### (3.14) Polar coordinate basis as partial derivatives · central · GA §3.1 p.39 (pdf 56)

$$
\mathbf{e}_r = \frac{\partial}{\partial r},\qquad \mathbf{e}_\theta = \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}
$$

Coordinate basis vectors are the partial derivatives with respect to the coordinates, which commute.

### (3.15) Normalized polar basis as operators · supporting · GA §3.1 p.39 (pdf 56)

$$
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_r = \frac{\partial}{\partial r},\qquad \hat{\mathbf{e}}_\theta = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}
$$

The unit theta vector is a position-dependent multiple of a partial derivative, which is what spoils commutation.

### (3.16) Lie bracket of the normalized polar basis · central · GA §3.1 p.39 (pdf 56)

$$
[\hat{\mathbf{e}}_r,\hat{\mathbf{e}}_\theta]f = \frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\right) - \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\frac{\partial f}{\partial r} = -\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta} = -\frac{\hat{\mathbf{e}}_\theta}{r}f
$$

Non-zero, so the normalized basis is anholonomic; the sign says the loop gap points in the minus theta direction.

### (3.17) General vector transformation rule · central · GA §3.2 p.39 (pdf 56)

$$
X^{\mu'} = \frac{\partial x^{\mu'}}{\partial x^\nu}X^\nu
$$

The test any vector must pass under a general coordinate change.

### (3.18) Coordinates related by coefficient matrix · supporting · GA §3.2 p.39 (pdf 56)

$$
x^{\mu'} = A^{\mu'}{}_\nu x^\nu
$$

If A depends on position, partial x^{mu'}/partial x^nu is not A and x^mu fails 3.17; only constant A (linear homogeneous maps) lets coordinates act as vector components.

**Symbols:** A coefficient matrix, possibly position dependent

### (3.19) Minkowski line element · central · GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = \eta_{\mu\nu}\mathrm{d}x^\mu\mathrm{d}x^\nu = -\mathrm{d}t^2 + \mathrm{d}x^2 + \mathrm{d}y^2 + \mathrm{d}z^2
$$

A non-Euclidean but flat metric with signature (-,+,+,+).

**Symbols:** eta Minkowski metric; units with c = 1

### (3.20) Circumference of a circle on a sphere · central · GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57)

$$
C = 2\pi r\,\mathrm{sinc}\frac{r}{R},\qquad \mathrm{sinc}\,x = \frac{\sin x}{x}
$$

Circles on a sphere are shorter than 2 pi r; the Euclidean value returns when r is much smaller than R.

**Symbols:** r radius measured along the surface; R sphere radius

### (3.21) Girard's theorem · central · GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57)

$$
\sum_i \alpha_i = \pi + \frac{A}{R^2}
$$

Angle sum of a spherical triangle exceeds pi by its area in units of R^2.

**Symbols:** alpha_i interior angles; A triangle area; R sphere radius. The running text prints the sum as over alpha_1, a typo for alpha_i.

### (3.22) Cartesian x basis vector in the polar coordinate basis · supporting · GA p.41 (pdf 58)

$$
\mathbf{e}_x = \frac{x}{r}\mathbf{e}_r - \frac{y}{r^2}\mathbf{e}_\theta
$$

Inverse of the Example 3.2 transformation (Exercise 3.1).

### (3.23) Cartesian y basis vector in the polar coordinate basis · supporting · GA p.41 (pdf 58)

$$
\mathbf{e}_y = \frac{y}{r}\mathbf{e}_r + \frac{x}{r^2}\mathbf{e}_\theta
$$

Inverse of the Example 3.2 transformation (Exercise 3.1).

### (3.24) Derivatives of polar basis vectors · supporting · GA p.41 (pdf 58)

$$
\frac{\partial\mathbf{e}_r}{\partial\theta} = \frac{\mathbf{e}_\theta}{r},\quad \frac{\partial\mathbf{e}_\theta}{\partial\theta} = -r\,\mathbf{e}_r,\quad \frac{\partial\mathbf{e}_r}{\partial r} = 0,\quad \frac{\partial\mathbf{e}_\theta}{\partial r} = \frac{\mathbf{e}_\theta}{r}
$$

Basis vectors change across the plane; the coefficients are the flat-space Christoffel symbols in polar coordinates (Exercise 3.2).

### Circle law in terms of curvature · supporting · GA p.41 (pdf 58)

$$
K = \frac{1}{R^2},\qquad C = 2\pi r\,\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(r\sqrt{K}\right)
$$

Rewrites 3.20 so that only the intrinsic quantity K appears.

**Symbols:** K Gaussian curvature

### (3.25) Curvature from small circles · central · GA p.41 (pdf 58)

$$
K = \lim_{r\to 0}\frac{3}{\pi r^3}\left(2\pi r - C\right)
$$

Operational, intrinsic definition of curvature: compare measured circumferences with 2 pi r for ever smaller circles. Follows from sinc x = 1 - x^2/6 + ...

### Area bookkeeping in the proof of Girard's theorem (unit sphere) · derivation-step · GA p.41 (pdf 58)

$$
4\pi - 2A = 2\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(2\alpha_i - A\right)\ \Rightarrow\ \alpha_1+\alpha_2+\alpha_3 = \pi + A
$$

The sphere minus two antipodal triangles is tiled by six strips, each a lune of angle alpha_i minus the triangle; the book gives the pieces, the assembled line is ours.

### (3.26) Transformation to a uniformly rotating frame · supporting · GA p.42 (pdf 59)

$$
t = t',\quad x = x'\cos\Omega t' - y'\sin\Omega t',\quad y = x'\sin\Omega t' + y'\cos\Omega t',\quad z = z'
$$

Coordinate change for Exercise 3.5.

**Symbols:** Omega angular speed

### Minkowski metric in the rotating frame (our derivation, answer to Exercise 3.5) · supporting · GA p.42 (pdf 59)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = -\left[1-\Omega^2\left(x'^2+y'^2\right)\right]\mathrm{d}t'^2 + 2\Omega\left(x'\,\mathrm{d}y' - y'\,\mathrm{d}x'\right)\mathrm{d}t' + \mathrm{d}x'^2+\mathrm{d}y'^2+\mathrm{d}z'^2
$$

Still flat spacetime, but the metric is non-diagonal and position dependent: g_tt changes sign beyond the light cylinder rho = 1/Omega and the cross terms encode Coriolis effects and the Sagnac effect. Not printed in the chapter; derived by us and consistent with the answer in Appendix E (eqn E.15).

**Symbols:** c = 1; rho^2 = x'^2 + y'^2

## Worked examples

### Example 3.1 · intro · GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54)

**Problem:** Transform the components of an infinitesimal displacement from Cartesian (dx, dy) to plane polar (dr, dtheta) using the component transformation law.

**Method:** Compute the four partial derivatives of x and y with respect to r and theta and the four of r and theta with respect to x and y; arrange the latter as the Jacobian matrix acting on (dx, dy) and rewrite its entries using r and theta.

**Key insight:** The transformation matrix of Chapter 2 generalizes to a Jacobian whose entries depend on the point, which is the new feature of non-linear coordinate changes.

**Result:** dr = cos(theta) dx + sin(theta) dy; dtheta = -(sin(theta)/r) dx + (cos(theta)/r) dy.

**Concepts:** Jacobian matrix of a coordinate transformation, transformation law for vector components, infinitesimal displacement vector, plane polar coordinates

### Example 3.2 · intro · GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

**Problem:** Find the polar coordinate basis vectors e_r and e_theta in terms of e_x and e_y.

**Method:** Insert the derivatives of x and y with respect to r and theta into the basis-vector law e_{mu'} = (partial x^nu / partial x^{mu'}) e_nu; also write the result as the Cartesian basis row times the Jacobian matrix.

**Key insight:** Mechanically applying the law yields a basis in which e_theta carries a factor r, unlike the familiar unit vectors, setting up the coordinate versus non-coordinate distinction.

**Result:** e_r = cos(theta) e_x + sin(theta) e_y; e_theta = -r sin(theta) e_x + r cos(theta) e_y.

**Concepts:** transformation law for basis vectors, coordinate basis, plane polar coordinates

### Example · intro · GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

**Problem:** Check whether the basis found in Example 3.2 is normalized and compare with the textbook unit vectors (worked in the prose after Example 3.2).

**Method:** Take dot products using the Euclidean metric in the Cartesian basis; compare with e_hat_r and e_hat_theta; interpret via the four-step loop picture of Fig. 3.3.

**Key insight:** The r-dependent length of e_theta is exactly what makes the outer arc of a coordinate loop longer than the inner arc so that the loop closes.

**Result:** e_r . e_r = 1 and e_theta . e_theta = r^2; the normalized pair is e_hat_theta = e_theta / r.

**Concepts:** coordinate basis, orthonormal polar basis, loop-closure test for a coordinate basis, metric components as basis-vector dot products

### Example 3.3 · standard · GA §3.1 p.39 (pdf 56)

**Problem:** Show that basis vectors can be represented by partial derivatives and use this to test whether the coordinate and normalized polar bases commute.

**Method:** Differentiate a scalar function along a parametrized path by the chain rule, strip off the function to obtain an operator with the form components times basis, identify e_mu with partial_mu, then apply the commutator of e_hat_r = partial_r and e_hat_theta = (1/r) partial_theta to an arbitrary function.

**Key insight:** Once vectors are derivative operators, the Lie bracket is a routine calculation: partial derivatives commute, but a position-dependent prefactor produces an extra term from the product rule.

**Result:** [e_r, e_theta] = 0; [e_hat_r, e_hat_theta] = -(1/r^2) partial_theta = -e_hat_theta / r, which is non-zero.

**Concepts:** directional derivative along a curve, tangent vectors as derivative operators, Lie bracket, coordinate basis, non-coordinate basis

### Example · intro · GA §3.2 p.39 (pdf 56)

**Problem:** Decide whether the coordinates themselves transform like vector components (argument of Section 3.2 and footnote 4).

**Method:** Write the coordinate relation with a coefficient matrix and compare the coefficients with the Jacobian; note that they agree only when the coefficients are constant. Footnote 4 points to Example 3.1: the displacement Jacobian does not turn (x, y) into (r, theta).

**Key insight:** The Jacobian is a local, point-by-point object that maps infinitesimal differences; applying it to finite coordinate values is meaningless unless the map is linear and homogeneous.

**Result:** Position vector excluded from the list of genuine vectors; coordinates kept as labels. (Our check: the matrix of eqn 3.6 applied to (x, y) gives (r, 0), not (r, theta).)

**Concepts:** position vector, coordinates are not vectors, linear homogeneous transformation

### Example 3.4 · intro · GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57)

**Problem:** Show by example that Euclid's circle and triangle results fail on the surface of a sphere of radius R.

**Method:** Quote the circumference of a circle of surface radius r and Girard's angle-sum formula, then examine the limits of small r and small area; proofs are left to Exercises 3.3 and 3.4.

**Key insight:** Deviations from Euclidean results are controlled by size relative to the curvature radius, so a small enough patch always looks Euclidean.

**Result:** C = 2 pi r sinc(r/R), tending to 2 pi r for r much less than R; angle sum = pi + A/R^2, tending to pi for A much less than R^2.

**Concepts:** circumference of a circle on a sphere, Girard's theorem, non-Euclidean space, spherical triangle


## Analogies and intuitions

### Puck's promise to put a girdle round the Earth: coordinates as a net thrown over a world so that positions can be named. → coordinates as labels · weak · GA p.36 (pdf 53)

Frames the chapter's reluctant return from coordinate-free geometry to explicit coordinate grids needed for concrete problems.

**Where it breaks down:** Purely literary; says nothing about how coordinates transform or why some grids are better than others.

### Newton's law viewed from a merry-go-round: the same motion acquires centrifugal and Coriolis terms. → coordinate-free formulation of physical laws · useful · GA p.36 (pdf 53)

Shows that complexity in component equations can be a property of the chosen frame, motivating geometric statements.

**Where it breaks down:** Rotating frames involve time-dependent coordinate changes; the chapter's later examples are static spatial coordinate changes, so the link is qualitative until Exercise 3.5.

**App idea:** Split-screen: a puck sliding freely on a rotating table seen from above in the inertial frame (straight line) and in the co-rotating frame (curving path with drawn Coriolis and centrifugal arrows).

### Walking a square: step out, step round, step back, step round again, and see whether you are home. → coordinate basis · strong · GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

Gives a tangible criterion for holonomic bases: the loop closes only if the sideways steps scale with radius.

**Where it breaks down:** With finite unit steps the picture is only qualitative; the precise statement concerns vanishing Lie brackets, with the gap scaling as step size squared. It must not be confused with the holonomy of parallel transport, which detects curvature.

**App idea:** The loop-walker demo described for Fig. 3.3.

### The chain rule looks like a vector: components dx^mu/dlambda times basis objects partial/partial x^mu. → tangent vectors as derivative operators · strong · GA §3.1 p.39 (pdf 56)

A formal resemblance is promoted to a definition, making basis vectors into operators whose commutators can be computed.

**Where it breaks down:** Presented as a trick; why a derivative operator deserves to be called a vector (linearity, Leibniz rule, tangent spaces) is deferred to Chapter 31.

**App idea:** A curve on a colour-mapped scalar field: the learner slides along the curve and sees df/dlambda assembled as the tangent arrow's components times the gradient components.

### An arrow from New York to Tokyo would burrow through the Earth, while a route of tiny steps can stay on the surface. → tangent vector · strong · GA §3.2 p.40 (pdf 57)

Explains why finite displacement vectors are not objects of a curved space whereas infinitesimal ones are.

**Where it breaks down:** Relies on the Earth being embedded in 3D space; spacetime has no such embedding, so the arrow does not exist at all rather than going somewhere else.

**App idea:** Globe view with two pinned cities: toggle between the straight chord (dips inside, depth readout) and a great-circle path built from many short tangent arrows that each lie in the local tangent plane.

### Growth of e_theta is not a bug but a feature. → coordinate basis · useful · GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

Recasts the unsettling unnormalized basis vector as the property that ties basis vectors to coordinate steps.

**Where it breaks down:** Rhetorical rather than explanatory; the explanation comes from the loop picture and the operator identification.

### A circle drawn on the Death Star: geometry on a big ball in space. → circumference of a circle on a sphere · useful · GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57)

A playful picture of a circle whose radius is measured along a spherical surface.

**Where it breaks down:** The drawn radius arrow does not make clear that r is measured along the surface rather than as a straight chord.

**App idea:** The circle-on-a-sphere lab described for Fig. 3.4, with a toggle showing the surface radius versus the straight-line chord.

### Curvature measured by a surveyor who compares circle circumferences with 2 pi r for smaller and smaller circles. → Gaussian curvature · strong · GA p.41 (pdf 58)

Exercise 3.3 turns the circumference deficit into a limit that defines curvature from inside the surface.

**Where it breaks down:** Works cleanly for 2D surfaces; in higher dimensions curvature has many components and needs the Riemann tensor.

**App idea:** Flatland surveyor game: the learner measures circles drawn with a string on hidden surfaces (plane, sphere, saddle) and must infer the sign and size of curvature from the data.


## Misconceptions addressed

### The coordinates of a point form the components of a vector (the position vector) in any coordinate system. · GA §3.2 p.39 (pdf 56)

**Correction:** Coordinates transform by the full nonlinear coordinate map, not by the Jacobian, unless the map is linear and homogeneous; they are labels. Only infinitesimal displacements dx^mu transform as vector components.

**Why tempting:** In introductory mechanics and in special relativity with Lorentz transformations the position vector works perfectly.

### Good basis vectors must always be of unit length, so an e_theta of length r is a mistake. · GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

**Correction:** Coordinate basis vectors are sized to match unit steps in the coordinate; e_theta must have length r. Unit vectors are allowed but form a non-coordinate basis with different properties.

**Why tempting:** Elementary physics uses unit vectors r-hat and theta-hat exclusively.

### Extra terms that appear when a law is written in rotating coordinates are new physical forces. · GA p.36 (pdf 53)

**Correction:** The centrifugal and Coriolis terms reflect the choice of frame; the underlying vector law is unchanged.

**Why tempting:** People in rotating frames genuinely feel them and they are named 'forces'.

### Non-Euclidean means curved. · GA p.41 (pdf 58)

**Correction:** In the book's usage a non-Euclidean metric can be flat: Minkowski spacetime has metric eta, not delta, yet no curvature.

**Why tempting:** Popular accounts of non-Euclidean geometry are all about curved surfaces, and the chapter's second example (the sphere) is curved.

### Distance in polar coordinates obeys Pythagoras directly, ds^2 = dr^2 + dtheta^2. · GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54)

**Correction:** The angular step has physical length r dtheta, so ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2 dtheta^2.

**Why tempting:** Treating any pair of coordinates as if they were Cartesian.

### Any two points in a curved space are joined by a displacement vector belonging to that space. · GA §3.2 p.40 (pdf 57)

**Correction:** A finite straight arrow generally leaves the space (the chord from New York to Tokyo); vectors live at points and describe infinitesimal displacements or tangents.

**Why tempting:** In flat space a single arrow can join any two points and be slid around freely.

### Without a position vector there can be no velocity vector. · GA §3.2 p.40 (pdf 57)

**Correction:** Velocity is the tangent to the world line, built from infinitesimal displacements; it is reconstructed in Chapter 7.

**Why tempting:** Velocity was taught as the derivative of position.

### The Jacobian matrix can be applied to the coordinate values themselves to get the new coordinates. · GA §3.2 p.39 (pdf 56)

**Correction:** The Jacobian maps differentials and vector components at a point; applied to (x, y) it gives (r, 0), not (r, theta).

**Why tempting:** For Lorentz transformations the same matrix maps both coordinates and components.

### A non-zero Lie bracket between basis vectors means the space is curved. · GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

**Correction:** The Euclidean plane is flat, yet the normalized polar basis has a non-zero bracket. Brackets diagnose whether a basis comes from coordinates; curvature is diagnosed by the Riemann tensor or by circle and triangle tests.

**Why tempting:** Both involve going round a loop and failing to return to the same state, and the chapter places the loop idea just before discussing non-Euclidean space.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The expression e_r e_theta - e_theta e_r multiplies two arrows together. · GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

**Correction:** The bracket only makes sense with vectors acting as derivative operators on functions: apply one, then the other, and subtract.

**Why tempting:** The book writes the bracket as a product before introducing the operator interpretation.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### A metric that looks complicated in some coordinates (position-dependent, with cross terms) must describe a curved spacetime. · GA p.42 (pdf 59)

**Correction:** Exercise 3.5 produces such a metric just by rotating the frame of flat Minkowski spacetime; flatness is a coordinate-independent property.

**Why tempting:** Curved-spacetime metrics like Schwarzschild's also have position-dependent components.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Newton's law on a rotating platform · GA p.36 (pdf 53)

**Setup:** Describe the same freely moving particle in an inertial frame and in a frame rotating relative to it.

**Lesson:** The vector equation is identical, but its components in the rotating frame pick up pseudo-force terms, so statements free of coordinates are cleaner.

**App idea:** Rotating-table puck demo with a frame toggle and the component equations displayed for each frame.

### The four-step loop on a polar grid · GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

**Setup:** Starting at a point, step along the first basis vector, then the second, then reverse the first, then reverse the second, once with coordinate polar basis vectors and once with unit ones.

**Lesson:** Coordinate basis steps close the loop; normalized steps leave a gap, which is the geometric content of a non-zero Lie bracket.

**App idea:** Loop walker with step-size slider and gap-versus-step log plot.

### Arrow from New York to Tokyo · GA §3.2 p.40 (pdf 57)

**Setup:** Imagine a two-dimensional world that is only the Earth's surface and try to draw the displacement vector between two distant cities.

**Lesson:** The straight arrow leaves the world; only chains of infinitesimal displacements lying in the surface are meaningful, so vectors must be local.

**App idea:** Globe with chord versus great-circle route, showing chord depth and local tangent planes along the route.

### Surveying circles on a sphere · GA p.41 (pdf 58)

**Setup:** An inhabitant of a sphere draws circles of known surface radius and measures their circumferences, shrinking the circles toward zero.

**Lesson:** The shortfall from 2 pi r, divided by r^3 and suitably scaled, reveals the curvature 1/R^2 without leaving the surface.

**App idea:** Surveyor game on hidden surfaces with a data table and a curvature fit.

### Adding up triangle angles on a globe · GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57)

**Setup:** Draw a triangle from three great-circle arcs on a sphere and add its interior angles; compare triangles of different sizes.

**Lesson:** The excess over pi grows with area, vanishing for small triangles; the octant triangle has three right angles.

**App idea:** Spherical triangle builder with live angle sum and area.


## Applications and observations

- **How far a straight New York to Tokyo arrow would dip below the surface** (numerical-estimate, GA §3.2 p.40 (pdf 57)): Our estimate for the book's example: with Earth radius 6371 km and a great-circle distance of about 10,850 km, the central angle is about 1.70 rad (98 degrees). The chord is 2R sin(angle/2) and its deepest point lies R(1 - cos(angle/2)) below the surface. Key numbers: Chord about 9,590 km versus surface route about 10,850 km; maximum depth about 2,170 km (our estimate).
- **Circumference deficit of a large circle on the Earth** (numerical-estimate, GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57)): Our estimate using eqn 3.20: the fractional shortfall of C below 2 pi r is about r^2/(6R^2) for r much less than R. For a circle of surface radius 1000 km on the Earth it is roughly 0.4 percent; for a 1 km circle it is about 4 parts in 10^9. Key numbers: r = 1000 km: deficit about 0.41 percent (about 26 km of a 6,280 km circumference); r = 1 km: about 4e-9 (our estimates).
- **Angle excess of the octant triangle** (numerical-estimate, GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57)): Our illustration of eqn 3.21: the triangle with vertices at the North Pole and two equator points 90 degrees apart covers one eighth of the sphere, so A/R^2 = pi/2 and its angle sum is 3 pi/2, three right angles. Key numbers: Angle sum 270 degrees; excess 90 degrees = (4 pi R^2 / 8)/R^2 (our example).
- **Light cylinder of the rotating-frame metric** (numerical-estimate, GA p.42 (pdf 59)): Our extension of Exercise 3.5: in the rotating coordinates g_tt = -(1 - Omega^2 rho^2) with c = 1, which vanishes at rho = c/Omega, beyond which a point at rest in the rotating frame would have to move faster than light. For a frame rotating with the Earth this radius is far outside the Solar System's inner planets; for a millisecond pulsar it is only tens of kilometres. Key numbers: Earth: Omega = 7.29e-5 rad/s gives c/Omega about 4.1e12 m (about 27 AU); pulsar with 1.6 ms period: c/Omega about 76 km (our estimates).

## Historical notes

- **Euclid (c. 300 BC):** Axiomatic plane geometry in the Elements, including the triangle angle sum of 180 degrees. — Defines the flat benchmark whose failure on curved surfaces the chapter uses to introduce non-Euclidean geometry. (GA §3.1 p.36 (pdf 53))
- **Rene Descartes (1637):** Introduced the idea of locating points in the plane by a pair of numbers, the Cartesian coordinates. — The standard coordinate system on Euclidean space and the starting point for all transformations in the chapter. (GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54))
- **Thomas Harriot, Albert Girard, Galileo Galilei, Willebrord Snell, Ibn Sahl:** A footnote records that Harriot found the spherical-excess theorem later credited to Girard, drew the Moon through a telescope some months before Galileo, and found the law of refraction about twenty years before Snell, who was in turn preceded by Ibn Sahl centuries earlier. — A reminder that naming conventions misattribute discoveries. (Our addition, not in the chapter: Girard published the theorem in 1629, and Harriot's lunar drawing dates from 1609.) (GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57))

## Notation and conventions

- **Metric signature and units:** Minkowski line element written ds^2 = -dt^2 + dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2, signature (-,+,+,+), with c = 1. — Matches the rest of the book (see Appendix B). (GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57))
- **Metric symbols:** delta_mu_nu for the Euclidean metric components (identity matrix), eta_mu_nu for the Minkowski metric. (GA §3.1 p.36 (pdf 53))
- **Primed indices:** A prime on the index, x^{mu'} or x^{alpha'}, marks components in the new coordinate system; the letter itself (mu or alpha) is not significant. — Example 3.1 uses alpha' for (r, theta) while the text uses mu'; footnote 4 swaps the roles to x^alpha = (x, y). (GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54))
- **Transformation matrix index placement:** Lambda^{mu'}_nu = partial x^{mu'} / partial x^nu transforms components; Lambda^nu_{mu'} = partial x^nu / partial x^{mu'} transforms basis vectors. The two are inverse matrices. — The upper-left/lower-right ordering encodes which index is new; the book does not spell out the inverse relation here. (GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54))
- **Vectors and basis vectors:** Bold letters for vectors, X = X^mu e_mu with upper-index components and lower-index basis vectors, summed by the Einstein convention. (GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54))
- **Hats on basis vectors:** e_hat_r, e_hat_theta denote unit-normalized basis vectors; unhatted e_r, e_theta denote coordinate basis vectors. — Chapter 10 uses hats for orthonormal frame components more generally. (GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55))
- **Partial derivatives with fixed variables:** (partial x / partial r)_theta indicates the variable held constant, in thermodynamics style. (GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54))
- **Basis vectors as derivatives:** e_mu is identified with partial/partial x^mu (often written partial_mu). — Justified in Chapter 31. (GA §3.1 p.39 (pdf 56))
- **Lie bracket:** [X, Y] = XY - YX, understood as acting on functions. (GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55))
- **sinc function:** sinc x = (sin x)/x (unnormalized, no factor of pi). — Some fields define sinc x = sin(pi x)/(pi x). (GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57))
- **Dimension count:** (3+1)-dimensional means three space coordinates and one time coordinate. (GA §3.3 p.40 (pdf 57))

## Teaching gems

### Derive the polar basis by blindly applying the transformation law, let the learner be surprised that e_theta has length r, then explain the surprise instead of normalizing it away. · GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

**Why it works:** A productive surprise anchors the idea that basis vectors are tied to coordinate steps, and it prevents the learner from silently importing unit-vector habits.

**App idea:** Prediction prompt before the reveal: learner guesses the length of e_theta at r = 2, then a basis-field view shows the true arrows growing across the plane.

### Test a basis by walking a four-step loop and seeing whether you return home. · GA §3.1 p.38 (pdf 55)

**Why it works:** Converts the abstract commutation property into a visible, checkable outcome; the coordinate and normalized cases differ in an obvious way on the same grid.

**App idea:** Loop walker with epsilon slider, trail, gap vector, and a log-log gap-versus-epsilon plot matching epsilon^2 / r.

### Discover basis vectors as derivative operators by writing the chain rule along a path and noticing it has the shape components times basis. · GA §3.1 p.39 (pdf 56)

**Why it works:** Uses a formula every calculus student trusts to motivate a definition that otherwise feels arbitrary, and immediately pays off by making the bracket computable.

**App idea:** Symbolic playground: learner picks two operator fields (e.g. partial_r and (1/r) partial_theta), applies them to a chosen function in both orders, and sees the product-rule term that survives.

### Retire the position vector with a geography example: the New York to Tokyo arrow would tunnel through the Earth. · GA §3.2 p.40 (pdf 57)

**Why it works:** Physical and memorable; it moves the learner from 'vectors are arrows between points' to 'vectors live at a point'.

**App idea:** Globe with chord versus surface route and a depth gauge, then zoom in to show short steps lying in tangent planes.

### Keep one example (plane polar coordinates in a flat plane) for every new concept in the section. · GA §3.1 p.37 (pdf 54)

**Why it works:** Separates what is new (bases, brackets, operators) from what is familiar (the flat plane), so curvature is not confused with coordinate effects.

**App idea:** A persistent polar-plane canvas reused across lesson steps, with layers added for grid, basis fields, loops, and operator actions.

## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Section 3.2 says the coefficients relating coordinates depend on position 'in curved spacetime', and footnote 5 says the condition holds in flat spacetime, yet the chapter's own polar-coordinate example is flat and already breaks the rule.** — Learners may conclude that coordinates are vectors whenever space is flat, or that the position vector problem is caused by curvature rather than by nonlinear coordinate changes. *Suggestion:* State clearly that the position vector fails for any nonlinear (curvilinear or non-inertial) coordinates, even in flat space, and that in curved space there are no global linear coordinates at all.
- **Writing a general nonlinear coordinate change as x' = A(x) x is not a natural or unique representation.** — The argument can seem to hinge on an arbitrary factorization. *Suggestion:* Phrase the test directly: x^{mu'} is a vector only if x^{mu'}(x) equals (partial x^{mu'}/partial x^nu) x^nu, which holds for linear homogeneous maps; show the polar counterexample explicitly (the Jacobian gives (r, 0)).
- **The term non-Euclidean is used for any metric other than delta, grouping flat Minkowski spacetime with the curved sphere, while in mathematics non-Euclidean geometry means curved geometry.** — Confusion between signature (how the metric counts time) and curvature (whether the space is flat). *Suggestion:* Present two independent axes, signature (Euclidean or Lorentzian) and curvature (flat or curved), and place the plane, Minkowski spacetime, the sphere and Schwarzschild spacetime on the grid.
- **The Lie bracket is written as a product e_r e_theta - e_theta e_r and described loosely ('basis vectors are independent and don't depend on each other') before the operator interpretation is available.** — The bracket may seem like multiplication of arrows, and the vague independence language does not explain why normalized vectors fail. *Suggestion:* Introduce vectors as derivative operators first, define the bracket on a test function, and then show the loop picture as its geometric meaning.
- **Fig. 3.3 uses finite unit steps (a whole radian of theta in the coordinate case), whereas the bracket statement is about infinitesimal loops where the gap is second order in step size.** — Learners cannot see how a bracket value such as -e_hat_theta / r relates to the size of the gap, or why finite coordinate loops close exactly. *Suggestion:* Show loops for shrinking step size and the scaling gap = epsilon^2 [A, B] + ...; note that coordinate loops close exactly for any step size because they are just coordinate increments.
- **The loop-closure test for coordinate bases sits right next to the idea of returning 'in the same geometric state', which later (Chapter 11) describes parallel transport holonomy and curvature.** — Easy to confuse anholonomic bases (a property of a choice of basis in flat or curved space) with curvature (a property of the space). *Suggestion:* Explicitly contrast: normalized polar basis in the flat plane has a non-zero bracket but zero curvature; parallel transport round a loop on the sphere changes a vector because of curvature.
- **The flat metric in polar coordinates, ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2 dtheta^2, is never written even though eqn 3.11 and footnote 2 imply it.** — The connection between basis-vector lengths and the metric components is missed until Chapter 5. *Suggestion:* Write the polar line element next to eqn 3.11 and note g_theta_theta = e_theta . e_theta = r^2.
- **The inverse relationship between the component Jacobian and the basis Jacobian is asserted ('transform the opposite way') but not demonstrated.** — Index placement on Lambda^{mu'}_nu versus Lambda^nu_{mu'} becomes a memorization task. *Suggestion:* Multiply the matrix of eqn 3.6 by the matrix built from eqn 3.4 to get the identity, and show that X^mu e_mu is unchanged.
- **Minor printed slips: Example 3.1 refers to cylindrical polar coordinates for a plane problem, the text before eqn 3.21 prints the angle sum over alpha_1 instead of alpha_i, and the appE answer to Exercise 3.3 is garbled (sin(r/sqrt K) and a stray equals sign).** — Small but distracting for careful readers. *Suggestion:* Read as plane polar coordinates, as a sum over alpha_i, and as C = 2 pi r (1 - K r^2/6 + ...) for r much less than 1/sqrt(K).
- **In eqn 3.20 and Fig. 3.4 it is not made explicit that r is measured along the surface (a geodesic radius), not as the straight-line radius of the circle in 3D, which would be R sin(r/R).** — Learners attempting Exercise 3.3 may use the wrong radius and fail to reproduce the formula. *Suggestion:* Draw both radii on the sphere: surface radius r = R phi and planar radius R sin phi, so C = 2 pi R sin(r/R).
- **The summary claims Minkowski spacetime is flat, but the chapter provides no criterion for flatness; Exercise 3.5 then gives a complicated metric for the same flat spacetime without comment.** — Learners may be unsure how to tell a genuinely curved metric from a flat one in odd coordinates. *Suggestion:* Preview that curvature is judged by the Riemann tensor (Chapter 11) and that coordinate changes alone never create it; provide the rotating-frame answer for self-checking.

## Tutor notes

- Open with a question: 'If a law of physics is true, should it care which coordinates you use?' Then ask what happens to F = ma on a merry-go-round, to surface the idea of coordinate artefacts.
- Order of explanation that works: (1) the flat plane in two labellings; (2) displacement components transform by the Jacobian; (3) basis vectors transform by the inverse; (4) surprise that e_theta has length r; (5) loop walk; (6) vectors as derivatives and the bracket; (7) why coordinates are not vectors; (8) signature versus curvature; (9) circles and triangles on a sphere.
- Before revealing eqn 3.8, ask the learner to predict the length of e_theta at radius r. Most will say 1; use the wrong prediction as the teaching moment.
- Check for understanding: 'Is the normalized polar basis a coordinate basis? How would you tell without computing a bracket?' Expected answer: walk a small loop and see if it closes; or ask whether (1/r) partial_theta can be a partial derivative with respect to some coordinate.
- Quick computation check: have the learner apply the matrix of eqn 3.6 to (x, y). They get (r, 0). Ask why theta did not appear; the answer is that the Jacobian acts on differentials, not on coordinate values, which is the whole point of Section 3.2.
- Keep two distinctions separate and name them explicitly: coordinate versus non-coordinate basis (a choice) and flat versus curved space (a fact about the space). The normalized polar basis in a flat plane is the key counterexample.
- Similarly separate signature from curvature: Euclidean plane (flat, positive-definite), Minkowski (flat, Lorentzian), sphere (curved, positive-definite), Schwarzschild (curved, Lorentzian).
- For the bracket, work one line on a test function with the learner: partial_r((1/r) partial_theta f) - (1/r) partial_theta partial_r f. Emphasise that the only surviving term comes from differentiating 1/r.
- Common learner question: 'If there is no position vector, what is x^mu?' Answer: a list of labels for an event, like a street address; differences of nearby labels form a vector.
- Common learner question: 'Why does the sign of [e_hat_r, e_hat_theta] matter?' Show that the gap in Fig. 3.3(b) points clockwise, the minus theta direction, matching -e_hat_theta / r.
- For the sphere results, check limits first: ask what C and the angle sum become for tiny circles and triangles, then ask for the octant triangle (three right angles) as a concrete surprise.
- When doing Exercise 3.5, let the learner notice that the rotating-frame metric has cross terms and a g_tt that changes sign at rho = 1/Omega, then ask whether spacetime has become curved (it has not). This motivates the need for the Riemann tensor.
- Learner level adaptation: for novices stay with pictures (grids, loops, globes) and the two headline sphere formulas; for advanced learners connect Exercise 3.2 to Christoffel symbols and the bracket to the Lie derivative and to non-coordinate frames used in Chapter 10.
