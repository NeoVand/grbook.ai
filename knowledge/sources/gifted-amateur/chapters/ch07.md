# GA ch07 · Parallel lines and the covariant derivative

> Builds a derivative of vectors that works in curved spacetime: parallelism is defined from the viewpoint of observers trapped in a surface, the naive derivative of v = v^mu e_mu is split into component change plus basis-vector change, the basis change is packaged into connection coefficients Gamma (shown to be non-tensorial and non-zero even in flat polar coordinates), the covariant derivative nabla_u v and its along-a-curve form Dv/dlambda are defined, parallel transport becomes nabla_u v = 0, and metric compatibility nabla g = 0 is argued from length preservation, pointing ahead to Gamma built from dg.

**Pages:** printed 81–90 · pdf 98–107 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

The computations are light (product rule, relabelling dummy indices, polar-coordinate derivatives), but the conceptual step from embedded surfaces to intrinsic comparison of vectors is subtle and the text blurs several points: eqn 7.26 drops u^alpha without comment, the sign logic of 'subtract the coordinate change' versus the added Gamma term is confusing, affine parameters are defined vaguely and then mischaracterized in a margin note, and compatibility is presented as fixing the connection without mentioning the torsion-free condition. Many results are quoted rather than derived (7.11, 7.34, 7.35, 7.37). Reading-copy LaTeX is clean apart from index placement in semicolon expressions.

## Role in the book

Second chapter of Part II. Chapter 6 argued that gravity is geometry and that spacetime is locally flat; before any equation of motion or curvature tensor can be written, the book needs a way to compare and differentiate vectors at different points. This chapter supplies that tool in a light, component-first way (connection coefficients, covariant derivative, parallel transport, affine parameters, four-velocity along curves, metric compatibility), so that Chapter 8 can write free fall as a geodesic, Chapter 9 can extract Gamma efficiently from the metric (eqn 7.37 is quoted in advance) and Chapter 11 can build a genuine curvature test from Gammas and their derivatives (as margin note 7 promises). Rigour is explicitly postponed: the transformation law of Gamma, covariant derivatives of general tensors and the Leibniz rule are promised for Part V (Chapter 34 revisits the covariant derivative in pictures), in keeping with the book's spiral design.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can explain why a vector drawn as a straight arrow between two points is useless on a curved surface and why tangent vectors to curves are used instead.
- Reader can state what it means for observers confined to a surface to call two vectors at different points parallel, and why such vectors look rotated to an observer using the embedding space.
- Reader can differentiate v = v^mu e_mu with the product rule and identify the extra term coming from position-dependent basis vectors.
- Reader can define connection coefficients through de_mu/dx^alpha = Gamma^lambda_{alpha mu} e_lambda and compute them by brute force for plane polar coordinates.
- Reader can argue from the polar-coordinate example that non-zero connection coefficients do not by themselves signal curvature.
- Reader can explain, using the inhomogeneous second-derivative term in the transformation law, why Gamma is not a tensor.
- Reader can write the component formula v^mu_{;alpha} = v^mu_{,alpha} + Gamma^mu_{alpha lambda} v^lambda and use comma/semicolon notation fluently.
- Reader can form the directional covariant derivative nabla_u v = u^alpha nabla_alpha v and state the parallel-transport condition nabla_u v = 0 in components.
- Reader can parametrize simple curves, recognise when a reparametrization is affine, and write the tangent vector u = (dx^mu/dlambda) e_mu.
- Reader can describe Dv/dlambda as 'transport the later vector back, subtract, divide, take the limit' and check that it reduces to dA^alpha/dlambda in flat Cartesian coordinates.
- Reader can state metric compatibility nabla g = 0 and show, via the product rule, that it is what keeps lengths of parallel-transported vectors fixed.
- Reader can write covariant derivatives of (0,2) and (2,0) tensors, with the sign pattern for lower and upper indices, and use them to check g_{mu nu;alpha} = 0.

## Teaching approach

A short, deliberately non-rigorous chapter that moves from a picture to a recipe. It opens with ants and humans confined to a curved surface to motivate an insider's notion of parallelism, then turns immediately to components: the product rule applied to v^mu e_mu exposes a basis-change term, which is named, computed by brute force for flat polar coordinates, and flagged as non-tensorial. Word equations in large brackets ('change in vector' minus 'change due to coordinates') carry the conceptual load before and after each formula. The authors then generalize from basis directions to arbitrary directions by analogy with the Euclidean directional derivative, recast everything along parametrized curves (introducing D/dlambda, the tangent vector and the four-velocity), give a limit-based picture of the derivative using parallel transport back to the starting point, and finish by asserting that the metric forces this connection through the compatibility condition, proved heuristically from length conservation. Almost all heavy lifting (transformation law of Gamma, tensor covariant derivatives, Leibniz rule, Gamma from the metric) is quoted and deferred to Chapter 9 or Part V.

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, components-first, conversational-informal, example-driven, computational-recipe, spiral-revisit, diagram-driven, analogy-driven

**Narrative arc**

1. Motivating need: physics is written as fields and their derivatives, and ordinary derivatives of vectors break in curved spacetime (chapter opening).
2. Model of a curved spacetime: a manifold covered by many small flat maps, like street maps patched over the globe, locally Minkowskian (Example 7.1).
3. Insider viewpoint: observers trapped in a surface cannot use chord-like arrows, so vectors are tangents to paths; parallelism is defined by carrying local axes along and keeping components unchanged (§7.1, Figs 7.1-7.3).
4. Conceptual split: the change of a vector along a direction equals its intrinsic change plus a contribution from the changing coordinate system; the covariant derivative keeps only the intrinsic part (eqn 7.1).
5. Warm-up from calculus: gradient and directional derivative in Euclidean space as the template for a derivative along a vector (Example 7.3).
6. Formalism: product rule on v^mu e_mu, definition of connection coefficients from derivatives of basis vectors (eqns 7.5-7.6).
7. Check by brute force: flat plane in polar coordinates has non-zero Gammas, so Gamma is not a curvature detector (Example 7.4); symmetry of Gamma noted (7.9).
8. Caveat: Gamma is not a tensor because of the second-derivative term in its transformation law (Example 7.5).
9. Assemble the covariant derivative nabla_alpha v and its semicolon components; flat Cartesian check gives ordinary derivatives (eqns 7.12-7.19, Example 7.6).
10. Generalize to arbitrary directions via the connection operator, analogous to u . grad f (eqns 7.20-7.23).
11. Parallel transport becomes the equation nabla_u v = 0, read as component change equals coordinate-induced change (eqns 7.24-7.27).
12. Move to curves: parametrization, affine reparametrizations, vector fields, D/dlambda along a curve, tangent vector and four-velocity with u.u = -1 (§7.4).
13. Picture of the definition: transport the vector at lambda_0 + epsilon back, subtract, divide and take the limit (Example 7.9, Fig. 7.6); flat-space check with the chain rule (Example 7.10).
14. Closing constraint: the metric fixes the connection through nabla g = 0, justified by requiring lengths to survive parallel transport (§7.5, Example 7.11).
15. Forward look: since g_{mu nu} = e_mu . e_nu and Gamma comes from derivatives of e_mu, Gamma should be built from first derivatives of g (boxed rule 7.38; formula 7.37 quoted for Chapter 9).

**Signature moves**

- Large-bracket word equations (eqns 7.1, 7.4, 7.27, 7.28, 7.30) state each derivative in plain language before or alongside its components.
- Frames curved space from the standpoint of confined observers (ants on a football, people on the Earth) who are forbidden to use the embedding space, and contrasts their judgment of parallelism with that of an outside observer (Figs 7.1, 7.2).
- Uses a flat space in curvilinear coordinates (plane polar) as the 'brute force and ignorance' test case, extracting a separation between coordinate effects and genuine curvature.
- Relegates notation bridges to margin notes (nabla_alpha = nabla_{e_alpha}; components of derivative versus derivative of components; comma/semicolon shaded box) so the main line stays short.
- Gives an operational four-step recipe for the covariant derivative (take later vector, transport back, subtract, divide and limit) paired with a labelled diagram (Fig. 7.6).
- Checks each new formula against flat spacetime in Cartesian coordinates, where it must collapse to ordinary partial or total derivatives (Examples 7.6, 7.10).
- Uses boxed-symbol arrow rules (dg -> Gamma) as a dependency map of which geometric objects are built from which; the text says this link will be expanded in coming chapters.
- Quotes results that will be derived later (transformation law of Gamma, tensor covariant derivatives, Gamma from metric) with explicit forward pointers rather than deriving them now.

## Section by section

### Chapter opening: why a new derivative, and manifolds as patched maps — p.81 (pdf 98)

States the goal: equations for fields in spacetime need derivatives of vectors and tensors, and curvature undermines the usual rules. Example 7.1 introduces a manifold via the analogy of covering the curved Earth with many small rectangular street maps that join smoothly; spacetime is likewise covered by locally flat four-coordinate charts, looks Minkowskian over small enough regions, and can be curved on larger scales. A margin note pictures a 2D manifold as a smooth, unpunctured rubber sheet.

**Concepts:** manifold, local flatness, differential geometry

**Key moves**
- Physics needs differential equations for fields, hence a derivative of vectors valid in curved spacetime
- Earth's surface: flat street maps work only locally but can be stitched smoothly over the globe
- Spacetime as a smooth manifold covered by local four-coordinate maps
- Smoothness implies Minkowski-like behaviour over sufficiently small regions; curvature shows up over larger distances

### §7.1 Parallelism — p.82 (pdf 99)

Observers are confined to a curved 2D surface and may not use the surrounding 3D space. A vector as a directed chord between points leaves the surface (New York to Paris tunnel), so vectors are instead tangents to paths. On a path that does not turn according to the insiders, such as a great circle, the tangent is judged the same everywhere; moving a vector so that it matches the local tangent is parallel transport. Generalizing, insiders carry their local axes along and call vectors parallel when they have equal components in these carried frames; from outside, those same vectors appear to rotate (Fig. 7.1; London and Tokyo north directions). Example 7.2 contrasts vectors that are parallel in R^3 but not tangent to a sphere (Fig. 7.2) with correctly transported vectors on the sphere (Fig. 7.3). It closes by setting a requirement for what follows: differentiating a field made of mutually parallel vectors has to give zero.

**Concepts:** intrinsic (insider) viewpoint on geometry, tangent vector to a curve, parallel transport, parallelism in curved space

**Key moves**
- Forbid use of the embedding space: all measurements are made within the surface
- Replace chord vectors by tangent vectors to paths
- Tangent of a non-turning path (great circle) is declared parallel to itself along the path
- Parallelism for general vectors: equal components in local frames that are themselves transported
- Outside view: insider-parallel vectors appear to change direction (Fig. 7.1)
- Embedding-parallel vectors fail to be tangent to the sphere, so they are not parallel for insiders (Example 7.2, Fig. 7.2)
- Requirement carried forward: the derivative of a parallel field must be zero

### §7.2 Derivatives and connections — p.83 (pdf 100)

Separates the change of a vector into its intrinsic change and the change caused by position-dependent coordinates, defining the covariant derivative in words as the former (eqn 7.1) and noting that it is directional. Example 7.3 recalls the Euclidean gradient and directional derivative u . grad f as the model. The product rule applied to v^mu e_mu gives a component term and a basis-vector term; the latter is expanded on the basis to define connection coefficients (Christoffel symbols) Gamma^lambda_{alpha mu}, which say how neighbouring local coordinate systems are connected. Example 7.4 reads off the polar-coordinate Gammas from known derivatives of e_r and e_theta, showing that flat space can have non-zero Gamma. The Gammas used in the book are symmetric in their lower indices (torsion free), and Example 7.5 quotes their transformation law, whose second-derivative term shows they are not tensor components.

**Concepts:** covariant derivative, directional derivative, gradient, position-dependent basis vectors, connection coefficients, Christoffel symbols of plane polar coordinates, torsion-free (symmetric) connection, non-tensorial transformation of connection coefficients, tensor transformation law

**Key moves**
- Word equation: covariant derivative = change in vector minus change due to coordinate system (7.1)
- Directional derivative in Euclidean space as template (7.2-7.4)
- Product rule on v^mu e_mu isolates the basis-vector term (7.5)
- Define Gamma by expanding de_mu/dx^alpha on the basis (7.6)
- Polar coordinates: read off Gamma^theta_{r theta} = Gamma^theta_{theta r} = 1/r and Gamma^r_{theta theta} = -r from derivatives of basis vectors (7.7-7.8)
- Non-zero Gamma in flat space means Gamma alone cannot diagnose curvature
- Symmetry Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} = Gamma^mu_{beta alpha} assumed throughout (7.9)
- Inhomogeneous term in the transformation law spoils tensor behaviour (7.10-7.11)

### §7.3 The covariant derivative — p.85 (pdf 102)

Substitutes the definition of Gamma into the product-rule expression and relabels dummy indices to obtain nabla_alpha v = (dv^mu/dx^alpha + Gamma^mu_{alpha lambda} v^lambda) e_mu, a vector for each alpha. Margin notes clarify that components of the derivative differ from derivatives of components, write nabla acting on components and on basis vectors separately, and introduce comma (partial) and semicolon (covariant) notation. In flat Cartesian coordinates all Gammas vanish and the covariant derivative reduces to partial derivatives (Example 7.6). A connection operator nabla is introduced so that e_alpha . nabla = nabla_alpha; acting on scalars it is the partial derivative, and replacing e_alpha by any u gives directional derivatives nabla_u f and nabla_u v = u^alpha nabla_alpha v. Parallel transport along u is the statement nabla_u v = 0, which in components says the change of components is entirely due to the Gamma term (Example 7.7).

**Concepts:** covariant derivative, comma and semicolon notation, connection operator, covariant derivative of a scalar, directional covariant derivative, parallel transport equation, components of a derivative versus derivatives of components

**Key moves**
- Insert (7.6) into (7.5) and relabel dummies to factor out e_mu (7.12-7.13)
- Rename the result nabla_alpha v and note its (1,0) character for fixed alpha (7.18)
- Distinguish (nabla_alpha v)^mu from dv^mu/dx^alpha; nabla_alpha e_mu = Gamma^lambda_{alpha mu} e_lambda (7.15-7.17)
- Flat Cartesian check: Gamma = 0 recovers partial derivatives (7.19)
- Define nabla via e_alpha . nabla = nabla_alpha, act on scalars, extend linearly to any direction u (7.20-7.23)
- Parallel transport: nabla_u v = 0 (7.24), components obey u^alpha(dv^mu/dx^alpha + Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} v^beta) = 0 (7.25)
- Interpretation: component change equals coordinate-induced change (7.26-7.27)

### §7.4 Parametrized paths — p.86 (pdf 103)

Recasts the covariant derivative for curves such as world lines. A curve is x(lambda) with lambda increasing monotonically between endpoints (Fig. 7.4); Example 7.8 parametrizes a line, a parabola and a circle. If lambda is an allowed parameter, any linear re-labelling lambda = alpha eta + beta is equally good; the book calls such parametrizations affine. For a vector field v(x), the rate of change along the curve is written Dv/dlambda and equals nabla_u v with the tangent u = (dx^mu/dlambda) e_mu (Fig. 7.5); choosing lambda = tau for massive particles makes u the velocity with u.u = -1. Example 7.9 gives the limit recipe: transport v from lambda_0 + epsilon back to lambda_0, subtract v(lambda_0), divide by epsilon (Fig. 7.6). Example 7.10 checks that in flat Cartesian coordinates the components of DA/dlambda are just dA^alpha/dlambda by the chain rule.

**Concepts:** parametrized curve, affine parameter, vector field, covariant derivative along a curve, tangent vector to a curve, four-velocity, limit definition of the covariant derivative via parallel transport

**Key moves**
- Curve as x(lambda) with a monotonic parameter marking progress (Fig. 7.4, Example 7.8)
- Affine reparametrization: linear relation between parameters
- Define Dv/dlambda for a vector field along a curve (7.28)
- Tangent vector u = (dx^mu/dlambda) e_mu supplies the direction (7.29), so Dv/dlambda = nabla_u v (7.30)
- Proper time as parameter gives four-velocity with u.u = -1
- Operational definition: parallel transport back, subtract, divide, take limit (7.31, Fig. 7.6)
- Flat-space check via chain rule: (DA/dlambda)^alpha = dA^alpha/dlambda (7.32)

### §7.5 Enter the metric — p.88 (pdf 105)

Asks whether the covariant derivative could have been built differently and answers that the metric removes the freedom: the connection must be compatible, nabla_alpha g = 0, i.e. g_{mu nu;alpha} = 0. Margin notes quote the covariant derivatives of (0,2) and (2,0) tensors and reinterpret affine parameters in terms of vector lengths under transport. Example 7.11 argues that if a parallel vector A keeps its length g_{mu nu}A^mu A^nu, the product rule forces g_{mu nu;alpha} = 0. Because g_{mu nu} = e_mu . e_nu and Gamma describes how e_mu change, Gamma should be a combination of first derivatives of the metric, summarized by the boxed rule dg -> Gamma, with the explicit formula (7.37) promised for Chapter 9.

**Concepts:** metric compatibility of the connection, preservation of vector length under parallel transport, covariant derivative of tensors, Leibniz rule for covariant derivatives, connection coefficients from the metric, metric tensor

**Key moves**
- The metric, not free choice, fixes the connection: impose nabla g = 0 (7.33)
- Quote tensor covariant derivatives: minus Gamma per lower index, plus Gamma per upper index (7.34-7.35)
- Without compatibility, lengths would drift under parallel transport, which is physically unacceptable
- Product rule on g(A,A) with A^mu_{;alpha} = 0 isolates g_{mu nu;alpha} (7.36)
- g_{mu nu} = e_mu . e_nu plus Gamma from de_mu/dx^alpha suggests Gamma is built from first derivatives of g (7.38, 7.37)

### Chapter summary — p.89 (pdf 106)

Three bullet points: parallel transport compares vectors at different points by keeping components fixed in local frames; the covariant derivative nabla_u v measures genuine change of vector fields and has the component form of eqn 7.39; the proper-time tangent to a massive particle's world line is a timelike velocity with u.u = -1.

**Concepts:** parallel transport, covariant derivative, four-velocity

**Key moves**
- Restates eqn 7.23 as 7.39
- Omits metric compatibility from the summary even though §7.5 introduced it

## Concepts

### manifold

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: smooth manifold, rubber-sheet space

A space that can be covered by overlapping local coordinate patches, each looking like ordinary R^n, which join smoothly where they overlap; spacetime is modelled as a 4-dimensional manifold.

**How introduced:** Through covering the curved Earth with many small rectangular street maps that blend into one another; a margin note adds the image of a smooth rubber surface such as a sphere or torus.

**Prerequisites:** coordinates

**Notes:** Full treatment promised in Part V and Appendix C. The rubber-sheet note speaks of not being punctured, which is loose: a plane with a point removed is still a manifold.

**Where:** GA p.81 (pdf 98)

### differential geometry

*definition · mention*

The mathematical study of smooth spaces (manifolds) using calculus: tangent vectors, connections, curvature and related structures.

**How introduced:** Named in passing in Example 7.1 as the subject of Part V.

**Prerequisites:** manifold

**Where:** GA p.81 (pdf 98)

### local flatness

*principle · mention* · also: locally Minkowskian spacetime

Over a sufficiently small region around any point, a smooth spacetime can be described by coordinates in which it looks like Minkowski spacetime; curvature only becomes apparent over larger separations.

**How introduced:** Asserted as a consequence of smoothness in Example 7.1, continuing the street-map analogy.

**Prerequisites:** manifold, Minkowski spacetime

**Notes:** Precisely, the metric can be made Minkowski with vanishing first derivatives at a point; second derivatives (curvature) cannot be removed. Developed in GA ch6 §6.4 and ch10.

**Where:** GA p.81 (pdf 98)

### intrinsic (insider) viewpoint on geometry

*principle · developed* · also: confined observers, ants on a football

Geometry of a space should be describable using only measurements made within the space itself, without reference to any higher-dimensional space in which it might be embedded; this is the only viewpoint available for spacetime.

**How introduced:** Ants confined to a football and humans confined to the Earth's surface are forbidden to float above it; this rules out chord vectors and motivates tangent vectors and insider parallelism.

**Prerequisites:** manifold

**Notes:** Part V (Chapter 30, Classical curvature) returns to curvature of surfaces judged from inside versus from an embedding; Appendix D treats embedding.

**Where:** GA §7.1 p.82 (pdf 99)

### tangent vector to a curve

*mathematical-object · core* · also: tangent vector, u = dx^mu/dlambda e_mu

For a curve x^mu(lambda), the vector u = (dx^mu/dlambda) e_mu at each point, which lies in the tangent space at that point and gives the direction and rate of progress along the curve.

**How introduced:** First qualitatively in §7.1 as the replacement for chord vectors on a curved surface; later in §7.4 as the direction needed to take a covariant derivative along a curve (Fig. 7.5).

**Prerequisites:** parametrized curve, coordinate basis vectors

$$
u = \frac{\mathrm{d}x^\mu(\lambda)}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} \boldsymbol{e}_\mu
$$

**Notes:** Margin note 14 stresses that it is not written dx/dlambda because the position vector x does not transform properly in general coordinates.

**Where:** GA §7.1 p.82 (pdf 99); GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104)

### parallelism in curved space

*definition · developed* · also: parallel vectors at different points

Two vectors at different points of a curved space are called parallel (relative to a chosen path) when one is obtained from the other by parallel transport; for insiders this means they have the same components in local frames that have themselves been carried along the path.

**How introduced:** Built from the tangent of a non-turning path (great circle) and generalized by transporting the insiders' local axes; contrasted with vectors that are parallel in the embedding space but not tangent to the sphere (Fig. 7.2).

**Prerequisites:** intrinsic (insider) viewpoint on geometry, tangent vector to a curve

**Notes:** The book does not emphasize here that the outcome depends on the path taken; that path dependence becomes the curvature test in Chapter 11.

**Where:** GA §7.1 p.82 (pdf 99)

### parallel transport

*operation · core* · also: parallel transported vector, covariantly constant along a curve

Moving a vector along a curve so that it undergoes no intrinsic change, i.e. its covariant derivative along the curve's tangent vanishes, nabla_u v = 0; all apparent change in its components is then due to the changing coordinate basis.

**How introduced:** Pictorially in §7.1 (great circles on a sphere, Figs 7.1 and 7.3), then as the equation nabla_u v = 0 in §7.3, then as the ingredient of the limit definition of D/dlambda in §7.4 (Fig. 7.6).

**Prerequisites:** parallelism in curved space, covariant derivative

$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}} \boldsymbol{v} = 0
$$

$$
u^\alpha \frac{\partial v^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha} + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} v^\beta u^\alpha = 0
$$

**Notes:** Along a curve the condition is best written dv^mu/dlambda + Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} u^alpha v^beta = 0; the book's eqn 7.26, which drops u^alpha, only holds for a field that is parallel in every direction.

**Where:** GA §7.1 p.82 (pdf 99); GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100); GA §7.3 p.86 (pdf 103); GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104)

### position-dependent basis vectors

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: changing coordinate basis, coordinate basis vectors

In curvilinear coordinates (and in any coordinates on a curved space) the coordinate basis vectors e_mu differ from point to point, so differentiating a vector v = v^mu e_mu requires differentiating the basis as well as the components.

**How introduced:** Through the product rule in eqn 7.5 and the explicit plane polar example, where e_r = cos theta e_x + sin theta e_y and e_theta = -r sin theta e_x + r cos theta e_y (margin note 6).

**Prerequisites:** plane polar coordinates, vector components

$$
\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{v}}{\partial x^\alpha} = \frac{\partial v^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}\boldsymbol{e}_\mu + v^\mu \frac{\partial \boldsymbol{e}_\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}
$$

**Where:** GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100); GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

### directional derivative

*operation · revisited* · also: u . grad f, derivative along a vector

The rate of change of a field along a given vector u; for a scalar f in Euclidean space it is u . grad f = u^i df/dx^i, roughly the value of f at the tip of u minus its value at the base for small u.

**How introduced:** Example 7.3 recalls it from vector calculus as the template that the covariant derivative generalizes; later nabla_u f = u^alpha df/dx^alpha (7.21).

**Prerequisites:** gradient

$$
\vec{u}\cdot\vec{\nabla} f = u^x \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + u^y \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} + u^z \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}
$$

$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}} f = u^\alpha \frac{\partial f}{\partial x^\alpha}
$$

**Notes:** The tip-minus-base reading (7.4) is only first-order accurate and needs division by the length of u to be a rate.

**Where:** GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100); GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102)

### gradient

*operation · mention* · also: grad f

For a scalar field in Euclidean 3-space, the vector of partial derivatives, grad f = (df/dx) e_x + (df/dy) e_y + (df/dz) e_z, which points normal to surfaces of constant f.

**How introduced:** Recalled in Example 7.3 as background for the directional derivative.

**Prerequisites:** partial derivatives

$$
\vec{\nabla} f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\vec{e}_x + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\vec{e}_y + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\vec{e}_z
$$

**Notes:** Chapter 31 revisits the gradient as a 1-form rather than a vector.

**Where:** GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100)

### covariant derivative

*operation · core* · also: nabla_alpha v, v^mu_{;alpha}, intrinsic derivative

A derivative of vector (and tensor) fields that measures only the genuine change of the field, removing the part caused by position-dependent basis vectors; in coordinates (nabla_alpha v)^mu = dv^mu/dx^alpha + Gamma^mu_{alpha lambda} v^lambda, and the result transforms as a tensor.

**How introduced:** Defined in words (eqn 7.1) as change of vector minus change due to coordinates, then derived by applying the product rule to v^mu e_mu and substituting the connection coefficients (7.12-7.18).

**Prerequisites:** position-dependent basis vectors, connection coefficients, directional derivative

$$
\nabla_\alpha \boldsymbol{v} = \left(\frac{\partial v^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha} + v^\lambda \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\lambda}\right)\boldsymbol{e}_\mu
$$

$$
v^\mu{}_{;\alpha} = v^\mu{}_{,\alpha} + v^\lambda\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\lambda}
$$

**Notes:** For fixed alpha, nabla_alpha v is a vector; with alpha left free, nabla v is a (1,1) tensor (used in Exercise 7.3d).

**Where:** GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100); GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102); GA §7.3 p.86 (pdf 103)

### connection coefficients

*mathematical-object · core* · also: Christoffel symbols, Gamma^lambda_{alpha mu}, connection

Functions Gamma^lambda_{alpha mu}(x) defined by expanding the derivative of a basis vector on the basis, de_mu/dx^alpha = Gamma^lambda_{alpha mu} e_lambda; they tell how local frames at neighbouring points are related and supply the correction term in covariant derivatives.

**How introduced:** Needed to express the awkward basis-vector term in the product rule (7.5); read as the rate at which e_mu turns toward e_lambda when moving along e_alpha (margin note 8); computed by brute force for polar coordinates.

**Prerequisites:** position-dependent basis vectors

$$
\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{e}_\mu}{\partial x^\alpha} = \Gamma^\lambda_{\alpha\mu}\boldsymbol{e}_\lambda
$$

**Notes:** The book prefers 'connection coefficients' to 'Christoffel symbols'. In a genuinely curved space with no embedding, eqn 7.6 is really a definition of how to compare nearby tangent spaces, not something derivable; this subtlety is left to Part V.

**Where:** GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100); GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101); GA §7.5 p.89 (pdf 106)

### Christoffel symbols of plane polar coordinates

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: polar-coordinate connection

For the flat plane in (r, theta) coordinates the only non-zero connection coefficients are Gamma^theta_{r theta} = Gamma^theta_{theta r} = 1/r and Gamma^r_{theta theta} = -r.

**How introduced:** Example 7.4 differentiates the polar basis vectors expressed on the Cartesian basis and reads off coefficients using eqn 7.6.

**Prerequisites:** connection coefficients, position-dependent basis vectors

$$
\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = \Gamma^\theta_{\theta r} = \frac{1}{r}, \quad \Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = -r
$$

**Notes:** Reused in Exercise 7.3(c), which asks for covariant derivatives in polar coordinates 'using the connection coefficients given in the chapter'. Chapter 9 gives a faster way to obtain the same values from the metric.

**Where:** GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

### non-zero connection coefficients do not imply curvature

*principle · introduced*

Connection coefficients can be non-zero purely because of curvilinear coordinates on a flat space, so their presence cannot decide whether a space is curved; a curvature test needs a combination of Gamma and its derivatives (the Riemann tensor).

**How introduced:** Drawn as the moral of Example 7.4 with a forward pointer to Chapter 11.

**Prerequisites:** Christoffel symbols of plane polar coordinates

**Where:** GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

### torsion-free (symmetric) connection

*definition · introduced* · also: symmetric connection, torsion free

A connection whose coefficients in a coordinate basis are symmetric in the two lower indices, Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} = Gamma^mu_{beta alpha}; equivalently its torsion tensor vanishes.

**How introduced:** Stated as a property holding in all the coordinate frames used in the book (eqn 7.9), and used to excuse the choice of lower-index ordering.

**Prerequisites:** connection coefficients

$$
\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} = \Gamma^\mu_{\beta\alpha}
$$

**Notes:** True in coordinate bases for the Levi-Civita connection; in non-coordinate (e.g. orthonormal) bases connection coefficients need not be symmetric, which matters in Chapter 10 and Cartan's method (Chapter 36).

**Where:** GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

### non-tensorial transformation of connection coefficients

*identity · introduced* · also: Gamma is not a tensor

Under a change of coordinates the connection coefficients pick up an extra term involving second derivatives of the old coordinates with respect to the new ones, so they do not transform as tensor components and can be made zero at a point by a suitable coordinate choice.

**How introduced:** Example 7.5 contrasts the tensor transformation law (7.10) with the quoted law for Gamma (7.11), deferring the proof to Part V.

**Prerequisites:** tensor transformation law, connection coefficients

$$
\Gamma^{\alpha'}{}_{\gamma'\beta'} = \frac{\partial x^{\alpha'}}{\partial x^\alpha}\left(\frac{\partial^2 x^\alpha}{\partial x^{\beta'}\partial x^{\gamma'}} + \frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x^{\beta'}}\frac{\partial x^\gamma}{\partial x^{\gamma'}}\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\gamma\beta}\right)
$$

**Notes:** The book offers this as the reason for the names 'coefficients' and 'symbols'. The connection to local flatness (Gamma can be transformed away at a point) is not drawn here.

**Where:** GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

### tensor transformation law

*identity · revisited*

Components of an (m,n) tensor transform with one Jacobian factor dx'/dx for each upper index and one dx/dx' for each lower index, with no additional terms.

**How introduced:** Recalled in Example 7.5 for a (1,2) object as the standard against which Gamma fails.

**Prerequisites:** coordinate transformations

$$
T^{\alpha'}{}_{\gamma'\beta'} = \frac{\partial x^{\alpha'}}{\partial x^\alpha}\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x^{\beta'}}\frac{\partial x^\gamma}{\partial x^{\gamma'}} T^\alpha{}_{\gamma\beta}
$$

**Where:** GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

### comma and semicolon notation

*convention · introduced* · also: comma derivative, semicolon derivative, f_{,alpha}, v^mu_{;alpha}

Shorthand in which a comma before an index denotes a partial derivative, f_{,alpha} = df/dx^alpha, and a semicolon denotes a covariant derivative, v^mu_{;alpha} = (nabla_alpha v)^mu.

**How introduced:** Shaded margin box in §7.3, with the authors' remark that compactness comes at the price of punctuation-heavy expressions.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative

$$
f_{,\alpha} \equiv \frac{\partial f}{\partial x^\alpha}
$$

$$
(\nabla_\alpha \boldsymbol{v})^\mu \equiv v^\mu{}_{;\alpha} = v^\mu{}_{,\alpha} + v^\lambda\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\lambda}
$$

**Where:** GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102); GA §7.3 p.86 (pdf 103)

### components of a derivative versus derivatives of components

*principle · developed*

The mu-component of the covariant derivative of v is generally not the derivative of the component v^mu, because the basis vectors also change; nabla_alpha acting on the scalar functions v^mu gives dv^mu/dx^alpha, while nabla_alpha e_mu = Gamma^lambda_{alpha mu} e_lambda supplies the rest.

**How introduced:** Margin note 10 with eqns 7.15-7.17, reinforced in margin note 13 for D/dlambda.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative, connection coefficients

$$
\nabla_\alpha(v^\mu) = \frac{\partial v^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}
$$

$$
\nabla_\alpha(\boldsymbol{e}_\mu) = \Gamma^\lambda_{\alpha\mu}\boldsymbol{e}_\lambda
$$

**Notes:** Eqn 7.16 treats each v^mu as a scalar function, which is a notational convention; the combination is not a tensor.

**Where:** GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102); GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104)

### connection operator

*operation · introduced* · also: nabla, del operator on vectors

The operator nabla defined so that e_alpha . nabla = nabla_alpha; contracting it with any vector u gives the covariant derivative along u, nabla_u = u . nabla = u^alpha nabla_alpha.

**How introduced:** Introduced in §7.3 to pass from derivatives along basis directions to derivatives along arbitrary vectors, by analogy with u . grad f.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative, directional derivative

$$
\boldsymbol{e}_\alpha\cdot\nabla = \nabla_\alpha
$$

$$
\nabla_\alpha \equiv \nabla_{\boldsymbol{e}_\alpha}
$$

**Notes:** The dot notation u . nabla is informal; in Part V nabla v is treated as a (1,1) tensor into whose slot u is inserted.

**Where:** GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102)

### covariant derivative of a scalar

*definition · introduced* · also: nabla_alpha f

Acting on a scalar field, the covariant derivative is simply the partial derivative, nabla_alpha f = df/dx^alpha, because a scalar has no basis vectors to change; along a curve Df/dlambda = df/dlambda.

**How introduced:** Defined in eqn 7.20 as the starting point for the connection operator; restated in margin note 13.

**Prerequisites:** connection operator

$$
\nabla_\alpha f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x^\alpha}
$$

**Where:** GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102); GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104)

### directional covariant derivative

*operation · core* · also: nabla_u v, covariant derivative along a vector

The covariant derivative of v in the direction of a vector u, nabla_u v = u^alpha nabla_alpha v = u^alpha (dv^mu/dx^alpha + Gamma^mu_{alpha lambda} v^lambda) e_mu, linear in u.

**How introduced:** Obtained by replacing the basis direction e_alpha with a general vector u in eqn 7.20 and applying the same rule to vectors (7.22-7.23); becomes the chapter summary formula 7.39.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative, connection operator

$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v} = u^\alpha\left(\frac{\partial v^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha} + v^\lambda\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\lambda}\right)\boldsymbol{e}_\mu
$$

**Where:** GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102); GA §7.3 p.86 (pdf 103); GA p.89 (pdf 106)

### parallel transport equation

*law · developed* · also: nabla_u v = 0 in components

The component statement of parallel transport along u: u^alpha dv^mu/dx^alpha + Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} u^alpha v^beta = 0, meaning the change in components is exactly compensated by the connection term.

**How introduced:** Example 7.7 writes out nabla_u v = 0 in components and interprets it with a word equation (7.27).

**Prerequisites:** parallel transport, directional covariant derivative

$$
u^\alpha\frac{\partial v^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha} + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}v^\beta u^\alpha = 0
$$

**Notes:** With u^alpha d/dx^alpha = d/dlambda this becomes the ODE dv^mu/dlambda = -Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} u^alpha v^beta, which is what one integrates along a curve; applied to v = u it is the geodesic equation of Chapter 8.

**Where:** GA §7.3 p.86 (pdf 103)

### parametrized curve

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: path x(lambda), world line parametrization

A curve described by coordinate functions x^mu(lambda) of a parameter lambda that increases monotonically along it from one endpoint value to the other.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapter 1 with Fig. 7.4 and the examples of a line, parabola and circle in R^2 (Example 7.8).

**Prerequisites:** coordinates

$$
x^\mu = x^\mu(\lambda), \quad a \le \lambda \le b
$$

**Where:** GA §7.4 p.86 (pdf 103)

### affine parameter

*definition · introduced* · also: affine parametrization

A parameter along a curve that is related to a preferred parameter (arc length for spacelike curves, proper time for timelike ones, or any parameter making a geodesic's tangent parallel transported) by a linear map lambda = alpha eta + beta; for non-null curves it keeps the length of the tangent vector constant.

**How introduced:** Given loosely as parameters that mark off regular intervals, with any linear rescaling lambda = alpha eta + beta also allowed; margin note 16 later ties it to lengths under parallel transport, and Exercise 7.2 contrasts arc length s on a unit circle with the non-affine t = sin s.

**Prerequisites:** parametrized curve, tangent vector to a curve

$$
\lambda = \alpha\eta + \beta
$$

**Notes:** The book's characterization is imprecise (see gaps); the sharp statement comes with the geodesic equation in Chapter 8, where only affine parameters give D u/dlambda = 0.

**Where:** GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104); GA §7.5 p.88 (pdf 105); GA p.90 (pdf 107)

### vector field

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: v(x)

An assignment of a vector (in the tangent space) to each point of a region of spacetime, v(P).

**How introduced:** Described in §7.4 as a machine taking a position and returning a vector there, whose rate of change along a curve is sought.

**Prerequisites:** tangent vector to a curve

**Where:** GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104)

### covariant derivative along a curve

*operation · core* · also: Dv/dlambda, absolute derivative, intrinsic derivative along a curve

The rate of intrinsic change of a vector field along a parametrized curve, Dv/dlambda = nabla_u v with u the tangent; in components (Dv/dlambda)^mu = dv^mu/dlambda + Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} u^alpha v^beta.

**How introduced:** Defined by a word equation (7.28, 7.30) identifying it with nabla_u v, then pictured as a limit using parallel transport back along the curve (7.31, Fig. 7.6).

**Prerequisites:** directional covariant derivative, tangent vector to a curve, affine parameter

$$
\frac{\mathrm{D}\boldsymbol{v}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} \equiv \nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}
$$

**Notes:** The book reserves D/dlambda for an affine parameter, but the definition works for any parameter; affine parameters matter for geodesics.

**Where:** GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104); GA §7.4 p.88 (pdf 105)

### four-velocity

*physical-quantity · revisited* · also: velocity vector u, timelike tangent

The tangent vector to a massive particle's world line parametrized by proper time, u = (dx^mu/dtau) e_mu, normalized so that u . u = -1 in the book's signature.

**How introduced:** Mentioned as the preferred special case of the tangent vector (lambda = tau) in §7.4 and in the chapter summary.

**Prerequisites:** tangent vector to a curve, proper time

$$
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{u} = -1
$$

**Notes:** Introduced in special relativity in Part I; here it becomes the u in D/dtau used for free fall in Chapter 8.

**Where:** GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104); GA p.89 (pdf 106)

### limit definition of the covariant derivative via parallel transport

*technique · developed* · also: transport-back-and-subtract definition

Dv/dlambda at lambda_0 is the limit, as epsilon goes to zero, of [v(lambda_0 + epsilon) parallel transported back to lambda_0, minus v(lambda_0)] divided by epsilon; transport makes the two vectors live in the same tangent space so their difference is meaningful.

**How introduced:** Example 7.9 presents it as a four-step recipe modelled on [f(x + dx) - f(x)]/dx and illustrates it with Fig. 7.6.

**Prerequisites:** parallel transport, covariant derivative along a curve

$$
\frac{\mathrm{D}\boldsymbol{v}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} = \lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\boldsymbol{v}(\lambda_0+\varepsilon)_{\parallel\to\lambda_0} - \boldsymbol{v}(\lambda_0)}{\varepsilon}
$$

**Notes:** Revisited pictorially in Chapter 34 (covariant derivative in pictures).

**Where:** GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104)

### metric compatibility of the connection

*principle · developed* · also: compatibility condition, nabla g = 0, g_{mu nu;alpha} = 0

The requirement that the covariant derivative of the metric tensor vanishes, nabla_alpha g = 0, so that parallel transport preserves lengths and angles; together with a symmetric (torsion-free) connection it fixes Gamma uniquely in terms of the metric.

**How introduced:** Posed as the answer to 'could we have defined the derivative differently?'; justified in Example 7.11 by demanding that a parallel vector keep its length.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative of tensors, metric tensor, parallel transport

$$
\nabla_\alpha \boldsymbol{g} = 0
$$

$$
g_{\mu\nu;\alpha} = 0
$$

**Notes:** The book implies the metric alone forces the connection; uniqueness also needs the symmetry (7.9). Exercise 7.4 verifies compatibility directly from eqn 7.37.

**Where:** GA §7.5 p.88 (pdf 105); GA §7.5 p.89 (pdf 106); GA p.90 (pdf 107)

### preservation of vector length under parallel transport

*principle · introduced*

For a metric-compatible connection, the inner product g(A, B) of two vectors parallel transported along the same curve is constant, so lengths and angles are unchanged by transport.

**How introduced:** Given as the physical reason compatibility must hold (a world in which rulers change length on being moved would be unphysical), and used as the premise of Example 7.11.

**Prerequisites:** metric compatibility of the connection

$$
\frac{\mathrm{D}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\left(g_{\mu\nu}A^\mu A^\nu\right) = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §7.5 p.88 (pdf 105)

### Leibniz rule for covariant derivatives

*identity · introduced* · also: product rule

The covariant derivative of a tensor product or contraction of tensors obeys the ordinary product rule: differentiate each factor in turn with the others held fixed and add the results; for the squared length of a vector this yields one term containing the derivative of the metric and two containing the derivative of the vector.

**How introduced:** Assumed in Example 7.11 with a margin note deferring justification to Part V; also used implicitly in eqn 7.5.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative of tensors

$$
(g_{\mu\nu}A^\mu A^\nu)_{;\alpha} = g_{\mu\nu;\alpha}A^\mu A^\nu + g_{\mu\nu}A^\mu{}_{;\alpha}A^\nu + g_{\mu\nu}A^\mu A^\nu{}_{;\alpha}
$$

**Where:** GA §7.5 p.88 (pdf 105); GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100)

### covariant derivative of tensors

*operation · introduced* · also: covariant derivative of (0,2) and (2,0) tensors

Extension of the covariant derivative to higher-rank tensors: each upper index gets a +Gamma correction and each lower index a -Gamma correction, e.g. g_{mu nu;alpha} = g_{mu nu,alpha} - Gamma^beta_{alpha mu}g_{beta nu} - Gamma^beta_{alpha nu}g_{mu beta}.

**How introduced:** Quoted without derivation in margin note 15 (eqns 7.34, 7.35) and in Exercise 7.4 (7.40), with derivation deferred to Part V.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative, tensor transformation law

$$
T^{\mu\nu}{}_{;\alpha} = T^{\mu\nu}{}_{,\alpha} + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}T^{\beta\nu} + \Gamma^\nu_{\alpha\beta}T^{\mu\beta}
$$

$$
g_{\mu\nu;\alpha} = g_{\mu\nu,\alpha} - \Gamma^\beta_{\alpha\mu}g_{\beta\nu} - \Gamma^\beta_{\alpha\nu}g_{\mu\beta}
$$

**Notes:** The minus sign for lower indices follows from requiring f = w_mu v^mu to obey nabla_alpha f = df/dx^alpha with the Leibniz rule; the book does not show this here.

**Where:** GA §7.5 p.88 (pdf 105); GA p.90 (pdf 107)

### connection coefficients from the metric

*identity · mention* · also: Levi-Civita connection, dg -> Gamma

For a metric-compatible, torsion-free connection the coefficients are fixed by first derivatives of the metric: g_{rho lambda}Gamma^rho_{mu sigma} = (1/2)(g_{lambda mu,sigma} + g_{lambda sigma,mu} - g_{mu sigma,lambda}).

**How introduced:** Motivated heuristically: metric components are dot products of basis vectors and Gamma measures how basis vectors change, so Gamma should come from dg (boxed rule 7.38); the formula is quoted in margin note 18 for use in Chapter 9.

**Prerequisites:** metric compatibility of the connection, torsion-free (symmetric) connection, metric tensor

$$
g_{\rho\lambda}\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial g_{\lambda\mu}}{\partial x^\sigma} + \frac{\partial g_{\lambda\sigma}}{\partial x^\mu} - \frac{\partial g_{\mu\sigma}}{\partial x^\lambda}\right)
$$

**Notes:** Derived in Chapter 9 and again in Chapter 34.

**Where:** GA §7.5 p.89 (pdf 106)

### metric tensor

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: g, g_{mu nu} = e_mu . e_nu

The symmetric (0,2) tensor giving inner products of vectors, with coordinate components g_{mu nu} = g(e_mu, e_nu) = e_mu . e_nu; it defines lengths and is the fundamental field describing spacetime geometry.

**How introduced:** Recalled in §7.5 as the object that constrains the connection and whose derivatives build Gamma.

**Prerequisites:** position-dependent basis vectors

$$
g_{\mu\nu} = \boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{e}_\mu, \boldsymbol{e}_\nu) = \boldsymbol{e}_\mu\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_\nu
$$

**Where:** GA §7.5 p.88 (pdf 105); GA §7.5 p.89 (pdf 106)

### position vector is not a vector in general coordinates

*principle · mention* · also: farewell to the displacement vector

The object x = x^mu e_mu pointing from an origin to a coordinate point does not transform as a vector in curvilinear coordinates or curved space, so tangent vectors are built from dx^mu/dlambda rather than from d(x)/dlambda.

**How introduced:** Margin note 14 recalls Chapter 3 to explain why the tangent is not written dx/dlambda; margin note 2 gives the related picture of a chord leaving the surface.

**Prerequisites:** tangent vector to a curve

**Where:** GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104); GA §7.1 p.82 (pdf 99)

## Key equations

### (7.1) Covariant derivative in words · central · GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100)

$$
\left(\text{covariant derivative of } \boldsymbol{v}\right)_{\boldsymbol{u}} = \left(\text{change in vector } \boldsymbol{v}\right) - \left(\text{change due to coordinate system}\right)
$$

Conceptual definition: the covariant derivative along u keeps only the intrinsic change of v by removing the part caused by changing coordinates.

**Symbols:** v vector being differentiated; u direction of differentiation

### (7.2) Euclidean gradient · supporting · GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100)

$$
\vec{\nabla} f(x,y,z) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\vec{e}_x + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\vec{e}_y + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\vec{e}_z
$$

Gradient of a scalar field, normal to its level surfaces.

**Symbols:** f scalar field; e_x, e_y, e_z Cartesian unit vectors

### (7.3) Euclidean directional derivative · supporting · GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100)

$$
\vec{u}\cdot\vec{\nabla} f = u^x\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + u^y\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} + u^z\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}
$$

Rate of change of f along the vector u; the template the covariant derivative generalizes.

**Symbols:** u^i Cartesian components of u

### (7.4) Directional derivative in words · supporting · GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100)

$$
\vec{u}\cdot\vec{\nabla} f \approx f(\text{tip of } \vec{u}) - f(\text{base of } \vec{u})
$$

Heuristic reading of the directional derivative as the difference of f between the ends of u. The book writes it as an equality; it holds only to first order for a short u, and a rate also needs division by the step length.

**Symbols:** Valid to first order for small u

### (7.5) Product rule on a vector · derivation-step · GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100)

$$
\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{v}}{\partial x^\alpha} = \frac{\partial v^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}\boldsymbol{e}_\mu + v^\mu\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{e}_\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}
$$

Differentiating v = v^mu e_mu produces a term from changing components and a term from changing basis vectors.

**Symbols:** v^mu components; e_mu coordinate basis vectors

### (7.6) Definition of connection coefficients · central · GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

$$
\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{e}_\mu}{\partial x^\alpha} = \Gamma^\lambda_{\alpha\mu}\boldsymbol{e}_\lambda
$$

The derivative of each basis vector is expanded on the basis; the expansion coefficients are the connection coefficients.

**Symbols:** Gamma^lambda_{alpha mu}: lambda component of the change of e_mu along e_alpha (derivative index first)

### (7.7) Derivatives of polar basis vectors · derivation-step · GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

$$
\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{e}_r}{\partial r} = 0,\quad \frac{\partial \boldsymbol{e}_r}{\partial \theta} = \frac{\boldsymbol{e}_\theta}{r},\quad \frac{\partial \boldsymbol{e}_\theta}{\partial r} = \frac{\boldsymbol{e}_\theta}{r},\quad \frac{\partial \boldsymbol{e}_\theta}{\partial \theta} = -r\,\boldsymbol{e}_r
$$

How the coordinate basis of plane polar coordinates changes with position (from Chapter 3).

**Symbols:** e_r = cos theta e_x + sin theta e_y; e_theta = -r sin theta e_x + r cos theta e_y (unnormalized)

### Polar basis vectors on the Cartesian basis (margin note 6) · derivation-step · GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

$$
\boldsymbol{e}_r = \cos\theta\,\boldsymbol{e}_x + \sin\theta\,\boldsymbol{e}_y, \qquad \boldsymbol{e}_\theta = -r\sin\theta\,\boldsymbol{e}_x + r\cos\theta\,\boldsymbol{e}_y
$$

Coordinate basis vectors obtained from e_r = (dx/dr) e_x + (dy/dr) e_y and similarly for theta; differentiating them gives (7.7).

**Symbols:** x = r cos theta, y = r sin theta

### (7.8) Plane polar connection coefficients · supporting · GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

$$
\Gamma^r_{rr} = \Gamma^\theta_{rr} = \Gamma^r_{\theta r} = \Gamma^r_{r\theta} = \Gamma^\theta_{\theta\theta} = 0,\quad \Gamma^\theta_{\theta r} = \Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = \frac{1}{r},\quad \Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = -r
$$

All eight connection coefficients of the flat plane in polar coordinates; non-zero despite zero curvature.

### (7.9) Symmetric (torsion-free) connection · supporting · GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

$$
\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} = \Gamma^\mu_{\beta\alpha}
$$

Symmetry in the lower indices holding in all coordinate frames used in the book.

### (7.10) Tensor transformation law for a (1,2) tensor · supporting · GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

$$
T^{\alpha'}{}_{\gamma'\beta'} = \frac{\partial x^{\alpha'}}{\partial x^\alpha}\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x^{\beta'}}\frac{\partial x^\gamma}{\partial x^{\gamma'}}T^\alpha{}_{\gamma\beta}
$$

Homogeneous linear transformation expected of genuine tensor components.

**Symbols:** Primed indices refer to new coordinates

### (7.11) Transformation law of connection coefficients · supporting · GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

$$
\Gamma^{\alpha'}{}_{\gamma'\beta'} = \frac{\partial x^{\alpha'}}{\partial x^\alpha}\left(\frac{\partial^2 x^\alpha}{\partial x^{\beta'}\partial x^{\gamma'}} + \frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x^{\beta'}}\frac{\partial x^\gamma}{\partial x^{\gamma'}}\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\gamma\beta}\right)
$$

Gamma transforms with an extra second-derivative term, so it is not a tensor.

### (7.12) · derivation-step · GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102)

$$
\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{v}}{\partial x^\alpha} = \frac{\partial v^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}\boldsymbol{e}_\mu + v^\mu\Gamma^\lambda_{\alpha\mu}\boldsymbol{e}_\lambda
$$

Product-rule expression with the basis derivative replaced by connection coefficients.

### (7.13) · derivation-step · GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102)

$$
\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{v}}{\partial x^\alpha} = \left(\frac{\partial v^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha} + v^\lambda\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\lambda}\right)\boldsymbol{e}_\mu
$$

After relabelling dummy indices, both contributions multiply the same basis vector.

### (7.14) Shorthand for derivative along a basis vector · supporting · GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102)

$$
\nabla_\alpha \equiv \nabla_{\boldsymbol{e}_\alpha}
$$

nabla_alpha is the covariant derivative along the coordinate basis direction e_alpha.

### (7.15) Old and new notation · supporting · GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102)

$$
\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{v}}{\partial x^\alpha} \equiv (\nabla_\alpha\boldsymbol{v})^\mu\boldsymbol{e}_\mu
$$

The full derivative of the vector has components (nabla_alpha v)^mu, which generally differ from dv^mu/dx^alpha.

### (7.16) nabla acting on components · supporting · GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102)

$$
\nabla_\alpha(v^\mu) = \frac{\partial v^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}
$$

Treating each component as a scalar function, its derivative is the ordinary partial derivative.

### (7.17) nabla acting on basis vectors · supporting · GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102)

$$
\nabla_\alpha(\boldsymbol{e}_\mu) = \Gamma^\lambda_{\alpha\mu}\boldsymbol{e}_\lambda
$$

Restatement of eqn 7.6 in nabla notation.

### (7.18) Covariant derivative along a basis direction · central · GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102)

$$
\nabla_\alpha\boldsymbol{v} = \left(\frac{\partial v^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha} + v^\lambda\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\lambda}\right)\boldsymbol{e}_\mu
$$

Main component formula for the covariant derivative of a vector; for fixed alpha the result is a vector.

**Symbols:** Gamma^mu_{alpha lambda} connection coefficients

### Comma and semicolon notation (margin box) · central · GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102)

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^\alpha} \equiv f_{,\alpha}, \qquad (\nabla_\alpha\boldsymbol{v})^\mu \equiv v^\mu{}_{;\alpha} = v^\mu{}_{,\alpha} + v^\lambda\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\lambda}
$$

Compact notation for partial (comma) and covariant (semicolon) derivatives.

### (7.19) Flat spacetime in Cartesian coordinates · supporting · GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102)

$$
\nabla_\alpha\boldsymbol{v} = \frac{\partial v^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha}\boldsymbol{e}_\mu \quad (\text{flat spacetime})
$$

With constant Cartesian basis vectors all Gammas vanish and the covariant derivative is the partial derivative of components.

### (7.20) Connection operator on scalars · supporting · GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102)

$$
\boldsymbol{e}_\alpha\cdot\nabla f = \nabla_\alpha f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x^\alpha}
$$

Definition of the connection operator via basis directions, acting on a scalar as the partial derivative.

### (7.21) Directional derivative of a scalar · supporting · GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102)

$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}} f = \boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla f = u^\alpha\boldsymbol{e}_\alpha\cdot\nabla f = u^\alpha\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^\alpha}
$$

Derivative of a scalar along an arbitrary vector u.

### (7.22) Directional covariant derivative of a vector · derivation-step · GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102)

$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v} = u^\alpha\boldsymbol{e}_\alpha\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v} = u^\alpha\nabla_\alpha\boldsymbol{v}
$$

The covariant derivative along u is linear in u: a u^alpha-weighted combination of derivatives along basis directions.

### (7.23) Covariant derivative along a vector in components · central · GA §7.3 p.86 (pdf 103)

$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v} = u^\alpha\left(\frac{\partial v^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha} + v^\lambda\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\lambda}\right)\boldsymbol{e}_\mu
$$

Working formula for the covariant derivative of v along u; in semicolon form nabla_u v = u^alpha v^mu_{;alpha} e_mu (margin note 11).

### (7.24) Parallel transport condition · central · GA §7.3 p.86 (pdf 103)

$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v} = 0 \quad (\text{parallel transport})
$$

A vector is parallel transported along u when it has no intrinsic change in that direction.

### (7.25) Parallel transport in components · central · GA §7.3 p.86 (pdf 103)

$$
u^\alpha\frac{\partial v^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha} + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}v^\beta u^\alpha = 0
$$

Component form of the parallel transport condition; with u^alpha d/dx^alpha = d/dlambda it is an ODE for v^mu along the curve.

### (7.26) · derivation-step · GA §7.3 p.86 (pdf 103)

$$
\frac{\partial v^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha} = -\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}v^\beta
$$

Component change fully accounted for by the coordinate (connection) term.

### (7.27) Parallel transport in words · supporting · GA §7.3 p.86 (pdf 103)

$$
\left(\text{change in a vector's components}\right) = \left(\text{change due to coordinate system}\right)
$$

For a transported vector, all component change is attributable to the moving basis.

### Affine reparametrization · supporting · GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104)

$$
\lambda = \alpha\eta + \beta
$$

Linear change of parameter that preserves the property of marking regular intervals; defines affine parametrizations.

**Symbols:** alpha, beta constants

### (7.28) Rate of change along a curve · supporting · GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104)

$$
\left(\text{rate of change of } \boldsymbol{v} \text{ with respect to } \lambda\right) \equiv \frac{\mathrm{D}\boldsymbol{v}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}
$$

Introduces D/dlambda for the covariant derivative along a parametrized curve.

### (7.29) Tangent vector · central · GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104)

$$
\boldsymbol{u} = \left(\frac{\mathrm{d}x^\mu(\lambda)}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\right)\boldsymbol{e}_\mu
$$

The tangent vector to the curve provides the direction for the covariant derivative; for lambda = tau it is the four-velocity.

### (7.30) Covariant derivative along a curve · central · GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104)

$$
\frac{\mathrm{D}\boldsymbol{v}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} \equiv \nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v}
$$

The rate of change of v along the curve equals its covariant derivative along the tangent vector.

**Symbols:** u tangent vector field to the curve

### Four-velocity normalization · supporting · GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104)

$$
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{u} = -1
$$

With proper time as parameter the tangent to a timelike world line has unit negative norm (signature -+++).

### (7.31) Limit definition via parallel transport · central · GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104)

$$
\frac{\mathrm{D}\boldsymbol{v}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} = \nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v} = \lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{v}(\lambda_0+\varepsilon)_{(\text{parallel transport to }\lambda_0)} - \boldsymbol{v}(\lambda_0)}{\varepsilon}\right)
$$

Operational meaning: move the later vector back by parallel transport so the difference is taken within one tangent space.

### (7.32) Flat-space check along a curve · supporting · GA §7.4 p.88 (pdf 105)

$$
\left(\frac{\mathrm{D}\boldsymbol{A}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\right)^\alpha = u^\mu\left(\frac{\partial A^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu} + \Gamma^\alpha_{\mu\nu}A^\nu\right) = u^\mu\frac{\partial A^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu} = \frac{\mathrm{d}x^\mu}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\frac{\partial A^\alpha}{\partial x^\mu} = \frac{\mathrm{d}A^\alpha}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}
$$

With vanishing Gamma (Cartesian coordinates in flat spacetime) the chain rule makes the covariant derivative the ordinary derivative of components.

### (7.33) Metric compatibility · central · GA §7.5 p.88 (pdf 105)

$$
\nabla_\alpha\boldsymbol{g} = 0 \quad\text{or}\quad g_{\mu\nu;\alpha} = 0
$$

The covariant derivative of the metric vanishes in every direction, binding the connection to the metric and preserving lengths under transport.

### (7.34) Covariant derivative of the metric · supporting · GA §7.5 p.88 (pdf 105)

$$
g_{\mu\nu;\alpha} = g_{\mu\nu,\alpha} - \Gamma^\beta_{\alpha\mu}g_{\beta\nu} - \Gamma^\beta_{\alpha\nu}g_{\mu\beta}
$$

Component formula for the covariant derivative of a (0,2) tensor: one minus-Gamma term per lower index (quoted, derived in Part V).

### (7.35) Covariant derivative of a (2,0) tensor · supporting · GA §7.5 p.88 (pdf 105)

$$
T^{\mu\nu}{}_{;\alpha} = T^{\mu\nu}{}_{,\alpha} + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}T^{\beta\nu} + \Gamma^\nu_{\alpha\beta}T^{\mu\beta}
$$

One plus-Gamma term per upper index; needed later for conservation of the energy-momentum tensor.

### (7.36) Product rule on the squared length · derivation-step · GA §7.5 p.88 (pdf 105)

$$
g_{\mu\nu;\alpha}A^\mu A^\nu + g_{\mu\nu}A^\mu{}_{;\alpha}A^\nu + g_{\mu\nu}A^\mu A^\nu{}_{;\alpha} = 0
$$

If a parallel vector keeps its length, the two terms with A^mu_{;alpha} vanish, leaving g_{mu nu;alpha}A^mu A^nu = 0 and hence compatibility.

**Symbols:** A a parallel (covariantly constant) vector

### (7.37) Connection coefficients from the metric · central · GA §7.5 p.89 (pdf 106)

$$
g_{\rho\lambda}\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial g_{\lambda\mu}}{\partial x^\sigma} + \frac{\partial g_{\lambda\sigma}}{\partial x^\mu} - \frac{\partial g_{\mu\sigma}}{\partial x^\lambda}\right)
$$

Quoted ahead of Chapter 9: for a compatible symmetric connection, Gamma is determined by first derivatives of the metric.

### (7.38) Rule of thumb: dg gives Gamma · supporting · GA §7.5 p.89 (pdf 106)

$$
\boxed{\partial g} \rightarrow \boxed{\Gamma}
$$

Dependency map: connection coefficients are built from first derivatives of metric components.

### (7.39) Summary formula · supporting · GA p.89 (pdf 106)

$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{u}}\boldsymbol{v} = u^\alpha\left(\frac{\partial v^\mu}{\partial x^\alpha} + v^\lambda\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\lambda}\right)\boldsymbol{e}_\mu
$$

Restatement of eqn 7.23 in the chapter summary.

### (7.40) Covariant derivative of a (0,2) tensor (Exercise 7.4) · supporting · GA p.90 (pdf 107)

$$
(\nabla_\alpha\boldsymbol{\xi})_{\mu\nu} = \frac{\partial \xi_{\mu\nu}}{\partial x^\alpha} - \Gamma^\lambda_{\alpha\mu}\xi_{\lambda\nu} - \Gamma^\lambda_{\alpha\nu}\xi_{\mu\lambda}
$$

General (0,2) formula supplied in the exercise so that compatibility can be checked using eqn 7.37.

**Symbols:** xi any (0,2) tensor

## Worked examples

### Example 7.1 · intro · GA p.81 (pdf 98)

**Problem:** Give a concrete picture of how a curved space can still be described by flat coordinates, and what a manifold is.

**Method:** Analogy with navigating the Earth: flat rectangular street maps work in a town but not globally, yet many overlapping small maps can blend smoothly to cover the globe; the same is done for 4D spacetime with local four-coordinate charts.

**Key insight:** Smoothness means spacetime is Minkowski-like in a small enough region, so curvature is a large-scale property that the new derivative must be able to handle.

**Result:** Working definition of a manifold and the statement of local flatness.

**Concepts:** manifold, local flatness, differential geometry

### Example 7.2 · intro · GA §7.1 p.82 (pdf 99)

**Problem:** Decide whether vectors that are all parallel in R^3 and drawn on a sphere are parallel for inhabitants of the sphere.

**Method:** Compare each vector with the tangent plane at its base point (Fig. 7.2), then contrast with vectors correctly transported along great circles (Fig. 7.3), which have identical components in the inhabitants' local frames.

**Key insight:** Parallelism defined by the embedding space is not available to insiders; their parallel vectors must stay in the tangent plane and so rotate as seen from outside.

**Concepts:** parallelism in curved space, parallel transport, intrinsic (insider) viewpoint on geometry

### Example 7.3 · intro · GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100)

**Problem:** Recall the gradient and directional derivative of a scalar field in Euclidean 3-space as a model for a derivative along a vector.

**Method:** Write grad f in Cartesian components, contract with u to get u . grad f, and read it as the difference in f between the tip and base of u.

**Key insight:** A derivative along a direction is linear in the direction vector; the covariant derivative will keep this structure for vectors in curved space.

**Result:** u . grad f = u^x f_x + u^y f_y + u^z f_z

**Concepts:** gradient, directional derivative

### Example 7.4 · standard · GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

**Problem:** Find all connection coefficients of the flat plane in polar coordinates by brute force.

**Method:** Express e_r and e_theta on the Cartesian basis using x = r cos theta, y = r sin theta (margin note 6), differentiate them with respect to r and theta (7.7), and match each derivative to Gamma^lambda_{alpha mu} e_lambda (7.6).

**Key insight:** Curvilinear coordinates on a flat space already produce non-zero Gammas, so Gamma measures coordinate behaviour and cannot on its own detect curvature.

**Result:** Gamma^theta_{r theta} = Gamma^theta_{theta r} = 1/r, Gamma^r_{theta theta} = -r, all others zero.

**Concepts:** Christoffel symbols of plane polar coordinates, connection coefficients, non-zero connection coefficients do not imply curvature

### Example 7.5 · standard · GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

**Problem:** Show why connection coefficients are not the components of a tensor.

**Method:** Compare the homogeneous tensor transformation law (7.10) with the quoted transformation law for Gamma (7.11), which contains an additional second-derivative term; the proof is deferred to Part V.

**Key insight:** The inhomogeneous term means Gamma can vanish in one coordinate system and not in another, which is impossible for a tensor.

**Concepts:** non-tensorial transformation of connection coefficients, tensor transformation law

### Example · standard · GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102)

**Problem:** Derive the component formula for the covariant derivative of a vector along a basis direction.

**Method:** Insert the definition of Gamma (7.6) into the product-rule expansion (7.5), swap the dummy labels mu and lambda in the second term so both terms multiply e_mu (7.12-7.13), and rename the result nabla_alpha v (7.18).

**Key insight:** Relabelling dummy indices is what turns 'derivative of components plus derivative of basis' into a single set of components; those components differ from the partial derivatives of v^mu by Gamma v.

**Result:** (nabla_alpha v)^mu = dv^mu/dx^alpha + Gamma^mu_{alpha lambda} v^lambda

**Concepts:** covariant derivative, connection coefficients, components of a derivative versus derivatives of components

### Example 7.6 · intro · GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102)

**Problem:** Evaluate the covariant derivative in Minkowski spacetime using Cartesian coordinates.

**Method:** Note that Cartesian basis vectors are constant, so every connection coefficient vanishes, and substitute into eqn 7.18.

**Key insight:** The covariant derivative reduces to the familiar partial derivative whenever the basis does not change, which is why special relativity never needed it.

**Result:** nabla_alpha v = (dv^mu/dx^alpha) e_mu in flat spacetime with Cartesian coordinates.

**Concepts:** covariant derivative, connection coefficients

### Example 7.7 · standard · GA §7.3 p.86 (pdf 103)

**Problem:** Write the parallel transport condition nabla_u v = 0 in components and interpret it.

**Method:** Expand eqn 7.23 set to zero to get u^alpha dv^mu/dx^alpha + Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} v^beta u^alpha = 0 (7.25), then (dropping u^alpha) dv^mu/dx^alpha = -Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} v^beta (7.26), read as a word equation (7.27).

**Key insight:** Under parallel transport the components still change, but only by exactly the amount forced by the changing basis.

**Concepts:** parallel transport equation, parallel transport

### Example 7.8 · intro · GA §7.4 p.86 (pdf 103)

**Problem:** Parametrize a straight line, a parabola and a circle in the plane.

**Method:** Use x = lambda with y = m lambda + c for the line and y = lambda^2 for the parabola; use x = a cos lambda, y = a sin lambda for the circle.

**Key insight:** A curve is a map from a parameter to coordinates; the same geometric curve admits many parametrizations.

**Result:** (lambda, m lambda + c); (lambda, lambda^2); (a cos lambda, a sin lambda)

**Concepts:** parametrized curve

### Example 7.9 · standard · GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104)

**Problem:** Give an operational, limit-based definition of Dv/dlambda that parallels the ordinary derivative.

**Method:** Four steps: take v at lambda_0 + epsilon, parallel transport it back to lambda_0, form the difference with v(lambda_0), divide by epsilon and let epsilon go to zero (7.31, Fig. 7.6).

**Key insight:** Vectors at different points cannot be subtracted directly; parallel transport supplies the missing identification, and that is exactly what removes the coordinate-induced change.

**Concepts:** limit definition of the covariant derivative via parallel transport, covariant derivative along a curve, parallel transport

### Example 7.10 · intro · GA §7.4 p.88 (pdf 105)

**Problem:** Check that DA/dlambda reduces to the ordinary derivative of components in flat spacetime.

**Method:** Write (DA/dlambda)^alpha = u^mu(dA^alpha/dx^mu + Gamma^alpha_{mu nu} A^nu), set Gamma = 0 for Cartesian coordinates, replace u^mu by dx^mu/dlambda and use the chain rule (7.32).

**Key insight:** The D/dlambda notation is designed to look like d/dlambda because it becomes exactly that when the basis is constant.

**Result:** (DA/dlambda)^alpha = dA^alpha/dlambda

**Concepts:** covariant derivative along a curve, tangent vector to a curve

### Example 7.11 · standard · GA §7.5 p.88 (pdf 105)

**Problem:** Show that requiring parallel-transported vectors to keep their lengths forces the metric compatibility condition.

**Method:** Take a covariantly constant vector A (A^mu_{;alpha} = 0), assume the product rule for the covariant derivative of g_{mu nu}A^mu A^nu (7.36), set the total to zero because the length is constant, and observe the two terms containing A^mu_{;alpha} vanish.

**Key insight:** Length preservation and nabla g = 0 are two faces of the same requirement; the metric thereby constrains which connection is allowed.

**Result:** g_{mu nu;alpha} = 0

**Concepts:** metric compatibility of the connection, preservation of vector length under parallel transport, Leibniz rule for covariant derivatives


## Analogies and intuitions

### Ants on a football or humans on the Earth who may not float above the surface → intrinsic (insider) viewpoint on geometry · strong · GA §7.1 p.82 (pdf 99)

Sets the rules: every measurement and every notion of 'same direction' must be made inside the surface, just as we cannot step outside spacetime.

**Where it breaks down:** A 2D surface visibly sits inside 3D space, which invites learners to sneak in extrinsic reasoning; spacetime has no such outside, and the Lorentzian signature has no everyday picture.

**App idea:** Toggleable 'ant camera' that shows only the local tangent plane view while the learner steers along a surface, versus the god's-eye 3D view.

### Patching the globe with small rectangular street maps → manifold · strong · GA p.81 (pdf 98)

Explains that a curved space can be covered by locally flat coordinate charts that blend smoothly, and that flatness holds only locally.

**Where it breaks down:** Suggests charts are always rectangular grids and hides the transition functions and the fact that some manifolds need several charts for topological reasons.

**App idea:** Atlas builder: tile a sphere with small square maps; enlarge one map and watch its grid distort from the true distances.

### A 2D manifold as a smooth rubber sheet that may be shaped into a sphere or torus but not torn → manifold · weak · GA p.81 (pdf 98)

Emphasizes smoothness and continuity as defining features.

**Where it breaks down:** Rubber suggests stretching is irrelevant, which is true for topology but wrong for geometry where lengths matter; 'not punctured' is loose since a punctured plane is still a manifold.

### Straight-line path from New York to Paris tunnels hundreds of kilometres below ground → position vector is not a vector in general coordinates · strong · GA §7.1 p.82 (pdf 99)

Shows why vectors as straight arrows between distant points leave the space being described, motivating tangent vectors.

**Where it breaks down:** Only works while the embedding space is available to draw the chord; the deeper point about tangent spaces is left implicit.

**App idea:** Globe with two draggable cities: show the great-circle route and the straight chord, with the chord's maximum depth displayed.

### Tourists in London and Tokyo each have a 'north' on their street maps, defined the same way but pointing differently in absolute 3D terms → parallel transport · useful · GA §7.1 p.82 (pdf 99)

Illustrates vectors defined identically in local frames that are nonetheless different arrows as seen from outside the Earth.

**Where it breaks down:** North is defined by a global field (toward the pole), not by transporting along a chosen path; parallel transport along different routes between the cities would not all give north.

**App idea:** Transport a north arrow from London to Tokyo along a great circle and along a latitude line, and compare both results with local north on arrival.

### Directional derivative as 'value at the tip of u minus value at its base' → directional derivative · useful · GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100)

Builds intuition that a derivative along a vector compares a field at two nearby points, preparing for comparing vectors at two points.

**Where it breaks down:** For scalars the comparison is trivial; for vectors the two values live in different tangent spaces, which is exactly the difficulty the chapter resolves.

### Connection coefficients as the rate at which e_mu swings toward e_lambda while travelling along e_alpha → connection coefficients · strong · GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

Gives each index of Gamma a geometric job (margin note 8) and justifies the word 'connection' as translating between neighbouring local frames.

**Where it breaks down:** Speaks of rotation, but Gamma also encodes stretching of basis vectors (e.g. e_theta grows with r, giving Gamma^theta_{r theta} = 1/r).

**App idea:** Polar grid with basis arrows at a draggable point; step along e_r or e_theta and display how the arrows turn and stretch, labelling the change with the corresponding Gamma values.

### Covariant derivative as an ordinary difference quotient [f(x + dx) - f(x)]/dx, but with the later vector carried back by parallel transport first → limit definition of the covariant derivative via parallel transport · strong · GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104)

Connects the new derivative with the familiar limit definition and makes clear what extra ingredient curved space needs.

**Where it breaks down:** Hides the fact that the transported vector depends on the chosen connection; the picture is flat, so the rotation from transport is not visible.

**App idea:** See the Fig. 7.6 redesign: animated transport-back and shrinking epsilon.

### Bracketed word equations: covariant derivative equals change in vector minus change due to the coordinate system → covariant derivative · useful · GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100)

Carries the logic before and after the index calculations (eqns 7.1, 7.27, 7.30).

**Where it breaks down:** The sign bookkeeping is slippery: in the formula the Gamma term is added to the component derivative, which learners may think contradicts 'minus the coordinate change'.

**App idea:** Stacked-bar decomposition of dv^mu/dlambda into intrinsic part and coordinate part for a field in polar coordinates, with the sign of each piece shown.

### Length drift under transport as physically unacceptable (the book states this in one sentence; the 'moving rulers' image is our framing) → metric compatibility of the connection · useful · GA §7.5 p.88 (pdf 105)

The book's physical reason for imposing nabla g = 0: if the condition failed, parallel transport would change the lengths of vectors, which it calls highly undesirable for describing the real world.

**Where it breaks down:** Metric-incompatible connections exist mathematically (e.g. Weyl's theory), so compatibility is a choice justified by physics, not a logical necessity.


## Misconceptions addressed

### If the connection coefficients are non-zero, the space must be curved. · GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

**Correction:** Flat space in polar coordinates has non-zero Gammas; curvature is detected by a specific combination of Gammas and their derivatives (Riemann tensor).

**Why tempting:** Gamma appears exactly where curved spaces need corrections, and it vanishes in the flat Cartesian case learners know best.

### Connection coefficients are tensor components like everything else with indices. · GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

**Correction:** Their transformation law contains a second-derivative term, so they are not a tensor; only combinations such as the covariant derivative or Riemann tensor are.

**Why tempting:** They carry one upper and two lower indices and are placed like (1,2) tensor components.

### Vectors pointing in the same direction in the surrounding 3D space are parallel on a curved surface. · GA §7.1 p.82 (pdf 99)

**Correction:** Such vectors generally stick out of the tangent plane; insiders' parallel vectors must remain tangent and therefore rotate relative to the embedding.

**Why tempting:** Everyday parallelism is defined in flat 3D space.

### A parallel transported vector keeps a fixed direction in absolute terms. · GA §7.1 p.82 (pdf 99)

**Correction:** It keeps fixed components in transported local frames; seen from an embedding it changes direction (Fig. 7.1).

**Why tempting:** In flat Cartesian space both statements coincide.

### The components of the derivative of a vector are the derivatives of its components. · GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102)

**Correction:** (nabla_alpha v)^mu = dv^mu/dx^alpha + Gamma^mu_{alpha lambda} v^lambda; only for constant basis vectors (or for scalars) do the two agree.

**Why tempting:** True in Cartesian coordinates, which is where learners met vector calculus.

### Straight arrows from one point to another (displacement vectors) are legitimate vectors in curved space. · GA §7.1 p.82 (pdf 99)

**Correction:** Such chords leave the space; vectors live in the tangent space at a single point, and the position vector does not transform properly in general coordinates.

**Why tempting:** Special relativity and school physics use displacement vectors freely.

### Parallel transport means the components of the vector stay constant. · GA §7.3 p.86 (pdf 103)

**Correction:** Components change along the path according to dv^mu/dlambda = -Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} u^alpha v^beta; only the intrinsic change is zero.

**Why tempting:** The chapter summary says components are the same 'in local coordinate systems', which is easily misread as 'in the global coordinates'.

### Since the Gamma term 'removes the coordinate change', it should enter the covariant derivative with a minus sign. · GA §7.3 p.86 (pdf 103)

**Correction:** The component derivative dv^mu/dx^alpha already contains the coordinate effect with the opposite sign; adding +Gamma v cancels it. For lower indices the Gamma term does come with a minus sign.

**Why tempting:** Word equation 7.1 is phrased as a subtraction, while formula 7.18 shows an addition.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### nabla v is a vector because nabla_alpha v is a vector. · GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102)

**Correction:** For each fixed alpha the result is a vector, but with alpha free the covariant derivative is a (1,1) tensor; contracting with u gives the vector nabla_u v.

**Why tempting:** The book stresses that nabla_alpha v is a (1,0) object.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Locally flat means the metric is exactly Minkowski throughout some small but finite region. · GA p.81 (pdf 98)

**Correction:** Only at a point can the metric be made Minkowski with zero first derivatives; second derivatives (curvature) remain and produce tidal effects in any finite region.

**Why tempting:** Example 7.1 says spacetime is flat over a sufficiently small region.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Any parametrization of a curve is as good as any other when differentiating along it. · GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104)

**Correction:** Covariant derivatives along a curve rescale with the parameter. A quantity such as u . Du/dlambda = (1/2) d(u . u)/dlambda vanishes only when |u| is constant, i.e. when the parameter is a linear function of arc length or proper time (Exercise 7.2).

**Why tempting:** The text says the precise parametrization choice is not crucial.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The metric by itself uniquely determines the covariant derivative. · GA §7.5 p.88 (pdf 105)

**Correction:** Compatibility nabla g = 0 plus the symmetry (torsion-free) condition together pick out a unique connection; compatibility alone allows torsionful alternatives.

**Why tempting:** Section 7.5 states that the metric forces this version of the derivative on us.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Ants carrying their axes along a great circle · GA §7.1 p.82 (pdf 99)

**Setup:** Surface-bound observers walk along a path that never turns from their point of view, carrying a local frame built from the path's tangent and a perpendicular direction in the surface, and compare vectors by their components in that frame.

**Lesson:** Parallelism can be defined without leaving the surface, and the resulting parallel vectors look rotated to anyone using the embedding space.

**App idea:** First-person ant walk on a sphere with a head-up display of the carried frame and a picture-in-picture outside view.

### Rigid arrows on a globe · GA §7.1 p.82 (pdf 99)

**Setup:** Draw many arrows on a sphere, all aligned with one fixed direction of 3D space, and ask surface dwellers whether they are parallel.

**Lesson:** Most of them are not even tangent to the surface, so embedding-space parallelism is meaningless to insiders.

**App idea:** See the Fig. 7.2 redesign.

### What if rulers changed length when transported? · GA §7.5 p.88 (pdf 105)

**Setup:** Imagine a connection under which a vector's length drifts as it is parallel transported along a curve. The book raises this only as a one-sentence counterfactual in §7.5; the thought-experiment framing is ours.

**Lesson:** Comparisons of lengths at different places would be ambiguous, which is unacceptable physically; demanding invariance leads to nabla g = 0.

**App idea:** Toggle between a compatible connection and a deliberately incompatible one on a surface; a transported arrow visibly grows or shrinks in the incompatible case.


## Applications and observations

- **Chord versus great-circle route between New York and Paris** (numerical-estimate, GA §7.1 p.82 (pdf 99)): Used in a margin note to show that a straight-line vector between two points on the Earth leaves the surface, passing hundreds of kilometres underground. Key numbers: Our estimate: great-circle distance about 5,800 km subtends about 0.92 rad at the Earth's centre (R = 6,371 km); the chord's deepest point is R(1 - cos 0.46) which is roughly 650 km below the surface.
- **Local street maps on a curved Earth** (observation, GA p.81 (pdf 98)): Flat rectangular maps work within a town but cannot be extended over the whole planet, illustrating local flatness and the need for many charts. Key numbers: Our estimate: for a map of side L on a sphere of radius R, fractional distance errors scale like (L/R)^2; a 10 km town map has errors of order 10^-6, a 1,000 km map of order 10^-2.
- **Four-velocity of massive particles** (observation, GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104)): Choosing proper time as parameter along timelike world lines makes the tangent vector the physical velocity, normalized to u.u = -1 (units with c = 1); this is the u used in the geodesic equation of Chapter 8. Key numbers: u.u = -1

## Historical notes

- **Elwin Bruno Christoffel (1869):** Introduced the symbols now called Christoffel symbols or connection coefficients. — Supplied the key object for differentiating vectors in curved spaces, later used by Einstein. (GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100))
- **Gregorio Ricci-Curbastro (c. 1880s-1900):** Developed Christoffel's ideas into the tensor calculus (absolute differential calculus) that Einstein adopted for general relativity. — Provided the mathematical machinery of general relativity decades before the theory existed. (GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100))

## Notation and conventions

- **Lower-index order of connection coefficients:** Gamma^lambda_{alpha mu} with the derivative (direction) index first: de_mu/dx^alpha = Gamma^lambda_{alpha mu} e_lambda, so (nabla_alpha v)^mu = v^mu_{,alpha} + Gamma^mu_{alpha lambda} v^lambda. — Margin note 8 says Hawking and Ellis use this order and MTW do not; harmless for the symmetric connections used in the book. The text on printed p.89 writes Gamma^lambda_{mu alpha} for the same derivative, silently switching order. (GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101))
- **nabla_alpha:** Short for nabla_{e_alpha}, the covariant derivative along the coordinate basis vector e_alpha; nabla_u = u^alpha nabla_alpha. (GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102))
- **Comma and semicolon:** f_{,alpha} = df/dx^alpha; v^mu_{;alpha} = (nabla_alpha v)^mu, the derivative index written after the punctuation. — Index placement in the reading copy's LaTeX sometimes puts the semicolon index as a subscript beneath the superscript; the book's typeset form is v^mu_{;alpha}. (GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102))
- **Dot notation for the connection operator:** e_alpha . nabla = nabla_alpha and u . nabla v = nabla_u v. — Borrowed from vector calculus; not standard in modern GR texts, where nabla_u v is preferred. (GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102))
- **D/dlambda:** Covariant derivative along a curve with respect to its (affine) parameter lambda; d/dlambda is kept for ordinary derivatives of scalars and components. — Many books write D/dtau or nabla_u; some use delta/dlambda. (GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104))
- **Metric signature:** Mostly plus (-,+,+,+), implied by u.u = -1 for the four-velocity; units with c = 1. (GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104))
- **Tensor type (m,n):** m upper (vector) slots and n lower (1-form) slots; nabla_alpha v for fixed alpha is described as a (1,0) object, g as (0,2), T^{mu nu} as (2,0). (GA §7.3 p.85 (pdf 102))
- **Vector typography:** Bold symbols for spacetime vectors and tensors (v, e_mu, g); arrows over symbols for ordinary Euclidean 3-vectors in Example 7.3. (GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100))
- **Coordinate basis vectors:** e_mu are unnormalized coordinate basis vectors (e.g. |e_theta| = r in polar coordinates), with g_{mu nu} = e_mu . e_nu. — Contrast with the normalized unit vectors of elementary vector calculus; Chapter 10 introduces orthonormal frames. (GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101))
- **Boxed-arrow dependency rule:** A boxed symbol arrow such as [dg] -> [Gamma] means 'is built from'. — The chapter says it will expand on this in coming chapters; a similar boxed chain for curvature is a natural extension for teaching. (GA §7.5 p.89 (pdf 106))

## Teaching gems

### Separate the insider's and outsider's verdicts on parallelism: the same transported arrow has constant components in the carried local frame but visibly swings in the embedding axes. · GA §7.1 p.82 (pdf 99)

**Why it works:** Makes 'parallel' a relational, intrinsic notion before any formula, and pre-empts the flat-space instinct that parallel means fixed absolute direction.

**App idea:** Split-screen sphere walk: 3D view with fixed XYZ arrows beside an ant's-eye local frame; both component readouts update as the learner drags the ant.

### Brute-force Gammas for the flat plane in polar coordinates, followed by the punchline that non-zero Gamma does not mean curvature. · GA §7.2 p.84 (pdf 101)

**Why it works:** A familiar flat space with a changing basis isolates exactly what Gamma measures and inoculates against using Gamma as a curvature detector.

**App idea:** Polar grid with draggable point: arrows e_r, e_theta and their neighbours; nudging along r or theta shows the basis change, with the non-zero Gamma values lighting up; a second tab shows the Riemann tensor staying zero.

### Product rule on v = v^mu e_mu: the derivative of a vector has a component part and a basis part, and the basis part is named Gamma. · GA §7.2 p.83 (pdf 100)

**Why it works:** Derives the covariant derivative from one line of calculus the learner already trusts, making the Gamma term inevitable rather than mysterious.

**App idea:** Step-through derivation card with each term colour-coded; the learner chooses a coordinate system (Cartesian, polar, spherical) and sees which terms survive.

### Four-step recipe for Dv/dlambda: take the vector a little further along, carry it back by parallel transport, subtract, divide and take the limit. · GA §7.4 p.87 (pdf 104)

**Why it works:** Connects directly to the limit definition of an ordinary derivative and makes clear why a connection is needed: to move vectors to a common point before subtracting.

**App idea:** Animated Fig. 7.6 on a cone or sphere with an epsilon slider and a toggle for 'subtract without transporting' to expose the error.

### Word equations in big brackets that restate each formula in plain language: covariant change = total change minus coordinate change; under transport, component change equals coordinate change. · GA §7.3 p.86 (pdf 103)

**Why it works:** Gives voice-tutor-ready sentences that anchor the index expressions and can be recalled when the algebra gets heavy.

**App idea:** Hover over each term in eqns 7.18 and 7.25 to reveal its word-equation label and a mini animation of the effect it represents.

### Justify metric compatibility by insisting transported rulers keep their lengths, then prove it in one line with the product rule on g(A, A). · GA §7.5 p.88 (pdf 105)

**Why it works:** Physical requirement first, compact algebra second; it also sets up why Gamma must come from derivatives of the metric.

**App idea:** Transport experiment with a 'connection knob' that adds an incompatible piece; the arrow length drifts and a readout of g_{mu nu;alpha} leaves zero.

### Flat-Cartesian sanity checks after each new definition (nabla_alpha v and DA/dlambda both collapse to ordinary derivatives). · GA §7.4 p.88 (pdf 105)

**Why it works:** Teaches the habit of testing new geometric machinery against the known limit and reassures learners the new derivative contains the old one.

**App idea:** Coordinate switch on any derivative demo: flipping to Cartesian in flat space greys out all Gamma terms and shows the ordinary derivative result.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Eqn 7.26 drops the u^alpha from eqn 7.25, which is only valid if v is parallel transported in every direction; along a single curve the constraint is the contraction u^alpha(...) = 0. The following sentence also mislabels the derivative index (nu instead of alpha).** — Learners may think parallel transport fixes all partial derivatives of v, or try to use 7.26 for a vector defined only along a curve where partial derivatives off the curve are meaningless. *Suggestion:* Teach the transport condition as the ODE dv^mu/dlambda + Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} (dx^alpha/dlambda) v^beta = 0 along the curve, and reserve 7.26 for covariantly constant fields.
- **Sign logic: eqn 7.1 and margin note 12 describe subtracting the coordinate change, yet the Gamma term is added in eqn 7.18; the sign flips because dv^mu/dx^alpha contains the coordinate effect with the opposite sign.** — Confusion about whether the Gamma term should be plus or minus, compounded later when lower indices get minus signs. *Suggestion:* Work polar coordinates explicitly: take a constant Cartesian field, show its polar components change, and show +Gamma v exactly cancels that change so nabla v = 0.
- **Eqns 7.5-7.6 differentiate basis vectors as if vectors at different points could be subtracted directly, which only makes sense with an embedding (as in the polar example); on a genuine curved manifold eqn 7.6 is effectively the definition of the connection.** — Circularity is hidden: the derivative is derived using the very comparison of vectors at different points that it is supposed to supply. *Suggestion:* State openly that in curved space one must choose a rule for comparing nearby tangent spaces; Gamma encodes that rule and metric compatibility plus symmetry pick the natural one (Chapter 34).
- **Affine parametrization is defined vaguely (parameters that 'mark regular intervals', closed under linear maps) and margin note 16 says affine parameters are those along which transported vectors keep their length, but length preservation under transport holds for any parameter given compatibility.** — No usable criterion for affinity; the note may be read as saying non-affine parameters break length conservation. *Suggestion:* Say: for non-null curves a parameter is affine if it is a linear function of arc length or proper time (so |u| is constant); for geodesics, affine parameters are exactly those with Du/dlambda = 0. Use Exercise 7.2 as the demonstration.
- **Section 7.5 says the metric forces this covariant derivative, omitting that the symmetry condition 7.9 is also needed for uniqueness.** — Misses the fundamental theorem of Riemannian geometry and is unprepared for torsion or non-coordinate bases. *Suggestion:* State: a unique connection is both metric compatible and torsion free (the Levi-Civita connection); eqn 7.37 follows from those two conditions together.
- **Example 7.11 assumes a vector field A with nabla_alpha A = 0 in all directions, which generally does not exist in curved space, and concludes g_{mu nu;alpha} = 0 from its contraction with A^mu A^nu without the polarization step.** — The proof looks airtight but relies on an object that may not exist; careful learners may be puzzled. *Suggestion:* Rephrase along a curve: for all vectors A transported along any direction u, u^alpha g_{mu nu;alpha} A^mu A^nu = 0; since u and A are arbitrary and g is symmetric, polarization gives g_{mu nu;alpha} = 0.
- **The claim that a smooth manifold is flat over a sufficiently small region is stated without the qualification that only first derivatives of the metric can be removed at a point.** — Encourages the belief that curvature vanishes in small regions, conflicting with tidal effects in Chapter 11. *Suggestion:* Say 'approximately flat, with deviations growing like the square of the region's size', and connect to local inertial frames.
- **Index ordering inconsistency: printed p.89 writes the basis-vector derivative with Gamma^lambda_{mu alpha} although eqn 7.6 uses Gamma^lambda_{alpha mu}.** — Minor confusion about conventions, harmless for symmetric connections but not in non-coordinate frames. *Suggestion:* Keep one convention (derivative index first) throughout the app and flag when bases are non-coordinate.
- **The chapter summary omits metric compatibility and the dg -> Gamma link, and states parallel transport only in terms of 'same components in local coordinate systems'.** — Learners may skip §7.5 as optional and misread the summary as 'components stay constant'. *Suggestion:* Add summary points: nabla g = 0 preserves lengths; parallel transport changes coordinate components according to -Gamma u v.
- **Minor slips: Exercise 7.3 says 'cylindrical polar' for 2D plane polar coordinates; after eqn 7.30 'u us' is a typo for 'u is'; Example 7.5 says 'braces' for parentheses. Exercise 7.3(b) also asks for 'the transformations from Chapter 3' but prints none. The page render confirms this is the book's own omission, not an export loss.** — Small distractions; the Exercise 7.3 wording might send learners looking for a z coordinate. *Suggestion:* Use 'plane polar' in any rewritten version, and supply the component transformations explicitly: v^r = cos theta v^x + sin theta v^y, v^theta = (-sin theta v^x + cos theta v^y)/r.
- **The distinction that nabla v (alpha free) is a (1,1) tensor is only implicit in Exercise 7.3(d); the text emphasizes nabla_alpha v being a (1,0) object.** — Learners struggle with Exercise 7.3(d) and later with nabla_mu T^{mu nu} = 0. *Suggestion:* Explicitly introduce the (1,1) tensor nabla v with components v^mu_{;alpha} and show nabla_u v as inserting u into its lower slot.
- **Fig. 7.6 is drawn on a flat page, so the 'parallel transported' dashed arrow is just a translated copy of v(lambda_0 + epsilon). Nothing in the picture shows how transport differs from naive sliding.** — The figure can reinforce the idea that one simply slides arrows to a common base point, exactly the flat-space habit the chapter is trying to break. *Suggestion:* Pair Fig. 7.6 with a curved example: transport along a latitude circle on a sphere, or on a cone via its unrolled sector, where the transported arrow visibly differs from a rigid translation in the embedding.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'If you were an ant on a football and could never leave the surface, how would you decide whether two arrows at different places point the same way?' Let the learner discover that chords are not allowed and that a local frame must be carried along.
- Second opener for calculus-minded learners: 'In polar coordinates, a constant vector field has components that change from point to point. So what should its derivative be?' This sets up the need for a Gamma term.
- Recommended order: insider viewpoint and tangent vectors -> embedding versus insider parallelism (Figs 7.1-7.3) -> product rule on v^mu e_mu -> define Gamma -> polar Gammas and 'Gamma is not curvature' -> nabla_alpha v and semicolon notation -> nabla_u v and the transport condition -> curves, tangent vectors, D/dlambda -> transport-back limit picture -> metric compatibility and dg -> Gamma.
- Do a concrete polar check before or right after eqn 7.18: take v = e_x written in polar components (cos theta, -sin theta / r) and verify with the polar Gammas that nabla_r v and nabla_theta v vanish. This settles the sign of the Gamma term.
- Check for understanding: 'The flat plane in polar coordinates has Gamma^r_{theta theta} = -r. Does that mean the plane is curved?' Expected: no; Gamma reflects the coordinates.
- Check for understanding: 'Why can't we just subtract v at lambda_0 + epsilon from v at lambda_0?' Expected: they live in different tangent spaces; parallel transport brings them together.
- Check for understanding: 'Under parallel transport, do the components of the vector stay the same?' Expected: not in general coordinates; they change by -Gamma u v, and only the intrinsic change is zero.
