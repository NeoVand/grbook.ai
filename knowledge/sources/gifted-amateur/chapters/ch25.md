# GA ch25 · Black holes

> Takes the Schwarzschild geometry all the way down to r = 2M and below: shows that the light cones narrow and then tip over so that inside the horizon decreasing r is the future, introduces the tortoise coordinate to tame the logarithms in coordinate time, contrasts the finite proper time of a plunging astronaut with the diverging coordinate time and logarithmically delayed signals seen from afar, computes her tidal stretching and her view of the sky, and ends with stellar collapse drawn on spacetime and Penrose diagrams.

**Pages:** printed 262–271 · pdf 279–288 · **Difficulty:** math 2/5, conceptual 4/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

The algebra is light: one-line substitutions, a logarithmic integral and results imported from Chapters 11, 22 and 24. The difficulty is conceptual: accepting that coordinates can swap causal roles, holding finite proper time and infinite coordinate time in mind at once, and reading Penrose diagrams of collapse. Several results are quoted rather than derived (plunge formulas, critical angle, Riemann components), a sign is misprinted in eqn 25.10, and the viewpoint in §25.4 slides between static and falling observers, which can confuse careful readers. The exercises on rain coordinates are noticeably harder than the chapter text.

## Role in the book

Chapters 21-24 built the Schwarzschild metric and studied radial plunges, orbits and photon paths outside a star. Chapter 25 is the first chapter that treats the metric as describing empty space all the way in, so the awkward radius r = 2M can no longer be hidden inside matter. It stays deliberately within Schwarzschild coordinates, collecting every symptom of trouble at the horizon (closing light cones, infinite coordinate time, infinite redshift) together with the evidence that nothing local goes wrong there (finite proper time, smooth tidal components). This sets up Chapter 26, which classifies singularities and removes the horizon pathology with Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates, and Chapter 27, which completes the picture with Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates and a fuller Penrose diagram. The Penrose diagrams here reuse the conformal machinery of Chapter 19, and the observational examples anticipate gravitational waves in Chapter 46.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can derive the radial light-cone slope dt/dr = +/-(1 - 2M/r)^-1 from ds^2 = 0 and use it to sketch how cones narrow as r approaches 2M from outside.
- Reader can substitute r = 2M - epsilon into the Schwarzschild metric and show that, inside the horizon, a displacement in r at fixed t is timelike, so r plays the role of time and t that of space.
- Reader can explain why every future-directed worldline inside r = 2M must reach r = 0, and why this makes r = 2M an event horizon.
- Reader can define the tortoise coordinate, integrate it to r* = r + 2M ln|r/2M - 1| + const, and write the radial metric in the conformally flat form (1 - 2M/r)(-dt^2 + dr*^2).
- Reader can state what the tortoise coordinate fixes (45-degree radial light rays, no metric blow-up) and what it does not (the horizon sits at r* = -infinity).
- Reader can contrast the finite proper time of a radial plunge with the divergent Schwarzschild coordinate time, and read both behaviours off Figs. 25.5 and 25.6.
- Reader can use the orthonormal-frame Riemann components to show that tidal accelerations are finite at r = 2M, stretch radially and squeeze transversely, and diverge as r goes to 0.
- Reader can compute the coordinate travel time of an outgoing radial photon, eqn 25.15, and explain from its logarithm why a distant observer sees an infalling source slow down, fade and never cross.
- Reader can use the critical angle sin(psi_c) = sqrt(27) M (1 - 2M/r)^(1/2)/r to find the fraction of sky a black hole blocks for a static observer, including the half-sky result at r = 3M.
- Reader can derive the blueshift factor (1 - 2M/r)^(-1/2) of starlight measured by a static observer from conservation of p_t and the vielbein.
- Reader can describe the collapse of a sufficiently massive star through r = 2M, explain why outside observers never see the singularity, and interpret the Penrose diagrams of a collapse black hole, including why r = 0 is a spacelike singularity.
- Reader can summarize the four main lines of observational evidence for black holes and quote the scale r_S of about 3 km per solar mass.

## Teaching approach

An informal, symptom-by-symptom investigation that refuses to leave Schwarzschild coordinates. Every section poses a physical question (what happens to light cones at r = 2M, what does a falling astronaut feel, what do we see of her, what does she see, how does a real star get there) and answers with a short calculation from the metric plus a picture. The recurring tension is between quantities that blow up in these coordinates and quantities that stay finite for the traveller, and the chapter deliberately leaves that tension partly unresolved so that the better coordinates of the next two chapters arrive as a relief. Humour (Achilles, Brad Pitt, 'bon voyage'), boxed examples and margin notes carry the history and the observational context.

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, thought-experiment, diagram-driven, analogy-driven, conversational-informal, observational-astrophysics, example-driven

**Narrative arc**

1. Define a black hole, restate Schwarzschild via Birkhoff, and point to the two suspicious radii r = 0 and r = 2M, noting that for real black holes the region below 2M is empty space rather than stellar interior.
2. Promise that r = 2M is a coordinate artefact (finite proper time, smooth curvature) and pause for the observational case for black holes (Sgr A*, X-ray binaries, quasars, gravitational waves).
3. Outside the horizon: derive the radial light-cone slope and watch the cones close up as r approaches 2M, linking this to the infinite coordinate time of the plunge from Chapter 22.
4. Inside the horizon: substitute r = 2M - epsilon, discover that fixed-t displacements in r are timelike, and conclude that the future points to r = 0; name the event horizon.
5. Assemble a full light-cone picture in Schwarzschild coordinates and flag its apparently singular null curves as illusory, deferring the cure.
6. Declare that studying black holes is largely a search for good coordinates, introduce the tortoise coordinate to package the recurring logarithms, illustrate it with Zeno's race, and show it straightens radial light rays while pushing the horizon to r* = -infinity.
7. Death of an astronaut: compare proper time and coordinate time for the plunge, compute her tidal stretching from the Riemann tensor, and confirm that nothing special happens locally at r_S while r = 0 is a genuine physical singularity.
8. Signals from the astronaut: integrate the outgoing null condition to get a logarithmically divergent delay, so distant observers see pulses spread out, redden and dim.
9. Looking around: use the photon critical angle and time-reversal symmetry to show the hole swallowing more and more of the sky, and use conserved p_t to show incoming starlight is blueshifted.
10. Gravitational collapse: stellar endpoints and the Chandrasekhar limit, a collapsing star crossing r = 2M with its light cones, the frozen appearance from outside, and Penrose diagrams showing a spacelike singularity hidden behind the horizon.
11. Exercises build Painleve-Gullstrand ('rain') coordinates, which are regular at r = 2M, as a preview of Chapter 26.

**Signature moves**

- Diagnose the causal character of a coordinate by freezing all the others and checking the sign of ds^2, turning the slogan 'the future is inwards' into a two-line calculation.
- Draw the same physical process twice against two different clocks (proper time and Schwarzschild time, Figs. 25.5 and 25.6) so that the coordinate nature of the horizon divergence is visible at a glance.
- Let the traveller's body be the curvature detector: tidal accelerations from orthonormal Riemann components are smooth at r_S and blow up only at r = 0.
- Name a coordinate after a paradox (Achilles and the tortoise) so that its defining asymptotic behaviour, approaching but never reaching, sticks.
- Tell the story from both viewpoints: what the falling astronaut experiences, what the distant observer receives, and what the astronaut herself sees.
- Cash in a result prepared earlier: the critical photon angle and its time-reversed reading were set up in §24.2 with an explicit promise, and here they become the astronaut's view of the sky, followed by a new blueshift calculation.
- Deliberately leave the horizon pathology unresolved within the chapter, flagging it as illusory, so that the next chapter's coordinate change has a clear motivation; the end-of-chapter exercises then quietly provide one fix.
- Use margin notes for coordinate-philosophy cautions ('a variable called t has no special claim to be time') at exactly the moment the metric makes that point.

## Section by section

### Chapter opening: what a black hole is and the two awkward radii — p.262 (pdf 279)

Defines a black hole as a region from which light cannot get out and argues via Birkhoff's theorem that a non-rotating one is described by the Schwarzschild metric. Points out that the line element misbehaves at r = 0 and at r = 2M: at the latter g_tt vanishes (static clocks would record no time, signals are infinitely redshifted) and g_rr diverges. Restoring units, r_S is about 3 km per solar mass, which for ordinary stars lies deep inside the matter, but for a black hole all the mass sits at the centre and the region below 2M is vacuum. The chapter then asserts that local physics and curvature are smooth at r_S, that a traveller crosses it in finite proper time, and that the trouble stems from the coordinates.

**Concepts:** black hole, Birkhoff's theorem, Schwarzschild metric, Schwarzschild radius, coordinate singularity, curvature singularity

**Key moves**
- Invoke spherical symmetry plus Birkhoff to justify using the Schwarzschild metric for the whole exterior of a non-rotating hole.
- Identify two separate failures at r = 2M: vanishing g_tt (clock rate and redshift) and diverging g_rr (proper radial distance per dr).
- Put in numbers: r_S = 2GM/c^2 is about 3 km for one solar mass.
- Observe that for a black hole the region 0 < r < 2M lies outside the mass, so the horizon can no longer be ignored.
- Anticipate the resolution: finite proper time to cross, smooth curvature, so the divergence is a coordinate artefact to be removed later.

### Example 25.1: observational evidence for black holes — p.263 (pdf 280)

A boxed survey of indirect evidence in four families: stellar proper motions around Sagittarius A*, whose shadow and bright ring were imaged by the Event Horizon Telescope; X-ray binaries in which an unseen companion is too heavy to be a neutron star, with Cygnus X-1 as the standard case; compact, massive, highly luminous engines at galactic centres such as quasars; and gravitational-wave signals from merging black holes. A margin note dispels the idea that black holes vacuum up their surroundings: outside the horizon the geometry is that of any spherical star.

**Concepts:** supermassive black hole, accretion disc, X-ray binary, quasar, Event Horizon Telescope image, black-hole exterior equals stellar exterior

**Key moves**
- Stellar orbits near the Galactic Centre require a compact mass of about 4 million solar masses.
- In a binary, the visible star's motion fixes the companion's mass; above neutron-star limits it must be a black hole; accreted gas heated by its orbital motion shines in X-rays.
- Galactic nuclei and quasars need enormous energy output from a small volume, explained by accretion onto supermassive holes.
- Gravitational-wave detections of mergers are flagged for Chapter 46.

### §25.1 The surface r = 2M — p.264 (pdf 281)

Outside r = 2M, setting ds^2 = 0 for radial motion gives light-cone slopes dt/dr = +/-(1 - 2M/r)^-1, which are 45 degrees far away and become vertical as r approaches 2M from above, so cones close up and any worldline takes ever more coordinate time to change r. Inside, writing r = 2M - epsilon shows that a displacement purely in epsilon at fixed t is timelike while t has become spacelike. Hence the light cones tip over, the future of every event points to smaller r, even light is trapped, and nothing inside can be seen from outside: r = 2M is the event horizon. A full light-cone map in Schwarzschild coordinates (Fig. 25.3) shows null curves racing to infinite t at r_S, a feature the authors label as illusory.

**Concepts:** radial light cones in Schwarzschild coordinates, closing of light cones near the horizon, exchange of timelike and spacelike roles of t and r inside the horizon, inevitability of reaching r = 0 inside the horizon, event horizon, coordinates have no intrinsic metric meaning, coordinate singularity

**Key moves**
- Set ds^2 = 0 with dtheta = dphi = 0 to get dt/dr = +/-(1 - 2M/r)^-1.
- Read off asymptotic slope +/-1 at large r and slope diverging as r approaches 2M from above: cones become needle-thin in the (r, t) plane.
- Link narrowing cones to the infinite coordinate time for a plunge found in Chapter 22.
- Substitute r = 2M - epsilon to get ds^2 = epsilon/(2M - epsilon) dt^2 - (2M - epsilon)/epsilon depsilon^2 + (2M - epsilon)^2 dOmega^2.
- Freeze t, theta, phi: ds^2 < 0, so epsilon is a time coordinate that must keep increasing, driving r to 0; t is spacelike.
- Apply to a photon emitted inside: both edges of its cone head to smaller r, so it and every massive particle are trapped.
- Name r = 2M the event horizon by analogy with the Earth's horizon.

### §25.2 The tortoise coordinate — p.265 (pdf 282)

Because coordinate-time integrals of the form int dr/(1 - 2M/r) keep producing logarithms, the authors define r* by dr*/dr = (1 - 2M/r)^-1, giving r* = r + 2M ln|r/2M - 1| + const, which runs to minus infinity at the horizon. Example 25.2 motivates the name with Zeno's race: as r* is decreased without limit, r creeps toward 2M but never reaches it. Radial null curves become t = +/- r* + const and the radial metric takes the form (1 - 2M/r)(-dt^2 + dr*^2), so cones no longer close and nothing blows up at r = 2M. The price is that the horizon has been pushed off to r* = -infinity, so better coordinates are still needed.

**Concepts:** tortoise coordinate, logarithmic divergence of coordinate time at the horizon, conformally flat form of the radial Schwarzschild metric, radial null geodesics in Schwarzschild spacetime

**Key moves**
- Spot the recurring integral Delta t = int dr/(1 - 2M/r) and its logarithm.
- Define dr*/dr = (1 - 2M/r)^-1 and integrate to r* = r + 2M ln|r/2M - 1| + const.
- Tell the Achilles race: dr/dr* goes to zero near 2M, so r approaches 2M only as r* goes to minus infinity.
- Write light rays as t = +/- r* + const.
- Factor the radial metric as (1 - 2M/r)(-dt^2 + dr*^2): radial cones are at 45 degrees in (r*, t).
- Note the remaining drawback: the horizon is at infinite r*, motivating Chapter 26.

### §25.3 Death of an astronaut — p.266 (pdf 283)

An astronaut plunges radially, flashing a torch at regular intervals. Her proper time as a function of r is a smooth power law, so she reaches r_S and then r = 0 in finite time with nothing special on her watch; the Schwarzschild coordinate time, which is the proper time of distant observers, diverges logarithmically at r_S. Example 25.3 reuses the orthonormal-frame tidal equations: radial stretching at rate 2M/r^3 and transverse squeezing at M/r^3, finite at the horizon but unbounded as r goes to 0, which marks r = 0 as a true physical singularity. Integrating the outgoing radial null condition gives the photon delay (r2 - r1) + r_S ln[(r2 - r_S)/(r1 - r_S)], so distant watchers see her pulses arrive ever further apart and increasingly redshifted, and she never quite appears to reach the horizon.

**Concepts:** radial plunge, proper time versus coordinate time at the horizon, tidal stretching and compression, geodesic deviation, Riemann tensor in the static orthonormal frame, curvature singularity, radial null geodesics in Schwarzschild spacetime, apparent freezing and redshifting of infalling sources

**Key moves**
- Quote tau(r) for a plunge from rest at infinity: finite and smooth through r_S (Fig. 25.5).
- Quote t(r) with its logarithmic term: divergent at r_S (Fig. 25.6).
- Evaluate the deviation equation with Schwarzschild orthonormal Riemann components to get d^2chi/dtau^2 = +2M/r^3 chi radially and -M/r^3 chi transversely.
- Note the components depend only on M/r^3, so nothing singular happens at 2M but they diverge at r = 0.
- Integrate dt/dr = (1 - r_S/r)^-1 from emission to reception to find the flat-space delay plus a logarithm that diverges as emission approaches r_S.
- Translate into observations: pulses spread out, the image appears to hover near the horizon, and light is increasingly redshifted (quantitative falling-observer redshift deferred to Chapter 26).

### §25.4 Looking around near a black hole — p.267 (pdf 284)

Reuses the critical photon angle from Chapter 24: light emitted at radius r beyond psi_c spirals in, and psi_c shrinks to zero at the horizon. Since the metric is symmetric under time reversal, the same angle limits which incoming rays reach an observer at r; the remaining directions are dark, and that dark patch is the black hole as seen. Example 25.4 finds that at r = 3M the hole fills half the sky, and a margin note adds that there one can see the back of one's own head. Close to the horizon the outside universe shrinks to a small bright cone, and incoming starlight measured by a static observer is blueshifted by (1 - 2M/r)^(-1/2), obtained from conserved p_t and the static vielbein.

**Concepts:** critical angle for photon capture, photon sphere, time-reversal invariance of the Schwarzschild metric, apparent angular size of a black hole, gravitational blueshift of infalling light, measured energy from the orthonormal frame, conserved Killing energy

**Key moves**
- Recall sin psi_c = sqrt(27) M (1 - 2M/r)^(1/2)/r and note psi_c goes to 0 at r = 2M.
- Invoke time-reversal symmetry to convert an escape criterion for emitted photons into an acceptance criterion for received photons.
- Evaluate at r = 3M: psi_c = pi/2, so the hole covers half the sky.
- Compute E_obs as the hatted time component of p using (e_t)^t-hat = (1 - 2M/r)^(1/2).
- Use p_t = (1 - 2M/r)p^t constant to get p^t(r) = hbar omega_inf (1 - 2M/r)^-1, hence E_obs = hbar omega_inf (1 - 2M/r)^(-1/2).

### §25.5 Gravitational collapse — p.268 (pdf 285)

Sketches stellar endpoints: ordinary stars end as white dwarfs, but above the Chandrasekhar limit (about 1.4 solar masses) collapse continues to a neutron star or, for still larger mass, through r = 2M to a central singularity. Figures 25.8 and 25.9 show the stellar surface worldline with light cones tipping as it crosses the horizon. From outside the star seems to slow, redden and freeze just above r_S; light emitted exactly at r_S hovers there, and light emitted later falls in whichever way it is aimed, forming a closed trapped surface. The Penrose diagrams (Figs. 25.10 and 25.11) place the horizon H at 45 degrees and draw r = 0 as a spacelike line, explained by the swapped roles of r and t inside; anyone who crosses H meets it, while observers reaching i+ see everything except the interior.

**Concepts:** gravitational collapse, Chandrasekhar limit, neutron star, apparent freezing and redshifting of infalling sources, closed trapped surface, Penrose diagram of a collapse black hole, spacelike singularity, event horizon

**Key moves**
- Chain the stellar endpoints: white dwarf below about 1.4 solar masses, neutron star above, black hole for the heaviest remnants.
- Follow the stellar surface through r = 2M in an (r, t) sketch and a 3D sketch with light cones.
- Reuse the delayed-pulse result: the image freezes and blackens as the surface nears r_S from outside.
- Classify light emitted at and after horizon crossing: outgoing front stays at 2M, everything later is dragged inward.
- Draw the Penrose diagram with horizon H and a double-line singularity, and argue the singularity is spacelike because r is timelike inside.
- Read visibility off the Penrose diagram: observers ending at i+ see everything except r < 2M, while anyone inside meets the singularity along 45-degree light rays and has a future horizon cutting off light from most of space.
- Add the stellar interior (hatched fluid) to the diagram, showing its surface crossing H and ending on the singularity.

### Chapter summary and exercises — p.270 (pdf 287)

The summary restates the four headline points: Schwarzschild describes non-rotating holes, r_S is the horizon beyond which r = 0 is unavoidable, crossing r_S is locally unremarkable while the tidal field grows without bound toward r = 0, and proper time to the singularity is finite though coordinate time diverges. The four exercises construct Painleve-Gullstrand ('global rain') coordinates by a coordinate change and by a physical array of dropped clocks, use the rain metric to show inward motion inside the horizon, and analyse whether a tangentially launched particle can avoid capture.

**Concepts:** Painleve-Gullstrand coordinates, radial plunge, event horizon, conserved Killing energy

**Key moves**
- Coordinate change dt = dT - R(r) dr chosen so that g_rr = 1 yields a metric regular at r = 2M with flat constant-T slices.
- Operational construction of rain time from clocks dropped from rest at infinity at a steady rate.
- Use the rain metric's null directions to show inward motion inside the horizon.
- Relate a local tangential speed to conserved E and L to test capture.

## Concepts

### black hole

*solution-or-model · core* · also: dark star (historical)

A region of spacetime bounded by an event horizon, from inside which no signal, not even light, can reach distant observers; the non-rotating, uncharged vacuum case is described by the Schwarzschild geometry.

**How introduced:** Opens the chapter as a working definition, tied to Birkhoff's theorem, with a margin history from Michell and Laplace's escape-speed 'dark stars' to Oppenheimer-Snyder and Wheeler's name.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild metric, event horizon, Birkhoff's theorem

**Notes:** The Newtonian dark-star idea gets the radius 2GM/c^2 right by coincidence; light in Newtonian gravity could still rise and fall back, whereas in GR nothing inside the horizon even moves outward.

**Where:** GA p.262 (pdf 279); GA p.270 (pdf 287)

### Schwarzschild metric

*solution-or-model · revisited* · also: Schwarzschild line element, Schwarzschild geometry

The unique spherically symmetric vacuum solution of Einstein's equation, ds^2 = -(1 - 2M/r)dt^2 + (1 - 2M/r)^-1 dr^2 + r^2 dOmega^2, describing the exterior of any spherical mass and the whole of an uncharged non-rotating black hole.

**How introduced:** Restated at the start as the geometry that Birkhoff's theorem forces on a spherical black hole; then examined near and inside r = 2M in its own coordinates.

**Prerequisites:** Birkhoff's theorem

$$
ds^2=-\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)dt^2+\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}dr^2+r^2 d\Omega^2
$$

**Where:** GA p.262 (pdf 279); GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281)

### Birkhoff's theorem

*theorem · mention*

Any spherically symmetric vacuum region of spacetime is a piece of the Schwarzschild geometry, whatever the (spherical) matter inside is doing, so it is static and fixed by the single parameter M.

**How introduced:** Cited in one sentence to justify using Schwarzschild for a non-rotating black hole; also underlies the margin remark that the exterior of a hole is no different from that of a star of the same mass.

**Where:** GA p.262 (pdf 279); GA p.263 (pdf 280)

### Schwarzschild radius

*physical-quantity · core* · also: r_S, r = 2M

The radius r_S = 2GM/c^2 (2M in geometrized units) at which g_tt of the Schwarzschild metric vanishes and g_rr diverges; for a black hole it is the location of the event horizon.

**How introduced:** Singled out as one of two radii where the line element misbehaves; numbers are restored to show it is about 3 km per solar mass, inside ordinary stars but in empty space for a black hole.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild metric

$$
r_S=\frac{2GM}{c^2}\approx 3\,\mathrm{km}\,\frac{M}{M_\odot}
$$

**Where:** GA p.262 (pdf 279); GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281); GA p.270 (pdf 287)

### coordinate singularity

*definition · developed* · also: apparent singularity, coordinate artefact

A place where metric components blow up or vanish only because of a poor choice of coordinates; invariant quantities such as curvature and the proper time of travellers remain finite there, and a coordinate change removes it.

**How introduced:** Built up by accumulation: curvature is smooth at 2M, a plunging clock crosses in finite proper time, tidal forces are unremarkable, yet coordinate time and light-cone slopes diverge. The chapter names the conclusion but saves the systematic treatment for Chapter 26.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild radius, proper time versus coordinate time at the horizon, Riemann tensor in the static orthonormal frame

**Where:** GA p.263 (pdf 280); GA §25.1 p.265 (pdf 282); GA §25.3 p.267 (pdf 284)

### curvature singularity

*phenomenon · core* · also: physical singularity, r = 0 singularity, true singularity

A boundary of spacetime at which curvature, and hence tidal accelerations felt by any object, grow without bound, so that no coordinate change can make it regular; for Schwarzschild it is r = 0.

**How introduced:** Contrasted with r = 2M: the tidal equations of Example 25.3 scale as M/r^3 and diverge only as r goes to 0, so any body reaching r = 0 is destroyed.

**Prerequisites:** tidal stretching and compression, Riemann tensor in the static orthonormal frame

$$
|R_{\hat\tau\hat r\hat\tau\hat r}|=\frac{2M}{r^3}\to\infty\ \text{as}\ r\to 0
$$

**Notes:** The book gives component values rather than an invariant; the Kretschmann scalar 48M^2/r^6 (not in this chapter) is the cleaner proof.

**Where:** GA p.262 (pdf 279); GA §25.3 p.267 (pdf 284); GA §25.5 p.269 (pdf 286)

### event horizon

*definition · core* · also: horizon, surface of no return, H

The boundary of the region of spacetime from which no future-directed causal curve can reach distant (future null) infinity; for Schwarzschild it is the null surface r = 2M.

**How introduced:** Named after showing that light cones inside r = 2M tip over so that even outward-aimed photons move to smaller r; compared with the Earth's horizon beyond which we cannot see; later drawn as a 45-degree line H on Penrose diagrams.

**Prerequisites:** exchange of timelike and spacelike roles of t and r inside the horizon, inevitability of reaching r = 0 inside the horizon, light cone

$$
r=r_S=2M
$$

**Notes:** Only the local, coordinate-based argument is given here; the global definition via future null infinity is implicit in the Penrose-diagram discussion.

**Where:** GA §25.1 p.265 (pdf 282); GA §25.5 p.269 (pdf 286); GA p.270 (pdf 287)

### radial light cones in Schwarzschild coordinates

*phenomenon · core* · also: null cone slope, light-cone structure

For radial motion ds^2 = 0 gives dt/dr = +/-(1 - 2M/r)^-1, so in an (r, t) plot the light cones have 45-degree edges far away, become arbitrarily thin as r approaches 2M from above, and are tipped over for r < 2M.

**How introduced:** Derived in one line from the metric and illustrated with a row of cones (Fig. 25.2) and a map of null curves (Fig. 25.3).

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild metric, light cone

$$
\frac{dt}{dr}=\pm\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}
$$

**Where:** GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281); GA §25.1 p.265 (pdf 282)

### closing of light cones near the horizon

*phenomenon · developed* · also: eclipsing light cones

In Schwarzschild coordinates the coordinate speed of light dr/dt = +/-(1 - 2M/r) goes to zero at r = 2M, so cones pinch to vertical lines and any causal worldline needs unbounded coordinate time to change r.

**How introduced:** Read off eqn 25.3 as r decreases toward 2M and tied to the infinite coordinate time of the Chapter 22 plunge.

**Prerequisites:** radial light cones in Schwarzschild coordinates

**Notes:** A coordinate effect: in coordinates regular at the horizon the cones do not pinch.

**Where:** GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281)

### light cone

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: null cone

The set of null directions (ds^2 = 0) at an event, dividing tangent directions into timelike (inside), null (on) and spacelike (outside); massive particles move inside it.

**How introduced:** Assumed from special relativity and used as the main diagnostic tool of the chapter.

**Where:** GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281)

### exchange of timelike and spacelike roles of t and r inside the horizon

*phenomenon · core* · also: the future is inwards, r becomes timelike

For r < 2M the metric coefficient of dr^2 is negative and that of dt^2 positive, so a displacement along r is timelike and along t spacelike; the Schwarzschild r then behaves as a time coordinate that must decrease into the future.

**How introduced:** Found by substituting r = 2M - epsilon, freezing t, theta and phi, and noticing that the remaining interval is negative; a margin note stresses that a coordinate labelled t has no special claim on time.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild metric, light cone, coordinates have no intrinsic metric meaning

$$
ds^2\big|_{dt=d\theta=d\phi=0}=-\frac{2M-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\,d\varepsilon^2<0
$$

**Notes:** Whether r must decrease rather than increase is a choice of time orientation (black hole versus white hole); the chapter assumes the black-hole branch.

**Where:** GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281); GA §25.5 p.269 (pdf 286)

### inevitability of reaching r = 0 inside the horizon

*phenomenon · developed* · also: trapping of light and matter, no escape inside r_S

Inside a Schwarzschild horizon every future-directed timelike or null worldline has decreasing r and ends at r = 0 within a finite affine parameter or proper time, regardless of the rocket thrust applied.

**How introduced:** Follows from the tipped-over cones: an astronaut inside who fires a photon in any direction still sends it inward.

**Prerequisites:** exchange of timelike and spacelike roles of t and r inside the horizon

**Where:** GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281); GA §25.3 p.267 (pdf 284); GA p.270 (pdf 287)

### coordinates have no intrinsic metric meaning

*principle · developed* · also: coordinate freedom, labels are not measurements

Coordinates are arbitrary labels of events; physical durations and lengths come only from the metric, so a coordinate named t need not be a time and a coordinate range need not be finite physically.

**How introduced:** Stated in margin notes at the moment t turns spacelike inside the horizon, and again when r* is described as a strange race timer.

**Where:** GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281); GA §25.2 p.265 (pdf 282)

### tortoise coordinate

*technique · core* · also: r*, Regge-Wheeler coordinate

The radial coordinate r* defined by dr*/dr = (1 - 2M/r)^-1, so r* = r + 2M ln|r/2M - 1| + const; it matches r far away and tends to minus infinity as r approaches 2M from outside.

**How introduced:** Motivated by the logarithms that appear whenever coordinate time is integrated along radial light rays, named after Zeno's paradox (Example 25.2), then used to straighten radial null lines.

**Prerequisites:** radial light cones in Schwarzschild coordinates, logarithmic divergence of coordinate time at the horizon

$$
\frac{dr^*}{dr}=\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}
$$

$$
r^*=r+2M\ln\left|\frac{r}{2M}-1\right|+\text{const}
$$

**Notes:** The alias Regge-Wheeler coordinate is standard elsewhere, not used in GA. The same formula applies for r < 2M through the absolute value, but that branch is not discussed.

**Where:** GA §25.2 p.265 (pdf 282); GA §25.2 p.266 (pdf 283)

### logarithmic divergence of coordinate time at the horizon

*phenomenon · developed* · also: infinite coordinate time to reach r_S

Integrals of dr/(1 - 2M/r) contain 2M ln|r - 2M|, so the Schwarzschild time for any radial approach to r = 2M, by light or matter, grows without bound even though the approach is finite in proper time.

**How introduced:** Pointed out as the common thread behind the plunge result, the photon travel time and the need for r*.

**Prerequisites:** closing of light cones near the horizon

$$
\Delta t=\int\frac{dr}{1-2M/r}=r+2M\ln|r-2M|+\text{const}
$$

**Where:** GA §25.2 p.265 (pdf 282); GA §25.3 p.266 (pdf 283); GA §25.3 p.267 (pdf 284)

### conformally flat form of the radial Schwarzschild metric

*identity · developed* · also: tortoise form of the metric

In coordinates (t, r*) the Schwarzschild metric reads (1 - 2M/r)(-dt^2 + dr*^2) + r^2 dOmega^2, so the radial two-dimensional part is a positive multiple of flat 1+1 Minkowski space and radial light rays are 45-degree lines.

**How introduced:** Obtained by substituting dr = (1 - 2M/r) dr* into the metric after noting light rays are t = +/- r* + const.

**Prerequisites:** tortoise coordinate

$$
ds^2=\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\left[-dt^2+(dr^*)^2\right]+r^2d\Omega^2
$$

**Notes:** Conformal factor still vanishes at r = 2M; the horizon has moved to r* = -infinity rather than becoming regular.

**Where:** GA §25.2 p.266 (pdf 283)

### radial null geodesics in Schwarzschild spacetime

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: radial light rays

Radial light rays satisfy t = +/- r* + const; the outgoing ray from r1 to r2 (both above r_S) takes coordinate time (r2 - r1) + r_S ln[(r2 - r_S)/(r1 - r_S)].

**How introduced:** First as the light-cone edges of §25.1, then written with r*, then integrated explicitly for the astronaut's torch pulses.

**Prerequisites:** radial light cones in Schwarzschild coordinates, tortoise coordinate

$$
t=\pm r^*+\text{const}
$$

$$
t_2-t_1=(r_2-r_1)+r_S\ln\frac{r_2-r_S}{r_1-r_S}

**Where:** GA §25.2 p.266 (pdf 283); GA §25.3 p.267 (pdf 284)

### radial plunge

*solution-or-model · revisited* · also: radial infall from rest at infinity

Free radial fall of a massive particle released from rest at infinity (E-tilde = 1), for which dr/dtau = -(2M/r)^(1/2), giving tau(r) proportional to r^(3/2) and a Schwarzschild time t(r) with a logarithmic divergence at r = 2M.

### proper time versus coordinate time at the horizon

*phenomenon · core* · also: finite proper time, infinite coordinate time

A radially plunging observer's proper time to reach r_S and then r = 0 is finite (4M/3 from horizon to singularity for fall from rest at infinity), while the Schwarzschild time, which equals the proper time of static observers far away, diverges as she approaches r_S.

**How introduced:** Shown with two side-by-side plots of the same fall (Figs. 25.5 and 25.6) and restated in the chapter summary.

**Prerequisites:** radial plunge, logarithmic divergence of coordinate time at the horizon

### tidal stretching and compression

*phenomenon · developed* · also: spaghettification, tidal forces near a black hole

Relative acceleration of neighbouring freely falling particles caused by spacetime curvature: near a Schwarzschild mass, separations along the radial direction grow at rate 2M/r^3 and transverse ones shrink at M/r^3, so extended bodies are stretched radially and squeezed sideways.

**How introduced:** Example 25.3 asks what the astronaut feels and reuses the orthonormal-frame deviation equations from Chapter 11; the verdict is nothing special at r_S and fatal, unbounded stretching approaching r = 0.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic deviation, Riemann tensor in the static orthonormal frame

$$
\frac{d^2\chi^{\hat r}}{d\tau^2}=\frac{2M}{r^3}\chi^{\hat r},\quad \frac{d^2\chi^{\hat\theta}}{d\tau^2}=-\frac{M}{r^3}\chi^{\hat\theta}
$$

**Notes:** The formula uses static-frame components; for a radial boost these particular components are unchanged, which is why they apply to the falling astronaut, a point the book does not spell out.

**Where:** GA §25.3 p.266 (pdf 283); GA §25.3 p.267 (pdf 284); GA p.270 (pdf 287)

### geodesic deviation

*law · revisited* · also: equation of geodesic deviation

The separation vector chi between nearby geodesics obeys d^2 chi^i/dtau^2 = -R^i_{tau j tau} chi^j in an orthonormal frame, so the Riemann tensor directly sets relative (tidal) accelerations.

**How introduced:** Quoted in a margin note as the engine behind Example 25.3.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann tensor in the static orthonormal frame

$$
\frac{d^2\chi^{\hat i}}{d\tau^2}=-R^{\hat i}{}_{\hat\tau\hat j\hat\tau}\chi^{\hat j}

**Where:** GA §25.3 p.266 (pdf 283)

### Riemann tensor in the static orthonormal frame

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: orthonormal Riemann components of Schwarzschild

In the orthonormal frame of Schwarzschild the non-zero curvature components are proportional to M/r^3, with magnitudes 2M/r^3 for the (tau r) and (theta phi) planes and M/r^3 for the mixed planes; none is singular at r = 2M.

**How introduced:** Listed in a margin note to back the claims that curvature is smooth at the horizon and that tidal accelerations follow the pattern of eqn 25.12.

$$
R_{\hat\tau\hat r\hat\tau\hat r}=-\frac{2M}{r^3},\ R_{\hat\theta\hat\phi\hat\theta\hat\phi}=\frac{2M}{r^3},\ R_{\hat\tau\hat\theta\hat\tau\hat\theta}=\frac{M}{r^3},\ R_{\hat r\hat\theta\hat r\hat\theta}=-\frac{M}{r^3}

**Where:** GA §25.3 p.266 (pdf 283)

## Key equations

### (25.1) Schwarzschild line element · central · GA p.262 (pdf 279)

$$
ds^2=-\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)dt^2+\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}dr^2+r^2\left(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)
$$

Geometry of empty space around a spherical mass, used here all the way down to r = 0; g_tt vanishes and g_rr diverges at r = 2M.

**Symbols:** M mass (geometrized); t, r, theta, phi Schwarzschild coordinates

### (25.2) Schwarzschild radius in SI units · central · GA p.262 (pdf 279)

$$
r_S=\frac{2GM}{c^2}\approx\frac{3M}{M_\odot}\,\mathrm{km}
$$

Size scale of the horizon: about 3 km per solar mass.

**Symbols:** G Newton's constant; c speed of light; M_sun about 2e30 kg

### (25.3) Radial light-cone slope · central · GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281)

$$
\frac{dt}{dr}=\pm\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}
$$

Edges of radial light cones in the (r, t) plane; slope 1 far away, vertical at r = 2M, so cones close up approaching the horizon.

### (25.4) Metric just inside the horizon · central · GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281)

$$
ds^2=\frac{\varepsilon}{2M-\varepsilon}dt^2-\frac{2M-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}d\varepsilon^2+(2M-\varepsilon)^2d\Omega^2
$$

Schwarzschild metric with r = 2M - epsilon, epsilon > 0: the dt^2 term is now positive (spacelike) and the depsilon^2 term negative (timelike).

**Symbols:** epsilon = 2M - r

### Interval at fixed t, theta, phi inside the horizon · derivation-step · GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281)

$$
ds^2=-\frac{2M-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\,d\varepsilon^2<0
$$

A pure change in r inside the horizon is timelike, so r acts as time and must change monotonically.

### (25.5) Coordinate-time integral along radial light · derivation-step · GA §25.2 p.265 (pdf 282)

$$
\Delta t=\int\frac{dr}{1-\frac{2M}{r}}
$$

Generic integral whose logarithmic piece causes divergent coordinate times at the horizon.

### (25.6) Definition of the tortoise coordinate · central · GA §25.2 p.265 (pdf 282)

$$
\frac{dr^*}{dr}=\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}
$$

New radial coordinate chosen so that radial light moves at unit coordinate speed.

**Symbols:** r* tortoise coordinate

### (25.7) Tortoise coordinate · central · GA §25.2 p.265 (pdf 282)

$$
r^*=r+2M\ln\left|\frac{r}{2M}-1\right|+\text{const}
$$

r* agrees with r at large radius and runs to minus infinity as r approaches 2M from outside (Fig. 25.4).

### (25.8) Radial light rays in tortoise form · supporting · GA §25.2 p.266 (pdf 283)

$$
t=\pm r^*+\text{const}
$$

Outgoing and ingoing radial null curves are straight 45-degree lines in (r*, t).

### (25.9) Schwarzschild metric in (t, r*) · central · GA §25.2 p.266 (pdf 283)

$$
ds^2=\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\left[-dt^2+(dr^*)^2\right]+r^2d\Omega^2
$$

Radial part is conformally flat, so light cones keep their 45-degree shape; the horizon is at r* = -infinity.

**Symbols:** r = r(r*) implicitly

### (25.10) Proper time of the radial plunge · supporting · GA §25.3 p.266 (pdf 283)

$$
\tau=\tau_*-\frac{2}{3}\frac{r^{3/2}}{r_S^{1/2}}
$$

Proper time along a fall from rest at infinity is a smooth function of r, finite at r_S and at r = 0. NOTE: the book prints a plus sign before the 2/3 term; the minus sign is required for tau to increase as r decreases and agrees with eqn 22.33 and Fig. 25.5.

**Symbols:** tau_* proper time at which r = 0 is reached

### (25.11) Schwarzschild time of the radial plunge · central · GA §25.3 p.266 (pdf 283)

$$
t=t_*+r_S\left[-\frac23\left(\frac{r}{r_S}\right)^{3/2}-2\left(\frac{r}{r_S}\right)^{1/2}+\ln\left|\frac{(r/r_S)^{1/2}+1}{(r/r_S)^{1/2}-1}\right|\right]
$$

Distant observers' time for the same fall; the logarithm diverges as r approaches r_S (Fig. 25.6).

**Symbols:** t_* integration constant

### Geodesic deviation in the orthonormal frame (margin note 11) · supporting · GA §25.3 p.266 (pdf 283)

$$
\frac{d^2\chi^{\hat i}}{d\tau^2}=-R^{\hat i}{}_{\hat\tau\hat j\hat\tau}\,\chi^{\hat j}

Relative acceleration of neighbouring free particles set by curvature.

**Symbols:** chi separation vector; hats denote orthonormal components

### Schwarzschild orthonormal Riemann components (margin note 11) · supporting · GA §25.3 p.266 (pdf 283)

$$
R_{\hat\tau\hat r\hat\tau\hat r}=-\frac{2M}{r^3},\quad R_{\hat\theta\hat\phi\hat\theta\hat\phi}=\frac{2M}{r^3},\quad R_{\hat\tau\hat\theta\hat\tau\hat\theta}=R_{\hat\tau\hat\phi\hat\tau\hat\phi}=\frac{M}{r^3},\quad R_{\hat r\hat\theta\hat r\hat\theta}=R_{\hat r\hat\phi\hat r\hat\phi}=-\frac{M}{r^3}

### (25.12) Tidal accelerations on a falling astronaut · central · GA §25.3 p.266 (pdf 283)

$$
\frac{d^2\chi^{\hat r}}{d\tau^2}=\frac{2M}{r^3}\chi^{\hat r},\quad\frac{d^2\chi^{\hat\theta}}{d\tau^2}=-\frac{M}{r^3}\chi^{\hat\theta},\quad\frac{d^2\chi^{\hat\phi}}{d\tau^2}=-\frac{M}{r^3}\chi^{\hat\phi}
$$

Radial stretching and transverse squeezing with strength M/r^3: unremarkable at r_S, unbounded as r goes to 0.

### (25.13) Null condition for radial light · derivation-step · GA §25.3 p.267 (pdf 284)

$$
0=-\left(1-\frac{r_S}{r}\right)dt^2+\frac{dr^2}{1-\frac{r_S}{r}}
$$

Starting point for the travel time of the astronaut's radial light pulses.

### (25.14) · derivation-step · GA §25.3 p.267 (pdf 284)

$$
\frac{dt}{dr}=\pm\frac{1}{1-\frac{r_S}{r}}
$$

Plus sign chosen for outgoing photons.

### (25.15) Coordinate travel time of an outgoing radial photon · central · GA §25.3 p.267 (pdf 284)

$$
t_2-t_1=\int_{r_1}^{r_2}\frac{dr}{1-\frac{r_S}{r}}=(r_2-r_1)+r_S\ln\left(\frac{r_2-r_S}{r_1-r_S}\right)
$$

Flat-space delay plus a logarithmic correction that diverges as the emission radius approaches r_S: pulses reach distant observers ever more slowly (Fig. 25.7).

**Symbols:** (t1, r1) emission event; (t2, r2) reception event; r1, r2 > r_S

### (25.16) Critical photon angle · central · GA §25.4 p.268 (pdf 285)

$$
\sin\psi_{\rm c}=\frac{\sqrt{27}M}{r}\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{1/2}
$$

Boundary between captured and escaping (or, by time reversal, visible and dark) directions for a static observer; zero at r = 2M, 90 degrees at r = 3M.

**Symbols:** psi_c angle from the radial direction measured in the static observer's frame

### (25.17) Energy measured by a static observer · derivation-step · GA §25.4 p.268 (pdf 285)

$$
E_{\rm obs}=p^{\hat t}=(\boldsymbol e_t)^{\hat t}p^t,\qquad(\boldsymbol e_t)^{\hat t}=\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{1/2}
$$

Project the photon momentum onto the static orthonormal frame.

### Conservation of p_t for infalling light (margin note 15) · derivation-step · GA §25.4 p.268 (pdf 285)

$$
p_t=\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)p^t=\text{const}\ \Rightarrow\ p^t(r)=\hbar\omega_\infty\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}
$$

Time-translation symmetry fixes how the coordinate energy component grows inward. (The book drops the sign of g_tt here; magnitudes are what matter.)

**Symbols:** omega_inf angular frequency at infinity

### (25.18) Blueshift of incoming starlight · central · GA §25.4 p.268 (pdf 285)

$$
E_{\rm obs}=\hbar\omega_\infty\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1/2}
$$

Static observers deep in the potential see starlight shifted to higher energy; the inverse of the gravitational redshift eqn 22.16.

### (25.19) Schwarzschild metric with v^2 = 2M/r (Exercise 25.1) · supporting · GA p.270 (pdf 287)

$$
ds^2=-(1-v^2)dt^2+(1-v^2)^{-1}dr^2+r^2d\Omega^2,\quad v^2=\frac{2M}{r}
$$

Rewriting that makes v the Newtonian escape (and plunge) speed.

**Symbols:** dOmega^2 = dtheta^2 + sin^2 theta dphi^2 (the exercise omits the square on dphi)

### (25.20) Painleve-Gullstrand metric (Exercise 25.1) · supporting · GA p.270 (pdf 287)

$$
ds^2=-dT^2+\left[dr+\left(\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{1/2}dT\right]^2+r^2d\Omega^2
$$

Result of dt = dT - R(r) dr with R = v/(1 - v^2); regular at r = 2M and flat on constant-T slices.

**Symbols:** T rain time

### (25.21) · derivation-step · GA p.271 (pdf 288)

$$
d\tilde t=\left(\frac{\partial\tilde t}{\partial t}\right)dt+\left(\frac{\partial\tilde t}{\partial r}\right)dr
$$

Rain time as a function of Schwarzschild t and r.

**Symbols:** t-tilde time read on dropped clocks

### (25.22) · derivation-step · GA p.271 (pdf 288)

$$
\frac{\partial\tilde t}{\partial t}=1
$$

Clocks dropped at a steady rate make rain time advance with t at fixed r.

### (25.23) Radial plunge proper speed · supporting · GA p.271 (pdf 288)

$$
\frac{dr}{d\tau}=-\left(\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{1/2}
$$

Rain clocks fall at the Newtonian escape speed in proper time.

### (25.24) Radial plunge coordinate speed · supporting · GA p.271 (pdf 288)

$$
\frac{dr}{dt}=-\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\left(\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{1/2}
$$

Coordinate speed goes to zero at the horizon.

### (25.25) · derivation-step · GA p.271 (pdf 288)

$$
dt_{\rm d}=\frac{dr}{(1-2M/r)(2M/r)^{1/2}}
$$

Drop-time interval between neighbouring clocks separated by dr at equal t.

**Symbols:** t_d drop time

### (25.26) Proper fall time between two radii · supporting · GA p.271 (pdf 288)

$$
\Delta\tau=\frac23\frac{r_A^{3/2}-r_B^{3/2}}{(2M)^{1/2}}
$$

Finite proper time from r_A to r_B for fall from rest at infinity.

### (25.27) · derivation-step · GA p.271 (pdf 288)

$$
\frac{\partial\tilde t}{\partial r}=\left(\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{1/2}\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}
$$

Radial dependence of rain time.

### (25.28) Global rain metric (Exercise 25.3) · supporting · GA p.271 (pdf 288)

$$
ds^2=-\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)d\tilde t^2+2\left(\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{1/2}d\tilde t\,dr+dr^2+r^2\left(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)
$$

Expanded Painleve-Gullstrand form; no component is singular at r = 2M, and its null directions show inward motion inside the horizon.

## Worked examples

### Example 25.1 · intro · GA p.263 (pdf 280)

**Problem:** Summarize the observational case that black holes exist even though they emit no light.

**Method:** Qualitative survey organised by the kind of evidence: stellar dynamics at the Galactic Centre, dynamical masses of X-ray-emitting binaries, compact energetic galactic nuclei, and gravitational-wave signals.

**Key insight:** All the evidence is indirect: a mass too large and too compact for any known stellar remnant, betrayed by its effect on stars, gas or spacetime ripples.

**Result:** Sgr A* about 4e6 solar masses; Cygnus X-1 about 15 solar masses in a source smaller than a few hundred km; quasar ULAS J1342+0928 about 8e8 solar masses.

**Concepts:** supermassive black hole, X-ray binary, accretion disc, quasar, Event Horizon Telescope image

### Example 25.2 · intro · GA §25.2 p.265 (pdf 282)

**Problem:** Give an intuition for why r* is called the tortoise coordinate.

**Method:** Cast the approach to the horizon as Zeno's race: Achilles' gap to the tortoise is r - r_S, and r* serves as a race clock that runs downward; use dr/dr* = 1 - 2M/r to show the closing speed dies away.

**Key insight:** Arbitrarily large decreases in r* buy ever smaller progress in r, so the horizon corresponds to r* = -infinity and is never reached in this label.

**Result:** r approaches 2M only asymptotically as r* goes to minus infinity.

**Concepts:** tortoise coordinate, coordinates have no intrinsic metric meaning

### Example 25.3 · standard · GA §25.3 p.266 (pdf 283)

**Problem:** What does an astronaut falling radially into the hole physically feel?

**Method:** Take the geodesic deviation equation in the orthonormal frame with the Schwarzschild Riemann components (from Chapter 11) to get relative accelerations along r-hat, theta-hat and phi-hat.

**Key insight:** The tidal field depends on M/r^3 only, so it is finite at r_S; it stretches radially and compresses sideways and grows without bound as r goes to 0, where the true singularity lies.

**Result:** d^2chi^r/dtau^2 = (2M/r^3) chi^r; transverse components get -(M/r^3) chi.

**Concepts:** tidal stretching and compression, geodesic deviation, Riemann tensor in the static orthonormal frame, curvature singularity

### Example 25.4 · intro · GA §25.4 p.268 (pdf 285)

**Problem:** How much of the sky does the black hole block for an observer at r = 3M, and what happens closer in?

**Method:** Evaluate the critical angle formula at r = 3M and note its trend as r decreases toward 2M.

**Key insight:** At the photon-orbit radius the critical angle is a right angle, so the dark region is exactly a hemisphere; deeper in, the visible universe contracts to a narrowing cone.

**Result:** sin psi_c = 1, psi_c = pi/2: black hole fills half the sky; tangential light returns to the emitter.

**Concepts:** critical angle for photon capture, photon sphere, apparent angular size of a black hole

### Example · intro · GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281)

**Problem:** Show that inside r = 2M the radial coordinate is timelike.

**Method:** Substitute r = 2M - epsilon into the Schwarzschild metric, then set dt = dtheta = dphi = 0 and inspect the sign of ds^2.

**Key insight:** The metric coefficients of dt^2 and dr^2 swap sign at r = 2M, so the coordinate that must advance monotonically along any worldline inside is r, not t.

**Result:** ds^2 = -(2M - epsilon) depsilon^2/epsilon < 0 at fixed t: epsilon must increase, r must decrease.

**Concepts:** exchange of timelike and spacelike roles of t and r inside the horizon, inevitability of reaching r = 0 inside the horizon

### Example · standard · GA §25.2 p.265 (pdf 282)

**Problem:** Integrate the tortoise coordinate and rewrite the metric in terms of it.

**Method:** Split 1/(1 - 2M/r) = 1 + 2M/(r - 2M) and integrate; then replace dr by (1 - 2M/r) dr* in the metric.

**Key insight:** Radial null lines become t = +/- r* + const and the (t, r*) metric is a conformal factor times flat space, but the factor vanishes where r* goes to minus infinity.

**Result:** r* = r + 2M ln|r/2M - 1| + const; ds^2 = (1 - 2M/r)(-dt^2 + dr*^2) + r^2 dOmega^2.

**Concepts:** tortoise coordinate, conformally flat form of the radial Schwarzschild metric

### Example · standard · GA §25.3 p.267 (pdf 284)

**Problem:** How long, in Schwarzschild time, does an outgoing radial light pulse take from r1 to r2?

**Method:** Take the plus root of the radial null condition and integrate dr/(1 - r_S/r) between the two radii.

**Key insight:** The answer is the flat-space distance plus r_S times a logarithm; as the emission point approaches r_S the logarithm, and so the delay, diverges.

**Result:** t2 - t1 = (r2 - r1) + r_S ln[(r2 - r_S)/(r1 - r_S)].

**Concepts:** radial null geodesics in Schwarzschild spacetime, apparent freezing and redshifting of infalling sources

### Example · standard · GA §25.4 p.268 (pdf 285)

**Problem:** Find the energy of starlight measured by a static observer at radius r.

**Method:** Use conservation of p_t to get p^t(r) from the frequency at infinity, then project with the static vielbein component (e_t)^t-hat = (1 - 2M/r)^(1/2).

**Key insight:** Two factors of (1 - 2M/r) combine to leave a net inverse square root, the redshift law run backwards.

**Result:** E_obs = hbar omega_inf (1 - 2M/r)^(-1/2), diverging at the horizon.

**Concepts:** gravitational blueshift of infalling light, conserved Killing energy, measured energy from the orthonormal frame


## Analogies and intuitions

### The horizon of the Earth beyond which ships vanish from view → event horizon · weak · GA §25.1 p.265 (pdf 282)

Supplies the name: just as we cannot see past the Earth's curvature, no event inside r = 2M can be seen from outside.

**Where it breaks down:** The Earth's horizon depends on where the observer stands and can be crossed and seen past by moving; the black-hole horizon is a null surface fixed by the spacetime, and once inside, no signal can ever get out.

**App idea:** Side-by-side: a ship sinking below a horizon that rises back into view when the observer climbs a mast, versus a photon inside r = 2M that stays hidden whatever the observer does.

### Achilles chasing the tortoise in Zeno's paradox → tortoise coordinate · useful · GA §25.2 p.265 (pdf 282)

Treats r - r_S as the gap Achilles must close and r* as a clock that can be run down forever; the gap shrinks ever more slowly, so the finish is never reached in r*.

**Where it breaks down:** In Zeno the paradox is resolved because the infinite steps take finite time; likewise the horizon is reached in finite proper time. The analogy's 'never reaches' is only true of the r* label, which the book says but a learner may miss.

**App idea:** Race animation with two clocks: the r* clock ticks forever while a proper-time clock for an infalling runner reaches the line in a finite count.

### Being stretched like spaghetti → tidal stretching and compression · strong · GA §25.3 p.267 (pdf 284)

Gives a vivid image for radial elongation and sideways squeezing from eqn 25.12.

**Where it breaks down:** Only describes the pattern; how early it becomes lethal depends strongly on mass (fatal outside the horizon for stellar holes, negligible at the horizon of supermassive ones).

**App idea:** A deformable astronaut figure that stretches and squeezes according to 2M/r^3 and M/r^3 times body size, with a mass slider showing where stretching exceeds bodily tolerance relative to r_S.

### The future is inwards → exchange of timelike and spacelike roles of t and r inside the horizon · strong · GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281)

Condenses the sign-flip calculation into a slogan: inside the horizon, decreasing r is as unavoidable as tomorrow.

**Where it breaks down:** Can suggest that r = 0 is a point in space one is pulled toward; it is better thought of as a moment in the future, as the spacelike singularity on the Penrose diagram shows.

**App idea:** Calendar metaphor: inside the horizon, a slider labelled 'r' can only be moved one way, just as a date slider only advances.

### After crossing r = 2M the star is effectively cut off, as though it had left for a separate universe → event horizon · useful · GA §25.5 p.269 (pdf 286)

Emphasizes that after crossing the horizon the stellar matter can no longer influence the outside, which only feels the horizon and exterior geometry.

**Where it breaks down:** The interior is not a separate universe: its mass and (for other holes) charge and spin remain imprinted on the exterior, and outside matter can still fall in.

### Michell and Laplace's dark star whose escape speed exceeds light speed → black hole · useful · GA p.262 (pdf 279)

Historical margin note locating the idea before general relativity.

**Where it breaks down:** Newtonian light could climb above the surface and fall back and could be seen by nearby observers; in GR nothing inside the horizon even moves outward, and the radius coincidence with 2GM/c^2 hides different physics.

**App idea:** Compare the Newtonian escape-speed radius with the GR horizon: launch a light pulse outward from just inside each and watch the Newtonian one rise and fall while the GR one moves inward.

### A variable called t has no more claim to be time than one called u or chi → coordinates have no intrinsic metric meaning · strong · GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281)

Defuses the shock that t becomes spacelike inside the horizon: names of coordinates do not determine their causal character; the metric does.

**Where it breaks down:** Not an analogy so much as a principle; learners still need the sign test on metric coefficients to apply it.


## Misconceptions addressed

### Something dramatic, such as an infinite force or a wall, happens to a traveller at r = 2M because the metric blows up there. · GA p.263 (pdf 280)

**Correction:** Curvature components and tidal accelerations are finite and smooth at r = 2M and the traveller's clock ticks normally; the divergence belongs to the Schwarzschild coordinates.

**Why tempting:** g_rr is literally infinite in the formula and coordinate time to reach r_S is infinite.

### A black hole sucks in everything around it like a cosmic vacuum cleaner. · GA p.263 (pdf 280)

**Correction:** Outside the horizon its gravity is exactly that of any spherical star of the same mass; orbits are stable and a rocket can always leave as long as it never crosses r_S.

**Why tempting:** Popular accounts stress that nothing escapes, which is only true inside the horizon.

### Since distant observers never see the astronaut cross, she never actually crosses the horizon. · GA §25.3 p.266 (pdf 283)

**Correction:** Her proper time to cross and to reach r = 0 is finite; the apparent freezing is due to signals from near r_S taking unboundedly long in Schwarzschild time to climb out.

**Why tempting:** Coordinate time is the distant observer's real clock, so its divergence feels like a physical statement.

### The coordinate named t always measures time. · GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281)

**Correction:** Inside the horizon t is a spacelike coordinate and r is timelike; only the sign of the metric along a displacement decides its character.

**Why tempting:** Outside, and in flat spacetime, t really is time for static observers.

### With a powerful enough rocket, an astronaut just inside the horizon could hover or climb back out. · GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281)

**Correction:** Inside, every future-pointing light cone points toward smaller r; even light fired outward moves inward, and no worldline can stay at constant r.

**Why tempting:** Outside the horizon, thrust can counter gravity at any radius, and the horizon looks like an ordinary sphere.

### The singularity r = 0 is a point at the centre of space that a clever trajectory could steer around. · GA §25.5 p.269 (pdf 286)

**Correction:** Inside the horizon r = 0 is a spacelike surface lying in every infalling observer's future, like a final moment of time; the Penrose diagram draws it as a horizontal line.

**Why tempting:** In the exterior r = 0 would be the spatial centre, and the phrase 'infinitely dense point at the origin' reinforces that picture.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Distant astronomers could eventually watch a star collapse through its horizon or see the singularity. · GA §25.5 p.269 (pdf 286)

**Correction:** The star's image slows, reddens and fades as its surface nears r_S; light from inside never gets out, so the singularity stays hidden.

**Why tempting:** Given enough patience, one expects to see every stage of a process.

### The tortoise coordinate removes the horizon problem. · GA §25.2 p.266 (pdf 283)

**Correction:** It keeps radial light rays at 45 degrees and the metric finite, but it moves r = 2M to r* = -infinity, so the horizon and interior are still unreachable in the chart.

**Why tempting:** The metric in (t, r*) looks as clean as flat space.

### The Event Horizon Telescope photographed the black hole itself or its event horizon. · GA p.263 (pdf 280)

**Correction:** The image shows radio emission from hot gas around the hole, with a central brightness deficit (the shadow) whose size is set by photon capture near r = 3M, larger than the horizon.

**Why tempting:** The caption calls it an image of the black hole and the telescope is named after the horizon.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The half-sky view at 3M and the final chink of blue light describe what a freely falling astronaut sees. · GA §25.4 p.268 (pdf 285)

**Correction:** Those results use static-observer frames. A falling observer moving inward suffers aberration and Doppler shift, so the hole looks smaller and starlight is less blueshifted than for a hovering observer at the same r.

**Why tempting:** The section is narrated as the plunging astronaut's view, while a margin note quietly says the vielbein is that of a stationary observer.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Astronaut with a torch falling into a black hole · GA §25.3 p.266 (pdf 283)

**Setup:** An astronaut falls radially from rest far away and flashes a light at regular intervals of her own time toward distant observers.

**Lesson:** She crosses the horizon and meets the singularity in finite proper time, feeling growing tidal forces but nothing at r_S; outside observers receive ever more widely spaced, dimmer, redder pulses and never see the crossing.

**App idea:** Split-screen: her cockpit view with a steady clock and stretching body on one side, the distant telescope feed with slowing, reddening flashes on the other.

### Photon fired outward from inside the horizon · GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281)

**Setup:** An astronaut already inside r = 2M shines a light in the outward radial direction.

**Lesson:** Because the cone is tipped over, the outward-aimed photon still moves to smaller r; nothing inside can escape or be seen.

**App idea:** Photon launcher on an EF diagram: aim left or right from any event and see whether r ever increases.

### Achilles and the tortoise race timed by r* · GA §25.2 p.265 (pdf 282)

**Setup:** Achilles' distance from the tortoise is r - r_S and the race clock reads r*, run downward without limit.

**Lesson:** In the r* label the gap closes ever more slowly and the finish is at r* = -infinity, which is why the horizon is inaccessible in tortoise coordinates.

**App idea:** Animated runners with an r* counter and a proper-time counter to show both views.

### Seeing the back of your head at r = 3M · GA §25.4 p.268 (pdf 285)

**Setup:** An observer at the photon-orbit radius emits light exactly tangentially.

**Lesson:** The light circles the hole and returns, so the observer would see herself from behind; the same radius marks where the hole fills half the sky.

**App idea:** Ray-traced first-person view for a static observer with a radius slider, showing the dark region grow and a ring image of the observer at 3M.

### Watching a star collapse · GA §25.5 p.269 (pdf 286)

**Setup:** Distant astronomers monitor the surface of a massive star whose core collapses through r = 2M.

**Lesson:** The image slows, reddens and blackens as the surface nears r_S; afterwards only the horizon and exterior geometry matter to outsiders, and the singularity stays hidden.

**App idea:** Light curve and colour of a collapsing star's surface versus distant-observer time, computed from outgoing null rays, with the curve approaching zero exponentially.


## Applications and observations

- **Schwarzschild radius scale** (numerical-estimate, GA p.262 (pdf 279)): Restoring G and c gives the horizon size for a given mass. Key numbers: r_S about 3 km per solar mass; M_sun about 2e30 kg.
- **Sagittarius A*** (astrophysical-system, GA p.263 (pdf 280)): Compact radio source at the Galactic Centre, weighed by the orbits of nearby stars and imaged by the EHT. Our own estimate: r_S about 1.2e7 km (0.08 AU), and the capture shadow diameter of about 5.2 r_S is roughly 0.4 AU, consistent in scale with the 0.3 AU region quoted. Key numbers: Mass about 4e6 solar masses within a region about 0.3 AU across (book); r_S about 0.08 AU (our estimate).
- **Cygnus X-1** (astrophysical-system, GA p.263 (pdf 280)): X-ray binary with the blue supergiant HDE 226868; companion motion fixes the unseen mass, too large for a neutron star; X-rays come from a hot accretion disc. Key numbers: Book: about 15 solar masses, source diameter below several hundred km. Later (2021) analyses revised the mass to about 21 solar masses.
- **Quasars and ULAS J1342+0928** (observation, GA p.263 (pdf 280)): Extremely luminous accreting supermassive black holes at galactic centres, with high redshifts and jets. Key numbers: ULAS J1342+0928 black hole about 8e8 solar masses (book); its 'most distant' status has since been overtaken.
- **Gravitational waves from black-hole mergers** (detection-event, GA p.263 (pdf 280)): Listed as the fourth line of evidence, deferred to Chapter 46.
- **Stellar remnant mass thresholds** (numerical-estimate, GA §25.5 p.268 (pdf 285)): White-dwarf limit, heaviest known neutron star and lightest black holes bracket the collapse outcomes. Key numbers: Chandrasekhar limit about 1.4 solar masses; heaviest neutron star about 2.4; lightest black hole about 4 (book values, evolving with new data).
- **Tidal stretching at the horizon versus mass (our own estimate)** (numerical-estimate, GA §25.3 p.267 (pdf 284)): Radial tidal acceleration gradient 2GM/r^3 evaluated at r = 2GM/c^2 equals c^6/(4G^2M^2), so heavier holes are gentler at the horizon. Key numbers: Our estimate: about 1e8 s^-2 for 10 solar masses (lethal for a 2 m body well outside r_S) versus about 6e-4 s^-2 for 4e6 solar masses (unnoticeable at the horizon).
- **Proper time from horizon to singularity (our own estimate)** (numerical-estimate, GA §25.3 p.266 (pdf 283)): For a fall from rest at infinity, eqn 22.33 between r = 2M and 0 gives 4M/3, that is (4/3)GM/c^3. Key numbers: Our estimate: about 6.6 microseconds per solar mass, so about 66 microseconds for 10 solar masses and about 26 s for Sgr A*.

## Historical notes

- **John Michell, Pierre-Simon Laplace (late 18th century):** Proposed dark stars so compact that the escape speed from their surface would exceed the speed of light. — Pre-relativistic precursor of the black-hole idea, with a radius numerically matching 2GM/c^2. (GA p.262 (pdf 279))
- **J. Robert Oppenheimer, Hartland Snyder (1939):** Published the key calculation of continued gravitational collapse of a massive star, following late-1930s work on neutron-star collapse. — First GR demonstration that collapse can produce what is now called a black hole. (GA p.262 (pdf 279))
- **John Wheeler (1960s):** Popularized the name 'black hole'. — Marks the period when black holes became mainstream physics. (GA p.262 (pdf 279))
- **Albert Einstein, Jacques Hadamard:** Einstein jokingly called the Schwarzschild radius singularity the 'Hadamard catastrophe', after Hadamard suggested it might be physically real. — Shows early uncertainty about whether r = 2M is physical, the question this chapter and the next resolve. (GA p.262 (pdf 279))
- **Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar:** Computed that white dwarfs above about 1.4 solar masses cannot support themselves against collapse. — Sets the threshold above which stellar remnants become neutron stars or black holes. (GA §25.5 p.268 (pdf 285))
- **Paul Painleve, Allvar Gullstrand, Georges Lemaitre (1922 (as dated by the book); 1933):** Painlevé (a mathematician who was twice French Prime Minister) and Gullstrand (1911 Nobel laureate in physiology or medicine) independently wrote down a spherically symmetric line element regular at r = 2M and took it as a sign that GR admitted too many solutions; Lemaître showed in 1933 that it is just Schwarzschild in new coordinates. The exercise note adds that Gullstrand, doubting relativity, stood in the way of a Nobel Prize for Einstein's relativity work. — An early concrete case of mistaking a coordinate change for new physics, and the historical root of the 'rain' coordinates built in the exercises. Painlevé's paper is usually dated 1921, and Einstein's 1921 prize cited the photoelectric effect, so the book's compressed anecdote should be told with care. (GA p.270 (pdf 287))
- **Event Horizon Telescope Collaboration (2022):** Released the radio image of the shadow of Sagittarius A*, obtained by linking radio observatories across Earth. — Direct imaging of the environment of the Milky Way's central black hole. (GA p.263 (pdf 280))

## Notation and conventions

- **Geometrized units:** G = c = 1, so M denotes GM/c^2 (a length) and r_S = 2M; eqn 25.2 restores units. (GA p.262 (pdf 279))
- **Metric signature:** (-, +, +, +): timelike intervals have ds^2 < 0. — Essential for the sign test inside the horizon. (GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281))
- **r_S:** Schwarzschild radius 2M, used interchangeably with 2M; section 25.3 writes formulas in r_S. (GA p.262 (pdf 279))
- **dOmega^2:** Shorthand for dtheta^2 + sin^2 theta dphi^2. — Exercise 25.1 misprints the last term without the square. (GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281))
- **epsilon:** epsilon = 2M - r, a positive coordinate inside the horizon. (GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281))
- **r*:** Tortoise coordinate with dr*/dr = (1 - 2M/r)^-1, additive constant left free. (GA §25.2 p.265 (pdf 282))
- **tau_*, t_*:** Integration constants in the plunge formulas: proper time and coordinate time labels associated with reaching r = 0. — Sign in eqn 25.10 is misprinted. (GA §25.3 p.266 (pdf 283))
- **Hatted indices:** Components in the static observer's orthonormal frame (vielbein), e.g. chi^r-hat, R_tau-hat r-hat tau-hat r-hat, (e_t)^t-hat. — The exported text renders the hats as dots. (GA §25.3 p.266 (pdf 283))
- **psi_c:** Critical photon angle measured from the radial direction in the static frame. (GA §25.4 p.268 (pdf 285))
- **Penrose diagram labels:** i- past timelike infinity, i0 spatial infinity, i+ future timelike infinity, scri-/scri+ past/future null infinity, H event horizon, double or thick horizontal line for the singularity; t', r' compactified coordinates; light at 45 degrees. (GA §25.5 p.269 (pdf 286))
- **Rain coordinates in exercises:** T or t-tilde for the time of clocks dropped from infinity; t_d drop-time interval; E-tilde and L-tilde energy and angular momentum per unit mass; v^2 = 2M/r. — The rendered page prints the rain time with a caron/breve accent over t; the exported text renders it as a tilde. Exercise 25.1 uses T for the same time. (GA p.270 (pdf 287))

## Teaching gems

### Freeze three coordinates and test the sign of ds^2 along the fourth: inside the horizon a step in r alone is timelike. · GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281)

**Why it works:** Replaces a mysterious slogan with a check any learner can do in two lines, and generalizes to every metric they will meet.

**App idea:** 'Causal character tester': learner picks a metric and a point, chooses a coordinate direction, and a gauge shows the sign of g along it as timelike, null or spacelike; dragging the point across r = 2M flips the gauge for t and r simultaneously.

### Plot the same fall against proper time and against coordinate time side by side. · GA §25.3 p.266 (pdf 283)

**Why it works:** The contrast between a curve that sails through r_S and one that runs up an asymptote makes the coordinate origin of the divergence visually obvious.

**App idea:** Linked tau(r) and t(r) plots with a shared scrubber and an animated faller; pulses at equal tau intervals are mapped to arrival times at infinity.

### Let tidal stretching decide whether a singularity is real: smooth M/r^3 at r_S, blow-up at r = 0. · GA §25.3 p.267 (pdf 284)

**Why it works:** Grounds the abstract distinction between coordinate and curvature singularities in something the traveller's body would feel.

**App idea:** Stretch meter for an astronaut of adjustable height falling into holes of adjustable mass, with the horizon and a 'pain threshold' marked on the r axis.

### Drawing a row of light cones that thin out and then fall over as r decreases. · GA §25.1 p.264 (pdf 281)

**Why it works:** A single strip of pictures carries the whole causal story of the chapter: slowing coordinate speed outside, trapping inside.

**App idea:** Draggable cone along the r axis with computed shape, plus a toggle to Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates where the same cones tilt smoothly.

### Read the photon travel time as flat-space delay plus a logarithm, and show the logarithm taking over as emission approaches r_S. · GA §25.3 p.267 (pdf 284)

**Why it works:** Splitting the answer into a familiar piece and a GR correction explains the freezing image quantitatively and reuses the tortoise logarithm.

**App idea:** Stacked-bar delay plot with a draggable emission radius and a real-mass clock readout.

### Turn a photon-escape formula into a view of the sky with time reversal, giving a black hole that fills half the sky at 3M. · GA §25.4 p.268 (pdf 285)

**Why it works:** The setup was planted in Chapter 24, so the payoff costs one evaluation (r = 3M) yet delivers a vivid picture; it also models how a symmetry argument turns an emission result into a reception result.

**App idea:** First-person sky renderer for a static observer (with an optional falling-observer aberration toggle) and a radius slider.

### Naming r* after Zeno's race so that 'approaches but never reaches in this label' is memorable. · GA §25.2 p.265 (pdf 282)

**Why it works:** A cultural hook attaches to exactly the asymptotic property that matters.

**App idea:** Race animation linked to the r*(r) graph with both an r* counter and a proper-time counter.

### Build horizon-regular coordinates from a physical array of clocks dropped from infinity (Exercise 25.2). · GA p.271 (pdf 288)

## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Eqn 25.10 is printed with a plus sign, tau = tau_* + (2/3) r^(3/2)/r_S^(1/2), which would make proper time decrease during infall.** — Learners checking against Fig. 25.5 or eqn 22.33 will find a contradiction and may doubt their understanding. *Suggestion:* Use tau = tau_* - (2/3) r^(3/2)/r_S^(1/2), with tau_* the time of reaching r = 0, and point out the misprint.
- **Section 25.4 narrates the plunging astronaut's view but uses the critical angle and blueshift for static observers; a margin note concedes the vielbein is stationary.** — Learners conclude that a freely falling astronaut sees the sky shrink to a tiny blue dot at the horizon, which is not correct once aberration and Doppler shift are included. *Suggestion:* State explicitly that these results are for an observer hovering at r; if possible show the falling observer's view (aberrated sky, finite shift at the horizon) as a contrast.
- **The critical-angle formula is quoted without saying from which direction psi_c is measured or which of psi_c and pi - psi_c bounds the visible region above and below r = 3M.** — Applying 'angles less than psi_c are visible' at r > 3M gives the wrong answer (the hole would seem to cover most of the sky far away). *Suggestion:* Define psi measured from the inward radial direction for the dark patch: at r > 3M the hole's angular radius is psi_c, at r < 3M it is pi - psi_c; check both limits.
- **Loose or confusing statements about the singularity: a margin note calls r_S a 'shell of singularities' and 'ring-like', and the text says the mass sits at an infinitely dense point.** — 'Ring singularity' is the Kerr term, r_S is not a singularity of spacetime, and picturing r = 0 as a point clashes with the later spacelike-singularity picture. *Suggestion:* Say that the coordinate breakdown happens on the sphere r = 2M, reserve 'ring' for Kerr, and describe r = 0 as a final moment rather than a place.
- **Smoothness of curvature at r = 2M is supported only by frame components in a margin note; no invariant is given.** — Sceptical learners may object that components in a frame that itself becomes singular (static observers cannot exist at r <= 2M) prove little. *Suggestion:* Add the Kretschmann scalar R_abcd R^abcd = 48M^2/r^6, finite at 2M and infinite at 0, and note that the radial-boost invariance of the tidal components carries them to infalling frames.
- **The Fig. 25.3 discussion of null curves 'shooting off to infinite t and returning' is terse, and the interior branches are not explained.** — Readers can't tell which curves are ingoing or outgoing inside r_S or how a single photon's path is split across the two charts. *Suggestion:* Plot t = +/- r* + const explicitly on both sides and then show the same rays in ingoing EF coordinates, where ingoing rays are straight lines through the horizon.
- **Typos and slips: the singularity written as r_S = 0 in §25.5; 'Painleve-Gullstand'; dOmega^2 missing the square on dphi in Exercise 25.1; §25.3 refers to 'the last chapter' for Chapter 22 results and §25.4 to 'the previous chapter' for Chapter 24.** — Minor confusion and hunting for the wrong reference. *Suggestion:* Correct to r = 0, Gullstrand, sin^2 theta dphi^2, and cite chapters by number.
- **Observational numbers are dated: Cygnus X-1's mass has been revised to about 21 solar masses (2021), the most distant quasar record has moved beyond ULAS J1342+0928, the first EHT image (M87*, 2019) is not mentioned, and gravitational-wave events have found compact objects in the claimed 2.4-4 solar-mass gap.** — Learners may quote superseded figures or think the mass gap is firm. *Suggestion:* Keep book values as illustrative but attach dates, and let the app pull current values for mass estimates and records.
- **The stellar-evolution story implies that stars above 1.4 solar masses collapse, conflating the initial stellar mass with the remnant core mass.** — Learners may believe the Sun's heavier siblings (say 3 solar masses) become black holes, whereas most stars below about 8 solar masses shed mass and end as white dwarfs. *Suggestion:* Distinguish initial mass from core or remnant mass and give rough initial-mass ranges for white dwarf, neutron star and black-hole outcomes.
- **Fig. 25.10 is introduced in the text as the conformal structure produced by collapse, yet it draws no stellar matter (that appears only in Fig. 25.11), and its horizon line H begins below the singularity without comment on when a horizon forms.** — Learners may think the horizon appears only when the surface crosses 2M or at the moment of singularity formation. *Suggestion:* Explain the horizon as the boundary of the past of scri+, which starts growing from the centre while the star is still larger than 2M, and use Fig. 25.11 to show this.
- **The opening says clocks 'at' r_S record no time and that signals from there are infinitely redshifted, without noting that no static observer can exist at r = 2M; later (margin note 12) the book warns that the static redshift formula does not apply to a falling emitter.** — Learners may picture a clock parked on the horizon that has stopped, which contradicts the chapter's later message that nothing local happens there. *Suggestion:* Phrase it as a limit: static clocks placed ever closer to 2M tick ever more slowly relative to infinity, and hovering becomes impossible at 2M; a falling clock crosses normally.

## Tutor notes

- Open with a prediction question: 'An astronaut falls toward a black hole flashing a torch every second. What do she and a far-away friend each record?' Collect both answers before any formulas.
- Order: (1) the two bad radii and the claim that one is fake; (2) light-cone slope and closing cones; (3) the sign test inside the horizon; (4) proper time versus coordinate time for the plunge; (5) tidal forces as the real-singularity detector; (6) photon delay and freezing; (7) the view from near the hole; (8) collapse and Penrose diagrams. Tortoise coordinates can be slotted after (6), where the logarithm has just appeared twice.
- Check for understanding: ask the learner to set dt = dtheta = dphi = 0 in the metric at r = M and say whether a small step in r is timelike or spacelike, then ask what that implies for someone at r = M.
- Check: 'If Schwarzschild coordinate time to reach r_S is infinite, why is it legitimate to say the astronaut crosses?' Look for the distinction between a coordinate label and a clock carried by the traveller.
- Common question: 'Does the astronaut see the whole future history of the universe flash by as she crosses?' Answer no: that picture comes from confusing static and falling observers; a free faller receives only a finite amount of outside history before reaching r = 0.
- Common question: 'Is the horizon a physical membrane?' Stress that locally it is unremarkable empty space; it is defined globally by which events can signal to infinity.
- Common question: 'Why is the singularity drawn horizontal on the Penrose diagram?' Connect to the role swap: r is time inside, so r = 0 is a moment, like the Big Bang in Chapter 19.
- Flag the misprint in eqn 25.10 when the learner reaches it, and have them verify the sign from dr/dtau = -(2M/r)^(1/2).
- When using the half-sky result at r = 3M, say explicitly that it is for a hovering observer, and offer the falling-observer caveat to advanced learners.
- Use real numbers to make tidal effects concrete: stellar-mass holes kill well outside r_S, supermassive ones allow a quiet crossing; horizon-to-singularity proper time is about 6.6 microseconds per solar mass for a fall from rest at infinity.
- For learners who ask how to fix the coordinates, point to Exercise 25.1 (a one-line time shift gives Painleve-Gullstrand) before the heavier EF and Kruskal machinery of Chapters 26-27.
- For novices, drop the tortoise coordinate details and keep only its moral: a clever label can straighten light rays but cannot by itself bring the horizon within reach.
