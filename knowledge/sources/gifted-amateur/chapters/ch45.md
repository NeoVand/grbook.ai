# GA ch45 · Weak gravitational fields

> Writes the metric as flat Minkowski plus a small symmetric perturbation h, linearizes curvature and the Einstein equation, uses infinitesimal coordinate freedom as a gauge symmetry to impose the Lorenz condition, arrives at a sourced wave equation for the trace-reversed field, and checks it by recovering the Newtonian weak-field metric and a gravitational analogue of Maxwell's equations.

**Pages:** printed 485–493 · pdf 502–510 · **Difficulty:** math 4/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 2/5

The ideas (small perturbation, gauge freedom, wave equation) are standard and well motivated by the EM analogy, but the chapter is dense index algebra with several steps skipped ('after some reordering', verification left to exercises), cramped comma notation with raised indices, a misprinted intermediate step (45.7), and sign/factor subtleties in the gravitomagnetic part. Readers who have not done ch42 and ch44 will miss much of the gauge framing. The exercise sequence on gyroscope precession is genuinely challenging.

## Role in the book

Part VI recasts gravity in the language of classical field theory (Lagrangians in ch40, the electromagnetic field in ch42, gauge fields in ch44). This chapter is the bridge from that field-theoretic toolkit back to general relativity: it revisits the weak-field arguments first sketched in ch14 (§14.1, Example 14.2, eqn 5.22) with the full linearized machinery, borrows the electromagnetic gauge idea to simplify the field equation, and so produces the wave equation that ch46 (gravitational waves) and ch47 (gravitons) solve and quantize. The exercises also reach forward to gyroscope precession (geodetic and Lense-Thirring effects).

## Learning objectives

- Reader can state what makes a gravitational field weak: slow sources, stresses small compared with energy density, and a dimensionless potential GM/(c^2 r) much less than one.
- Reader can write g = η + h and explain why h transforms as a tensor under Lorentz transformations of the background, while recognizing that this flat-plus-field picture is only an approximation.
- Reader can linearize the Christoffel symbols, Riemann tensor and Ricci tensor by discarding products of connection coefficients.
- Reader can define the trace-reversed perturbation h-bar, show trace reversal flips the sign of the trace, and invert it.
- Reader can derive how h changes under an infinitesimal coordinate shift x → x + ξ and show the linearized Riemann tensor is unchanged.
- Reader can explain why the Lorenz condition on h-bar can always be imposed and use it to reduce the linearized Einstein equation to a flat-space wave equation with source 16πT.
- Reader can write the retarded-integral solution for h-bar by analogy with the electromagnetic four-potential and restore the factor of G.
- Reader can recover the Newtonian weak-field line element ds^2 = -(1+2Φ)dt^2 + (1-2Φ)dx^2 from a static source.
- Reader can order the components of the dust energy-momentum tensor by powers of v/c and build the gravitoelectric and gravitomagnetic potentials and fields.
- Reader can write the static gravitational Maxwell equations and connect the gravitomagnetic field to frame dragging (Lense-Thirring effect).

## Teaching approach

A compact, derivation-driven chapter that treats weak gravity as an ordinary field on flat spacetime so that the tools of Part VI (field theory, gauge symmetry) apply. The authors begin with a physical criterion for weakness, split the metric, and immediately justify calling h a tensor via a Lorentz-transformation check, before warning that the picture is a convenient fiction. They grind through the linearized curvature, deliberately showing how messy the result is, then rescue it with a gauge argument that is explicitly modelled on electromagnetism. The payoff is checked twice: first against the familiar Newtonian metric, then extended to gravitomagnetism. Calculations are boxed as Examples; margin notes carry reminders, the missing factors of c, and the EM comparison.

**Style:** derivation-heavy, components-first, analogy-driven, spiral-revisit, physical-motivation-first, conversational-informal

**Narrative arc**

1. Contrast with earlier strong-field (black hole) chapters and motivate weak fields as the regime of everyday experience and the home of wave-like excitations.
2. Define the Newtonian limit physically: slow velocities, small stresses relative to energy density, and the small parameter GM/(c^2 r).
3. Split the metric into Minkowski plus a small perturbation and show by Lorentz transformation that the perturbation behaves as a symmetric tensor field on flat spacetime.
4. Caution that this flat-background view is an approximation valid only for Lorentz transformations, then embrace it because it makes gravity look like other classical field theories.
5. Diagnose nonlinearity as coming from the ΓΓ terms of the Riemann tensor and drop them to get linear Christoffel, Riemann, and Ricci expressions (Example 45.1).
6. Assemble the linearized Einstein equation, show its unwieldy form, and introduce trace reversal to tidy it.
7. Motivate a desirable divergence-free condition, then justify it by examining infinitesimal coordinate transformations and deriving the change in h (Example 45.2).
8. Show that curvature is gauge invariant (Example 45.3), identify coordinate choice with gauge choice, and impose the Lorenz gauge.
9. Obtain the sourced wave equation for h-bar and write its retarded solution by analogy with electromagnetism.
10. Check against Newton: a static source reproduces the weak-field metric of eqn 5.22 (Example 45.4).
11. Push the analogy to next order in v/c: gravitomagnetic potential, gravitational Maxwell equations, and frame dragging (Example 45.5).
12. Point forward: in vacuum the equation is a homogeneous wave equation, predicting gravitational waves (ch46).

**Signature moves**

- Warn explicitly that the flat-spacetime-plus-field picture is a fiction, yet use it as a working tool, so the reader holds both views at once.
- Show the ugly intermediate equation on purpose (calling it unmemorable and formidable) to make the gauge simplification feel earned.
- Run a side-by-side analogy with electromagnetism at every stage: gauge shift of A versus shift of h, Lorenz gauge, retarded potential, Poisson equations, Maxwell-like field equations.
- Use the answer-first move: state the condition we wish we could impose, then prove we are allowed to impose it.
- Contrast gauge transformations of internal variables (ch44 phase) with gauge transformations that relabel spacetime points themselves.
- Close the loop with an earlier chapter by re-deriving a known result (the weak-field metric) from the new formalism.
- Order source terms by powers of v/c to decide which pieces of T matter and which new physics (gravitomagnetism) appears at the next order.
- Delegate a substantial application (gyroscope precession, Lense-Thirring and geodetic effects) to a guided multi-part exercise sequence.

## Section by section

### Chapter opening: from strong to weak gravity — p.485 (pdf 502)

Opens with an Oliver Goldsmith epigraph about handling weaknesses gently, a pun on the chapter's subject. After black holes, the book turns to the opposite regime, which is the one we actually live in. The authors promise an approximate field theory with wave-like solutions and say the warm-up will be the Newtonian limit. A margin note says some of the argument was previewed in §14.1.

**Concepts:** weak gravitational field, Newtonian limit

**Key moves**
- Frame weak fields as the everyday counterpart of the strong-field physics of black holes.
- Announce the two goals: connect Einstein to Newton and build a linear field theory that supports waves.

### §45.1 The Newtonian limit — p.485 (pdf 502)

Defines the Newtonian limit as slow motion relative to some observer, so the time component of the four-velocity dominates. Keeping masses slow requires a weak field, which in turn constrains the source: stresses must be tiny compared with energy density. A footnote supplies the dimensionless small parameter GM/(c^2 r). Spacetime is then nearly flat, so the metric is Minkowski plus a small correction h. A Lorentz transformation of the split metric shows h transforms as a tensor, so weak gravity can be pictured as a symmetric tensor field on flat spacetime with indices moved by η. The authors stress that this is an approximation (h is not a tensor under general coordinate changes) but a very productive one, and impose h → 0 at infinity.

**Concepts:** Newtonian limit, weak gravitational field, compactness parameter, metric perturbation, Lorentz covariance of the metric perturbation, flat-background field picture of gravity, boundary condition at infinity

**Key moves**
- Slow motion: u ≈ (1, u^i) with u^i ≪ 1; weak field needed so gravity does not accelerate bodies to high speed.
- Source condition |T^{ij}|/T^{00} ≪ 1 as the working definition of weak gravity (eqn 45.2).
- Dimensional estimate: replace v^2 by GM/r to get ε = GM/(c^2 r) (eqn 45.1).
- Split g = η + h with |h| ≪ 1 (eqn 45.3).
- Apply a Lorentz transformation: η is invariant, so the leftover piece h transforms with two Λ factors (eqns 45.4-45.5).
- Caveat: the split is not coordinate-independent in full GR; use it anyway, raising and lowering with η.

### §45.2 Linearized theory of gravitation — p.487 (pdf 504)

Traces GR's nonlinearity to the products of connection coefficients in the Riemann tensor and discards them. With indices moved by η, the Christoffel symbols become linear in derivatives of h, and the Riemann and Ricci tensors become linear in second derivatives of h (Example 45.1). Substituting into the Einstein equation produces a cluttered expression, which is tidied by introducing the trace-reversed perturbation h-bar. In terms of h-bar the Einstein tensor has four terms, still formidable, setting up the need for gauge fixing.

**Concepts:** nonlinearity of general relativity, linearization, linearized Christoffel symbols, linearized Riemann tensor, linearized Ricci tensor, trace of the metric perturbation, trace-reversed metric perturbation, linearized Einstein equation

**Key moves**
- Identify the ΓΓ terms in R as the only source of nonlinearity and drop them.
- Write Γ = ½(h_α^μ,β + h_β^μ,α - h_αβ^,μ), extending comma notation to raised indices (eqn 45.6).
- Riemann becomes a combination of four second derivatives of h (eqn 45.8); contract to Ricci (eqn 45.9).
- Assemble G = R - ½gR = 8πT into eqn 45.10.
- Define h-bar = h - ½ηh; note its trace is -h, so doing it twice returns h (eqns 45.11-45.13).
- Rewrite the Einstein tensor in terms of h-bar (eqns 45.14-45.15).

### §45.3 Exploiting gauges — p.488 (pdf 505)

Wishes for the divergence condition on h-bar that would delete most terms, then shows it is legitimate. An infinitesimal coordinate shift x' = x + ξ leaves ordinary smooth fields essentially unchanged but alters the tiny metric perturbation by the symmetrized gradient of ξ (Example 45.2), while leaving the linearized Riemann tensor untouched (Example 45.3). Coordinate choice is therefore a gauge choice, as in electromagnetism, and the Lorenz gauge can be imposed. The Einstein equation collapses to a flat-space wave equation for h-bar sourced by 16πT. By analogy with the retarded four-potential the solution is written as a retarded integral with factor 4G. Two checks follow: a static source yields h-bar_00 = -4Φ and the familiar weak-field metric (Example 45.4), and the momentum-density terms of dust yield a gravitomagnetic potential, Poisson equations for Φ and A_g, and static gravitational Maxwell equations that underlie frame dragging (Example 45.5). The section ends by noting that in vacuum the equation becomes a homogeneous wave equation, heralding gravitational waves.

**Concepts:** gauge freedom in linearized gravity, infinitesimal coordinate transformation, gauge transformation of the metric perturbation, gauge invariance of the linearized Riemann tensor, Lorenz gauge, d'Alembertian, linearized Einstein equation in Lorenz gauge, retarded potential, weak-field metric, gravitoelectromagnetism, gravitational Maxwell equations, Lense-Thirring effect, gravitational waves

**Key moves**
- State the desired condition h-bar^{μα}_{,α} = 0 before justifying it (eqn 45.16).
- Transform the metric under x' = x + ξ, keeping first order in ∂ξ, to find h → h - ξ_μ,ν - ξ_ν,μ (eqns 45.17-45.24).
- Show Riemann's change is a sum of antisymmetrized commuting derivatives, hence zero (eqn 45.26).
- Identify coordinate choice with gauge choice and impose the Lorenz gauge (eqn 45.27), analogous to ∂_μ A^μ = 0.
- Obtain -∂^2 h-bar = 16πT (eqns 45.28-45.29).
- Map -∂^2 A = J and its retarded solution onto gravity to get h-bar = 4G∫T_ret/|x-y| (eqns 45.30-45.31).
- Static check: only T_00 = ρ matters, h-bar_00 = -4Φ, so h_00 = h_ii = -2Φ and the weak-field metric follows (eqns 45.32-45.37).
- Next order in v/c: T^{0i} = ρu^i defines a gravitomagnetic potential; derive Poisson and Maxwell-like equations (eqns 45.38-45.42).
- Vacuum limit gives a homogeneous wave equation: gravitational waves.

### Chapter summary — p.491 (pdf 508)

Recaps three results: weak gravity is Minkowski plus a small field h; in terms of trace-reversed h-bar the Einstein equation is a wave equation with source 16πT; and an infinitesimal coordinate change shifts h by minus twice the symmetrized gradient of ξ.

**Concepts:** metric perturbation, linearized Einstein equation in Lorenz gauge, gauge transformation of the metric perturbation

**Key moves**
- Restate -∂^2 h-bar_μν = 16πT_μν (eqn 45.43).
- Restate h → h - 2ξ_(μ,ν) (eqn 45.44).

### Exercises — p.492 (pdf 509)

Nine problems: verify the chapter's derivations; rewrite the field equation for h itself with the trace-reversed source; interpret gravitoelectric, gravitomagnetic and 'curvature' source densities for a perfect fluid (ρ+3p, (ρ+p)u, ρ-p); derive a Lorentz-force-like geodesic equation; show vacuum energy gravitationally repels; and a four-exercise sequence (following Ryder 2009) that derives gyroscope precession around a rotating mass, separating Lense-Thirring and geodetic (de Sitter-Fokker) contributions.

**Concepts:** active gravitational mass density, gravitoelectromagnetism, vacuum energy repulsion, geodetic precession, Lense-Thirring effect, spin four-vector

**Key moves**
- Trace the linearized equation to relate ∂^2 h to T and invert trace reversal.
- Geodesic equation for slow particles yields acceleration = -∇Φ plus a velocity-dependent term built from antisymmetric derivatives of h_ti.
- Parallel transport of a spin orthogonal to the four-velocity, redefined to have constant length, gives dΣ/dt = Ω × Σ.

## Concepts

### weak gravitational field

*definition · core* · also: weak-field limit, weak gravitation

A regime in which spacetime differs only slightly from Minkowski space, so that in suitable coordinates g = η + h with all |h_μν| ≪ 1; physically the sources are slow and their stresses are small compared with their energy density.

**How introduced:** Presented as the opposite of the black-hole regime and the gravity of everyday life; made operational through the condition |T^{ij}|/T^{00} ≪ 1 on the source.

**Prerequisites:** Minkowski metric, energy-momentum tensor

$$
|T^{ij}|/T^{00} \ll 1
$$

$$
g_{\alpha\beta}=\eta_{\alpha\beta}+h_{\alpha\beta},\ |h_{\alpha\beta}|\ll 1
$$

**Notes:** Weakness is not the same as slowness: gravitational waves are weak but move at c. The chapter's criterion mixes the two because it targets the Newtonian limit first.

**Where:** GA p.485 (pdf 502); GA §45.1 p.485 (pdf 502)

### Newtonian limit

*definition · developed*

The limit of general relativity in which bodies move slowly compared with light (as judged by a chosen observer) and gravitational fields are weak, so that the theory reduces to Newtonian gravity with potential Φ satisfying Poisson's equation.

**How introduced:** Defined via four-velocity components: the time component dominates the spatial ones. Slowness forces the field to be weak (otherwise gravity would accelerate things to high speed), which in turn restricts the energy-momentum tensor.

**Prerequisites:** four-velocity, weak gravitational field

$$
u^{\mu}\approx(1,\vec u),\ u^i\ll 1
$$

**Notes:** A margin note stresses observer dependence: a velocity may be slow for one observer and not for another.

**Where:** GA §45.1 p.485 (pdf 502); GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)

### compactness parameter

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: relativistic parameter ε, dimensionless potential GM/(c^2 r)

The dimensionless ratio ε = GM/(c^2 r) comparing gravitational potential energy per unit mass with c^2; relativistic effects are important when ε is of order one and negligible when ε ≪ 1.

**How introduced:** In a footnote, by noting that relativistic corrections scale as v^2/c^2 and using dimensional analysis to replace v^2 by GM/r, since units with c = 1 hide when effects are large.

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian limit

$$
\varepsilon=\frac{GM}{c^2 r}
$$

**Notes:** For a Schwarzschild black hole ε = 1/2 at the horizon; for virialized orbits v^2 ~ GM/r, so ε and v^2/c^2 are the same order.

**Where:** GA §45.1 p.485 (pdf 502)

### metric perturbation

*mathematical-object · core* · also: h-field, weak-field metric h(x), h_{αβ}

The small symmetric array h_αβ = g_αβ - η_αβ describing how the true metric deviates from Minkowski in nearly Lorentzian coordinates; to first order it carries all gravitational information.

**How introduced:** Near-flatness suggests writing the full metric as the Minkowski metric plus a small additive correction.

**Prerequisites:** Minkowski metric, weak gravitational field

$$
g_{\alpha\beta}=\eta_{\alpha\beta}+h_{\alpha\beta}
$$

**Notes:** The book calls h itself 'the weak-field metric' in the summary, which can be confused with the full line element of eqn 45.37.

**Where:** GA §45.1 p.486 (pdf 503); GA p.491 (pdf 508)

### Lorentz covariance of the metric perturbation

*theorem · developed* · also: h transforms as a tensor on flat spacetime

Under a global Lorentz transformation of the background coordinates, the Minkowski part of the metric is unchanged, so the perturbation transforms with two Lorentz matrices exactly like a rank-2 tensor on flat spacetime.

**How introduced:** Transform the components of g = η + h from primed to unprimed coordinates, use invariance of η, and read off the transformation of the leftover term.

**Prerequisites:** metric perturbation, Lorentz transformation, tensor transformation law

$$
h_{\alpha'\beta'}=\Lambda^{\mu}{}_{\alpha'}\Lambda^{\nu}{}_{\beta'}h_{\mu\nu}
$$

**Notes:** Only holds for transformations that preserve η; under general coordinate changes h mixes with η and is not a tensor. Infinitesimal coordinate shifts change h by a gauge term (see gauge transformation).

**Where:** GA §45.1 p.486 (pdf 503)

### flat-background field picture of gravity

*principle · core* · also: gravity as a tensor field on Minkowski space, the useful fiction

The approximation of treating weak gravity as a symmetric tensor field h(x) living on a fixed flat spacetime, with indices raised and lowered by η, so gravity can be handled like electromagnetism or any other classical field.

**How introduced:** Offered as the natural reading of the Lorentz-covariance result, immediately followed by a strong warning that real spacetime is curved and the picture fails beyond first order and for non-Lorentz coordinate changes.

**Prerequisites:** metric perturbation, Lorentz covariance of the metric perturbation, classical field theory

**Notes:** This is the conceptual foundation for Part VI's treatment of gravitational waves and gravitons (ch46-47).

**Where:** GA §45.1 p.486 (pdf 503)

### index raising and lowering with the Minkowski metric

*convention · introduced*

In linearized theory all indices on h, Γ, R and T are raised and lowered with η rather than the full metric g, since the difference is second order in h.

**How introduced:** Adopted as part of the flat-background picture and used explicitly when writing the linearized connection.

**Prerequisites:** metric perturbation

$$
h^{\mu}{}_{\nu}=\eta^{\mu\alpha}h_{\alpha\nu}
$$

**Notes:** Consequence: g^{μν} = η^{μν} - h^{μν} to first order, a fact used in Exercise 45.6(a).

**Where:** GA §45.1 p.486 (pdf 503); GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504)

### boundary condition at infinity

*convention · mention* · also: asymptotic flatness condition

The requirement that the perturbation vanish far from sources, h → 0 as r → ∞, mirroring the Newtonian choice Φ → 0 at infinity.

**How introduced:** Stated alongside the flat-background picture to fix the solution of the field equations.

**Prerequisites:** metric perturbation

$$
h(r\to\infty)=0
$$

**Where:** GA §45.1 p.486 (pdf 503)

### nonlinearity of general relativity

*principle · introduced*

The Einstein equation is nonlinear in the metric because the Riemann tensor contains products of connection coefficients; gravitational fields therefore do not superpose exactly.

**How introduced:** Pinned to the third and fourth terms of the Riemann formula, recalled in a margin note.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann curvature tensor, Christoffel symbols

$$
R^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\mu\nu}=\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\nu\beta,\mu}-\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\mu\beta,\nu}+\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\mu\sigma}\Gamma^{\sigma}{}_{\nu\beta}-\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\nu\sigma}\Gamma^{\sigma}{}_{\mu\beta}
$$

**Where:** GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504)

### linearization

*technique · core* · also: linearized theory, first-order perturbation theory

Approximating a nonlinear field theory by keeping only terms of first order in a small field, justified when the interaction strength is small; in GR this means discarding all terms quadratic or higher in h (equivalently products of Γs).

**How introduced:** Presented as a generic strategy for nonlinear field theories at weak coupling, then applied to the curvature tensors.

**Prerequisites:** nonlinearity of general relativity, metric perturbation

**Where:** GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504)

### linearized Christoffel symbols

*mathematical-object · developed*

To first order in h, the connection coefficients are half the η-raised combination of first derivatives of h: Γ^μ_αβ = ½η^{μν}(h_αν,β + h_βν,α - h_αβ,ν).

**How introduced:** Substituting g = η + h into the usual formula, noting η has zero derivatives, and raising with η.

**Prerequisites:** Christoffel symbols, metric perturbation, index raising and lowering with the Minkowski metric

$$
\Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\beta}=\tfrac12\left(h_{\alpha}{}^{\mu}{}_{,\beta}+h_{\beta}{}^{\mu}{}_{,\alpha}-h_{\alpha\beta}{}^{,\mu}\right)
$$

**Where:** GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504)

### linearized Riemann tensor

*mathematical-object · developed*

The first-order curvature, built from derivatives of the linear Christoffel symbols only: R_αμβν = ½(h_μβ,να + h_να,μβ - h_μν,αβ - h_αβ,μν).

**How introduced:** Drop the ΓΓ terms, lower the first index with η, and substitute the linear connection (Example 45.1).

**Prerequisites:** linearized Christoffel symbols, Riemann curvature tensor, linearization

$$
R_{\alpha\mu\beta\nu}=\tfrac12\left(h_{\mu\beta,\nu\alpha}+h_{\nu\alpha,\mu\beta}-h_{\mu\nu,\alpha\beta}-h_{\alpha\beta,\mu\nu}\right)
$$

**Notes:** The intermediate printed eqn 45.7 has a derivative index misplaced (see gaps); eqn 45.8 is correct.

**Where:** GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504); GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

### linearized Ricci tensor

*mathematical-object · developed*

The contraction of the linearized Riemann tensor, R_μν = ½(h_μ^α,να + h_ν^α,μα - □h_μν - h_,μν), with h the trace of the perturbation.

**How introduced:** Contract the linear Riemann expression at the same level of approximation (Example 45.1).

**Prerequisites:** linearized Riemann tensor, trace of the metric perturbation

$$
R_{\mu\nu}=\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\nu\mu,\alpha}-\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\alpha\mu,\nu}
$$

$$
R_{\mu\nu}=\tfrac12\left(h_{\mu}{}^{\alpha}{}_{,\nu\alpha}+h_{\nu}{}^{\alpha}{}_{,\mu\alpha}-h_{\mu\nu,\alpha}{}^{\alpha}-h_{,\mu\nu}\right)
$$

**Notes:** The Ricci scalar, needed for G but not printed, is R = h^{αβ}_{,αβ} - □h.

**Where:** GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504)

### trace of the metric perturbation

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: h

The scalar h = h^α_α = η^{αβ}h_αβ obtained by contracting the perturbation with the Minkowski metric.

**How introduced:** Appears as the last term in the linearized Ricci tensor and is named there.

**Prerequisites:** metric perturbation

$$
h=h^{\alpha}{}_{\alpha}=\eta^{\alpha\beta}h_{\alpha\beta}
$$

**Notes:** Not to be confused with the tensor h; the bold/plain distinction in print is easy to miss.

**Where:** GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504)

### trace-reversed metric perturbation

*definition · core* · also: h-bar, bar notation, trace reversal

The combination h-bar_μν = h_μν - ½η_μν h, whose trace is minus the trace of h; applying the operation twice returns the original tensor, so h_μν = h-bar_μν - ½η_μν h-bar.

**How introduced:** Introduced as a notational rescue for the cluttered linearized Einstein equation; a margin note recalls the name and observes that the Einstein tensor is the trace-reversed Ricci tensor.

**Prerequisites:** trace of the metric perturbation, Einstein tensor

$$
\bar h_{\mu\nu}=h_{\mu\nu}-\tfrac12\eta_{\mu\nu}h
$$

$$
\bar h=-h
$$

$$
h_{\mu\nu}=\bar h_{\mu\nu}-\tfrac12\eta_{\mu\nu}\bar h
$$

**Notes:** The factor 1/2 and the trace reversal property rely on four spacetime dimensions (η^μ_μ = 4). The margin note's statement that barring twice returns h is printed with a double overbar; the text export drops one bar.

**Where:** GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504); GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

### linearized Einstein equation

*law · core* · also: linearized field equation (general gauge)

The Einstein equation to first order in h, expressed in any gauge: -□h-bar_μν - η_μν h-bar_αβ^{,αβ} + h-bar_μα,ν^α + h-bar_να,μ^α = 16πT_μν.

**How introduced:** Build G = R - ½gR from the linear Ricci tensor and scalar, get an unmemorable expression in h, then rewrite it with h-bar.

**Prerequisites:** linearized Ricci tensor, trace-reversed metric perturbation, Einstein field equation

$$
G_{\mu\nu}=-\tfrac12\left[\bar h_{\mu\nu,\alpha}{}^{\alpha}+\eta_{\mu\nu}\bar h_{\alpha\beta,}{}^{\alpha\beta}-\bar h_{\mu\alpha,\nu}{}^{\alpha}-\bar h_{\nu\alpha,\mu}{}^{\alpha}\right]
$$

**Where:** GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504); GA §45.2 p.488 (pdf 505)

### infinitesimal coordinate transformation

*operation · core* · also: gauge transformation (linearized gravity)

A relabelling of spacetime points x^μ' = x^μ + ξ^μ(P) with ξ and its derivatives small enough that the perturbation remains small in the new coordinates.

**How introduced:** Introduced to explain why the desired divergence condition can be imposed; contrasted in a margin note with ch44's internal-phase gauge transformations, since here the coordinates themselves move so points must be labelled independently (P).

**Prerequisites:** metric perturbation, tensor transformation law

$$
x^{\mu'}(\mathcal P)=x^{\mu}(\mathcal P)+\xi^{\mu}(\mathcal P)
$$

**Notes:** Smooth O(1) fields change only by ξ·∂(field), which is negligible, but h is itself small, so its O(∂ξ) change is comparable to h.

**Where:** GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505)

### gauge transformation of the metric perturbation

*identity · core* · also: gauge shift of h

Under x → x + ξ the perturbation changes by minus the symmetrized gradient of ξ: h_μν → h_μν - ξ_μ,ν - ξ_ν,μ = h_μν - 2ξ_(μ,ν).

**How introduced:** Substitute the split metric and the inverse coordinate map into the metric transformation law and keep first-order terms (Example 45.2); compared in a margin note with A_μ → A_μ - (1/q)α_,μ.

**Prerequisites:** infinitesimal coordinate transformation, metric perturbation

$$
h^{\rm new}_{\mu\nu}=h^{\rm old}_{\mu\nu}-\xi_{\mu,\nu}-\xi_{\nu,\mu}
$$

$$
h_{\mu\nu}\to h_{\mu\nu}-2\xi_{(\mu,\nu)}
$$

**Notes:** Equivalently h changes by minus the Lie derivative of η along ξ.

**Where:** GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505); GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506); GA p.491 (pdf 508)

### gauge invariance of the linearized Riemann tensor

*theorem · developed*

The first-order Riemann tensor is unchanged by the gauge shift of h, because the added terms are antisymmetrized combinations of commuting partial derivatives of ξ and vanish identically.

**How introduced:** Sketched in Example 45.3 to convince the reader that gauge shifts are physically empty: the curvature, which encodes physical effects, is untouched.

**Prerequisites:** linearized Riemann tensor, gauge transformation of the metric perturbation

$$
R^{\rm new}_{\alpha\mu\beta\nu}=R^{\rm old}_{\alpha\mu\beta\nu}
$$

**Notes:** Gauge invariance of the linearized Riemann tensor parallels gauge invariance of F_μν in electromagnetism.

**Where:** GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

### gauge freedom in linearized gravity

*principle · core* · also: coordinate freedom as gauge freedom

Because infinitesimal coordinate changes alter h but not the curvature, many h fields describe the same physical spacetime; choosing coordinates is equivalent to choosing a gauge, which can be exploited to simplify the field equation.

**How introduced:** By explicit analogy with electromagnetism (A → A - ∂χ leaves Maxwell's equations intact) and with ch44's gauge fields; a margin note credits Weyl with the word 'gauge'.

**Prerequisites:** gauge transformation of the metric perturbation, gauge invariance of the linearized Riemann tensor, gauge invariance in electromagnetism

**Where:** GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505); GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

### gauge invariance in electromagnetism

*principle · revisited* · also: electromagnetic gauge freedom

Adding the gradient of any scalar to the four-potential, A_μ → A_μ - χ_,μ, leaves the field strength and Maxwell's equations unchanged, so χ can be chosen to simplify problems.

**How introduced:** Recalled in margin notes as the template for the gravitational gauge argument.

**Prerequisites:** electromagnetic four-potential

$$
A_{\mu}\to A_{\mu}-\chi_{,\mu}
$$

$$
A_{\mu}\to A_{\mu}-\tfrac1q\alpha_{,\mu}
$$

**Where:** GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505); GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

### Lorenz gauge

*convention · core* · also: harmonic gauge, de Donder gauge, Hilbert gauge

The gauge condition that the trace-reversed perturbation is divergence-free, h-bar^{μα}_{,α} = 0, analogous to ∂_μ A^μ = 0; it can always be reached by choosing ξ to solve □ξ^μ = h-bar^{μα}_{,α}.

**How introduced:** First stated as a wish because it would kill several terms, then justified via gauge freedom and named by analogy with electromagnetism.

**Prerequisites:** gauge freedom in linearized gravity, trace-reversed metric perturbation

$$
\bar h^{\mu\alpha}{}_{,\alpha}=0
$$

**Notes:** The book does not show that a suitable ξ always exists (it follows from h-bar changing by -ξ^μ,ν - ξ^ν,μ + η^{μν}ξ^α_,α, whose divergence is -□ξ^μ).

**Where:** GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505); GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

### d'Alembertian

*operation · introduced* · also: wave operator, ∂^2, box operator

The flat-spacetime second-order operator ∂^2 = η^{αβ}∂_α∂_β = -∂_t^2 + ∇^2 whose zero set defines the wave equation, with plane-wave solutions exp[-i(ωt - k·x)].

**How introduced:** Given as notation in margin notes when the gauge-fixed Einstein equation is rewritten; a reminder note gives the form of plane-wave solutions.

**Prerequisites:** Minkowski metric

$$
\partial^2=-\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}+\vec\nabla^2
$$

**Where:** GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

### linearized Einstein equation in Lorenz gauge

*law · core* · also: linear field theory of gravitation, weak-field Einstein equation

In Lorenz gauge the first-order Einstein equation becomes a flat-space wave equation for the trace-reversed perturbation sourced by the energy-momentum tensor: -∂^2 h-bar_μν = 16πT_μν (G = c = 1).

**How introduced:** Imposing the gauge condition deletes three of the four terms in the linearized Einstein tensor.

**Prerequisites:** linearized Einstein equation, Lorenz gauge, d'Alembertian

$$
-\partial^2\bar h_{\mu\nu}=16\pi T_{\mu\nu}
$$

**Notes:** Consistency requires T^{μν}_{,ν} = 0, which matches the Lorenz condition; the book does not remark on this.

**Where:** GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506); GA p.491 (pdf 508)

### retarded potential

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: retarded solution, retarded Green's function solution

The causal solution of a sourced wave equation, obtained by integrating the source over space evaluated at the earlier time t - |x - y| when a signal travelling at c must have left it, weighted by 1/|x - y|.

**How introduced:** Carried over from electromagnetism: -∂^2A = J has the retarded solution (1/4π)∫J_ret/|x-y|, so the gravitational equation, with 16πG in place of 1, has h-bar = 4G∫T_ret/|x-y|.

**Prerequisites:** linearized Einstein equation in Lorenz gauge, electromagnetic four-potential

$$
\bar h_{\mu\nu}(t,\vec x)=4G\int d^3y\,\frac{T_{\mu\nu}(t-|\vec x-\vec y|,\vec y)}{|\vec x-\vec y|}
$$

**Notes:** Justified by analogy only; the Green's function derivation is not given. The authors say it will be used in the next two chapters.

**Where:** GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)

### weak-field metric

*solution-or-model · revisited* · also: Newtonian weak-field line element, eqn 5.22 metric

The first-order metric of a static, slow, weakly gravitating source: ds^2 = -(1+2Φ)dt^2 + (1-2Φ)(dx^2+dy^2+dz^2), where Φ is the Newtonian potential; the time and space parts are perturbed equally.

**How introduced:** Recovered in Example 45.4 from the retarded integral with a static dust source, confirming the earlier result of ch5 and §14.1.

**Prerequisites:** retarded potential, trace-reversed metric perturbation, Newtonian gravitational potential

$$
\bar h_{00}=-4\Phi
$$

$$
h_{00}=h_{11}=h_{22}=h_{33}=-2\Phi
$$

$$
ds^2=-(1+2\Phi)dt^2+(1-2\Phi)(dx^2+dy^2+dz^2)
$$

**Notes:** The spatial part (1-2Φ) is what doubles light bending relative to a Newtonian estimate; not remarked on here.

**Where:** GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)

### post-Newtonian ordering of dust sources

*technique · introduced* · also: v/c hierarchy of T^{μν}

For dust T^{μν} = ρu^μu^ν, the energy density T^{00} is order ρc^2, the momentum density T^{0i} is smaller by v/c, and the stresses T^{ij} are smaller by (v/c)^2, so successive physical effects can be organised by powers of v/c.

**How introduced:** Used at the start of Example 45.5 to decide that the T^{0i} terms are the next effect to keep; a margin note restores the factors of c.

**Prerequisites:** energy-momentum tensor, Newtonian limit

$$
T^{00}=\rho c^2,\ T^{0i}=\rho c u^i,\ T^{ij}=\rho u^iu^j
$$

**Notes:** Keeping T^{0i} while dropping the time derivatives of the potentials is consistent only for stationary currents.

**Where:** GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)

### gravitoelectromagnetism

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: GEM, gravitomagnetism, gravitoelectric and gravitomagnetic fields

The reformulation of linearized gravity for slowly moving sources in which the Newtonian potential Φ plays the role of an electric scalar potential and the mass-current term h-bar_{0i} plays the role of a magnetic vector potential A_g; their gradient and curl define fields E_g = -∇Φ and B_g = ∇×A_g obeying Maxwell-like equations.

**How introduced:** Example 45.5 keeps the momentum-density part of dust, defines A_g by an integral like Φ but with mass current, and follows the electromagnetic analogy to fields and field equations.

**Prerequisites:** retarded potential, post-Newtonian ordering of dust sources, Maxwell's equations

$$
A_g^i(\vec x)=-G\int d^3y\,\frac{J^i(\vec y)}{|\vec x-\vec y|}
$$

$$
\bar h_{0i}=-4A_{gi}
$$

$$
\vec E_g=-\vec\nabla\Phi,\quad \vec B_g=\vec\nabla\times\vec A_g
$$

**Notes:** Sign and numerical factors in GEM are convention-dependent; with the book's normalization the velocity-dependent acceleration is 4v×B_g (up to sign), not v×B_g (see gaps).

**Where:** GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507); GA §45.3 p.491 (pdf 508); GA p.492 (pdf 509)

### gravitational Maxwell equations

*law · developed* · also: gravitoelectromagnetic field equations

The static field equations of gravitoelectromagnetism: ∇·E_g = -4πGρ, ∇·B_g = 0, ∇×E_g = 0, ∇×B_g = -4πG J, with signs opposite to electromagnetism because like masses attract.

**How introduced:** From Poisson equations for Φ and A_g obtained by dropping time derivatives in the gauge-fixed wave equation, then taking divergences and curls.

**Prerequisites:** gravitoelectromagnetism, Poisson equation

$$
\nabla^2\Phi=4\pi G\rho,\quad \nabla^2\vec A_g=4\pi G\vec J
$$

$$
\vec\nabla\cdot\vec E_g=-4\pi G\rho,\ \vec\nabla\cdot\vec B_g=0,\ \vec\nabla\times\vec E_g=0,\ \vec\nabla\times\vec B_g=-4\pi G\vec J
$$

**Notes:** The curl equation for B_g uses ∇·A_g = 0, which follows from the Lorenz gauge for a stationary source; the book does not say so.

**Where:** GA §45.3 p.491 (pdf 508)

### Lense-Thirring effect

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: frame dragging, Lense-Thirring precession

The dragging of nearby inertial frames by a rotating mass: its mass currents generate a gravitomagnetic field that exerts a velocity-dependent force on moving bodies and makes gyroscopes precess with angular velocity of dipole form (1/r^3)[3(J·r̂)r̂ - J].

**How introduced:** Mentioned at the end of Example 45.5 as the gravitational analogue of the magnetic force of a current; derived fully in the guided Exercises 45.6-45.9.

**Prerequisites:** gravitoelectromagnetism, parallel transport

$$
\vec\Omega_{\rm LT}=\frac{1}{r^3}\left[\frac{3(\vec J\cdot\vec r)\vec r}{r^2}-\vec J\right]
$$

**Where:** GA §45.3 p.491 (pdf 508); GA p.493 (pdf 510)

### geodetic precession

*phenomenon · mention* · also: de Sitter-Fokker effect, de Sitter precession, geodetic motion

Precession of a gyroscope carried along an orbit around a non-rotating mass, caused by parallel transport through curved space combined with the orbital motion; at leading order Ω = (3M/2r^3) r × v.

**How introduced:** Appears as the second term of the precession vector derived in Exercise 45.9.

**Prerequisites:** parallel transport, spin four-vector, weak-field metric

$$
\vec\Omega_{\rm geo}=\frac{3M}{2r^3}\vec r\times\vec v
$$

**Notes:** The book writes 'geodetic motion'; the usual name is geodetic precession.

**Where:** GA p.493 (pdf 510)

### spin four-vector

*mathematical-object · mention* · also: gyroscope spin 1-form S_μ

A four-vector (here a 1-form S_μ) representing a gyroscope's angular momentum, constrained to be orthogonal to the four-velocity and parallel transported along a free-fall world line.

**How introduced:** Defined in Exercise 45.7 and used to derive gyroscope precession; a rescaled 3-vector Σ of constant length is introduced in Exercise 45.9 so that its evolution is a pure rotation.

**Prerequisites:** parallel transport, four-velocity

$$
S_{\mu}u^{\mu}=0
$$

$$
\frac{d\vec\Sigma}{dt}=\vec\Omega\times\vec\Sigma
$$

**Where:** GA p.493 (pdf 510)

### active gravitational mass density

*physical-quantity · mention* · also: gravitoelectric energy density ρ_g, ρ + 3p

The combination that actually sources the Newtonian-like potential in linearized GR, ρ_g = 2T_tt - η_tt T, which for a slowly moving perfect fluid is ρ + 3p; pressure gravitates.

**How introduced:** Defined in Exercise 45.3 from the trace-reversed source of the equation for h, alongside a gravitomagnetic current (ρ+p)u_i and a 'curvature energy density' ρ - p sourcing the spatial diagonal of h.

**Prerequisites:** linearized Einstein equation in Lorenz gauge, perfect fluid energy-momentum tensor

$$
\rho_g=2T_{tt}-\eta_{tt}T\approx\rho+3p
$$

$$
\Pi_i=-T_{ti}+\tfrac12\eta_{ti}T\approx(\rho+p)u_i
$$

$$
\rho_c=2T_{ii}-\eta_{ii}T\approx\rho-p
$$

**Notes:** The book prints 'gravitometric current density', presumably meaning gravitomagnetic. It also writes the curvature density as 2T_ti - η_ti T, which cannot give ρ - p; the spatial diagonal component (2T_ii - η_ii T, no sum) is meant and is what is recorded here.

**Where:** GA p.492 (pdf 509)

### vacuum energy repulsion

*phenomenon · mention* · also: repulsive gravity of a cosmological constant

For vacuum energy p = -ρ, the active density ρ + 3p = -2ρ is negative, so the linearized potential satisfies ∇^2Φ = -Λ and grows as -Λr^2/6, pushing test bodies apart.

**How introduced:** Exercise 45.5, as an application of the active mass density.

**Prerequisites:** active gravitational mass density, cosmological constant

$$
\vec\nabla^2\Phi=-\Lambda
$$

$$
\Phi=\alpha r^2
$$

**Notes:** Our evaluation of the constant (not printed): α = -Λ/6.

**Where:** GA p.492 (pdf 509)

### gravitational waves

*phenomenon · mention*

Propagating ripples of spacetime curvature; in linearized theory they are source-free solutions of ∂^2 h-bar_μν = 0 travelling at the speed of light.

**How introduced:** Previewed twice: as the wave-like excitations promised in the opening and as the vacuum limit of the gauge-fixed equation at the end of §45.3.

**Prerequisites:** linearized Einstein equation in Lorenz gauge, d'Alembertian

$$
\left(\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}-\vec\nabla^2\right)\bar h_{\mu\nu}=0
$$

**Notes:** Developed in GA ch46.

**Where:** GA p.485 (pdf 502); GA §45.3 p.491 (pdf 508)

## Key equations

### (45.1) Relativistic small parameter · supporting · GA §45.1 p.485 (pdf 502)

$$
\varepsilon=\frac{GM}{c^2r}
$$

Measures how relativistic a gravitational field is; order one means strong gravity, much less than one means the Newtonian limit applies.

**Symbols:** G Newton's constant; M source mass; r distance; c speed of light

### (45.2) Weak-source condition · supporting · GA §45.1 p.485 (pdf 502)

$$
\frac{|T^{ij}|}{T^{00}}\ll 1
$$

Stresses (pressure, momentum flux) are negligible compared with energy density; the working definition of weak gravitation.

**Symbols:** T^{ij} spatial stress components; T^{00} energy density

### (45.3) Metric split · central · GA §45.1 p.486 (pdf 503)

$$
g_{\alpha\beta}=\eta_{\alpha\beta}+h_{\alpha\beta},\qquad |h_{\alpha\beta}|\ll 1
$$

The nearly flat metric is Minkowski plus a small symmetric perturbation.

**Symbols:** η Minkowski metric diag(-1,1,1,1); h perturbation

### (45.4) · derivation-step · GA §45.1 p.486 (pdf 503)

$$
g_{\alpha'\beta'}=\eta_{\alpha'\beta'}+h_{\alpha'\beta'}=\Lambda^{\mu}{}_{\alpha'}\Lambda^{\nu}{}_{\beta'}(\eta_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu})=\eta_{\alpha'\beta'}+\Lambda^{\mu}{}_{\alpha'}\Lambda^{\nu}{}_{\beta'}h_{\mu\nu}
$$

Lorentz-transforming the split metric leaves η unchanged, isolating the transformation of h.

**Symbols:** Λ Lorentz transformation matrix

### (45.5) Tensor law for h under Lorentz transformations · supporting · GA §45.1 p.486 (pdf 503)

$$
h_{\alpha'\beta'}=\Lambda^{\mu}{}_{\alpha'}\Lambda^{\nu}{}_{\beta'}h_{\mu\nu}
$$

The perturbation behaves as a rank-2 tensor on flat spacetime, licensing the field-on-Minkowski picture.

**Symbols:** Λ Lorentz matrix

### (45.6) Linearized Christoffel symbols · supporting · GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504)

$$
\Gamma^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\beta}=\tfrac12\eta^{\mu\nu}(h_{\alpha\nu,\beta}+h_{\beta\nu,\alpha}-h_{\alpha\beta,\nu})=\tfrac12\left(h_{\alpha}{}^{\mu}{}_{,\beta}+h_{\beta}{}^{\mu}{}_{,\alpha}-h_{\alpha\beta}{}^{,\mu}\right)
$$

Connection to first order in h, with indices raised by η.

**Symbols:** comma with raised index means η^{μν}∂_ν

### Riemann tensor from connection (margin reminder) · supporting · GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504)

$$
R^{\alpha}{}_{\beta\mu\nu}=\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\nu\beta,\mu}-\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\mu\beta,\nu}+\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\mu\sigma}\Gamma^{\sigma}{}_{\nu\beta}-\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\nu\sigma}\Gamma^{\sigma}{}_{\mu\beta}
$$

The last two (quadratic) terms are the source of GR's nonlinearity and are dropped in linearized theory.

**Symbols:** Γ Christoffel symbols; commas are partial derivatives

### (45.7) Linearized Riemann from connection · derivation-step · GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504)

$$
R_{\alpha\mu\beta\nu}\approx\eta_{\alpha\lambda}\left(\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\nu,\beta}-\Gamma^{\lambda}{}_{\mu\beta,\nu}\right)
$$

Riemann with the quadratic connection terms dropped and first index lowered. Written here in corrected form; the book prints the first term as Γ^λ_{μβ,ν}, which would make the bracket vanish identically.

**Symbols:** λ dummy index

### (45.8) Linearized Riemann tensor · central · GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504)

$$
R_{\alpha\mu\beta\nu}=\tfrac12\left(h_{\mu\beta,\nu\alpha}+h_{\nu\alpha,\mu\beta}-h_{\mu\nu,\alpha\beta}-h_{\alpha\beta,\mu\nu}\right)
$$

First-order curvature in terms of second derivatives of the perturbation; gauge invariant.

**Symbols:** h perturbation; pairs of commas are second partial derivatives

### (45.9) Linearized Ricci tensor · supporting · GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504)

$$
R_{\mu\nu}=\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\nu\mu,\alpha}-\Gamma^{\alpha}{}_{\alpha\mu,\nu}=\tfrac12\left(h_{\mu}{}^{\alpha}{}_{,\nu\alpha}+h_{\nu}{}^{\alpha}{}_{,\mu\alpha}-h_{\mu\nu,\alpha}{}^{\alpha}-h_{,\mu\nu}\right)
$$

Contraction of the linear Riemann tensor; the last term contains the trace h.

**Symbols:** h = η^{αβ}h_{αβ}

### (45.10) Linearized Einstein equation in terms of h · derivation-step · GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504)

$$
h_{\mu\alpha,\nu}{}^{\alpha}+h_{\nu\alpha,\mu}{}^{\alpha}-h_{\mu\nu,\alpha}{}^{\alpha}-h_{,\mu\nu}-\eta_{\mu\nu}\left(h_{\alpha\beta}{}^{,\alpha\beta}-h_{,\beta}{}^{\beta}\right)=16\pi T_{\mu\nu}
$$

Twice the linearized Einstein tensor equals 16πT; correct but cluttered, motivating trace reversal.

**Symbols:** T energy-momentum tensor; G = c = 1

### (45.11) Trace of the trace-reversed perturbation · supporting · GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504)

$$
\bar h=\bar h^{\alpha}{}_{\alpha}=-h
$$

Trace reversal flips the sign of the trace (in four dimensions), which is the origin of the name.

### (45.12) Inverse trace reversal · supporting · GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504)

$$
h_{\mu\nu}=\bar h_{\mu\nu}-\tfrac12\eta_{\mu\nu}\bar h
$$

Applying the bar operation twice gives back the original perturbation.

### (45.13) Trace-reversed perturbation · central · GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504)

$$
\bar h_{\mu\nu}=h_{\mu\nu}-\tfrac12\eta_{\mu\nu}h
$$

Definition of the variable in which the linearized field equation is simplest.

**Symbols:** h trace of the perturbation

### (45.14) Linearized Einstein tensor in trace-reversed form · supporting · GA §45.2 p.488 (pdf 505)

$$
G_{\mu\nu}=-\tfrac12\left[\bar h_{\mu\nu,\alpha}{}^{\alpha}+\eta_{\mu\nu}\bar h_{\alpha\beta,}{}^{\alpha\beta}-\bar h_{\mu\alpha,\nu}{}^{\alpha}-\bar h_{\nu\alpha,\mu}{}^{\alpha}\right]
$$

Einstein tensor to first order; three of its four terms involve the divergence of h-bar.

### (45.15) Linearized Einstein equation, general gauge · supporting · GA §45.2 p.488 (pdf 505)

$$
-\bar h_{\mu\nu,\alpha}{}^{\alpha}-\eta_{\mu\nu}\bar h_{\alpha\beta,}{}^{\alpha\beta}+\bar h_{\mu\alpha,\nu}{}^{\alpha}+\bar h_{\nu\alpha,\mu}{}^{\alpha}=16\pi T_{\mu\nu}
$$

Full first-order field equation before gauge fixing.

### (45.16) Desired divergence condition · derivation-step · GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505)

$$
\bar h^{\mu\alpha}{}_{,\alpha}=0
$$

The condition that would delete the divergence terms in 45.15; justified in the rest of §45.3.

### (45.17) Infinitesimal coordinate transformation · supporting · GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505)

$$
x^{\mu'}(\mathcal P)=x^{\mu}(\mathcal P)+\xi^{\mu}(\mathcal P)
$$

A tiny relabelling of each spacetime point P, small enough to keep the perturbation small.

**Symbols:** ξ^μ small displacement field; P a point of the manifold

### (45.18) Metric transformation law · derivation-step · GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505)

$$
g_{\rho'\sigma'}[x^{\alpha'}(\mathcal P)]=g_{\mu\nu}[x^{\alpha}(\mathcal P)]\,\frac{\partial x^{\mu}}{\partial x^{\rho'}}\frac{\partial x^{\nu}}{\partial x^{\sigma'}}
$$

Components of the metric at the same physical point in old and new coordinates.

### (45.19) · derivation-step · GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505)

$$
g_{\rho'\sigma'}(x^{\alpha'}=a^{\alpha})=\eta_{\rho'\sigma'}+h_{\rho'\sigma'}(x^{\alpha'}=a^{\alpha})-\xi_{\rho',\sigma'}-\xi_{\sigma',\rho'}+(\text{corrections})
$$

In new coordinates the metric is flat plus the old perturbation shifted by the symmetrized gradient of ξ.

**Symbols:** a^α coordinate values of the point

### (45.20) · derivation-step · GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505)

$$
x^{\mu}=x^{\mu'}-\xi^{\mu}
$$

Inverse of the infinitesimal map, to first order.

### (45.21) · derivation-step · GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505)

$$
\frac{\partial x^{\mu}}{\partial x^{\rho'}}=\delta^{\mu}{}_{\rho'}-\xi^{\mu}{}_{,\rho'}
$$

Jacobian of the inverse map.

**Symbols:** δ Kronecker delta

### (45.22) · derivation-step · GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

$$
\frac{\partial x^{\mu}}{\partial x^{\rho'}}\frac{\partial x^{\nu}}{\partial x^{\sigma'}}=\delta^{\mu}{}_{\rho'}\delta^{\nu}{}_{\sigma'}-\xi^{\mu}{}_{,\rho'}\delta^{\nu}{}_{\sigma'}-\xi^{\nu}{}_{,\sigma'}\delta^{\mu}{}_{\rho'}+\dots
$$

Product of Jacobians to first order in derivatives of ξ.

### (45.23) · derivation-step · GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

$$
(\eta_{\mu\nu}+h_{\mu\nu})\frac{\partial x^{\mu}}{\partial x^{\rho'}}\frac{\partial x^{\nu}}{\partial x^{\sigma'}}=\eta_{\rho'\sigma'}+h_{\rho'\sigma'}-\xi_{\sigma',\rho'}-\xi_{\rho',\sigma'}+\dots
$$

Lowest-order result of transforming the split metric; products of h with ∂ξ are dropped.

### (45.24) Gauge transformation of h · central · GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

$$
h^{\rm new}_{\mu\nu}=h^{\rm old}_{\mu\nu}-\xi_{\mu,\nu}-\xi_{\nu,\mu}
$$

An infinitesimal coordinate change shifts the perturbation by minus the symmetrized gradient of ξ, the gravitational analogue of A → A - ∂χ.

**Symbols:** ξ_μ = η_{μν}ξ^ν

### (45.25) · supporting · GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

$$
h^{\rm new}_{\mu\nu}=h^{\rm old}_{\mu\nu}-2\xi_{(\mu,\nu)}
$$

Same gauge law in symmetrization-bracket notation.

**Symbols:** (μν) symmetrization with factor 1/2

### (45.26) Gauge invariance of linearized Riemann · supporting · GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

$$
R^{\rm new}_{\alpha\mu\beta\nu}=R^{\rm old}_{\alpha\mu\beta\nu}-\xi_{\alpha[,\nu,\beta],\mu}+\xi_{\mu[,\nu,\beta],\alpha}-\xi_{\beta[,\mu,\alpha],\nu}+\xi_{\nu[,\mu,\alpha],\beta}
$$

Each extra term antisymmetrizes a pair of commuting partial derivatives and so vanishes; curvature is unchanged by gauge shifts.

**Symbols:** [ ] antisymmetrization over the enclosed derivative indices

### (45.27) Lorenz gauge condition · central · GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

$$
\bar h^{\mu\alpha}{}_{,\alpha}=0
$$

Coordinates chosen so the trace-reversed perturbation is divergence-free; analogue of ∂_μA^μ = 0.

### (45.28) · derivation-step · GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

$$
-\bar h_{\mu\nu,\alpha}{}^{\alpha}=16\pi T_{\mu\nu}
$$

The field equation after the gauge condition removes the divergence terms.

### (45.29) Linearized Einstein equation (Lorenz gauge) · central · GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

$$
-\partial^2\bar h_{\mu\nu}=16\pi T_{\mu\nu},\qquad \partial^2=-\partial_t^2+\vec\nabla^2
$$

Weak gravity is a linear wave equation on flat spacetime whose source is the energy-momentum tensor; in vacuum it is the free wave equation.

**Symbols:** ∂^2 d'Alembertian; G = c = 1

### (45.30) Electromagnetic retarded potential · supporting · GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)

$$
A^{\mu}(t,\vec x)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d^3y\,\frac{J^{\mu}(t-|\vec x-\vec y|,\vec y)}{|\vec x-\vec y|}
$$

Causal solution of -∂^2A^μ = J^μ, used as the template for gravity.

**Symbols:** J^μ four-current (Heaviside-Lorentz units, c = 1)

### (45.31) Retarded solution for h-bar · central · GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)

$$
\bar h_{\mu\nu}(t,\vec x)=4G\int d^3y\,\frac{T_{\mu\nu}(t-|\vec x-\vec y|,\vec y)}{|\vec x-\vec y|}
$$

The perturbation at a field point is an integral over the source at retarded time; basis for gravitational-wave generation in ch46.

**Symbols:** G restored; c = 1

### (45.32) · derivation-step · GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)

$$
\bar h_{\mu\nu}(\vec x)=4G\int d^3y\,\frac{T_{\mu\nu}(\vec y)}{|\vec x-\vec y|}
$$

Static limit of the retarded solution.

### (45.33) · derivation-step · GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)

$$
\bar h_{00}(\vec x)=4G\int d^3y\,\frac{\rho(\vec y)}{|\vec x-\vec y|}
$$

Only the energy density matters for a slow static source.

**Symbols:** ρ mass density

### (45.34) Newtonian potential · supporting · GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)

$$
\Phi(\vec x)=-G\int d^3y\,\frac{\rho(\vec y)}{|\vec x-\vec y|}
$$

Integral solution of Poisson's equation for Newtonian gravity.

### (45.35) · supporting · GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)

$$
\bar h_{00}(\vec x)=-4\Phi(\vec x)
$$

The time-time trace-reversed perturbation is minus four times the Newtonian potential.

### (45.36) · supporting · GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)

$$
h_{00}=h_{11}=h_{22}=h_{33}=-2\Phi
$$

Undoing the trace reversal perturbs time and all three space directions equally.

### (45.37) Weak-field metric · central · GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)

$$
ds^2=-(1+2\Phi)dt^2+(1-2\Phi)(dx^2+dy^2+dz^2)
$$

Line element of a static weak gravitational field, reproducing eqn 5.22 and the Newtonian limit.

**Symbols:** Φ Newtonian potential (negative near masses)

### (45.38) Gravitomagnetic vector potential · supporting · GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)

$$
A_g^i(\vec x)=-G\int d^3y\,\frac{J^i(\vec y)}{|\vec x-\vec y|},\qquad J^i=\rho u^i
$$

Mass currents source a vector potential exactly as mass density sources Φ.

**Symbols:** J^i mass-current density

### (45.39) · supporting · GA §45.3 p.491 (pdf 508)

$$
\bar h_{0i}(\vec x)=-4A_{gi}(\vec x)
$$

The time-space perturbation is proportional to the gravitomagnetic potential. Sign caution: with η_00 = -1, lowering the time index flips sign, so with the book's own definitions h-bar^{0i} = -4A_g^i while h-bar_{0i} = +4A_g^i.

**Symbols:** h_{0i} = h-bar_{0i} since η_{0i} = 0

### (45.40) Gravitational Poisson equations · supporting · GA §45.3 p.491 (pdf 508)

$$
\nabla^2\Phi=4\pi G\rho\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nabla^2\vec A_g=4\pi G\vec J
$$

Static limit of the wave equation for the scalar and vector gravitational potentials.

### (45.41) Electromagnetic Poisson equations (SI) · supporting · GA §45.3 p.491 (pdf 508)

$$
\nabla^2V=-\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nabla^2\vec A=-\mu_0\vec J
$$

Comparison equations; the opposite sign reflects repulsion of like charges versus attraction of masses.

**Symbols:** V electric potential; A magnetic vector potential

### (45.42) Gravitational Maxwell equations (static) · central · GA §45.3 p.491 (pdf 508)

$$
\vec\nabla\cdot\vec E_g=-4\pi G\rho,\quad \vec\nabla\cdot\vec B_g=0,\quad \vec\nabla\times\vec E_g=0,\quad \vec\nabla\times\vec B_g=-4\pi G\vec J
$$

Field equations for the gravitoelectric E_g = -∇Φ and gravitomagnetic B_g = ∇×A_g fields.

-\partial^2\bar h_{\mu\nu}=16\pi T_{\mu\nu}

Restatement of eqn 45.29.

h_{\mu\nu}\to h_{\mu\nu}-2\xi_{(\mu,\nu)}

Restatement of eqns 45.24-45.25.

### (45.45) Trace equation (Exercise 45.2) · supporting · GA p.492 (pdf 509)

$$
\partial^2 h=16\pi T
$$

Tracing the gauge-fixed equation relates the wave operator on the trace of h to the trace of T.

**Symbols:** T = T^α_α

### (45.46) Field equation for h with trace-reversed source · supporting · GA p.492 (pdf 509)

$$
-\partial^2 h_{\mu\nu}=16\pi\left(T_{\mu\nu}-\tfrac12\eta_{\mu\nu}T\right)
$$

Equivalent form in which the trace reversal is moved onto the source.

### (45.48) Static solution for h · supporting · GA p.492 (pdf 509)

$$
h_{\mu\nu}(\vec x)=2\int d\Sigma\,\frac{2T_{\mu\nu}-\eta_{\mu\nu}T}{|\vec x-\vec x'|}
$$

Each component of h for a stationary source is a Coulomb-like integral over its own effective source density.

**Symbols:** dΣ 3-volume element at x'

### (45.49) Active gravitational mass density · supporting · GA p.492 (pdf 509)

$$
\rho_g=2T_{tt}-\eta_{tt}T\approx\rho+3p
$$

Pressure contributes to the source of the Newtonian-like potential.

**Symbols:** p pressure

### (45.50) Gravitomagnetic current density (Exercise 45.3) · supporting · GA p.492 (pdf 509)

$$
\Pi_i=-T_{ti}+\tfrac12\eta_{ti}T\approx(\rho+p)u_i
$$

For a slow perfect fluid, pressure adds to density in the mass current that sources the off-diagonal metric terms.

**Symbols:** u_i velocity 1-form components

### (45.51) Curvature energy density (Exercise 45.3) · supporting · GA p.492 (pdf 509)

$$
\rho_c=2T_{ii}-\eta_{ii}T\approx\rho-p\quad(\text{no sum; book misprints the indices as }ti)
$$

The source of the spatial diagonal of h; with 45.49 it shows time and space parts of the metric respond differently to pressure.

**Symbols:** p pressure

### (45.52) Weak-field geodesic equation with gravitomagnetic term · supporting · GA p.492 (pdf 509)

$$
\frac{d^2x^i}{dt^2}\approx\eta^{ij}\left(-\partial_j\Phi+F_{jk}v^k\right),\qquad \Phi=-\tfrac12h_{tt},\quad F_{ij}=\partial_ih_{tj}-\partial_jh_{ti}
$$

Slow particles feel a Newtonian force plus a Lorentz-like velocity-dependent force from the antisymmetrized derivative of h_ti.

**Symbols:** v^k coordinate velocity

### (45.54) Potential sourced by the active density (Exercise 45.4) · supporting · GA p.492 (pdf 509)

$$
\Phi(\vec x)=-\int d^3x'\,\frac{\rho_g(\vec x')}{|\vec x-\vec x'|}
$$

The Newtonian-like potential felt by slow particles is sourced by ρ + 3p rather than by ρ alone.

**Symbols:** G = 1

### (45.55) Weak-field metric of a rotating body · supporting · GA p.492 (pdf 509)

$$
g_{\mu\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}-(1+2\phi)&\zeta_1&\zeta_2&\zeta_3\\ \zeta_1&1-2\phi&0&0\\ \zeta_2&0&1-2\phi&0\\ \zeta_3&0&0&1-2\phi\end{pmatrix},\quad \phi=-\frac{M}{r},\quad \zeta_i=\frac{2}{r^3}\varepsilon_{ijk}x^jJ^k
$$

Linearized exterior metric of a mass M with angular momentum J; the off-diagonal ζ terms carry frame dragging.

**Symbols:** J^k angular momentum of the source; ε_ijk Levi-Civita symbol

### (45.61) Constant-length spin 3-vector (Exercise 45.9) · derivation-step · GA p.493 (pdf 510)

$$
\vec S=(1-\phi)\vec\Sigma+\tfrac12(\vec v\cdot\vec\Sigma)\vec v
$$

Rescaling the spatial spin components removes the change in length caused by the potential and motion, so the remaining evolution is a pure rotation.

**Symbols:** Σ rescaled spin; φ = -M/r

### (45.65) Gyroscope precession equation · supporting · GA p.493 (pdf 510)

$$
\frac{d\vec\Sigma}{dt}=\vec\Omega\times\vec\Sigma,\qquad \vec\Omega=-\tfrac12\vec\nabla\times\vec\zeta-\tfrac32\vec v\times\vec\nabla\phi
$$

A constant-length spin vector carried in orbit precesses about Ω (eqns 45.65-45.66).

**Symbols:** Σ rescaled spin 3-vector

### (45.67) Lense-Thirring plus geodetic precession rate · central · GA p.493 (pdf 510)

$$
\vec\Omega=\frac{1}{r^3}\left[\frac{3(\vec J\cdot\vec r)\vec r}{r^2}-\vec J\right]+\frac{3M}{2r^3}\vec r\times\vec v
$$

First term: frame dragging by the body's spin, with dipole form. Second term: geodetic (de Sitter-Fokker) precession from orbital motion through curved space.

**Symbols:** G = c = 1

## Worked examples

### Example · intro · GA §45.1 p.486 (pdf 503)

**Problem:** Show that the small correction h to the Minkowski metric transforms as a tensor under Lorentz transformations.

**Method:** Write the transformed metric components with two Lorentz matrices acting on η + h, use the invariance of η under Lorentz transformations to cancel the flat parts, and read off the law for h.

**Key insight:** Because the background is Lorentz invariant, everything left over must carry the full tensor transformation, so h is a legitimate tensor field on flat spacetime, but only for transformations that preserve η.

**Result:** h_{α'β'} = Λ^μ_{α'}Λ^ν_{β'}h_{μν} (eqn 45.5).

**Concepts:** Lorentz covariance of the metric perturbation, flat-background field picture of gravity

### Example 45.1 · standard · GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504)

**Problem:** Express the linearized Riemann and Ricci tensors in terms of derivatives of the metric perturbation.

**Method:** Substitute the linear Christoffel symbols into Riemann with ΓΓ terms dropped, lower the first index with η, reorder the second derivatives; contract indices to get Ricci, naming the trace h.

**Key insight:** At first order, curvature is just second derivatives of h arranged with Riemann's symmetries; no products appear, so superposition holds.

**Result:** R_αμβν = ½(h_μβ,να + h_να,μβ - h_μν,αβ - h_αβ,μν) and R_μν = ½(h_μ^α_,να + h_ν^α_,μα - □h_μν - h_,μν) (eqns 45.8-45.9).

**Concepts:** linearized Riemann tensor, linearized Ricci tensor, trace of the metric perturbation

### Example · standard · GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504)

**Problem:** Assemble the linearized Einstein equation and rewrite it using the trace-reversed perturbation.

**Method:** Form R from the Ricci tensor, build G = R_μν - ½η_μνR, multiply by 2; then substitute h = h-bar - ½η h-bar and use h-bar = -h to collect terms (details left to Exercise 45.1).

**Key insight:** The trace terms that clutter the equation in h cancel when written in h-bar, leaving one wave-operator term and three divergence terms.

**Result:** Eqns 45.10, 45.14 and 45.15.

**Concepts:** linearized Einstein equation, trace-reversed metric perturbation

### Example 45.2 · standard · GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505)

**Problem:** Verify how the metric perturbation changes under the infinitesimal coordinate shift x' = x + ξ.

**Method:** Invert the map to first order, compute the Jacobian ∂x/∂x', multiply two Jacobians keeping terms linear in ∂ξ, contract with η + h, lower the index on ξ with η, and discard products of small quantities.

**Key insight:** A coordinate shift that is negligible for ordinary fields is not negligible for h, because h is itself first-order small; the change is the symmetrized gradient of ξ.

**Result:** h_new = h_old - ξ_μ,ν - ξ_ν,μ (eqn 45.24).

**Concepts:** infinitesimal coordinate transformation, gauge transformation of the metric perturbation

### Example 45.3 · standard · GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

**Problem:** Show that the linearized Riemann tensor is unchanged by the gauge transformation of h.

**Method:** Insert h - 2ξ_(μ,ν) into the linear Riemann formula and group the ξ terms into third derivatives antisymmetrized over pairs of derivative indices.

**Key insight:** Partial derivatives commute, so every antisymmetrized pair vanishes: curvature, hence physics, is gauge invariant, and the gauge can be chosen freely.

**Result:** R_new = R_old (eqn 45.26); verification assigned as Exercise 45.1(c).

**Concepts:** gauge invariance of the linearized Riemann tensor, gauge freedom in linearized gravity

### Example · standard · GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

**Problem:** Reduce the linearized Einstein equation using the Lorenz gauge and write its solution.

**Method:** Impose h-bar^{μα}_{,α} = 0 so three terms of 45.15 drop; recognise the remaining operator as the d'Alembertian; compare with -∂^2A = J and borrow the retarded potential, replacing 1 by 16πG.

**Key insight:** Gravity in this limit is a sourced wave equation of exactly the electromagnetic type, so the whole machinery of retarded potentials transfers with the factor 4G.

**Result:** -∂^2 h-bar_μν = 16πT_μν and h-bar_μν = 4G∫d^3y T_μν(t - |x-y|, y)/|x-y| (eqns 45.29, 45.31).

**Concepts:** Lorenz gauge, linearized Einstein equation in Lorenz gauge, retarded potential

### Example 45.4 · intro · GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)

**Problem:** Check the retarded solution against Newtonian gravity for a static, slowly moving mass distribution.

**Method:** Drop time dependence; keep only T_00 ≈ ρ; compare the resulting integral with the Newtonian potential integral to get h-bar_00 = -4Φ; undo the trace reversal (trace h-bar = 4Φ) to find every diagonal component of h is -2Φ; write the line element.

**Key insight:** Trace reversal spreads a single time-time source equally into time and space parts of the metric, so gravity curves space as well as time even in the Newtonian limit.

**Result:** ds^2 = -(1+2Φ)dt^2 + (1-2Φ)(dx^2+dy^2+dz^2), matching eqn 5.22 and Example 14.2 (eqn 45.37).

**Concepts:** weak-field metric, trace-reversed metric perturbation, retarded potential

### Example 45.5 · standard · GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)

**Problem:** Keep the next order in v/c for a dust source and develop the gravitational analogue of magnetism.

**Method:** Order T^{00}, T^{0i}, T^{ij} by powers of v/c; retain T^{0i} = ρu^i; define A_g by the same integral as Φ with mass current in place of density and relate it to h-bar_{0i}; drop time derivatives in the wave equation to get Poisson equations; define E_g and B_g and take divergence and curl.

**Key insight:** Moving mass produces a magnetic-like gravitational field; the resulting equations are Maxwell's equations with reversed signs, and the v × B_g force is the origin of frame dragging.

**Result:** ∇^2Φ = 4πGρ, ∇^2A_g = 4πGJ, and the static gravitational Maxwell equations (eqns 45.38-45.42).

**Concepts:** post-Newtonian ordering of dust sources, gravitoelectromagnetism, gravitational Maxwell equations, Lense-Thirring effect


## Analogies and intuitions

### Linearized gravity as electromagnetism with a tensor potential → linearized Einstein equation in Lorenz gauge · strong · GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

Each gravitational step is paired with its EM counterpart: A_μ ↔ h_μν, gauge shift by a gradient ↔ shift by a symmetrized gradient, Lorenz gauge ↔ divergence-free h-bar, -∂^2A = J ↔ -∂^2 h-bar = 16πT, retarded potential ↔ retarded h-bar.

**Where it breaks down:** The gravitational field is rank 2 and couples to all energy (including its own at higher order); the analogy is exact only at linear order, and polarization, radiation multipole order and sign of the force differ.

**App idea:** A two-column 'Rosetta stone' panel: learner clicks a step (gauge freedom, gauge condition, field equation, solution) and both EM and gravity versions highlight, with the differing index structure animated.

### Weak gravity as a field painted onto a flat stage → flat-background field picture of gravity · useful · GA §45.1 p.486 (pdf 503)

Invites the reader to picture Minkowski spacetime filled with a symmetric tensor field, like any classical field, so familiar field-theory methods apply.

**Where it breaks down:** The authors themselves label it a fiction: true spacetime is curved, h is not a tensor under general coordinate changes, and the picture breaks down beyond first order.

**App idea:** Toggle between a rigid flat grid with coloured ellipse glyphs for h at each point and the corresponding slightly warped grid, with a slider for field strength showing when the two pictures disagree.

### Gauge as in the gauge of wires and rails → gauge freedom in linearized gravity · weak · GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505)

A margin note traces Weyl's word 'gauge' to a choice of measuring scale, relating it to choosing the coordinate shift ξ.

**Where it breaks down:** Weyl's original scale-gauge idea was physically abandoned; the modern meaning is redundancy of description, not a literal length standard.

### Mass currents create gravitomagnetism like charge currents create magnetism → gravitoelectromagnetism · strong · GA §45.3 p.491 (pdf 508)

Moving or spinning masses are said to produce a magnetic-type gravitational field that exerts a velocity-dependent force, explaining frame dragging around a rotating body.

**Where it breaks down:** Signs are reversed (like masses attract), the coupling carries extra numerical factors (4 or 2 depending on normalization), and there is no gravitational analogue of opposite charges.

**App idea:** Spinning sphere with adjustable angular momentum; field lines of B_g drawn as a dipole; test particles and gyroscopes placed nearby show drag and precession, with a switch to reverse the EM analogue.

### Internal gauge rotation versus relabelling spacetime points → infinitesimal coordinate transformation · useful · GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505)

A margin note contrasts ch44's gauge change of an internal phase at fixed points with the gravitational case, where the coordinates move and points must be named abstractly.

**Where it breaks down:** Both are redundancies, but diffeomorphism gauge symmetry acts on the base manifold itself, which has deep consequences (no local gauge-invariant observables) not discussed here.

**App idea:** Side-by-side animation: left, phase arrows rotating at fixed lattice points; right, the lattice points themselves sliding by ξ while the painted field components update to compensate.


## Misconceptions addressed

### Linearized gravity shows that spacetime is really flat with a gravitational field on top. · GA §45.1 p.486 (pdf 503)

**Correction:** The split is an approximation; spacetime is curved, and h behaves as a tensor only under Lorentz transformations of the background. It is a computational device, not a statement about reality.

**Why tempting:** The formalism looks exactly like field theory on Minkowski space and the book itself recommends picturing it that way.

### The components of h are physical observables, so different h fields mean different gravitational situations. · GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

**Correction:** An infinitesimal coordinate change alters h by a symmetrized gradient without changing the curvature; h fields related this way describe the same physics.

**Why tempting:** In Newtonian gravity the potential feels directly physical and h_00 is tied to it.

### An infinitesimal coordinate shift is too small to matter for anything. · GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505)

**Correction:** It barely affects order-one fields but changes h at the same order as h itself, which is why it can be used to impose a gauge condition.

**Why tempting:** Small changes usually have small effects; one forgets that h is already a small quantity.

### Weak-field (Newtonian) gravity only affects the time part of the metric. · GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)

**Correction:** Trace reversal of h-bar_00 = -4Φ gives equal perturbations -2Φ in time and all space components; space is curved too.

**Why tempting:** Newtonian motion of slow particles depends only on g_00, and early GR arguments emphasise that component.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The Lorenz gauge is an extra physical assumption about the source or the field. · GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505)

**Correction:** It is always attainable by a suitable choice of ξ and changes nothing physical, just as in electromagnetism.

**Why tempting:** The chapter first presents the condition as something it would be 'helpful' to have, which can sound like a simplifying assumption.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Whether a situation is Newtonian is an absolute property of the system. · GA §45.1 p.485 (pdf 502)

**Correction:** The slow-motion condition depends on the observer; a margin note points out that not all observers agree a velocity is in the Newtonian limit.

**Why tempting:** Field strength via GM/(c^2 r) feels frame independent, masking the velocity part of the criterion.

### Only mass density produces gravity in the weak-field limit; pressure does not gravitate. · GA p.492 (pdf 509)

**Correction:** The effective source of the potential is ρ + 3p; pressure contributes, and negative pressure (vacuum energy) can make gravity repulsive.

**Why tempting:** Newton's theory sources gravity with mass density alone and the chapter's main examples use pressureless dust.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Gyroscope in orbit around a spinning Earth · GA p.493 (pdf 510)

**Setup:** A freely falling gyroscope orbits a rotating mass; its spin is parallel transported through the weak-field metric including the off-diagonal rotation terms.

**Lesson:** The spin direction precesses: one part from orbital motion through curved space (geodetic) and one from the Earth's rotation dragging frames (Lense-Thirring), the latter with the shape of a magnetic dipole field.

**App idea:** 3D orbit around a spinning globe with a gyroscope arrow; sliders for orbit inclination, radius and Earth's spin; separate trails show accumulated geodetic and frame-dragging tilts, exaggerated by a selectable factor.

### Relabelling points by a tiny shift · GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505)

**Setup:** Every spacetime point is given new coordinates displaced by a small vector field ξ, while the physical geometry is left untouched.

**Lesson:** The numbers in h change but the curvature does not, so the freedom can be spent to make the equations simpler.

**App idea:** Draw a vector field ξ on a 2D grid; the grid warps, a heat map of h components updates, while a separate curvature readout stays fixed.


## Applications and observations

- **When is gravity relativistic? Size of GM/(c^2 r)** (numerical-estimate, GA §45.1 p.485 (pdf 502)): The chapter supplies the criterion ε = GM/(c^2 r); the numbers here are our own estimates for tutoring, not from the book. Key numbers: Our estimates: Earth's surface ~7e-10; Sun's surface ~2e-6; white dwarf ~1e-4; neutron star ~0.1-0.2; Schwarzschild horizon 0.5.
- **Size of dust source components** (numerical-estimate, GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)): Momentum density is suppressed by v/c and stresses by (v/c)^2 relative to energy density, justifying which terms to keep. Illustrative numbers are our own. Key numbers: Our estimate: Earth's orbital speed gives v/c ~1e-4, so T^{ij}/T^{00} ~1e-8.
- **Gyroscope precession around the Earth (Lense-Thirring and geodetic)** (experiment, GA p.493 (pdf 510)): Exercises 45.6-45.9 derive the precession rate of an orbiting gyroscope, following Ryder (2009). The book does not quote measurements here; Gravity Probe B (2004-2005 flight, results 2011) is the experiment that tested both terms. Key numbers: Not in chapter (external): for a ~640 km polar orbit, predicted geodetic ~6606 mas/yr and frame dragging ~39 mas/yr; Gravity Probe B measured about 6602 ± 18 and 37 ± 7 mas/yr.
- **Gravitational waves as vacuum solutions** (observation, GA §45.3 p.491 (pdf 508)): The homogeneous wave equation for h-bar predicts gravitational waves; developed in ch46.
- **Repulsive gravity of vacuum energy** (numerical-estimate, GA p.492 (pdf 509)): Exercise 45.5 shows negative active mass density for p = -ρ and a quadratic repulsive potential, a linearized view of cosmic acceleration. Key numbers: Our evaluation: Φ = -Λr^2/6.

## Historical notes

- **Hermann Weyl:** Introduced the term 'gauge' for a choice of length scale in coordinates, borrowing from the gauge of wires and railway tracks. — Origin of the vocabulary now used for all redundancy symmetries, linking gravity's coordinate freedom to electromagnetic gauge freedom (Weyl's proposal dates from around 1918, not stated in the book). (GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505))
- **Josef Lense, Hans Thirring:** Named as the source of the frame-dragging effect in which a rotating mass drags nearby objects and gyroscopes around with it. — Gives physical content to gravitomagnetism; derived in Exercise 45.9 (published 1918, not stated in the book). (GA §45.3 p.491 (pdf 508))
- **Willem de Sitter, Adriaan Fokker:** Named for geodetic precession of a gyroscope orbiting a non-rotating mass. — The larger of the two gyroscope precession effects around the Earth. (GA p.493 (pdf 510))

## Notation and conventions

- **Units:** Geometrized units G = c = 1 throughout the derivation; G is restored in the retarded integrals and Examples 45.4-45.5, and a margin note restores c in T^{00} = ρc^2, T^{0i} = ρcu^i. — The exercises revert to G = c = 1 (e.g. φ = -M/r). (GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507))
- **Metric signature:** (-,+,+,+): η = diag(-1,1,1,1), so ∂^2 = -∂_t^2 + ∇^2 and g_00 = -(1+2Φ). (GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506))
- **Index gymnastics in linearized theory:** All indices raised and lowered with η; comma notation extended to raised indices, e.g. h_αβ^{,μ} = η^{μν}h_αβ,ν. — Placement of commas relative to raised indices is typographically delicate in eqns 45.9-45.15. (GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504))
- **Bar notation:** An overbar denotes trace reversal: X-bar_μν = X_μν - ½η_μν X. The Einstein tensor is the trace-reversed Ricci tensor. — Equation numbers 45.11-45.12 sit in the margin and 45.13 in the main text, so they appear out of order. (GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504))
- **Symmetrization and antisymmetrization brackets:** (μν) denotes symmetrization with a factor 1/2, [ ] antisymmetrization; ξ_(μ,ν) = ½(ξ_μ,ν + ξ_ν,μ). — In eqn 45.26 the brackets enclose comma-derivative indices, an unusual usage. (GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506))
- **Riemann tensor convention:** R^α_βμν = Γ^α_νβ,μ - Γ^α_μβ,ν + ΓΓ terms; Ricci by contraction on the first and third indices. — Matches MTW; the linear results 45.8 and 45.9 follow this convention. (GA §45.2 p.487 (pdf 504))
- **d'Alembertian:** Written ∂^2 (not □): ∂^2 = -∂^2/∂t^2 + ∇^2. (GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506))
- **Gauge direction:** New coordinates are x' = x + ξ, giving h → h - 2ξ_(μ,ν); other texts use x' = x - ξ and a plus sign. (GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505))
- **Points versus coordinates:** Calligraphic P labels a point of the manifold independent of its coordinate values, needed because coordinates themselves are transformed. (GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505))
- **Gravitoelectromagnetic symbols:** Φ Newtonian potential (negative near mass), A_g gravitomagnetic vector potential, E_g = -∇Φ, B_g = ∇×A_g, J = ρu mass current. The exercises use lower-case φ and ζ_i for the rotating-body metric. — Normalizations differ from much of the GEM literature (e.g. Mashhoon), which uses factors of 2 instead of 4. (GA §45.3 p.491 (pdf 508))

- index manipulation to verify the linearized Einstein tensor and gauge invariance of curvature
- taking traces and inverting trace reversal
- solving Poisson-type equations with Green's function integrals
- evaluating perfect-fluid energy-momentum components at low velocity
- deriving an equation of motion from the geodesic equation in a weak stationary metric
- computing Christoffel symbols of a linearized rotating-body metric
- parallel transport of a spin vector and extraction of a precession vector
- vector calculus for dipole fields

- **45.2** (standard): Trace the gauge-fixed equation, rewrite it as a wave equation for h with the trace-reversed source, and explain the Coulomb-integral form of the static solution for h. — *Gives the complementary form of the field equation where each metric component has its own effective source.* Skills: traces, trace reversal, Green's function for Laplacian
- **45.3** (standard): For a slow perfect fluid, evaluate three source combinations built from T and show they reduce to ρ+3p, (ρ+p)u_i and ρ-p. — *Shows pressure gravitates and identifies what sources the spatial part of the metric.* Skills: perfect fluid tensor, low-velocity approximation
- **45.4** (standard): Derive a Lorentz-force-like geodesic equation for slow motion in a stationary weak field and relate its potential to the active density. — *Makes the gravitomagnetic force concrete and fixes the numerical factor the chapter glosses.* Skills: geodesic equation, linearized Christoffel symbols
- **45.5** (intro): Show that a universe of vacuum energy has negative active density, repulsive gravity, and a quadratic potential sourced by Λ; find the coefficient. — *Short, striking link from linearized GR to dark energy and cosmic acceleration.* Skills: equation of state, Poisson equation in spherical symmetry
- **45.6** (standard): For the linearized metric of a rotating mass, show the inverse metric just flips the sign of φ at this order and compute the leading Christoffel symbols. — *Sets up the frame-dragging calculation following Ryder (2009).* Skills: inverse metric to first order, Christoffel symbols
- **45.7** (challenging): Define a spin 1-form orthogonal to the four-velocity, derive its parallel-transport equation in coordinate time, and specialize to the Earth's field. — *Bridges parallel transport to a measurable gyroscope effect.* Skills: orthogonality constraints, parallel transport
- **45.9** (challenging): Rescale the spin to a constant-length 3-vector, show it obeys a pure precession equation, and evaluate the precession vector as a Lense-Thirring dipole term plus a geodetic term. — *Culminates in the gyroscope precession formula tested by Gravity Probe B.* Skills: perturbative bookkeeping to fixed order, vector identities, dipole fields

## Teaching gems

### Show why h is a tensor in one line: η is Lorentz invariant, so whatever is left of g must carry the tensor law, then immediately flag the limits of that statement. · GA §45.1 p.486 (pdf 503)

**Why it works:** Gives a concrete justification rather than an assertion and, by adding the caveat at once, prevents the flat-space-is-real misconception from forming.

**App idea:** Interactive boost slider acting on a displayed 4×4 h matrix and on η: η stays fixed, h components mix; then a 'general coordinate change' toggle shows η components changing too, breaking the clean split.

### State the gauge condition you wish you had, show how much it would simplify, then prove you are entitled to it. · GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505)

**Why it works:** Motivation precedes technicality, so the gauge-transformation derivation is read as purposeful rather than arbitrary.

**App idea:** Show eqn 45.15 with its four terms as tiles; the learner toggles the Lorenz condition and three tiles fade, leaving the wave operator.

### Explain why a tiny coordinate shift matters for h but not for other fields: h is itself first-order small, so an O(∂ξ) change is comparable to it. · GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505)

**Why it works:** Resolves the natural objection that infinitesimal transformations cannot do anything, and clarifies orders of smallness.

**App idea:** Plot a smooth O(1) scalar field and a small h component along a line; apply a slider-controlled ξ; show fractional changes of each side by side.

### Build every gravitational step as a translation of an electromagnetic step (gauge shift, Lorenz gauge, wave equation, retarded potential, Poisson equations, Maxwell equations). · GA §45.3 p.489 (pdf 506)

**Why it works:** Learners reuse well-practised EM intuition and see precisely where gravity differs (rank, signs, factors).

**App idea:** A 'Rosetta stone' table where each row is clickable; a voice tutor can quiz by hiding one column and asking the learner to fill in the gravitational analogue.

### Check the new linear theory by recovering the old Newtonian weak-field metric, showing that trace reversal spreads the time-time source equally into space. · GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)

**Why it works:** Confirms the formalism against a trusted result and exposes a non-Newtonian feature (spatial curvature) hidden in a familiar metric.

**App idea:** Matrix animation: start with h-bar = diag(-4Φ,0,0,0), press 'trace reverse', watch the -½ηh-bar term fill in to give diag(-2Φ,-2Φ,-2Φ,-2Φ); a slider for Φ updates an embedded light-bending comparison.

### Organise source terms by powers of v/c to decide what to keep, then discover gravitomagnetism at the next order. · GA §45.3 p.490 (pdf 507)

**Why it works:** Teaches systematic approximation and makes new physics emerge naturally rather than being bolted on.

**App idea:** Bar chart of |T^{00}|, |T^{0i}|, |T^{ij}| on a log scale with a velocity slider; toggling each order switches on the corresponding metric component and field (Φ, A_g, stress terms) in a companion view.

### Contrast gauge transformations of an internal variable (phase at fixed points) with gauge transformations that move the coordinate labels themselves. · GA §45.3 p.488 (pdf 505)

**Why it works:** Clarifies what is genuinely new about gravity's gauge symmetry for readers coming from electromagnetism and ch44.

**App idea:** Split-screen animation: fixed lattice with rotating phase arrows versus a lattice whose nodes slide by ξ while tensor glyphs compensate.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Eqn 45.7 as printed has both connection terms differentiated with respect to ν with lower indices merely swapped; since Γ is symmetric in its lower indices this is identically zero. The first term should be Γ^λ_{μν,β}.** — A careful reader gets zero curvature and cannot reach eqn 45.8. *Suggestion:* Present the corrected R_αμβν ≈ η_αλ(Γ^λ_{μν,β} - Γ^λ_{μβ,ν}), derived from the footnoted Riemann formula, and verify it gives 45.8.
- **Sign of eqn 45.39: with the book's definitions (h-bar_μν = 4G∫T_μν/r and A_g^i = -G∫J^i/r), lowering the time index introduces a minus sign, so h-bar_{0i} = +4A_g^i and h-bar^{0i} = -4A_g^i; the printed h-bar_{0i} = -4A_gi only works if an extra sign is absorbed somewhere.** — Sign errors propagate into the direction of the gravitomagnetic force and frame dragging. *Suggestion:* Keep index positions explicit, state the convention for A_g, and check the final direction of frame dragging (co-rotating) as a physical sanity test.
- **The text says a mass current exerts a v×B_g force, omitting the numerical factor: with h_{0i} proportional to 4A_g, the geodesic equation (Exercise 45.4) gives an acceleration with 4v×B_g (sign depending on convention).** — Learners may over-trust the EM analogy and get frame-dragging magnitudes wrong by a factor of 4; the GEM literature using other normalizations adds confusion. *Suggestion:* Derive the force from Exercise 45.4 explicitly and tabulate normalization conventions (factor 4 here, factor 2 in Mashhoon-style GEM).
- **The existence of a gauge transformation reaching the Lorenz gauge is asserted but not shown (one must solve ∂^2ξ^μ = h-bar^{μα}_{,α}), and the residual gauge freedom is not mentioned.** — The gauge condition can seem like an assumption; later the transverse-traceless gauge in ch46 depends on the leftover freedom. *Suggestion:* Add the two-line computation of how the divergence of h-bar changes, and note that ξ with ∂^2ξ = 0 remains available.
- **Minor printed typos (confirmed on the page renders): a comma in η_{ρ',σ'} and missing commas on ξ in eqn 45.23; a stray prime on the Kronecker delta in 45.21; mismatched index in Exercise 45.7(a) (should be S_0 = -(dx^i/dt)S_i); a missing index on ∇φ in 45.59; 'gravitometric' for gravitomagnetic in Exercise 45.3; and 'This gauge cause' in the text after eqn 45.27.** — Slows readers checking every step and can suggest spurious derivatives of η. *Suggestion:* Use the cleaned forms recorded in key_equations and flag these when tutoring through the derivation.
- **The retarded solution is imported purely by analogy with electromagnetism; the Green's function and the consistency condition ∂_νT^{μν} = 0 with the Lorenz gauge are not discussed.** — Learners without an EM retarded-potential background have nothing to anchor eqn 45.31, and may not see why energy-momentum conservation is required. *Suggestion:* Show that taking the divergence of the gauge-fixed equation forces conservation of T at linear order, and animate the light-cone origin of retardation.
- **The Newtonian-limit criterion conflates weak fields and slow motion; gravitational waves are weak fields that are not slow.** — May think linearized theory requires slow sources and be confused when ch46 applies it to waves travelling at c. *Suggestion:* Separate the two small parameters explicitly: |h| ≪ 1 for linearization, v/c ≪ 1 for the Newtonian/quasistatic approximation.
- **The gravitomagnetic Maxwell equations are obtained by dropping time derivatives and implicitly using ∇·A_g = 0 (from the Lorenz gauge for stationary sources), which is not stated.** — The curl equation for B_g appears without justification. *Suggestion:* Add a one-line derivation using ∇×∇×A = ∇(∇·A) - ∇^2A with the gauge condition.
- **The summary calls h itself 'the weak-field metric', while eqn 45.37 (the full line element) is also called the weak-field metric.** — Terminology clash between the perturbation and the metric it perturbs. *Suggestion:* Consistently call h the metric perturbation and reserve 'weak-field metric' for η + h.
- **Exercise 45.3(c) prints the curvature energy density with mixed indices (t,i). A time-space component of a static fluid's source vanishes and cannot equal ρ - p; the spatial diagonal component is intended.** — A student following the printed definition gets zero or a vector, not the quoted ρ - p, and may doubt their perfect-fluid algebra. *Suggestion:* Tell learners to evaluate 2T_xx - η_xx T (any one spatial diagonal entry): with T = -ρ + 3p this is 2p + ρ - 3p = ρ - p.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'Newton's gravity is a single scalar potential obeying Poisson's equation. Einstein's is a nonlinear equation for ten metric functions. How could the first possibly be a limit of the second?' Then introduce g = η + h.
- Check the learner's electromagnetism first: can they state A → A - ∂χ, the Lorenz gauge and the retarded potential? If yes, run the whole chapter as an EM translation; if not, teach those three EM facts briefly before linearizing.
- Order of explanation: small parameters (ε and v/c) → metric split and Lorentz covariance with the 'useful fiction' caveat → drop ΓΓ terms → trace reversal → gauge transformation of h → curvature invariance → Lorenz gauge → wave equation → Newtonian check → gravitomagnetism.
- Use the corrected form of eqn 45.7; if a learner reports getting zero, praise the check and explain the misprint.
- Check for understanding: ask why an infinitesimal coordinate shift changes h at the same order as h, but not the curvature.
- Check for understanding: ask the learner to compute the trace of h-bar and to undo trace reversal on diag(-4Φ,0,0,0), arriving at -2Φ on every diagonal entry.
- Common question: 'Is spacetime flat in linearized gravity?' Answer: no; we compute as if on flat spacetime, but curvature (R linear in h) is physical and gauge invariant.
- Common question: 'Why trace reversal?' Answer: it is exactly the operation that turns Ricci into Einstein; in the h-bar variable the gauge condition and the field equation both become simple.
- Common question: 'Is gravitomagnetism real?' Point to frame dragging and gyroscope precession (Exercises 45.6-45.9) and to Gravity Probe B's measurement, stating explicitly that the numbers come from outside the chapter.
- Be careful with signs and factors in gravitomagnetism; state the normalization being used and avoid asserting a v×B_g force without its factor.
- For advanced learners, preview ch46: in vacuum the equation is a free wave equation; residual gauge freedom will allow transverse-traceless gauge.
- For novices, skip Examples 45.2-45.3 index details; keep the pictures (relabelling points; curvature unchanged) and the final rules h → h - 2ξ_(μ,ν) and -∂^2 h-bar = 16πT.
