# GA ch32 · Differential forms

> Generates the whole family of differential forms from 1-forms with the wedge product, pictures 2-forms as oriented tubes and 3-forms as cells, fixes the 1/p! and ordered-index component conventions, builds bivectors and p-vectors out of the signed area of a parallelogram, and pairs a p-form with a p-vector to get a number read as a count of tubes through an oriented patch.

**Pages:** printed 334–339 · pdf 351–356 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

Algebra is light (antisymmetrization, Kronecker deltas, determinants), and the sheet/tube/cell pictures give an unusually concrete entry point. What trips learners is convention bookkeeping: 1/2 and 1/p! factors, ordered versus free sums, a pairing normalized differently from contraction, and the unstated definition of wedging higher forms. Signs of orientation are never pinned to the pictures. Exercise 32.2 needs Part II covariant-derivative skills and a signature convention, making it harder than the chapter itself.

## Role in the book

Second chapter of Part V, the part where the book goes back over its geometric machinery with more care. Chapter 31 re-established vectors as arrows and derivative operators and 1-forms as stacks of surfaces; this chapter uses those two ingredients to manufacture antisymmetric tensors of every rank. It is pure set-up with pictures, and it deliberately stops before any differentiation: the exterior and Lie derivatives come in Chapter 33, the connection and curvature written as forms in Chapters 34-36 (Cartan's curvature 2-forms), Hodge duality, flux and volume forms in Chapter 37, and integration of forms with Stokes' theorem in Chapter 38. It also supplies the pairing rule (eqn 32.21) reused in later exercises and the 2-form language that Chapter 42 uses for the electromagnetic field.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can state the definition of the wedge product of two 1-forms as an antisymmetrized tensor product and deduce that the wedge of a 1-form with itself vanishes.
- Reader can sketch the tube structure made by intersecting the surfaces of two 1-forms and explain how swapping the factors reverses the tube orientation.
- Reader can expand a 2-form as (1/2)B_{mu nu} omega^mu wedge omega^nu, explain why the factor 1/2 appears only for unrestricted sums, and recover B_{alpha beta} by inserting basis vectors.
- Reader can compute the components of sigma wedge tau as sigma_mu tau_nu - tau_mu sigma_nu and read off individual components of a given 2-form.
- Reader can show that feeding one vector into a 2-form leaves a 1-form and write that 1-form explicitly.
- Reader can apply the rule mu wedge nu = (-1)^{pq} nu wedge mu to reorder wedge products of forms of different degree.
- Reader can write a general p-form both with the 1/p! normalization and with ordered (vertical-bar) indices, and convert between the two.
- Reader can list every type of form that exists in three dimensions, count its independent components, and explain why forms of degree above the dimension vanish.
- Reader can compute the signed area of a parallelogram as a determinant and identify it with the xy component of the bivector u wedge v.
- Reader can evaluate the pairing of a p-form with a p-vector via the generalized Kronecker delta, carry out the 3D 2-form/bivector case, and interpret the result as a count of tubes crossing an oriented patch.
- Reader can verify that the pairing of F with c wedge d equals F(c,d) and recognize that this pairing is 1/p! times a full index contraction.
- Reader can use a bivector built from acceleration and velocity to write the Fermi transport law and show it preserves orthogonality (Exercise 32.2).

## Teaching approach

A brief, picture-led construction chapter. The authors start from the stacked-surface image of a 1-form, intersect two stacks to produce tubes, and only then introduce the wedge product as the algebra that encodes the intersection. Conventions (the 1/2 and 1/p! factors, ordered indices) are justified by slot-machine component extraction rather than asserted, and heavy bookkeeping is pushed into margin notes. The vector side is introduced through the signed area of a parallelogram, and the chapter closes by pairing forms with multivectors, presented as a tube count that generalizes counting sheets pierced by an arrow. No derivatives appear; the payoff (curvature 2-forms, integration over forms) is promised for later chapters.

**Style:** geometric-first, diagram-driven, spiral-revisit, example-driven, conversational-informal

**Narrative arc**

1. Comic epigraph about a double act, then a recap of Chapter 31 (vectors as arrows, 1-forms as repeating surfaces) and a promise that curvature is compactly a 2-form and that integrals are really integrals of forms.
2. Geometric construction: overlay the surfaces of omega^x and omega^y in Cartesian coordinates to get a lattice of tubes, and declare this an antisymmetric (0,2) tensor, the first 2-form.
3. Algebraic counterpart: define the wedge product of two 1-forms as the antisymmetrized tensor product; note at once that sigma wedge sigma = 0.
4. Interpret antisymmetry as a handedness of the tubes, pictured as circulation inside each tube.
5. Components: expand a general 2-form with a factor 1/2, justify it by inserting basis vectors (Example 32.1), find components of sigma wedge tau (Example 32.2), show a single-component example in the margin, and fill one slot to get a 1-form (Example 32.3).
6. Generalize the picture to arbitrary 1-forms: skewed, parallelepiped tubes whose shape follows the component values.
7. Step up in degree: state the (-1)^{pq} reordering rule, write a general p-form with 1/p! or ordered indices, expand a 3-form built from three 1-forms (Example 32.4) and picture it as cells.
8. Build forms from functions times wedges of coordinate differentials and list every form in three dimensions, noting that repeated factors kill a product so nothing above a 3-form survives (Example 32.5).
9. Switch to vectors: compute the signed area of a parallelogram as a determinant, identify it with a component of the bivector u wedge v, and define p-vectors.
10. Define the pairing of p-forms and p-vectors via the generalized Kronecker delta, work the 3D 2-form/bivector case as a dot-product lookalike, interpret it as counting tubes through a parallelogram, and state the identity with F(c,d) (Example 32.6).
11. Summarize, then set two exercises: verify the identity, and an out-of-sequence problem on Fermi transport expressed through the bivector A wedge u.

**Signature moves**

- Rank-raising shown as adding one more family of sheets: planes, then tubes, then cells, with the wedge product as the symbolic version of intersecting sheet families.
- Normalization conventions justified by the slot-machine view: insert basis vectors and watch the 1/2 cancel the double counting.
- Main text stays geometric while explicit expansions, symbol definitions and index gymnastics live in margin notes.
- p-vectors motivated by a quantity everyone knows, the determinant area of a parallelogram, with the two ways of walking round it echoing u tensor v minus v tensor u.
- The form/multivector pairing sold as the higher-rank version of counting how many 1-form sheets an arrow crosses, now counting tubes through an oriented patch.
- A complete catalogue of forms in three dimensions with function coefficients, so the dimensional ceiling on p is seen rather than proved.
- Forward promises (curvature as a 2-form, integrals over forms) used to justify investment in new abstraction before any derivative is taken.

## Section by section

### Chapter opening: from 1-forms to a family of forms — p.334 (pdf 351)

A joke epigraph about a comedian's brother appearing on stage introduces a chapter about building things in pairs. The authors recall the Chapter 31 images of vectors as arrows and 1-forms as regularly repeating surfaces, announce that 1-forms generate a family called differential or p-forms, and motivate the effort by two later payoffs: curvature packaged as a 2-form, and the reinterpretation of integration in terms of forms (Chapter 38).

**Concepts:** differential form, 1-form as a stack of surfaces, curvature 2-form, integration of forms

**Key moves**
- Recap the arrow and stacked-surface pictures before generalizing.
- Promise concrete GR payoffs (curvature, integration) to motivate the abstraction.

### §32.1 2-forms — p.334 (pdf 351)

Combining the sheets of the Cartesian basis 1-forms omega^x and omega^y produces a lattice of tubes, identified as an antisymmetric (0,2) tensor, the prototype 2-form. The wedge product of two 1-forms is defined as the tensor product minus its swap, so any 1-form wedged with itself is zero, and the example is written omega^x wedge omega^y. Antisymmetry appears geometrically as a handedness, drawn as circulation in each tube. A general 2-form is expanded with a factor 1/2 and antisymmetric components; inserting basis vectors shows the 1/2 makes B(e_alpha, e_beta) exactly B_alpha beta, and a margin example with a single component gamma shows that no 1/2 appears when a component is written explicitly. The components of sigma wedge tau come out as sigma_mu tau_nu minus tau_mu sigma_nu. Filling only one slot of a 2-form leaves a 1-form. Finally, two arbitrary 1-forms give skewed parallelepiped tubes, again with a sense of circulation.

**Concepts:** 2-form, wedge product, antisymmetric tensor, tube picture of a 2-form, orientation (handedness), component expansion of a 2-form, component extraction by inserting basis vectors, components of a wedge of two 1-forms, partial evaluation of a form, tensor product, bivector

**Key moves**
- Intersecting the sheet families of omega^x and omega^y yields tubes running along the remaining axis.
- Define sigma wedge tau = sigma tensor tau - tau tensor sigma; hence sigma wedge sigma = 0.
- Wedge products give only the antisymmetric subset of (0,2) tensors; antisymmetry is read as tube handedness.
- Expand B = (1/2) B_mu nu omega^mu wedge omega^nu and insert e_alpha, e_beta: the Kronecker deltas produce (B_alpha beta - B_beta alpha)/2 = B_alpha beta.
- Explicit single-component form gamma omega^2 wedge omega^3 gives B_23 = gamma, B_32 = -gamma without any 1/2.
- Insert basis vectors into sigma wedge tau to get B_mu nu = sigma_mu tau_nu - tau_mu sigma_nu.
- Feeding one vector u into a 2-form leaves sigma(u) tau - tau(u) sigma, a 1-form.
- General 1-forms have tilted sheets with spacing 1/sigma_mu along each axis, so their wedge gives skewed tubes.

### §32.2 p-forms — p.336 (pdf 353)

The wedge product is extended so that a p-form wedged with a q-form gives a (p+q)-form, with the order-swap sign (-1)^{pq}. Functions count as 0-forms. A general p-form is written either with 1/p! and a free sum or with strictly increasing (vertical-bar) indices and no factorial; a margin note carries out the 3D 2-form expansion and collapses six terms to three. A 3-form built from three 1-forms expands into six signed tensor products, one per permutation, and is pictured as a cellular structure. Forms can be assembled from functions multiplying wedges of coordinate differentials; in three dimensions this gives exactly a function, a 1-form with three coefficients, a 2-form with three and a 3-form with one, and no 4-form because a repeated differential kills the product.

**Concepts:** differential form, wedge product, graded commutativity of the wedge product, 0-form, 3-form, ordered-index summation, coordinate differentials as basis 1-forms, dimensional limit on p-forms

**Key moves**
- State mu wedge nu = (-1)^{pq} nu wedge mu for a p-form and a q-form.
- Write alpha = (1/p!) alpha_{i1...ip} omega^{i1} wedge ... wedge omega^{ip} = alpha_{|i1...ip|} omega^{i1} wedge ... wedge omega^{ip}.
- In 3D, use B_mu nu = -B_nu mu and omega^mu wedge omega^nu = -omega^nu wedge omega^mu to merge the six terms of (1/2)B_mu nu omega^mu wedge omega^nu into three ordered ones.
- Expand sigma wedge tau wedge kappa as the signed sum over the six orderings, and note associativity.
- Picture the ladder: sheets (1-form), tubes (2-form), cells (3-form).
- Build p-forms as function times dx^{i1} wedge ... wedge dx^{ip}; list all forms in 3D; repeated factor gives zero so p cannot exceed 3.

### §32.3 p-vectors — p.337 (pdf 354)

In the Euclidean plane the parallelogram spanned by u and v has a signed area given by a 2x2 determinant, equivalently epsilon_ij u^i v^j with the Levi-Civita symbol; its sign supplies a handedness. This area is tied to the bivector b = u wedge v, an antisymmetric (2,0) tensor whose xy component, found by inserting omega^x and omega^y, equals the determinant (developed further in Chapter 37). Wedge products of vectors define p-vectors with the same 1/p! or ordered-index expansions. Using the generalized Kronecker delta, a p-form and a p-vector combine into a number. In 3D the 2-form/bivector pairing is a three-term sum resembling a dot product and is interpreted as the number of 2-form tubes inside the bivector's parallelogram; when b = c wedge d, the pairing equals F(c,d). The chapter summary restates that p-forms are antisymmetric (0,p) tensors, 2-forms are oriented tubes, and p-vectors pair with p-forms to give numbers.

**Concepts:** signed area of a parallelogram, Levi-Civita symbol, bivector, p-vector, generalized Kronecker delta, inner product of a p-form and a p-vector, tube counting interpretation of the pairing, evaluation identity for a 2-form on a wedge of vectors, orientation (handedness)

**Key moves**
- Signed area = u^x v^y - v^x u^y = epsilon_ij u^i v^j; antisymmetry in u and v gives orientation.
- Define b = u wedge v = u tensor v - v tensor u and note b(omega^x, omega^y) = b^{xy} is the signed area.
- Expand p-vectors as (1/p!) v^{i1...ip} e_{i1} wedge ... wedge e_{ip} or with ordered indices.
- Define the pairing by <omega^{i1} wedge ... wedge omega^{ip}, e_{j1} wedge ... wedge e_{jp}> = generalized delta, so <alpha, v> = alpha_{|i1...ip|} v^{i1...ip}.
- 3D case: <F, b> = F_xy b^xy + F_xz b^xz + F_yz b^yz, read as a tube count through a parallelogram.
- State <F, c wedge d> = F(c, d), leaving the proof to Exercise 32.1.

### Exercises — p.339 (pdf 356)

Two exercises. The first asks for a component proof of the identity between the pairing and direct evaluation of a 2-form. The second, on Fermi transport, describes a sphere carrying three mutually orthogonal markers along an accelerated world line and gives the transport law in terms of the bivector formed from acceleration and velocity; five parts ask for the component form, the geodesic limit, preservation of mutual orthogonality, preservation of orthogonality to the four-velocity, and a comparison with parallel transport.

**Concepts:** evaluation identity for a 2-form on a wedge of vectors, Fermi transport, bivector

**Key moves**
- Expand c wedge d in components and convert the ordered sum into a free one using antisymmetry.
- Contract the bivector A wedge u with a lowered vector to get the Fermi transport law in components.
- Use antisymmetry of A wedge u to show inner products of transported vectors are constant.

## Concepts

### differential form

*mathematical-object · core* · also: p-form, exterior form, form

A p-form is a totally antisymmetric (0,p) tensor (field): a multilinear machine that eats p vectors, returns a number, and flips sign whenever two of its inputs are exchanged. Functions are 0-forms and covectors are 1-forms.

**How introduced:** Presented as the family obtained from 1-forms by repeated wedge products, with a geometric ladder from sheets (1-form) to tubes (2-form) to cells (3-form); the chapter summary states the tensor definition.

**Prerequisites:** 1-form as a stack of surfaces, tensor product, antisymmetric tensor, wedge product

$$
\tilde\alpha = \frac{1}{p!}\alpha_{i_1\dots i_p}\,\omega^{i_1}\wedge\dots\wedge\omega^{i_p}
$$

**Where:** GA §32.1 p.334 (pdf 351); GA §32.2 p.336 (pdf 353); GA §32.3 p.338 (pdf 355)

### wedge product

*operation · core* · also: exterior product, wedge

A bilinear, associative product that antisymmetrizes the tensor product. For 1-forms it is sigma wedge tau = sigma tensor tau - tau tensor sigma; a p-form wedged with a q-form is a (p+q)-form, and reversing the order multiplies by (-1)^{pq}. The same operation on vectors produces bivectors and p-vectors.

**How introduced:** Brought in as the symbolic way to combine the sheets of two 1-forms into tubes; defined explicitly for two 1-forms, then extended to higher degree by a stated sign rule and a six-term expansion for three 1-forms.

**Prerequisites:** tensor product, 1-form as a stack of surfaces, antisymmetric tensor

$$
\tilde\sigma\wedge\tilde\tau = \tilde\sigma\otimes\tilde\tau - \tilde\tau\otimes\tilde\sigma
$$

$$
\tilde\sigma\wedge\tilde\sigma = 0
$$

$$
\tilde\mu\wedge\tilde\nu = (-1)^{pq}\,\tilde\nu\wedge\tilde\mu
$$

**Notes:** The book uses the determinant normalization (no 1/2 in the two-factor case, as in MTW and Schutz). Authors who define the wedge through an averaging antisymmetrizer get results smaller by factorials; check before mixing sources. The general p-with-q definition is never written down, only its properties.

**Where:** GA §32.1 p.334 (pdf 351); GA §32.2 p.336 (pdf 353); GA §32.3 p.337 (pdf 354)

### 1-form as a stack of surfaces

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: covector, dual vector, one-form

A linear map from vectors to real numbers, visualized as an infinite set of equally spaced parallel surfaces; its value on a vector is the number of surfaces the arrow crosses. For sigma = sigma_mu omega^mu in Cartesian coordinates the surfaces cross the mu axis at intervals 1/sigma_mu.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapter 31 as the starting material; basis 1-forms omega^x, omega^y have sheets perpendicular to e_x, e_y, and a margin note links the picture to reciprocal lattice vectors labelling crystal planes.

**Prerequisites:** tensor product

$$
\langle\omega^\mu, e_\nu\rangle = \delta^\mu{}_\nu
$$

**Notes:** Margin note 1 prints the duality with e_mu where e_nu is meant.

**Where:** GA §32.1 p.334 (pdf 351); GA §32.1 p.336 (pdf 353)

### antisymmetric tensor

*definition · developed* · also: skew-symmetric tensor, alternating tensor

A tensor that changes sign when any two of its slots of the same type are swapped; for a (0,2) tensor, B(x,y) = -B(y,x) for all vectors x, y, equivalently B_mu nu = -B_nu mu.

**How introduced:** Recalled in a margin note from Chapter 31, with the remark that wedge products do not produce an arbitrary (0,2) tensor but only antisymmetric ones.

**Prerequisites:** tensor product

$$
\tilde B(x,y) = -\tilde B(y,x)
$$

$$
B_{\mu\nu} = -B_{\nu\mu}
$$

**Where:** GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352); GA §32.3 p.338 (pdf 355)

### 2-form

*mathematical-object · core* · also: two-form

An antisymmetric (0,2) tensor. In n dimensions it has n(n-1)/2 independent components and can be written (1/2) B_mu nu omega^mu wedge omega^nu. Geometrically it is an oriented density of tubes; paired with a bivector it gives the (signed) number of tubes through the corresponding patch.

**How introduced:** First example built from omega^x wedge omega^y as a lattice of tubes; then a general sigma wedge tau, its components, and skewed tubes for arbitrary 1-forms.

**Prerequisites:** wedge product, antisymmetric tensor, 1-form as a stack of surfaces

$$
\tilde\beta(\,,\,) = \omega^x\wedge\omega^y
$$

$$
\tilde B = \tfrac12 B_{\mu\nu}\,\omega^\mu\wedge\omega^\nu
$$

**Where:** GA §32.1 p.334 (pdf 351); GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352); GA §32.1 p.336 (pdf 353)

### tube picture of a 2-form

*technique · developed* · also: honeycomb picture, oriented tubes, circulating tubes

A way to draw a 2-form: the surfaces of two 1-forms intersect in a lattice of tubes; tighter tubes mean a larger 2-form, the tubes run along the direction common to both sheet families, and each tube carries a sense of circulation that reverses when the two factors are swapped.

**How introduced:** Introduced from the Cartesian example, then generalized to skewed parallelepiped tubes; the circulation arrows are offered as a visualization of antisymmetry.

**Prerequisites:** 1-form as a stack of surfaces, wedge product, orientation (handedness)

**Notes:** Works cleanly only for a single wedge of two 1-forms (a simple 2-form). In three dimensions every 2-form is simple, but in four dimensions a sum such as dt wedge dx + dy wedge dz is not, and needs two tube families. The book does not say which circulation sense corresponds to omega^x wedge omega^y.

**Where:** GA §32.1 p.334 (pdf 351); GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352); GA §32.1 p.336 (pdf 353); GA §32.3 p.338 (pdf 355)

### component expansion of a 2-form

*convention · developed* · also: one-half convention

Writing a 2-form as (1/2) B_mu nu omega^mu wedge omega^nu with a free double sum. Each independent pair of indices appears twice (as mu nu and nu mu), so the 1/2 ensures B(e_alpha, e_beta) returns exactly B_alpha beta. When individual terms are written out explicitly, no 1/2 is used.

**How introduced:** Stated as a convention, justified by inserting basis vectors in Example 32.1, and clarified by a margin example with a single non-zero component.

**Prerequisites:** 2-form, component extraction by inserting basis vectors

$$
\tilde B(e_\alpha,e_\beta) = \tfrac12(B_{\alpha\beta}-B_{\beta\alpha}) = B_{\alpha\beta}
$$

**Where:** GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352); GA §32.2 p.336 (pdf 353)

### ordered-index summation

*convention · introduced* · also: vertical-bar indices, strictly increasing indices

Indices enclosed in vertical bars, as in alpha_{|i1...ip|}, are summed only over i1 < i2 < ... < ip, so each independent basis p-form appears once and the 1/p! factor is dropped.

**How introduced:** Introduced with the general p-form expansion; a margin note demonstrates it by collapsing the six-term 3D expansion of a 2-form to three terms.

**Prerequisites:** component expansion of a 2-form, differential form

$$
\alpha_{|i_1\dots i_p|}\,\omega^{i_1}\wedge\dots\wedge\omega^{i_p} = \frac{1}{p!}\alpha_{i_1\dots i_p}\,\omega^{i_1}\wedge\dots\wedge\omega^{i_p}
$$

**Notes:** In eqn 32.21 the bars sit on one factor only, which is what makes the pairing sum each independent component once.

**Where:** GA §32.2 p.336 (pdf 353); GA §32.3 p.338 (pdf 355)

### component extraction by inserting basis vectors

*technique · revisited* · also: slot-machine evaluation

The components of a tensor are its values on basis inputs; for a 2-form, B_mu nu = B(e_mu, e_nu), evaluated using the duality between basis 1-forms and basis vectors.

**How introduced:** Reused from Chapter 31's slot-machine picture to justify the 1/2 convention and to find the components of sigma wedge tau.

**Prerequisites:** 1-form as a stack of surfaces, tensor product

$$
B_{\mu\nu} = \tilde B(e_\mu, e_\nu)
$$

**Where:** GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352)

### components of a wedge of two 1-forms

*identity · introduced*

If B = sigma wedge tau then B_mu nu = sigma_mu tau_nu - tau_mu sigma_nu, the antisymmetric part of the outer product of the two component lists (in 3D these are the components of the cross product arranged as a 2-form).

**How introduced:** Example 32.2, by inserting basis vectors into the two slots.

**Prerequisites:** wedge product, component extraction by inserting basis vectors

$$
(\tilde\sigma\wedge\tilde\tau)_{\mu\nu} = \sigma_\mu\tau_\nu - \tau_\mu\sigma_\nu
$$

**Where:** GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352)

### partial evaluation of a form

*operation · introduced* · also: interior product, inserting a single vector, contraction of a vector into a form

Filling one slot of a p-form with a vector leaves a (p-1)-form. For a 2-form sigma wedge tau with u in the first slot the result is the 1-form sigma(u) tau - tau(u) sigma.

**How introduced:** Example 32.3, using the order-preserving tensor product to track which slot is filled.

**Prerequisites:** 2-form, tensor product

$$
\tilde B(u,\,) = \tilde\sigma(u)\,\tilde\tau - \tilde\tau(u)\,\tilde\sigma
$$

**Notes:** The book does not name the operation; the standard name is the interior product. It returns in Exercise 32.2, where a bivector with one slot filled gives a vector.

**Where:** GA §32.1 p.336 (pdf 353)

### graded commutativity of the wedge product

*identity · introduced* · also: (-1)^{pq} rule, supercommutativity

For a p-form mu and q-form nu, mu wedge nu = (-1)^{pq} nu wedge mu. Two odd-degree forms anticommute; if either degree is even they commute. In particular a 1-form wedged with itself vanishes.

**How introduced:** Stated without proof when the wedge product is extended beyond 1-forms.

**Prerequisites:** wedge product

$$
\tilde\mu\wedge\tilde\nu = (-1)^{pq}\,\tilde\nu\wedge\tilde\mu
$$

**Notes:** Consequence worth teaching: a 2-form wedged with itself need not vanish (e.g. in 4D).

**Where:** GA §32.2 p.336 (pdf 353)

### 0-form

*definition · introduced* · also: scalar function

A function on the space, treated as the degree-zero member of the form family; wedging with a 0-form is ordinary multiplication.

**How introduced:** Defined in a one-line margin note and used as the first entry of the 3D catalogue.

**Prerequisites:** differential form

$$
\tilde\alpha = f(x,y,z)
$$

**Where:** GA §32.2 p.336 (pdf 353); GA §32.2 p.337 (pdf 354)

### 3-form

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: three-form

A totally antisymmetric (0,3) tensor. Built from three 1-forms it is the signed sum over all six orderings of their tensor product; pictured as cells formed by three sheet families. In three dimensions it has one component, G dx wedge dy wedge dz.

**How introduced:** Example 32.4 expands sigma wedge tau wedge kappa, then the geometric ladder sheets-tubes-cells is completed; Example 32.5 gives the 3D form.

**Prerequisites:** wedge product, 2-form

$$
\tilde\sigma\wedge\tilde\tau\wedge\tilde\kappa = \sum_{\pi\in S_3}\mathrm{sgn}(\pi)\,\pi(\tilde\sigma\otimes\tilde\tau\otimes\tilde\kappa)
$$

**Where:** GA §32.2 p.336 (pdf 353); GA §32.2 p.337 (pdf 354)

### dimensional limit on p-forms

*theorem · introduced* · also: no forms above the dimension

In n dimensions any wedge product that repeats a basis 1-form is zero, so every p-form with p > n vanishes; for p <= n there are n!/(p!(n-p)!) independent basis p-forms.

**How introduced:** Observed at the end of Example 32.5: in three dimensions nothing beyond a 3-form survives.

**Prerequisites:** wedge product, graded commutativity of the wedge product

$$
dx\wedge dy\wedge dx = 0
$$

$$
\dim\Lambda^p = \binom{n}{p}
$$

**Notes:** The binomial count is standard but not stated in the chapter.

**Where:** GA §32.2 p.337 (pdf 354)

### coordinate differentials as basis 1-forms

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: dx^i basis

In a coordinate basis the basis 1-forms are the differentials dx^i of the coordinates, so a general p-form is a sum of functions times wedges of the dx^i.

**How introduced:** Used to build concrete p-forms from functions (eqn 32.16) and to catalogue all forms in 3D with omega^1 = dx, omega^2 = dy, omega^3 = dz.

**Prerequisites:** 1-form as a stack of surfaces, 0-form

$$
\tilde\beta = f\,dx^{i_1}\wedge dx^{i_2}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{i_p}
$$

**Where:** GA §32.2 p.337 (pdf 354)

### bivector

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: 2-vector

An antisymmetric (2,0) tensor, for example u wedge v = u tensor v - v tensor u, whose two slots take 1-forms. It represents an oriented plane element: its xy component b(omega^x, omega^y) is the signed area of the parallelogram spanned by u and v projected onto the xy plane.

**How introduced:** First recalled in a margin note on the opening page; developed in §32.3 from the determinant area of a parallelogram; reused in Exercise 32.2 as A wedge u.

**Prerequisites:** wedge product, signed area of a parallelogram, tensor product

$$
b = u\wedge v = u\otimes v - v\otimes u
$$

$$
b = \tfrac12 b^{\mu\nu}\,e_\mu\wedge e_\nu
$$

$$
b^{xy} = u^x v^y - v^x u^y
$$

**Where:** GA §32.1 p.334 (pdf 351); GA §32.3 p.337 (pdf 354); GA §32.3 p.338 (pdf 355); GA p.339 (pdf 356)

### p-vector

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: multivector

A totally antisymmetric (p,0) tensor formed from wedge products of vectors and expanded as (1/p!) v^{i1...ip} e_{i1} wedge ... wedge e_{ip}; it represents an oriented p-dimensional parallelepiped element.

**How introduced:** Introduced briefly by analogy with p-forms immediately after the bivector.

**Prerequisites:** bivector, wedge product, ordered-index summation

$$
v = \frac{1}{p!}v^{i_1\dots i_p}\,e_{i_1}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{i_p} = v^{|i_1\dots i_p|}\,e_{i_1}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{i_p}
$$

**Where:** GA §32.3 p.337 (pdf 354)

### signed area of a parallelogram

*definition · developed* · also: oriented area, 2x2 determinant area

For vectors u and v in the plane, the determinant u^x v^y - v^x u^y = epsilon_ij u^i v^j. Its magnitude is the area they span, and its sign is positive when v lies anticlockwise from u and negative otherwise; swapping u and v flips the sign.

**How introduced:** Constructed by drawing the parallelogram from u and v, walking round it both ways, and computing the determinant; used to motivate the bivector.

**Prerequisites:** Levi-Civita symbol, orientation (handedness)

$$
(\mathrm{Area}) = \begin{vmatrix} u^x & u^y \\ v^x & v^y \end{vmatrix} = u^x v^y - v^x u^y = \varepsilon_{ij}u^i v^j
$$

**Where:** GA §32.3 p.337 (pdf 354)

### Levi-Civita symbol

*definition · introduced* · also: permutation symbol, epsilon symbol

The symbol epsilon_{i1...in} equal to +1 when its indices are an even permutation of 1...n, -1 for an odd permutation, and 0 when any index repeats; in 2D, epsilon_12 = 1 and epsilon_21 = -1.

**How introduced:** Given in a margin note to write the parallelogram area compactly.

$$
\varepsilon_{i_1\dots i_n} = (-1)^P
$$

**Where:** GA §32.3 p.337 (pdf 354)

### generalized Kronecker delta

*definition · introduced*

The symbol with p upper and p lower indices that equals +1 if the upper indices are an even permutation of the lower (all distinct), -1 for an odd permutation, and 0 if any indices repeat or the two index sets differ.

**How introduced:** Defined in a margin note to state the pairing rule for basis p-forms and basis p-vectors.

**Prerequisites:** Levi-Civita symbol

$$
\delta^{i_1\dots i_p}_{j_1\dots j_p}
$$

**Where:** GA §32.3 p.338 (pdf 355)

### inner product of a p-form and a p-vector

*operation · developed* · also: pairing of forms and multivectors, angle-bracket pairing

The number <alpha, v> obtained by declaring that the wedge of basis 1-forms paired with the wedge of basis vectors gives the generalized Kronecker delta; the result sums each independent component product once, alpha_{|i1...ip|} v^{i1...ip}, which is 1/p! times the full index contraction.

**How introduced:** Stated as a rule once p-vectors exist; the 3D 2-form/bivector case is worked and compared with the dot product.

**Prerequisites:** generalized Kronecker delta, p-vector, differential form, ordered-index summation

$$
\langle\tilde\alpha, v\rangle = \alpha_{|i_1\dots i_p|}\,v^{i_1\dots i_p}
$$

$$
\langle\tilde F, b\rangle = F_{xy}b^{xy} + F_{xz}b^{xz} + F_{yz}b^{yz}
$$

**Notes:** Reduces to the Chapter 31 pairing <sigma, v> = sigma_mu v^mu when p = 1. Later used in Chapter 34 exercises.

**Where:** GA §32.3 p.338 (pdf 355)

### tube counting interpretation of the pairing

*technique · developed* · also: 2-form tubes through a patch

The pairing of a 2-form with a bivector gives the signed number of the 2-form's tubes that thread the oriented parallelogram represented by the bivector, just as a 1-form on a vector counts sheets crossed by the arrow.

**How introduced:** Offered after Example 32.6 and drawn in Fig. 32.5, where a patch covering a 2x2 block of tube openings gives 4.

**Prerequisites:** tube picture of a 2-form, bivector, inner product of a p-form and a p-vector

**Notes:** Sign and tilt are not discussed: tilting the patch reduces the count, and reversing its orientation relative to the tubes' circulation makes it negative.

**Where:** GA §32.3 p.338 (pdf 355)

### evaluation identity for a 2-form on a wedge of vectors

*identity · introduced* · also: <F, c wedge d> = F(c,d)

For a 2-form F and vectors c and d, pairing F with the bivector c wedge d equals feeding c and d into F's two slots.

**How introduced:** Stated after Example 32.6 and left as Exercise 32.1.

**Prerequisites:** inner product of a p-form and a p-vector, bivector

$$
\langle\tilde F, c\wedge d\rangle = \tilde F(c,d)
$$

**Where:** GA §32.3 p.338 (pdf 355); GA p.339 (pdf 356)

### orientation (handedness)

*definition · developed* · also: sense of circulation, sign of an area element

The sign attached to an ordered set of directions spanning an area or volume: exchanging any two directions reverses it. Antisymmetric tensors encode exactly this information.

**How introduced:** Appears three times: as circulation arrows in 2-form tubes, as the sign of the determinant area, and implicitly in the sign of the tube count.

**Prerequisites:** antisymmetric tensor

**Where:** GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352); GA §32.1 p.336 (pdf 353); GA §32.3 p.337 (pdf 354)

### tensor product

*operation · revisited* · also: outer product

The product that builds a tensor whose slots are those of its factors in order: (sigma tensor tau)(x, y) = sigma(x) tau(y). It is not commutative, which is why antisymmetrizing it is meaningful.

**How introduced:** Assumed from Chapter 31; a reminder in Example 32.3 stresses that it keeps track of slot order.

$$
(\tilde\sigma\otimes\tilde\tau)(x,y) = \tilde\sigma(x)\,\tilde\tau(y)
$$

**Where:** GA §32.1 p.334 (pdf 351); GA §32.1 p.336 (pdf 353)

### Fermi transport

*operation · mention* · also: Fermi-Walker transport

The rule for carrying vectors along an accelerated timelike world line with four-velocity u and acceleration A = nabla_u u: nabla_u a = -(A wedge u)( , a-tilde) in the (-,+,+,+) signature. It preserves all inner products among transported vectors, keeps vectors orthogonal to u orthogonal, carries u into itself, and reduces to parallel transport when A = 0; a non-rotating gyroscope's axes obey it.

**How introduced:** Appears only in Exercise 32.2 as an application of a bivector with one slot filled.

**Prerequisites:** bivector, partial evaluation of a form

$$
\nabla_u a = -(A\wedge u)(\,\cdot\,,\tilde a)
$$

**Notes:** Exercise placement is out of sequence: it needs the covariant derivative from Part II rather than anything new from this chapter.

**Where:** GA p.339 (pdf 356)

### curvature 2-form

*mathematical-object · mention* · also: curvature as a 2-form

The Riemann tensor packaged by its antisymmetric last index pair into a matrix of 2-forms, Omega^mu_nu = (1/2) R^mu_{nu alpha beta} omega^alpha wedge omega^beta.

**How introduced:** Only promised in the chapter opening as a motivation for learning forms.

**Prerequisites:** 2-form

$$
\Omega^\mu{}_\nu = \tfrac12 R^\mu{}_{\nu\alpha\beta}\,\omega^\alpha\wedge\omega^\beta
$$

**Notes:** Developed in GA ch36 (Cartan's method). The formula is standard, not printed in this chapter.

**Where:** GA p.334 (pdf 351)

### integration of forms

*principle · mention* · also: forms as integrands

A p-form is the natural object to integrate over a p-dimensional region: it assigns a number to each small oriented p-dimensional patch, and summing gives the integral; Stokes' theorem relates such integrals over a region and its boundary.

**How introduced:** Promised in the opening paragraph as a reason to study forms.

**Prerequisites:** differential form, p-vector

**Notes:** Developed in GA ch38.

**Where:** GA p.334 (pdf 351)

## Key equations

### (32.1) Bivector as a wedge of vectors · supporting · GA §32.1 p.334 (pdf 351)

$$
u\wedge v = u\otimes v - v\otimes u
$$

Margin recap: wedging two vectors gives an antisymmetric (2,0) tensor whose slots take 1-forms.

**Symbols:** u, v vectors; otimes order-preserving tensor product

### (32.2) Wedge product of 1-forms · central · GA §32.1 p.334 (pdf 351)

$$
\tilde\sigma\wedge\tilde\tau = \tilde\sigma\otimes\tilde\tau - \tilde\tau\otimes\tilde\sigma
$$

Definition of the 2-form built from two 1-forms: the tensor product minus its swap (no factor 1/2 in this book).

**Symbols:** sigma, tau 1-forms

### (32.3) · supporting · GA §32.1 p.334 (pdf 351)

$$
\tilde\sigma\wedge\tilde\sigma = 0
$$

Any 1-form wedged with itself vanishes; the seed of the dimensional limit on forms.

**Symbols:** sigma any 1-form

### (32.4) Prototype 2-form · supporting · GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352)

$$
\tilde\beta(\,,\,) = \omega^x\wedge\omega^y
$$

The tube lattice of Fig. 32.1 written symbolically; the blank slots mark it as a two-input machine.

**Symbols:** omega^x, omega^y Cartesian basis 1-forms

### (32.5) Component expansion of a 2-form · central · GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352)

$$
\tilde B(\,,\,) = \tfrac12 B_{\mu\nu}\,\omega^\mu\wedge\omega^\nu,\qquad B_{\mu\nu} = -B_{\nu\mu}
$$

Any 2-form in terms of antisymmetric components, with 1/2 compensating the double counting of index pairs in the free sum.

**Symbols:** B_mu nu components; omega^mu basis 1-forms

### (32.6) Single-component 2-form · supporting · GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352)

$$
\tilde B = \gamma\,(\omega^2\wedge\omega^3)
$$

A 2-form in Euclidean 3-space with only the 23 component non-zero, written without any 1/2.

**Symbols:** gamma a constant

### (32.7) · derivation-step · GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352)

$$
B_{\mu\nu} = \gamma\left[\omega^2(e_\mu)\,\omega^3(e_\nu) - \omega^3(e_\mu)\,\omega^2(e_\nu)\right] = \gamma\left[\delta^2_\mu\delta^3_\nu - \delta^3_\mu\delta^2_\nu\right]
$$

Evaluating eqn 32.6 on basis vectors gives B_23 = gamma and B_32 = -gamma.

**Symbols:** delta Kronecker delta

### (32.8) · derivation-step · GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352)

$$
\tilde B = \tfrac12 B_{\mu\nu}\,\omega^\mu\wedge\omega^\nu = \tfrac12 B_{\mu\nu}\left(\omega^\mu\otimes\omega^\nu - \omega^\nu\otimes\omega^\mu\right)
$$

Example 32.1: rewrite the 2-form as tensor products so basis vectors can be inserted.

### (32.9) Justification of the 1/2 · supporting · GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352)

$$
\tilde B(e_\alpha,e_\beta) = \frac{B_{\mu\nu}}{2}\left(\delta^\mu{}_\alpha\delta^\nu{}_\beta - \delta^\nu{}_\alpha\delta^\mu{}_\beta\right) = \tfrac12\left(B_{\alpha\beta}-B_{\beta\alpha}\right) = B_{\alpha\beta}
$$

With the 1/2 in place, feeding basis vectors into the 2-form returns exactly its component.

**Symbols:** e_alpha basis vectors

### (32.10) Components of sigma wedge tau · supporting · GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352)

$$
B_{\mu\nu} = \tilde B(e_\mu,e_\nu) = \tilde\sigma(e_\mu)\,\tilde\tau(e_\nu) - \tilde\tau(e_\mu)\,\tilde\sigma(e_\nu) = \sigma_\mu\tau_\nu - \tau_\mu\sigma_\nu
$$

Components of a simple 2-form are the antisymmetrized products of the 1-form components.

**Symbols:** sigma_mu, tau_mu 1-form components

### (32.11) One vector into a 2-form · supporting · GA §32.1 p.336 (pdf 353)

$$
\tilde B(u,\,) = (\tilde\sigma\wedge\tilde\tau)(u,\,) = \tilde\sigma(u)\,\tilde\tau(\,) - \tilde\tau(u)\,\tilde\sigma(\,)
$$

Filling one slot leaves a 1-form. The book writes otimes between the number sigma(u) and the 1-form tau( ); it is just scalar multiplication.

**Symbols:** u a vector

### (32.12) Graded commutativity · central · GA §32.2 p.336 (pdf 353)

$$
\tilde\mu\wedge\tilde\nu = (-1)^{pq}\,\tilde\nu\wedge\tilde\mu
$$

Swapping a p-form and a q-form in a wedge product costs a sign (-1)^{pq}.

**Symbols:** mu a p-form; nu a q-form

### (32.13) General p-form · central · GA §32.2 p.336 (pdf 353)

$$
\tilde\alpha = \frac{1}{p!}\alpha_{i_1 i_2\dots i_p}\,\omega^{i_1}\wedge\omega^{i_2}\wedge\dots\wedge\omega^{i_p} = \alpha_{|i_1 i_2\dots i_p|}\,\omega^{i_1}\wedge\omega^{i_2}\wedge\dots\wedge\omega^{i_p}
$$

Two equivalent expansions: free sum with 1/p!, or strictly increasing indices with no factorial.

**Symbols:** vertical bars mean i_1 < i_2 < ... < i_p

### (32.14) 3D 2-form in ordered components · supporting · GA §32.2 p.336 (pdf 353)

$$
\tilde B = \tfrac12 B_{\mu\nu}\,\omega^\mu\wedge\omega^\nu = B_{12}\,\omega^1\wedge\omega^2 + B_{13}\,\omega^1\wedge\omega^3 + B_{23}\,\omega^2\wedge\omega^3 = B_{|\mu\nu|}\,\omega^\mu\wedge\omega^\nu
$$

Margin computation: antisymmetry of both components and wedges merges the six terms into three.

### (32.15) 3-form from three 1-forms · supporting · GA §32.2 p.336 (pdf 353)

$$
\tilde\sigma\wedge(\tilde\tau\wedge\tilde\kappa) = (\tilde\sigma\wedge\tilde\tau)\wedge\tilde\kappa = \tilde\sigma\otimes\tilde\tau\otimes\tilde\kappa - \tilde\sigma\otimes\tilde\kappa\otimes\tilde\tau + \tilde\kappa\otimes\tilde\sigma\otimes\tilde\tau - \tilde\kappa\otimes\tilde\tau\otimes\tilde\sigma + \tilde\tau\otimes\tilde\kappa\otimes\tilde\sigma - \tilde\tau\otimes\tilde\sigma\otimes\tilde\kappa
$$

Associative triple wedge equals the signed sum over the six orderings (even permutations positive, odd negative).

**Symbols:** sigma, tau, kappa 1-forms

### (32.16) p-form from a function · supporting · GA §32.2 p.337 (pdf 354)

$$
\tilde\beta = f(x^1,\dots)\,dx^{i_1}\wedge dx^{i_2}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{i_p}
$$

A simple p-form: one function times a wedge of coordinate differentials; sums of such terms give any p-form.

**Symbols:** f scalar function; dx^i coordinate basis 1-forms

### (32.17) Catalogue of forms in 3D · supporting · GA §32.2 p.337 (pdf 354)

$$
\tilde\alpha = f,\quad \tilde\beta = A\,dx + B\,dy + C\,dz,\quad \tilde\mu = D\,dx\wedge dy + E\,dy\wedge dz + F\,dz\wedge dx,\quad \tilde\nu = G\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz
$$

Every 0-, 1-, 2- and 3-form in three dimensions, with coefficient functions of (x,y,z); no 4-form exists.

**Symbols:** A...G functions of x, y, z

### (32.18) Signed area of a parallelogram · central · GA §32.3 p.337 (pdf 354)

$$
(\mathrm{Area}) = \begin{vmatrix} u^x & u^y \\ v^x & v^y\end{vmatrix} = u^x v^y - v^x u^y = \varepsilon_{ij}\,u^i v^j
$$

Determinant form of the oriented area spanned by u and v; antisymmetric in the two vectors.

**Symbols:** epsilon_ij 2D Levi-Civita symbol

### (32.19) Bivector from two vectors · central · GA §32.3 p.337 (pdf 354)

$$
b(\,,\,) = u\wedge v = u\otimes v - v\otimes u
$$

The antisymmetric (2,0) tensor tied to the parallelogram of Fig. 32.4.

**Symbols:** b bivector

### Area as a bivector component · derivation-step · GA §32.3 p.337 (pdf 354)

$$
b^{xy} = b(\omega^x,\omega^y) = u^x v^y - v^x u^y
$$

Inserting omega^x and omega^y into u wedge v reproduces the determinant of eqn 32.18.

### Bivector components (margin note 9) · supporting · GA §32.3 p.337 (pdf 354)

$$
b = \tfrac12\,b^{\mu\nu}\,e_\mu\wedge e_\nu
$$

Same 1/2 convention as for 2-forms.

**Symbols:** b^{mu nu} antisymmetric components

### Levi-Civita symbol (margin note 8) · supporting · GA §32.3 p.337 (pdf 354)

$$
\varepsilon_{12} = 1,\ \varepsilon_{21} = -1,\ \varepsilon_{11} = \varepsilon_{22} = 0;\qquad \varepsilon_{i_1\dots i_n} = (-1)^P\ \text{(0 if an index repeats)}
$$

Sign of a permutation of 1...n, with P the number of pairwise swaps needed to restore order.

**Symbols:** P parity of the permutation

### (32.20) p-vector expansion · supporting · GA §32.3 p.337 (pdf 354)

$$
v = \frac{1}{p!}v^{i_1 i_2\dots i_p}\,e_{i_1}\wedge e_{i_2}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{i_p} = v^{|i_1 i_2\dots i_p|}\,e_{i_1}\wedge e_{i_2}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{i_p}
$$

Multivectors expanded exactly like p-forms, on wedge products of basis vectors.

**Symbols:** e_i basis vectors

### Generalized Kronecker delta (margin note 10) · supporting · GA §32.3 p.338 (pdf 355)

$$
\delta^{i_1\dots i_p}_{j_1\dots j_p} = \begin{cases} +1 & (i) \text{ an even permutation of } (j)\\ -1 & (i) \text{ an odd permutation of } (j)\\ 0 & \text{repeated indices, or the index sets differ}\end{cases}
$$

Symbol used to pair basis p-forms with basis p-vectors.

### (32.21) Pairing of a p-form and a p-vector · central · GA §32.3 p.338 (pdf 355)

$$
\langle\tilde\alpha, v\rangle = \alpha_{|i_1\dots i_p|}\,v^{|j_1\dots j_p|}\,\langle\omega^{i_1}\wedge\dots\wedge\omega^{i_p}, e_{j_1}\wedge\dots\wedge e_{j_p}\rangle = \alpha_{|i_1\dots i_p|}\,v^{|j_1\dots j_p|}\,\delta^{i_1\dots i_p}_{j_1\dots j_p} = \alpha_{|i_1\dots i_p|}\,v^{i_1\dots i_p}
$$

Generalized inner product that turns a p-form and a p-vector into a number, counting each independent component pair once.

**Symbols:** alpha p-form; v p-vector

### (32.22) 2-form with bivector in 3D · supporting · GA §32.3 p.338 (pdf 355)

$$
\langle\tilde F, b\rangle = F_{xy}b^{xy} + F_{xz}b^{xz} + F_{yz}b^{yz}
$$

Three-term sum resembling a dot product; geometrically the number of F tubes threading the parallelogram b.

**Symbols:** F 2-form; b bivector

### (32.23) Pairing equals evaluation · central · GA §32.3 p.338 (pdf 355)

$$
\langle\tilde F, c\wedge d\rangle = \tilde F(c,d)
$$

For a simple bivector, the pairing coincides with feeding the two vectors into the 2-form (proved in Exercise 32.1).

**Symbols:** c, d vectors

### (32.24) Fermi transport equations · supporting · GA p.339 (pdf 356)

$$
\nabla_u a = -(A\wedge u)(\,\cdot\,,\tilde a),\quad \nabla_u b = -(A\wedge u)(\,\cdot\,,\tilde b),\quad \nabla_u c = -(A\wedge u)(\,\cdot\,,\tilde c),\qquad A = \nabla_u u
$$

Transport of an orthogonal triad along an accelerated world line without rotation; in components nabla_u a^mu = -A^mu (u.a) + u^mu (A.a). The sign fits the book's (-,+,+,+) signature.

**Symbols:** u four-velocity; A four-acceleration; a-tilde the 1-form obtained by lowering a

## Worked examples

### Example 32.1 · intro · GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352)

**Problem:** Explain why a 2-form is expanded with a factor 1/2 in front of B_mu nu omega^mu wedge omega^nu.

**Method:** Rewrite the wedge as a difference of tensor products, insert basis vectors e_alpha and e_beta, collapse the Kronecker deltas, and use B_beta alpha = -B_alpha beta.

**Key insight:** The unrestricted sum counts every independent component twice; the 1/2 removes that so evaluation on basis vectors returns the component itself.

**Result:** B(e_alpha, e_beta) = B_alpha beta.

**Concepts:** component expansion of a 2-form, component extraction by inserting basis vectors

### Example 32.2 · intro · GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352)

**Problem:** Find the components of B = sigma wedge tau in terms of the components of sigma and tau.

**Method:** Feed e_mu and e_nu into the two slots of the antisymmetrized tensor product and use sigma(e_mu) = sigma_mu.

**Key insight:** A simple 2-form's components are the antisymmetrized outer product of the two component lists (the cross-product pattern in 3D).

**Result:** B_mu nu = sigma_mu tau_nu - tau_mu sigma_nu.

**Concepts:** components of a wedge of two 1-forms, wedge product

### Example · intro · GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352)

**Problem:** Margin note 4: a Euclidean 3-space 2-form with only one non-zero component, gamma omega^2 wedge omega^3; find its components.

**Method:** Evaluate on basis vectors using duality to get differences of Kronecker delta products.

**Key insight:** When a component is written explicitly rather than summed, no factor 1/2 is involved, and antisymmetry supplies the partner component automatically.

**Result:** B_23 = gamma, B_32 = -gamma, all others zero.

**Concepts:** component expansion of a 2-form, antisymmetric tensor

### Example 32.3 · intro · GA §32.1 p.336 (pdf 353)

**Problem:** Insert a single vector u into the first slot of the 2-form sigma wedge tau and identify what remains.

**Method:** Keep track of slot order in the tensor products: the first factor of each term receives u, the second slot stays open.

**Key insight:** Each filled slot lowers the rank by one; a 2-form with one vector in it is a 1-form waiting for a second vector.

**Result:** B(u, ) = sigma(u) tau - tau(u) sigma, a 1-form.

**Concepts:** partial evaluation of a form, tensor product

### Example · standard · GA §32.2 p.336 (pdf 353)

**Problem:** Margin note 7: expand (1/2) B_mu nu omega^mu wedge omega^nu explicitly in three dimensions.

**Method:** Write all six off-diagonal terms, then pair each term with its index-swapped partner using antisymmetry of both the components and the wedge products.

**Key insight:** Pairs merge, cancelling the 1/2 and leaving one term per independent component, which motivates the ordered-index notation.

**Result:** B = B_12 omega^1 wedge omega^2 + B_13 omega^1 wedge omega^3 + B_23 omega^2 wedge omega^3 = B_{|mu nu|} omega^mu wedge omega^nu.

**Concepts:** ordered-index summation, component expansion of a 2-form

### Example 32.4 · standard · GA §32.2 p.336 (pdf 353)

**Problem:** Build a 3-form from three 1-forms sigma, tau and kappa.

**Method:** Use associativity of the wedge and expand into tensor products, one term for each of the six orderings, with the sign of the permutation.

**Key insight:** A triple wedge is the fully antisymmetrized product; geometrically it adds a third sheet family, turning tubes into cells.

**Result:** Eqn 32.15: three positive (cyclic) and three negative (odd) orderings.

**Concepts:** 3-form, wedge product

### Example 32.5 · intro · GA §32.2 p.337 (pdf 354)

**Problem:** With basis dx, dy, dz in three dimensions, list the p-forms that can be made from coordinate functions.

**Method:** Multiply functions by all non-vanishing wedge products of distinct differentials and observe which products die.

**Key insight:** Repeated differentials vanish, so the menu stops at one 3-form component; no 4-form exists in three dimensions. The component counts 1, 3, 3, 1 match scalars, vectors, pseudovectors and pseudoscalars of vector calculus.

**Result:** 0-form f; 1-form A dx + B dy + C dz; 2-form D dx wedge dy + E dy wedge dz + F dz wedge dx; 3-form G dx wedge dy wedge dz.

**Concepts:** dimensional limit on p-forms, coordinate differentials as basis 1-forms, 0-form, 3-form

### Example · intro · GA §32.3 p.337 (pdf 354)

**Problem:** Find the area of the parallelogram spanned by u and v in the Euclidean plane and relate it to the bivector u wedge v.

**Method:** Compute the 2x2 determinant, rewrite it with the Levi-Civita symbol, then evaluate b = u wedge v on omega^x and omega^y.

**Key insight:** The same antisymmetric combination u^x v^y - v^x u^y is both the signed area and the xy component of the bivector, so bivectors are oriented areas.

**Result:** Area = u^x v^y - v^x u^y = epsilon_ij u^i v^j = b^{xy}.

**Concepts:** signed area of a parallelogram, bivector, Levi-Civita symbol

### Example 32.6 · standard · GA §32.3 p.338 (pdf 355)

**Problem:** In three dimensions, combine a 2-form F with a bivector b to produce a number, and relate the result to evaluating F on two vectors.

**Method:** Apply the pairing rule 32.21 with ordered indices (xy, xz, yz); interpret geometrically as a tube count; state the identity for b = c wedge d.

**Key insight:** The pairing looks like a dot product over the three independent planes, and for a simple bivector it is the same as plugging the two vectors into the 2-form.

**Result:** <F, b> = F_xy b^xy + F_xz b^xz + F_yz b^yz, and <F, c wedge d> = F(c, d).

**Concepts:** inner product of a p-form and a p-vector, tube counting interpretation of the pairing, evaluation identity for a 2-form on a wedge of vectors


## Analogies and intuitions

### Reciprocal lattice vectors in a crystal label families of lattice planes, with A_i . a_j = 2 pi delta_ij → 1-form as a stack of surfaces · useful · GA §32.1 p.334 (pdf 351)

Margin note 1 points out that 1-forms label repeating surfaces in the same way that reciprocal lattice vectors label crystal planes, with the duality relation playing the role of the 2 pi delta condition.

**Where it breaks down:** Flat space only, uses a dot product and a factor 2 pi; a genuine 1-form needs no metric.

**App idea:** Toggle between a 2D crystal lattice with its reciprocal vectors and the same picture relabelled as basis vectors and basis 1-form sheets.

### Two sets of sheets crossing to make a honeycomb of tubes → tube picture of a 2-form · strong · GA §32.1 p.334 (pdf 351)

The primary mental image for a 2-form, derived directly from the sheet picture of 1-forms and used again for arbitrary 2-forms and for the pairing.

**Where it breaks down:** Only simple 2-forms have one tube family; in four dimensions a general 2-form is a sum that no single tube lattice represents. Magnitude as tube density needs care once sheets are tilted.

**App idea:** 3D sheet-intersection sandbox (see Fig. 32.1 redesign).

### Fluid circulating along each tube → orientation (handedness) · useful · GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352)

Arrows drawn in the tubes make the abstract antisymmetry of sigma wedge tau visible as a direction of circulation that reverses when the factors swap.

**Where it breaks down:** Nothing actually flows; the arrows encode only a sign. The book does not fix which sense goes with omega^x wedge omega^y.

**App idea:** Particles swirling in tubes whose direction flips when the learner swaps the wedge order.

### Ladder of dimensions: sheets, then tubes, then cells → differential form · strong · GA §32.2 p.336 (pdf 353)

Each additional 1-form factor adds another family of surfaces, turning planes into tubes and tubes into boxes, which makes the degree of a form tangible.

**Where it breaks down:** Stops being drawable beyond three sheet families; higher forms in spacetime must be reasoned about algebraically.

**App idea:** Three-step animation adding sheet families one at a time with a degree counter and component count.

### Counting how many tubes pass through a loop, like counting how many sheets an arrow pierces → tube counting interpretation of the pairing · strong · GA §32.3 p.338 (pdf 355)

Gives a concrete, countable meaning to <F, b>, extending the Chapter 31 picture of a 1-form eating a vector.

**Where it breaks down:** Counts are signed and generally fractional; the integer answer 4 relies on a patch aligned with the grid.

**App idea:** Patch-versus-tubes counter (see Fig. 32.5 redesign).

### The 2-form/bivector pairing looks like a dot product → inner product of a p-form and a p-vector · useful · GA §32.3 p.338 (pdf 355)

Eqn 32.22 is presented as a sum of products of matching components over the three planes xy, xz, yz, reminiscent of a . b.

**Where it breaks down:** No metric is involved, and the normalization differs from a full tensor contraction by 1/p!.

### Walking round a parallelogram two ways (u then v, or v then u) → bivector · useful · GA §32.3 p.337 (pdf 354)

The construction of Fig. 32.4 traces the two routes, mirroring the two terms u tensor v and v tensor u whose difference is the bivector, and the determinant supplies the sign.

**Where it breaks down:** The book does not spell out that the two routes correspond to opposite orientations; the learner must infer it.

**App idea:** Animate a dot walking both routes with a running signed-area readout.


## Misconceptions addressed

### Wedging two 1-forms produces a general (0,2) tensor. · GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352)

**Correction:** The wedge product only produces antisymmetric (0,2) tensors; symmetric parts such as a metric can never arise this way.

**Why tempting:** The wedge is built from the tensor product, which does produce general (0,2) tensors.

### The 1/2 in (1/2)B_mu nu omega^mu wedge omega^nu means the components are halved, so B(e_1, e_2) = B_12/2. · GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352)

**Correction:** The 1/2 compensates for summing over both orderings of each index pair; evaluating on basis vectors returns the full component, and explicitly written terms carry no 1/2.

**Why tempting:** Numerical prefactors normally rescale what they multiply.

### Wedge products always anticommute, so any form wedged with itself is zero. · GA §32.2 p.336 (pdf 353)

**Correction:** Only odd-degree forms anticommute; the sign is (-1)^{pq}, so a 2-form commutes with everything and a 2-form wedged with itself can be non-zero in four or more dimensions.

**Why tempting:** The first rules met are sigma wedge sigma = 0 and omega^mu wedge omega^nu = -omega^nu wedge omega^mu.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### One can keep wedging more 1-forms to get forms of any degree in any space. · GA §32.2 p.337 (pdf 354)

**Correction:** In n dimensions any product of more than n 1-forms must repeat a basis direction and vanishes; in 3D nothing above a 3-form exists.

**Why tempting:** Tensor products, unlike wedges, can be of any rank in any dimension.

### Area is always a positive number. · GA §32.3 p.337 (pdf 354)

**Correction:** The determinant area is signed: exchanging the two spanning vectors reverses the sign, and this orientation is exactly the information a bivector carries.

**Why tempting:** Everyday area is a magnitude.

### The pairing <alpha, v> is the full contraction alpha_{i1...ip} v^{i1...ip}. · GA §32.3 p.338 (pdf 355)

**Correction:** With the book's rule it is alpha_{|i1...ip|} v^{i1...ip}, which equals 1/p! times the full contraction; for p = 2, <F, b> = (1/2) F_mu nu b^mu nu.

**Why tempting:** For p = 1 the pairing is literally the contraction sigma_mu v^mu, and the notation in eqn 32.21 hides which indices are ordered.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The circulation arrows in the tubes mean a 2-form describes something flowing or rotating. · GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352)

**Correction:** The arrows are only a bookkeeping device for the sign (orientation) of the antisymmetric tensor.

**Why tempting:** Circulation arrows look like vorticity or magnetic field lines.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Every 2-form can be drawn as a single tube lattice from two 1-forms. · GA §32.1 p.336 (pdf 353)

**Correction:** True in three dimensions, but in spacetime a generic 2-form (such as the electromagnetic field with both E and B present) is a sum of two simple 2-forms and needs more than one tube family.

**Why tempting:** The chapter presents the arbitrary 2-form of Fig. 32.3 as a single wedge of two arbitrary 1-forms.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Fermi transport is just parallel transport along the world line. · GA p.339 (pdf 356)

**Correction:** They coincide only for geodesics (A = 0). On an accelerated world line parallel transport would tilt vectors out of the space orthogonal to u, whereas the A wedge u term keeps them orthogonal while still preserving their mutual angles.

**Why tempting:** Both describe carrying vectors without turning them, and both preserve inner products among the carried vectors.


## Thought experiments

### Carrying a non-rotating sphere along an accelerated world line · GA p.339 (pdf 356)

**Setup:** A small sphere carries three mutually orthogonal marker vectors, all orthogonal to its four-velocity, and moves on an accelerated path; ask what equation says the markers are not rotating.

**Lesson:** Non-rotation along an accelerated path is not parallel transport: a correction term built from the bivector of acceleration and velocity is needed to keep the markers orthogonal to the four-velocity while preserving their mutual angles.

**App idea:** Spacetime diagram of an accelerated world line with a spatial triad drawn at intervals, switchable between parallel transport (triad tips out of the simultaneity slice) and Fermi transport (triad stays orthogonal).

### Trying to add a fourth family of sheets in three dimensions · GA §32.2 p.337 (pdf 354)

**Setup:** Our framing, not a thought experiment posed in the book: the chapter only remarks (end of Example 32.5) that no 4-form exists in three dimensions. Starting from the cell structure of a 3-form in 3D, try to wedge in one more 1-form.

**Lesson:** Any new sheet family is a combination of the three already present, so the product contains a repeated factor and collapses to zero; degree is capped by dimension.

**App idea:** Let the learner add a fourth sheet stack with any orientation; the volume of the resulting cells is shown going to zero as its decomposition into the existing three is displayed.

## Notation and conventions

- **Forms versus vectors:** Forms carry a tilde and bold type (sigma-tilde, B-tilde, F-tilde); vectors are bold without tilde (u, v, b); basis 1-forms are bold omega^mu without tilde; coordinate basis 1-forms are bold dx^i. — Basis 1-forms dropping the tilde is a slight inconsistency to warn learners about. (GA §32.1 p.334 (pdf 351))
- **Wedge normalization:** sigma wedge tau = sigma tensor tau - tau tensor sigma, with no 1/2; higher wedges are signed sums over permutations without factorial prefactors. — Same as MTW and Schutz; differs from conventions that define wedge through an averaging antisymmetrizer. (GA §32.1 p.334 (pdf 351))
- **Empty slot notation:** Open slots are shown by blank arguments, e.g. beta( , ) for a two-slot form and B(u, ) for a partially filled one; in Exercise 32.2 a dot marks the open slot. (GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352))
- **Component normalization:** A p-form is (1/p!) alpha_{i1...ip} omega^{i1} wedge ... wedge omega^{ip} with free sums; bivectors are (1/2) b^{mu nu} e_mu wedge e_nu; explicitly written single terms carry no factorial. (GA §32.1 p.335 (pdf 352))
- **Vertical-bar indices:** alpha_{|i1...ip|} means sum only over i1 < i2 < ... < ip. — In eqn 32.21 the bars appear on only one factor of a contraction. (GA §32.2 p.336 (pdf 353))
- **Tensor product keeps slot order:** In sigma tensor tau the first slot belongs to sigma and the second to tau; this order is what makes antisymmetrization meaningful. (GA §32.1 p.336 (pdf 353))
- **Angle-bracket pairing:** <alpha, v> denotes the pairing of a p-form with a p-vector, fixed by <omega^{i1} wedge ... wedge omega^{ip}, e_{j1} wedge ... wedge e_{jp}> = generalized Kronecker delta. — Equals 1/p! times full contraction. (GA §32.3 p.338 (pdf 355))
- **Levi-Civita and generalized delta:** epsilon_{12} = +1 in 2D; epsilon_{i1...in} = (-1)^P; generalized delta is +1/-1 for even/odd permutations, 0 otherwise. (GA §32.3 p.337 (pdf 354))
- **Index labels:** Numeric (1,2,3), letter (x,y,z) and Greek or Latin indices are used interchangeably for Cartesian components, with (x^1, x^2, x^3) = (x, y, z) in Example 32.6. — Example 32.5's 2-form uses the cyclic order dz wedge dx, whereas the ordered-index convention would use dx wedge dz, which flips the sign of that coefficient. (GA §32.2 p.337 (pdf 354))
- **Metric signature:** The book's (-,+,+,+) signature is implicit in Exercise 32.2; the Appendix E solution uses u(u-tilde) = -1. — The minus sign in eqn 32.24 is correct for this signature and would flip for (+,-,-,-). (GA p.339 (pdf 356))
- **Zero-forms:** A 0-form is a function f(x). (GA §32.2 p.336 (pdf 353))

## Gaps and pitfalls

- **The wedge of a p-form with a q-form is never actually defined; eqn 32.12 and the six-term expansion 32.15 are stated without the normalization that produces them.** — Learners cannot check whether (sigma wedge tau) wedge kappa should carry a factor, and conflicts appear when they consult books with a different convention. *Suggestion:* State the determinant convention explicitly: the wedge of p 1-forms is the signed sum over all p! orderings of their tensor product, and give the general p-with-q formula with its (p+q)!/(p! q!) factor.
- **The pairing rule 32.21 is normalized so that it equals 1/p! times the full index contraction, but this is not said.** — Learners who compute F_mu nu b^mu nu directly get twice the book's answer. *Suggestion:* Show the p = 2 case both ways, (1/2) F_mu nu b^mu nu = sum over i<j of F_ij b^ij, and connect it to Exercise 32.1.
- **Orientation conventions for the pictures are never fixed: which circulation sense corresponds to omega^x wedge omega^y, and how the sign of a tube count is determined, are left open. The axes also change orientation between Fig. 32.1 and Fig. 32.2.** — The handedness in the pictures cannot be used to compute a sign. *Suggestion:* Adopt a right-hand rule (circulation from the first factor's normal toward the second's) and use one axis orientation in every demo.
- **With u and v drawn as in Fig. 32.4 (v to the right, u up and to the left), the determinant u^x v^y - v^x u^y is negative, although the text calls it the area.** — A learner checking the figure numerically finds a negative area and doubts the formula. *Suggestion:* Say explicitly that the formula gives a signed area and that the drawn order corresponds to clockwise orientation, or swap the labels in the redesign.
- **Tube pictures represent only simple 2-forms; the chapter implies an arbitrary 2-form is one wedge of two 1-forms, which fails in four dimensions.** — Misleads learners when they later meet the electromagnetic field 2-form, which in general is not simple. *Suggestion:* Add a 4D note: dt wedge dx + dy wedge dz cannot be written as sigma wedge tau (its wedge with itself is non-zero), so it needs two tube families.
- **Small printed slips: margin note 1 writes the duality as <omega^mu, e_mu> = delta^mu_nu; eqn 32.16 lists f's arguments as x^1...x^p although a coefficient function generally depends on all n coordinates; eqns 32.10-32.11 put a tensor product sign between plain numbers; margin note 4 has a duplicated word; the closing paragraph says q-vectors where p-vectors is meant.** — Minor confusion about indices and about which objects are numbers versus tensors. *Suggestion:* Correct silently in the app: use e_nu, write f(x^1,...,x^n), and use ordinary multiplication for scalar factors.
- **Example 32.5 writes the 2-form with dz wedge dx (cyclic order), while the ordered-index convention just introduced would use dx wedge dz.** — Sign errors when converting between F's coefficient and B_13 = -F. *Suggestion:* Point out that the cyclic ordering is chosen to match the flux or cross-product pattern (x with dy wedge dz, y with dz wedge dx, z with dx wedge dy), previewing the Hodge dual of Chapter 37.
- **Exercise 32.2 (Fermi transport) uses covariant derivatives and four-acceleration, not the chapter's new material. The Appendix E answer to (e) wrongly says parallel transport does not preserve angles.** — Learners may conclude that parallel transport distorts angles, contradicting metric compatibility of the connection. *Suggestion:* Teach the correct contrast: both transports preserve inner products among the carried vectors; only Fermi transport keeps them orthogonal to an accelerating u (and carries u into itself).
- **The payoff of forms (derivatives, integration, curvature) is entirely deferred, so the chapter can feel like notation for its own sake.** — Motivation drops, especially for physics-minded learners. *Suggestion:* Preview one concrete use alongside the tube picture, such as magnetic flux through a loop as a 2-form evaluated on a bivector.
- **The margin note citing Pauli gives only 'the book by Pauli, Section 10' with no title.** — Hard to follow up the reciprocal-lattice analogy. *Suggestion:* Point to any standard solid-state text for reciprocal lattices, and to MTW chapter 4 for the sheet and honeycomb pictures of forms.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'A 1-form is a stack of sheets and a vector is an arrow. What would you get by overlaying two different stacks of sheets?' Let the learner discover tubes before naming the wedge product.
- Order of explanation: sheets to tubes (picture), wedge definition and sigma wedge sigma = 0, handedness, components with the 1/2 justified by inserting basis vectors, one-slot evaluation, then degree p and the 3D catalogue, then signed area and bivectors, and finally the pairing as tube counting.
- Check for understanding: ask for the components of dx wedge dy in 3D (B_12 = 1, B_21 = -1, others 0), and then for (dx + dy) wedge dz expanded.
- Check for understanding: 'Why is there no 4-form in three dimensions?' Expect the answer that some basis 1-form must repeat and a repeated factor gives zero.
- Check for understanding: have the learner compute <F, c wedge d> both by the ordered sum and by F(c, d) for simple numbers, then compute F_mu nu b^mu nu to expose the factor of 2.
- Common learner question: 'Isn't a 2-form just a cross product?' In 3D the three components of sigma wedge tau match sigma cross tau, but the 2-form is the more basic object, needs no metric or handedness convention, and generalizes to any dimension; the identification with a vector is the Hodge dual (Chapter 37).
- Common learner question: 'Why does the book have no 1/2 in the wedge definition when another book does?' Explain that conventions differ by factorials and show how that propagates to components; stick to one convention per session.
- When the learner is visual, pair each formula with an orientation check: swap the two factors and ask what happens to the circulation arrows and to the sign of the area.
- For advanced learners, use Exercise 32.2 to show that the Fermi transport law also carries u into itself, and that orthogonality among the triad follows purely from antisymmetry of A wedge u.
- Flag for learners reading the book alongside: Fig. 32.4 as drawn gives a negative determinant; Appendix E's part (e) answer on parallel transport should not be trusted as written.
- If the learner asks why bother, preview that curvature becomes a matrix of 2-forms (Chapter 36) and that flux and Stokes' theorem are statements about integrating forms (Chapters 37-38).
