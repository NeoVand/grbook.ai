# GA ch33 · Exterior and Lie derivatives

> Introduces two derivatives that need neither a metric nor a connection: the exterior derivative d, which turns p-forms into (p+1)-forms and satisfies dd = 0, and the Lie derivative, which compares tensors by dragging them along the flow of a second vector field. The Lie derivative of a vector is shown to be the commutator [u,v], read geometrically as the gap that closes a failed parallelogram. The chapter ends by using the vanishing Lie derivative of the metric to define Killing vectors, which yield constants of geodesic motion.

**Pages:** printed 340–350 · pdf 357–367 · **Difficulty:** math 4/5, conceptual 4/5, novice-friendliness 2/5

Short but dense. The exterior-derivative part is mechanical once the wedge rules are known. The Lie derivative asks the reader to hold two fields, a flow and a push-forward at once, and Step I disguises an active drag as a coordinate transformation. The general tensor formula is index-heavy, and the Killing derivation relies on connection properties stated loosely (metric compatibility 'for any affine connection', unjustified comma-to-semicolon). Several printed slips add friction: the missing sin chi in eqn 33.15, the mis-indexed delta in 33.38, and the missing minus sign on dt^2 in the Schwarzschild metric (33.56). The Killing-vector payoff at the end is concrete and accessible.

## Role in the book

Part V goes back over the geometry that Parts I-IV used informally, this time rigorously. Chapter 31 rebuilt vectors as derivative operators and Chapter 32 built differential forms and the wedge product. This chapter adds the two primitive, connection-free ways of differentiating those objects before Chapter 34 brings in the connection, torsion and metric compatibility. The pieces set up here are the ones used later on. The exterior derivative drives Cartan's method (Ch 36), Stokes' theorem (Ch 38) and the electromagnetic Bianchi identity dF = 0 (Ch 43). The commutator and Lie dragging supply the clean geodesic-deviation derivation in Ch 35 and the torsion link in Ch 34. The Killing-vector material justifies the recipe that Chapter 22 used without proof for Schwarzschild orbits, and it feeds Noether's theorem in Chapter 40.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can state the four defining properties of the exterior derivative (linearity, action on functions, graded Leibniz rule, dd = 0) and explain why d raises form degree by one.
- Reader can compute d of a p-form in components, dropping repeated-differential terms using dx^mu wedge dx^mu = 0, e.g. for the 1-forms a(t) dchi and a(t) sin chi sin theta dphi.
- Reader can sketch the geometric picture of d acting on f(x) dy: new sheets appear normal to the direction in which the coefficient changes, and together with the original sheets they form tubes of dx wedge dy.
- Reader can explain why a chain of exterior derivatives stops at 4-forms in four dimensions and why applying d twice always gives zero.
- Reader can compute the commutator [u,v] of two vector fields in components and interpret it as the vector needed to close the quadrilateral built from u and v.
- Reader can use [e_a, e_b] = 0 as the test for whether a basis is a coordinate basis, and recognise orthonormal frames as typically non-coordinate.
- Reader can define integral curves and congruences, define Lie dragging, and derive L_u v = [u,v] from the push-forward of v along a small step of u.
- Reader can write component formulas for the Lie derivative of scalars, vectors, 1-forms, the metric and a general (k,l) tensor, with the correct sign pattern for upper and lower indices.
- Reader can explain why the Lie derivative cannot replace the covariant derivative for describing curvature.
- Reader can define an isometry and a Killing vector field via L_xi g = 0 and derive Killing's equation xi_(a;b) = 0 using a metric-compatible, torsion-free connection.
- Reader can read Killing vectors off a metric that does not depend on some coordinate, and prove that u . xi is constant along a geodesic.
- Reader can extract the conserved quantities u_t and u_phi for Schwarzschild geodesics and -x^2 u^t for Rindler geodesics.

## Teaching approach

A compact, formula-forward chapter that opens with a single framing problem: tensors at different points cannot be compared without extra structure. It then offers two answers that avoid a connection. The exterior derivative is given axiomatically as four boxed rules in the margin, turned into a component formula, and made concrete with a string of small calculations on 1-forms that reappear in cosmology. The Lie derivative is approached through the commutator, whose meaning is carried by one repeated diagram: a quadrilateral of u and v arrows that fails to close. The same drawing is reused, relabelled with P and Q, to show the Lie derivative as the closing vector, so the commutator identity is seen geometrically as well as derived in coordinates. A physical river-current image motivates dragging. The tensor generalisation is presented as an optional recipe that can be skipped. Everything converges on a practical payoff: Killing vectors found by inspection, and a three-line proof that they give conserved quantities along geodesics, applied to Schwarzschild and Rindler.

**Style:** geometric-first, components-first, derivation-heavy, diagram-driven, example-driven, spiral-revisit, conversational-informal, computational-recipe

**Narrative arc**

1. Motivating problem: derivatives compare values at neighbouring points, but tensor fields at distinct points live in different spaces; a connection solves this (deferred to Ch 34), so what can be done without one?
2. Exterior derivative, picture first: d of a 1-form measures how its coefficient changes and builds a 2-form by adding perpendicular sheets (Fig. 33.1).
3. Axioms in a margin box (linear; df from directional derivative; graded Leibniz; dd = 0), then the component formula for d of a general p-form.
4. Drill: exterior derivatives of dt, a(t) dchi, a(t) sin theta dtheta and a(t) sin chi sin theta dphi, showing how wedge antisymmetry removes terms.
5. Consequence of dd = 0 and dimension: function -> planes -> tubes -> cells, each exact form closed, and no 5-forms in 4D.
6. Pivot to the second derivative: define the commutator of vector fields as operators, compute it in components, and find it is a new vector field.
7. Geometric reading: the commutator is the closing vector of a would-be parallelogram (Fig. 33.2); corollary that a basis is a coordinate basis iff its commutators vanish.
8. Physical model for Lie differentiation: ride a river current u, carry a sample of v, compare with the local v; introduce streamlines, congruences and Lie dragging (Figs. 33.3, 33.4).
9. Formal machinery: integral curves exist by ODE theory; define L_u v as a limit; compute it in three steps (drag v, Taylor expand v, subtract) to get u.dv - v.du = [u,v] (Fig. 33.5).
10. Check and interpretation: along a coordinate basis vector the Lie derivative is just the partial derivative; caveat that the Lie derivative depends on neighbouring values and cannot serve as the curvature derivative.
11. Generalise via the Leibniz rule: Lie derivative of 1-forms, general tensors, and the metric (skippable technical section).
12. Application: isometries as flows that preserve the metric, L_xi g = 0; derive Killing's equation; find Killing vectors by spotting missing coordinates.
13. Payoff: prove u . xi is conserved along geodesics and apply it to Schwarzschild (energy and angular momentum) and Rindler.

**Signature moves**

- One diagram reused twice: the failed-parallelogram picture for the commutator reappears with P and Q labels as the Lie derivative, making L_u v = [u,v] visible before it is algebraically confirmed.
- Rules of the exterior derivative placed in a shaded margin box, with the main text immediately converting them into a usable component formula.
- Worked examples chosen with a later payoff in mind: the 1-forms differentiated in Example 33.1 are those of the expanding-Universe metric.
- Physical fluid language (river current, streamlines, comoving with the cosmological fluid) used to motivate a purely geometric construction.
- Explicit permission to skip: a curly-arrow box says the tensor recipe of Section 33.4 may be taken on trust if the reader keeps the metric result.
- Closing the loop with Part IV: the Killing-vector recipe stated without proof in Chapter 22 is now proved and re-applied to Schwarzschild.
- Short symmetry-contraction proof: a symmetric pair u^a u^b kills everything but the symmetric part of xi_{a;b}, which Killing's equation sets to zero.

## Section by section

### Chapter opening: derivatives without parallelism — p.340 (pdf 357)

Frames the chapter around the difficulty of comparing tensors located at different points. Parallelism supplied by a metric and a connection is one route, postponed to Chapter 34. Two more primitive routes are announced: the exterior derivative, restricted to forms and later used for curvature, and the Lie derivative, which works on any tensor by transporting it with an auxiliary vector field. Its uses in cosmology and for conserved quantities via Killing vectors are flagged up front. The epigraph jokes that there are many ways to reach the same goal.

**Concepts:** exterior derivative, Lie derivative, Killing vector field, affine connection

**Key moves**
- State the comparison problem for tensor fields at separated points
- Contrast connection-based parallelism with connection-free alternatives
- Preview the three destinations: forms and curvature, cosmological flow, conserved quantities

### §33.1 Exterior calculus — p.340 (pdf 357)

Defines the exterior derivative d as an operator that raises a p-form to a (p+1)-form. Pictorially, d of a 1-form such as f(x) dy adds sheets normal to the direction of change and creates dx wedge dy tubes. Four margin rules fix d: linearity, df via the directional derivative, a graded Leibniz rule with sign (-1)^p, and dd = 0. These give the component formula for d of a p-form, with or without the 1/p! factor depending on whether indices are summed freely or in increasing order. Example 33.1 differentiates four 1-forms in (t, chi, theta, phi) coordinates of the expanding Universe. Example 33.2 builds the ladder f -> df -> planes, A -> F = dA -> tubes, nu -> mu = d nu -> cells, noting that applying d twice gives zero at each stage and that a 5-form cannot exist in four dimensions.

**Concepts:** exterior derivative, p-form, wedge product, graded Leibniz rule for the exterior derivative, nilpotency of the exterior derivative, differential of a function, geometric picture of forms, vanishing of forms of degree above the dimension

**Key moves**
- Picture: coefficient change of a 1-form along x adds dx sheets, producing a 2-form of tubes
- Axioms I-IV (eqns 33.1-33.5) in a margin box
- Apply the rules to A = (1/p!) A_{ab..s} dx^a wedge ... to get dA = (1/p!) partial_mu A dx^mu wedge dx^a wedge ... (33.10)
- Alternative ordered-index form without 1/p! (33.9)
- Worked drill: d(dt) = 0; d(a dchi) = adot dt wedge dchi; derivative terms along an existing differential vanish by antisymmetry
- Ladder of exact forms each annihilated by a second d; degree capped at the spacetime dimension

### §33.2 Commutators — p.342 (pdf 359)

Treating vector fields as derivative operators u = d/dlambda and v = d/dmu, defines the commutator (Lie bracket) [u,v] = uv - vu. Acting on a test function in components, the second-derivative terms cancel and what remains is itself a first-order operator. So [u,v] is a vector field with components u^a partial_a v^b - v^a partial_a u^b, generally non-zero. Geometrically the two terms are the change of v across u and the change of u across v. Their difference is the arrow joining the ends of the two routes around a would-be parallelogram (Fig. 33.2), which vanishes for constant fields in Euclidean space. Applied to basis vectors, a vanishing bracket is exactly the condition for a coordinate basis. Orthonormal frames, where measurements are interpreted, are usually non-coordinate.

**Concepts:** Lie bracket of vector fields, vector as directional derivative operator, geometric interpretation of the Lie bracket, coordinate basis, non-coordinate basis, orthonormal frame

**Key moves**
- Define [u,v] as an operator and act on f (33.22, 33.23)
- Expand in coordinates; second-derivative terms cancel by symmetry of mixed partials (33.24)
- Strip f to obtain the component formula (33.25)
- Map each term to a finite-difference picture: Delta_2 (change in v across u) minus Delta_1 (change in u across v)
- Coordinate basis iff [e_a, e_b] = 0; link back to Chapters 3 and 10

### §33.3 Lie derivatives of vectors — p.344 (pdf 361)

Motivates Lie differentiation with an observer drifting in a river whose velocity field is u. The observer carries a vector of v downstream and compares it with the v found at the new spot. It introduces streamlines, congruences, the notation L_u Z and Lie dragging (zero Lie derivative). A dragged vector keeps spanning the same pair of neighbouring streamlines (Fig. 33.4). Example 33.4 guarantees integral curves exist, since dx^mu/dlambda = u^mu is a first-order ODE system. The Lie derivative is defined as the limit of [v(Q) - v'(Q)]/Delta lambda, where v' is v(P) pushed along the flow. Example 33.5 computes it in three steps. First the Jacobian of the infinitesimal shift x -> x + u dlambda drags v, adding (partial u) v. Next v is Taylor-expanded from P to Q, adding (u . partial) v. Subtracting gives [u,v] (Fig. 33.5). Rules are boxed for scalars (L_u f = u[f]) and vectors. A margin note gives a covariant rewrite that does not actually need the connection. The section warns that L_u v depends on values at neighbouring points, so it cannot be the curvature derivative. Example 33.6 shows that along a coordinate basis vector the Lie derivative reduces to a partial derivative.

**Concepts:** Lie derivative, Lie derivative of a vector field, Lie derivative of a scalar function, Lie dragging, integral curve, congruence of curves, diffeomorphism, comoving observers and cosmological fluid, non-tensorial dependence of the Lie derivative on the flow field, Lie derivative along a coordinate basis vector

**Key moves**
- River-current picture: extract, drift, compare
- Define congruence and integral curves via dx^mu/dlambda = u^mu (33.27)
- Limit definition L_u v(P) = lim [v(Q) - v'(Q)]/Delta lambda (33.28)
- Step I: drag v with the Jacobian delta + partial u dlambda (33.31)
- Step II: Taylor expand v from P to Q (33.32)
- Step III: subtract to get u^a partial_a v^b - v^a partial_a u^b = [u,v] (33.34, 33.35)
- Optional covariant form u^a v^b_{;a} - v^a u^b_{;a} (33.33)
- Special case u = e_1 gives partial_1 W^mu (33.38)

### §33.4 Lie derivatives of tensors — p.347 (pdf 364)

Extends Lie differentiation to 1-forms by requiring a Leibniz rule on the contraction <sigma, w>, a scalar whose Lie derivative is its directional derivative. Solving gives (L_v sigma)_mu = v^nu partial_nu sigma_mu + sigma_nu partial_mu v^nu. The same Leibniz logic, stated for tensor products and contractions in a margin note, produces the general component formula. It has a transport term, a minus-sign term for each upper index and a plus-sign term for each lower index. Example 33.8 specialises this to the metric. A margin box tells readers they may skip the section if they accept eqn 33.46. A closing paragraph lists physical uses: invariance along a curve, comoving coordinates in cosmology, and the separation vector in geodesic deviation, which is Lie dragged by the geodesic flow.

**Concepts:** Leibniz rule for the Lie derivative, Lie derivative of a 1-form, Lie derivative of a general tensor, Lie derivative of the metric, geodesic deviation vector, comoving observers and cosmological fluid

**Key moves**
- Leibniz rule on <sigma, w> with L_v f = partial_v f (33.41)
- Relabel dummy indices and cancel arbitrary w to get the 1-form formula (33.42, 33.43)
- Tensor-product Leibniz rule and contraction (33.39, 33.40, 33.44)
- General index-pattern formula (33.45)
- Metric specialisation (33.46)

### §33.5 Killing vectors — p.348 (pdf 365)

Links symmetry to conservation, citing Noether's theorem, then gives the geometric version. An isometry is a flow that leaves all distances unchanged. Its generator xi is a Killing vector, defined by L_xi g = 0. Example 33.9 writes this in coordinates, swaps partials for covariant derivatives, and uses metric compatibility to reach Killing's equation xi_{a;b} + xi_{b;a} = 0. Combining this with Example 33.6 gives the practical rule: if no metric component depends on x^1, then e_1 is a Killing vector. The conservation theorem is boxed: the dot product of a Killing vector with a geodesic's tangent is constant along it. Example 33.10 proves this with the Leibniz rule, nabla_u u = 0 and symmetric contraction. Examples 33.11 and 33.12 apply it to Schwarzschild (e_t and e_phi, giving u_t and u_phi) and Rindler (e_t, giving -x^2 u^t).

**Concepts:** isometry, Killing vector field, Killing's equation, Noether's theorem, metric compatibility, comma-to-semicolon rule, Killing vectors from metric independence of a coordinate, conserved quantity along a geodesic from a Killing vector, geodesic, Schwarzschild conserved energy and angular momentum, Rindler spacetime

**Key moves**
- Isometry: dragging along xi preserves distances, so L_xi g = 0 (33.47)
- Coordinates -> covariant derivatives -> use g_{ab;c} = 0 -> Killing's equation (33.49-33.51)
- Coordinate independence: L_{e_1} g = partial_1 g = 0 (33.52)
- Conservation proof: nabla_u(u.xi) = (nabla_u u).xi + u^a u^b xi_{a;b} = 0 (33.53-33.55)
- Apply to Schwarzschild and Rindler (33.56-33.59)

### Chapter summary and exercises — p.350 (pdf 367)

## Concepts

### exterior derivative

*operation · core* · also: exterior derivative operator d, exterior differentiation

The unique linear operator d on differential forms that maps a p-form to a (p+1)-form, reduces to the gradient 1-form df on functions, obeys the graded Leibniz rule d(alpha wedge beta) = d alpha wedge beta + (-1)^p alpha wedge d beta, and satisfies dd = 0. In components it differentiates each coefficient and wedges the new differential on the left.

**How introduced:** Motivated as a connection-free way to measure how a form changes. It is shown first as a picture (sheets of dy acquiring perpendicular dx sheets to become tubes), then fixed by four margin axioms, then turned into a component formula and exercised on 1-forms from the expanding-Universe metric.

**Prerequisites:** p-form, wedge product, differential of a function

$$
\mathbf{d}\tilde{A} = \frac{1}{p!}\,\partial_\mu A_{\alpha\beta\dots\sigma}\,\mathbf{d}x^\mu\wedge\mathbf{d}x^\alpha\wedge\dots\wedge\mathbf{d}x^\sigma
$$

**Notes:** Independent of any metric or connection, which is why Cartan's method (Ch 36) can compute curvature from it. The book notes d can be viewed as a (0,1)-type operator.

**Where:** GA §33.1 p.340 (pdf 357); GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358); GA §33.1 p.342 (pdf 359); GA p.350 (pdf 367)

### p-form

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: differential form, form field

A totally antisymmetric (0,p) tensor field, written in a coordinate basis as (1/p!) A_{a b ... s} dx^a wedge dx^b wedge ... wedge dx^s. It eats p vectors and returns a number that changes sign when any two inputs are swapped.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapter 32 as the input to the exterior derivative, with the visual dictionary of 1-forms as surfaces and 2-forms as tubes.

**Prerequisites:** wedge product

**Where:** GA §33.1 p.340 (pdf 357); GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

### wedge product

*operation · revisited* · also: exterior product

The antisymmetrised tensor product of forms; for 1-forms dx wedge dy = -dy wedge dx, so any differential wedged with itself vanishes.

**How introduced:** Used operationally in Example 33.1 to discard terms such as a(t) cos theta dtheta wedge dtheta.

**Prerequisites:** p-form

$$
\mathbf{d}x^\mu\wedge\mathbf{d}x^\mu = 0\ \text{(no sum)}
$$

**Where:** GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

### differential of a function

*definition · revisited* · also: gradient 1-form, df

For a scalar field f, the 1-form df whose contraction with any vector v gives the directional derivative v[f]; in coordinates df = (partial f/partial x^k) dx^k.

**How introduced:** Given as rule II of the exterior derivative, identifying df as d acting on a 0-form.

**Prerequisites:** vector as directional derivative operator

$$
\langle \mathbf{d}f, \mathbf{v}\rangle = \mathbf{v}[f] = \partial_{\mathbf{v}} f
$$

$$
\mathbf{d}f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x^k}\mathbf{d}x^k
$$

**Where:** GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

### graded Leibniz rule for the exterior derivative

*identity · introduced* · also: antiderivation property of d, rule III

The product rule for d acting on a wedge product of a p-form and a q-form: d(alpha wedge beta) = d alpha wedge beta + (-1)^p alpha wedge d beta, where the sign accounts for moving d past the p one-form factors of alpha.

**How introduced:** Listed as rule III in the margin box and used implicitly to justify the component formula for d.

**Prerequisites:** exterior derivative, wedge product

$$
\mathbf{d}(\tilde\alpha\wedge\tilde\beta) = \mathbf{d}\tilde\alpha\wedge\tilde\beta + (-1)^p\,\tilde\alpha\wedge\mathbf{d}\tilde\beta
$$

**Where:** GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

### nilpotency of the exterior derivative

*identity · developed* · also: dd = 0, d squared equals zero, rule IV

Applying the exterior derivative twice to any form gives zero. In components this follows from the symmetry of mixed partial derivatives contracted with antisymmetric wedge products; it generalises curl grad = 0 and div curl = 0.

**How introduced:** Stated as rule IV, used in Example 33.1 (ddt = 0) and dramatised in Example 33.2 as a ladder function -> planes -> tubes -> cells where each exact form is annihilated by a further d.

**Prerequisites:** exterior derivative

$$
\mathbf{d}^2 = \mathbf{d}\mathbf{d} = 0
$$

**Notes:** The book does not use the words closed or exact; for F = dA it gives dF = 0, which Chapter 43 identifies as the electromagnetic Bianchi identity.

**Where:** GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358); GA §33.1 p.342 (pdf 359)

### geometric picture of forms

*technique · revisited* · also: forms as sheets, tubes and cells, honeycomb picture of forms

A visual dictionary in which a 1-form is a stack of surfaces crossed by vectors, a 2-form is a bundle of tubes formed where two stacks intersect, and a 3-form is a lattice of cells; the exterior derivative adds a new family of sheets oriented by the direction in which the coefficient varies.

**How introduced:** Carried over from Chapter 32 and applied in Fig. 33.1 and Example 33.2 to make d concrete.

**Prerequisites:** p-form

**Where:** GA §33.1 p.340 (pdf 357); GA §33.1 p.342 (pdf 359)

### vanishing of forms of degree above the dimension

*theorem · introduced* · also: no 5-forms in 4D

In an n-dimensional space every p-form with p > n is identically zero, because an antisymmetric product of more than n basis 1-forms must repeat one of them.

**How introduced:** Stated at the end of Example 33.2 as the point where the exterior-derivative ladder must stop in spacetime.

**Prerequisites:** wedge product, p-form

**Notes:** The book places this beside dd = 0 as a constraint in 3+1 dimensions; strictly it follows from antisymmetry, not from dd = 0.

**Where:** GA §33.1 p.342 (pdf 359)

### vector as directional derivative operator

*definition · revisited* · also: vector as d/dlambda, tangent vector operator

A vector field u is identified with the first-order differential operator u = d/dlambda = u^a partial_a that returns the rate of change of a function along the curves it is tangent to.

**How introduced:** Assumed from Chapter 31 and used to define the commutator as an operator product.

$$
\mathbf{u}[f] = u^\alpha\,\partial_\alpha f
$$

**Where:** GA §33.2 p.342 (pdf 359); GA §33.2 p.343 (pdf 360)

### Lie bracket of vector fields

*operation · core* · also: commutator of vector fields, [u,v]

For vector fields u and v, the vector field [u,v] = uv - vu defined by its action on functions; its components u^a partial_a v^b - v^a partial_a u^b show that second derivatives cancel, leaving a genuine first-order operator.

**How introduced:** Introduced as the easiest route to the Lie derivative: define it as an operator commutator, act on a function in coordinates (Example 33.3), observe the components need not vanish, then read it geometrically.

**Prerequisites:** vector as directional derivative operator

$$
[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}] = \left(u^\alpha\partial_\alpha v^\beta - v^\alpha\partial_\alpha u^\beta\right)\partial_\beta
$$

**Notes:** Antisymmetric and obeys the Jacobi identity (quoted in the hint to Exercise 33.3).

**Where:** GA §33.2 p.342 (pdf 359); GA §33.2 p.343 (pdf 360); GA p.350 (pdf 367)

### geometric interpretation of the Lie bracket

*technique · developed* · also: failure of the parallelogram to close, closure-gap picture

Step along u then along v (evaluated at the new point), and separately along v then u. The two endpoints differ, to second order in the step size, by the commutator [u,v]. It is zero when the fields make closed quadrilaterals, e.g. constant fields in Euclidean space.

**How introduced:** Built from Fig. 33.2. The term u.partial v is the change of v between the base and tip of u (Delta_2). The term v.partial u is the change of u between the base and tip of v (Delta_1). Their difference is the arrow closing the figure. Exercise 33.1 asks for the formal version.

**Prerequisites:** Lie bracket of vector fields

$$
\mathcal{P}_4-\mathcal{P}_3 = [\mathbf{u}(\mathcal{P}_0)+\mathbf{v}(\mathcal{P}_1)]-[\mathbf{u}(\mathcal{P}_2)+\mathbf{v}(\mathcal{P}_0)]
$$

**Notes:** Reused in Chapter 34 to interpret torsion and in Chapter 35 for the loop derivation of curvature.

**Where:** GA §33.2 p.343 (pdf 360); GA p.350 (pdf 367)

### coordinate basis

*definition · developed* · also: holonomic basis

A set of basis vector fields that can be written as partial derivatives with respect to some coordinates, e_a = partial/partial x^a. A basis field set is a coordinate basis if and only if all their mutual Lie brackets vanish.

**How introduced:** Revisited from Chapters 3 and 10: since partial derivatives commute, coordinate basis vectors must have vanishing commutators; a non-zero bracket proves no coordinates generate the basis.

**Prerequisites:** Lie bracket of vector fields

$$
[\mathbf{e}_\alpha,\mathbf{e}_\beta] = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §33.2 p.343 (pdf 360); GA §33.2 p.344 (pdf 361)

### non-coordinate basis

*definition · introduced* · also: anholonomic basis

A basis of vector fields with at least one non-vanishing commutator, so it cannot be the set of partial derivatives of any coordinate system.

**How introduced:** Defined as the negation of the coordinate-basis test, with the remark that local orthonormal measurement frames are usually of this type.

**Prerequisites:** coordinate basis, Lie bracket of vector fields

**Where:** GA §33.2 p.344 (pdf 361)

### orthonormal frame

*definition · mention* · also: local orthonormal tetrad, measurement frame

A basis at each point whose vectors are mutually orthogonal and unit-normalised with respect to the metric; physical measurements are read off in such frames.

**How introduced:** Mentioned as the practical setting for measurements and as a typical non-coordinate basis.

**Prerequisites:** non-coordinate basis

**Where:** GA §33.2 p.344 (pdf 361)

### integral curve

*definition · introduced* · also: streamline, flow line of a vector field

A curve x^mu(lambda) whose tangent vector at every point equals the given vector field there, i.e. a solution of dx^mu/dlambda = u^mu(x(lambda)).

**How introduced:** Example 33.4 inverts the Chapter 31 logic, generating curves from a field rather than a field from curves. Existence and uniqueness near each point follow from standard theory of first-order ODEs.

**Prerequisites:** vector as directional derivative operator

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x^\mu}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} = u^\mu\big(x^1(\lambda),\dots,x^n(\lambda)\big)
$$

**Where:** GA §33.3 p.344 (pdf 361); GA §33.3 p.345 (pdf 362)

### congruence of curves

*definition · introduced* · also: congruence, family of streamlines

A family of curves such that exactly one passes through each point of a region, each tangent to a given vector field; the integral curves of a smooth vector field form a congruence.

**How introduced:** Introduced through the river's streamlines (Fig. 33.3) and then made precise in Example 33.4.

**Prerequisites:** integral curve

**Where:** GA §33.3 p.344 (pdf 361); GA §33.3 p.345 (pdf 362)

### diffeomorphism

*definition · mention* · also: flow map, active smooth transformation

A smooth invertible map of a manifold to itself with a smooth inverse; sliding every point a parameter distance along the integral curves of a vector field is an example.

**How introduced:** Margin note reassuring readers that being carried by a vector field is a legitimate geometric operation, generalising active coordinate transformations; details deferred to Appendix C.

**Prerequisites:** integral curve

**Where:** GA §33.3 p.344 (pdf 361)

### Lie dragging

*definition · developed* · also: Lie transport, Lie-dragged field

A tensor field is Lie dragged along u when carrying it along the flow of u reproduces the field already present, i.e. its Lie derivative along u vanishes. A Lie-dragged vector that joins two streamlines keeps joining the same two streamlines as it is carried along.

**How introduced:** Defined right after the L_u notation and illustrated by Fig. 33.4 with a connecting arrow between neighbouring streamlines at P and at Q.

**Prerequisites:** Lie derivative, congruence of curves

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{Z} = 0
$$

**Notes:** Used in Chapter 35: the geodesic separation vector n satisfies L_u n = 0.

**Where:** GA §33.3 p.344 (pdf 361); GA §33.3 p.345 (pdf 362); GA §33.4 p.348 (pdf 365)

### Lie derivative

*operation · core* · also: Lie derivative along a vector field, L_u

The derivative of a tensor field Z along a vector field u. In the book's construction the value at P is carried a parameter step Delta lambda along the integral curve of u to Q. It is subtracted from the field actually present at Q, and the difference is divided by Delta lambda as the step shrinks. Pulling the downstream value back to P instead gives the same result to first order. No metric or connection is needed, and (k,l) tensors map to (k,l) tensors.

**How introduced:** Motivated by riding a river current. Defined for vectors by a limit, computed in coordinates, found equal to the commutator, then extended to scalars, 1-forms and general tensors through the Leibniz rule.

**Prerequisites:** congruence of curves, Lie bracket of vector fields, tensor transformation law

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{v}(\mathcal{P}) = \lim_{\Delta\lambda\to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta\lambda}\{\mathbf{v}(\mathcal{Q})-\mathbf{v}'(\mathcal{Q})\}
$$

**Notes:** The book writes the symbol as a script L and jokes about the pound sign. The commutator identity for Lie derivatives (Exercise 33.3) shows they form a representation of the vector-field Lie algebra.

**Where:** GA p.340 (pdf 357); GA §33.3 p.344 (pdf 361); GA §33.3 p.345 (pdf 362); GA §33.3 p.346 (pdf 363); GA §33.4 p.347 (pdf 364); GA p.350 (pdf 367)

### Lie derivative of a scalar function

*definition · developed* · also: L_u f

The Lie derivative of a scalar field along u is simply its directional derivative u[f] = u^a partial_a f.

**How introduced:** Stated as a boxed rule once the vector result is in hand; also the base case for the Leibniz derivation of the 1-form formula.

**Prerequisites:** Lie derivative, vector as directional derivative operator

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}} f = \mathbf{u}[f] = \partial_{\mathbf{u}} f
$$

**Where:** GA §33.3 p.346 (pdf 363); GA §33.4 p.347 (pdf 364)

### Lie derivative of a vector field

*theorem · core* · also: L_u v = [u,v]

For vector fields, L_u v = [u,v], with components u^a partial_a v^b - v^a partial_a u^b. The first term is the change of v along the flow; the second corrects for the stretching and rotation the flow itself imposes on a dragged vector.

**How introduced:** Derived in Example 33.5 in three steps: push v(P) to Q with the Jacobian of x -> x + u dlambda, Taylor expand v to Q, subtract; confirmed geometrically by Fig. 33.5, which is Fig. 33.2 relabelled.

**Prerequisites:** Lie derivative, Lie bracket of vector fields, tensor transformation law

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{v} = [\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}] = \left(u^\alpha v^\beta{}_{,\alpha} - v^\alpha u^\beta{}_{,\alpha}\right)\partial_\beta
$$

**Where:** GA §33.3 p.345 (pdf 362); GA §33.3 p.346 (pdf 363); GA p.350 (pdf 367)

### tensor transformation law

*law · mention* · also: component transformation rule

Under a change of coordinates, vector components transform as A^{mu'} = (partial x^{mu'}/partial x^nu) A^nu, with the inverse Jacobian for lower indices.

**How introduced:** Invoked in Step I of Example 33.5 to express the dragged vector, treating the infinitesimal shift along u as a coordinate map.

$$
A^{\mu'} = \frac{\partial x^{\mu'}}{\partial x^\nu}A^\nu
$$

**Where:** GA §33.3 p.345 (pdf 362)

### Lie derivative along a coordinate basis vector

*theorem · developed* · also: Lie derivative as coordinate-free partial derivative

If u is the coordinate basis vector e_1 = partial/partial x^1, then the components of L_u T are just the partial derivatives partial T/partial x^1 of the components of T, because all derivatives of u's constant components vanish.

**How introduced:** Example 33.6 plugs u^nu = delta^nu_1 into the vector formula, and the text reads the result as a coordinate-free partial derivative; it becomes the engine of the Killing-vector inspection rule.

**Prerequisites:** Lie derivative of a vector field, coordinate basis

$$
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{e}_1}\mathbf{W})^\mu = \frac{\partial W^\mu}{\partial x^1}
$$

**Notes:** Conversely, any nowhere-vanishing vector field can locally be made a coordinate basis vector (straightening theorem), which the book does not state.

**Where:** GA §33.3 p.346 (pdf 363); GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366)

### non-tensorial dependence of the Lie derivative on the flow field

*principle · introduced* · also: why the Lie derivative is not the covariant derivative

L_u v at a point involves derivatives of u there, not just u's value, so it cannot be written as a linear map of u_P acting on v; hence it cannot supply a unique notion of rate of change in a direction, and a connection is needed for curvature.

**How introduced:** A short caution after the boxed rules: the Lie derivative at P depends on the fields near P, which the authors take as the reason a covariant derivative must still be introduced.

**Prerequisites:** Lie derivative of a vector field, covariant derivative

**Notes:** The book phrases the issue as dependence on neighbouring values of v and contrasts it with the partial derivative; the sharper statement is about dependence on derivatives of u.

**Where:** GA §33.3 p.346 (pdf 363)

### covariant derivative

*operation · mention* · also: nabla, semicolon derivative

The connection-based derivative nabla_u that compares tensors at nearby points by parallel transport; its components add Christoffel-symbol terms to partial derivatives.

**How introduced:** Used as a contrast throughout, in an optional rewrite of L_u v (margin note 9), in the derivation of Killing's equation, and in the geodesic conservation proof.

**Prerequisites:** affine connection

$$
v^\beta{}_{;\alpha} = \partial_\alpha v^\beta + \Gamma^\beta{}_{\alpha\gamma}v^\gamma
$$

**Where:** GA §33.3 p.346 (pdf 363); GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365); GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366)

### affine connection

*mathematical-object · mention* · also: connection

Additional structure that specifies how to transport tensors parallel to themselves between neighbouring points, encoded in connection coefficients Gamma.

**How introduced:** Named in the opening as the standard fix for comparing tensors at different points and explicitly deferred to Chapter 34.

**Where:** GA p.340 (pdf 357)

### Leibniz rule for the Lie derivative

*identity · developed* · also: product rule for L_v, Lie derivative commutes with contraction

The Lie derivative distributes over tensor products, L_v(S tensor T) = L_v S tensor T + S tensor L_v T, and commutes with contraction, so L_v <S, T> = <L_v S, T> + <S, L_v T>.

**How introduced:** Stated in margin note 10 and used as the workhorse for deducing how L_v acts on 1-forms and higher tensors; Exercise 33.2 asks for the commutation with contraction to be checked via L_u of the Kronecker delta.

**Prerequisites:** Lie derivative

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\langle\mathbf{S},\mathbf{T}\rangle = \langle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{S},\mathbf{T}\rangle + \langle\mathbf{S},\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{T}\rangle
$$

**Where:** GA §33.4 p.347 (pdf 364); GA p.350 (pdf 367)

### Lie derivative of a 1-form

*theorem · developed* · also: L_v sigma

For a 1-form sigma, (L_v sigma)_mu = v^nu partial_nu sigma_mu + sigma_nu partial_mu v^nu: a transport term plus a plus-signed correction from the flow's gradient.

**How introduced:** Deduced in Example 33.7 by applying the Leibniz rule to the scalar sigma_mu w^mu, inserting the vector result for L_v w, relabelling dummy indices and stripping the arbitrary w.

**Prerequisites:** Leibniz rule for the Lie derivative, Lie derivative of a vector field, Lie derivative of a scalar function

$$
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\tilde\sigma)_\mu = v^\nu\partial_\nu\sigma_\mu + \sigma_\nu\partial_\mu v^\nu
$$

**Where:** GA §33.4 p.347 (pdf 364)

### Lie derivative of a general tensor

*theorem · developed* · also: component formula for L_v T

For a (k,l) tensor, L_v T equals v^mu partial_mu of the components, minus one term T^{..mu..} partial_mu v^a for each upper index a, plus one term T_{..mu..} partial_nu v^mu for each lower index nu.

**How introduced:** Obtained by repeated Leibniz rule; presented as a recipe with a note on the neat index pattern, and flagged as skippable.

**Prerequisites:** Lie derivative of a 1-form, Lie derivative of a vector field

$$
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}T)^{\alpha\dots}{}_{\nu\dots} = v^\mu\partial_\mu T^{\alpha\dots}{}_{\nu\dots} - T^{\mu\dots}{}_{\nu\dots}\partial_\mu v^\alpha - \dots + T^{\alpha\dots}{}_{\mu\dots}\partial_\nu v^\mu + \dots
$$

**Where:** GA §33.4 p.347 (pdf 364); GA p.350 (pdf 367)

### Lie derivative of the metric

*theorem · developed* · also: L_v g

(L_v g)_{ab} = v^c partial_c g_{ab} + g_{cb} partial_a v^c + g_{ac} partial_b v^c. With a torsion-free metric-compatible connection this equals v_{a;b} + v_{b;a}. It measures how the flow of v distorts distances.

**How introduced:** Example 33.8 specialises the general formula to the metric, singled out as the result readers need even if they skip Section 33.4.

**Prerequisites:** Lie derivative of a general tensor

$$
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}g)_{\alpha\beta} = v^\gamma\partial_\gamma g_{\alpha\beta} + g_{\gamma\beta}\partial_\alpha v^\gamma + g_{\alpha\gamma}\partial_\beta v^\gamma
$$

**Notes:** The book calls g the 'metric 2-form'; g is a symmetric (0,2) tensor, not a differential form.

**Where:** GA §33.4 p.347 (pdf 364); GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365)

### geodesic deviation vector

*mathematical-object · mention* · also: separation vector between neighbouring geodesics, connecting vector

The vector joining corresponding points on two neighbouring geodesics of a congruence; it is Lie dragged by the geodesics' tangent field, so its Lie derivative along that field vanishes.

**How introduced:** Listed among the uses of the Lie derivative just before Section 33.5, anticipating the derivation in Chapter 35.

**Prerequisites:** Lie dragging, geodesic

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{n} = 0

**Where:** GA §33.4 p.348 (pdf 365)

### comoving observers and cosmological fluid

*definition · mention* · also: comoving coordinates, being carried with the cosmic fluid

Observers or coordinates that move with the local flow of matter, so that measuring change as one is carried along is naturally expressed by a Lie derivative along the fluid four-velocity.

**How introduced:** Invoked in the chapter opening, a margin note and the uses paragraph as the physical reason relativists care about Lie derivatives; fluids are deferred to Chapter 39.

### isometry

*definition · introduced* · also: distance-preserving symmetry, metric symmetry

A transformation of a space, here the flow along a vector field, that leaves all distances unchanged, i.e. maps the metric to itself.

**How introduced:** Introduced as the geometric form of symmetry: slide a set of points along streamlines and check that every separation stays the same.

**Prerequisites:** diffeomorphism, Lie derivative of the metric

**Where:** GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365)

### Killing vector field

*definition · core* · also: Killing vector, Killing field, infinitesimal isometry

A vector field xi whose flow is an isometry, equivalently one along which the metric is Lie dragged: L_xi g = 0.

**How introduced:** Defined from the isometry idea and the Lie derivative of the metric; then turned into Killing's equation, a practical inspection rule, and a conservation theorem.

**Prerequisites:** isometry, Lie derivative of the metric, Lie dragging

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}g = 0
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 33.4 shows the commutator of two Killing fields is again Killing, so they form a Lie algebra.

**Where:** GA p.340 (pdf 357); GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365); GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366); GA §33.5 p.350 (pdf 367)

### Killing's equation

*law · core* · also: xi_(a;b) = 0

The covariant condition xi_{a;b} + xi_{b;a} = 0, i.e. the symmetrised covariant derivative of the lowered Killing vector vanishes. It is equivalent to L_xi g = 0 for the Levi-Civita connection.

**How introduced:** Example 33.9 starts from the coordinate form of L_xi g = 0, replaces partial derivatives by covariant ones, and uses metric compatibility to lower indices.

**Prerequisites:** Killing vector field, covariant derivative, metric compatibility, comma-to-semicolon rule

$$
\xi_{\alpha;\beta} + \xi_{\beta;\alpha} = 0
$$

$$
\xi_{(\alpha;\beta)} = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365); GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366)

### metric compatibility

*principle · mention* · also: covariant constancy of the metric, g_{ab;c} = 0

The requirement that the connection preserves the metric under parallel transport, g_{ab;c} = 0, so indices can be raised and lowered through covariant derivatives.

**How introduced:** Cited in a margin note as the Chapter 7 compatibility property, used to kill the xi^c g_{ab;c} term in Killing's equation, and promised more detail in Chapter 34.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative

$$
g_{\alpha\beta;\gamma} = 0
$$

**Notes:** The text says this holds for any affine connection; it holds only for metric-compatible connections such as Levi-Civita.

**Where:** GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365)

### comma-to-semicolon rule

*technique · mention* · also: commas go to semicolons, minimal substitution

The practice of converting an expression written with partial derivatives into one with covariant derivatives. It is legitimate here only because the Christoffel terms introduced cancel for a symmetric connection, making both forms of L_xi g identical.

**How introduced:** Invoked without justification in Example 33.9 to pass from eqn 33.49 to 33.50.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative

**Where:** GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365)

### Killing vectors from metric independence of a coordinate

*technique · developed* · also: Killing vectors by inspection, cyclic coordinate rule

If no component of the metric depends on a coordinate x^k, then the coordinate basis vector e_k = partial/partial x^k is a Killing vector, because L_{e_k} g has components partial_k g_{ab}.

**How introduced:** Derived by combining Example 33.6 with L_xi g = 0; presented as the most straightforward way to find Killing vectors and then used for Schwarzschild and Rindler.

**Prerequisites:** Lie derivative along a coordinate basis vector, Killing vector field

$$
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{e}_1}g)_{\alpha\beta} = \partial_1 g_{\alpha\beta} = 0
$$

**Notes:** It finds only symmetries manifest in the chosen coordinates; e.g. rotations of the 2-sphere about the x and y axes are Killing but not coordinate basis vectors in (theta, phi).

**Where:** GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366); GA §33.5 p.350 (pdf 367)

### conserved quantity along a geodesic from a Killing vector

*theorem · core* · also: u . xi conservation, Killing constant of motion

If xi is a Killing vector and u the tangent to an affinely parametrised geodesic, then u . xi = g_{ab} u^a xi^b is constant along the geodesic.

**How introduced:** Stated in a shaded box, then proved in Example 33.10. The Leibniz rule splits nabla_u(u.xi). The geodesic condition removes the first term, and the second is u^a u^b xi_{a;b}, where only the symmetric part survives and Killing's equation sets it to zero.

**Prerequisites:** Killing's equation, geodesic, covariant derivative

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}) = u^\alpha u^\beta\xi_{(\alpha;\beta)} = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366); GA p.350 (pdf 367)

### geodesic

**How introduced:** Recalled as the property that removes one term in the conservation proof.

### Noether's theorem

### Schwarzschild conserved energy and angular momentum

*solution-or-model · revisited* · also: u_t and u_phi constants

Because the Schwarzschild metric is independent of t and phi, e_t and e_phi are Killing vectors and the covariant components u_t and u_phi of a geodesic's 4-velocity are constant; up to sign they are the energy and angular momentum per unit mass.

**How introduced:** Example 33.11 re-derives the Chapter 22 constants of motion from the new theorem.

### Rindler spacetime

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: Rindler metric, uniformly accelerated frame

Flat 1+1 spacetime in coordinates adapted to uniformly accelerated observers, ds^2 = -x^2 dt^2 + dx^2.

**How introduced:** Example 33.12 uses it as a second quick test of the inspection rule: t is absent, so e_t is Killing and -x^2 u^t is conserved on geodesics.

### Jacobi identity

For any three vector fields, [x,[y,z]] + [z,[x,y]] + [y,[z,x]] = 0.

## Key equations

### (33.1) Linearity of d · supporting · GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

$$
\mathbf{d}(\tilde\alpha+\tilde\beta) = \mathbf{d}\tilde\alpha + \mathbf{d}\tilde\beta
$$

Rule I: the exterior derivative distributes over sums of forms.

**Symbols:** alpha, beta: forms of the same degree

### (33.2) d on functions · supporting · GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

$$
\langle\mathbf{d}f,\mathbf{v}\rangle = \mathbf{v}[f] = \partial_{\mathbf{v}}f
$$

Rule II: feeding a vector into df returns the directional derivative of f along that vector.

**Symbols:** f scalar field; v vector

### (33.3) · supporting · GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

$$
\mathbf{d}f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x^k}\,\mathbf{d}x^k
$$

Coordinate expression of the gradient 1-form.

**Symbols:** dx^k coordinate basis 1-forms

### (33.4) Graded Leibniz rule · supporting · GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

$$
\mathbf{d}(\tilde\alpha\wedge\tilde\beta) = \mathbf{d}\tilde\alpha\wedge\tilde\beta + (-1)^p\,\tilde\alpha\wedge\mathbf{d}\tilde\beta
$$

Rule III: product rule for d with a sign from moving d past the p-form alpha.

**Symbols:** alpha a p-form, beta a q-form

### (33.5) Nilpotency · central · GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

$$
\mathbf{d}^2 = \mathbf{d}\mathbf{d} = 0
$$

Rule IV: two successive exterior derivatives always give zero.

### (33.6) General p-form · supporting · GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

$$
\tilde{A} = \frac{1}{p!}A_{\alpha\beta\dots\sigma}\,\mathbf{d}x^\alpha\wedge\mathbf{d}x^\beta\wedge\dots\wedge\mathbf{d}x^\sigma
$$

Component expansion of a p-form with antisymmetric coefficients summed over all index values.

**Symbols:** A_{ab..s} antisymmetric components

### (33.7) · derivation-step · GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

$$
\mathbf{d}\tilde{A} = \frac{1}{p!}\,\mathbf{d}A_{\alpha\beta\dots\sigma}\wedge\mathbf{d}x^\alpha\wedge\dots\wedge\mathbf{d}x^\sigma
$$

Consistency with rules II-IV: d acts only on the coefficient functions because d(dx^a) = 0.

### (33.8) · derivation-step · GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

$$
\mathbf{d}A_{\alpha\beta\dots\sigma} = \frac{\partial A_{\alpha\beta\dots\sigma}}{\partial x^\mu}\,\mathbf{d}x^\mu
$$

Each coefficient is a scalar, so its d is its gradient 1-form.

### (33.9) Ordered-index form · supporting · GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

$$
\mathbf{d}\tilde{A} = \frac{\partial A_{|\alpha\beta\dots\sigma|}}{\partial x^\mu}\,\mathbf{d}x^\mu\wedge\mathbf{d}x^\alpha\wedge\dots\wedge\mathbf{d}x^\sigma
$$

Same result with the sum restricted to increasing index order, so the 1/p! is absent (margin note).

**Symbols:** |...| sum over alpha < beta < ... < sigma

### (33.10) Exterior derivative in components · central · GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

$$
\mathbf{d}\tilde{A} = \frac{1}{p!}\frac{\partial A_{\alpha\beta\dots\sigma}}{\partial x^\mu}\,\mathbf{d}x^\mu\wedge\mathbf{d}x^\alpha\wedge\mathbf{d}x^\beta\wedge\dots\wedge\mathbf{d}x^\sigma
$$

Working formula: differentiate each coefficient and wedge the new differential in front; repeated in the summary as (33.60).

### (33.11) · supporting · GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

$$
\mathbf{d}\tilde{A} = \frac{\partial A_\alpha}{\partial x^\mu}\,\mathbf{d}x^\mu\wedge\mathbf{d}x^\alpha
$$

Special case p = 1 used throughout Example 33.1.

**Symbols:** x^mu = (t, chi, theta, phi)

### (33.13) · derivation-step · GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

$$
\mathbf{d}\big(a(t)\,\mathbf{d}\chi\big) = \frac{\partial a}{\partial t}\,\mathbf{d}t\wedge\mathbf{d}\chi
$$

Only the time dependence of the coefficient contributes.

**Symbols:** a(t) scale factor

### (33.14) · derivation-step · GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

$$
\mathbf{d}\big(a(t)\sin\theta\,\mathbf{d}\theta\big) = \dot a\sin\theta\,\mathbf{d}t\wedge\mathbf{d}\theta + a\cos\theta\,\mathbf{d}\theta\wedge\mathbf{d}\theta = \dot a\sin\theta\,\mathbf{d}t\wedge\mathbf{d}\theta

Differentiating the coefficient along the form's own direction produces a term that antisymmetry of the wedge product removes.

$$
\mathbf{d}\big(a\sin\chi\sin\theta\,\mathbf{d}\phi\big) = \dot a\sin\chi\sin\theta\,\mathbf{d}t\wedge\mathbf{d}\phi + a\cos\chi\sin\theta\,\mathbf{d}\chi\wedge\mathbf{d}\phi + a\sin\chi\cos\theta\,\mathbf{d}\theta\wedge\mathbf{d}\phi
$$

Three coordinates appear in the coefficient, so three 2-form terms survive. Corrected here: the printed first term omits the factor sin chi.

**Symbols:** dot a = da/dt

### (33.17) · supporting · GA §33.1 p.342 (pdf 359)

$$
\mathbf{d}\tilde\alpha = \mathbf{d}\mathbf{d}f = 0
$$

A gradient 1-form has zero exterior derivative (the analogue of curl grad = 0).

**Symbols:** alpha = df

### (33.19) · supporting · GA §33.1 p.342 (pdf 359)

$$
\tilde{F} = \mathbf{d}\tilde{A},\qquad \mathbf{d}\tilde{F} = \mathbf{d}\mathbf{d}\tilde{A} = 0
$$

A 2-form built as d of a 1-form automatically has zero exterior derivative; with A the potential this is later the homogeneous Maxwell equations.

**Symbols:** A 1-form, F 2-form

### (33.21) Ladder of exact forms · supporting · GA §33.1 p.342 (pdf 359)

$$
\tilde\mu = \mathbf{d}\tilde\nu,\qquad \mathbf{d}\tilde\mu = \mathbf{d}\mathbf{d}\tilde\nu = 0
$$

Last rung of the Example 33.2 ladder (eqns 33.20-33.21). A 3-form that comes from a 2-form has zero exterior derivative, and the chain ends at 4-forms in four dimensions.

**Symbols:** nu a 2-form, mu a 3-form

### (33.22) Commutator of vector fields · central · GA §33.2 p.342 (pdf 359)

$$
[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}] = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}\mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}
$$

Definition of the Lie bracket as an operator commutator.

**Symbols:** u = d/dlambda, v = d/dmu

### (33.23) · derivation-step · GA §33.2 p.343 (pdf 360)

$$
[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}][f] = \mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}[f]-\mathbf{v}\mathbf{u}[f]
$$

Action of the bracket on a test function.

### (33.24) · derivation-step · GA §33.2 p.343 (pdf 360)

$$
[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}][f] = u^\alpha\partial_\alpha(v^\beta\partial_\beta f) - v^\alpha\partial_\alpha(u^\beta\partial_\beta f) = \left(u^\alpha\partial_\alpha v^\beta - v^\alpha\partial_\alpha u^\beta\right)\partial_\beta f
$$

Second derivatives of f cancel, leaving a first-order operator: the bracket is a vector field.

### (33.25) Bracket in components · central · GA §33.2 p.343 (pdf 360)

$$
[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}] = \left(u^\alpha\frac{\partial v^\beta}{\partial x^\alpha} - v^\alpha\frac{\partial u^\beta}{\partial x^\alpha}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\beta}
$$

Component formula; first term is the change of v along u, second the change of u along v.

### Coordinate basis criterion · supporting · GA §33.2 p.344 (pdf 361)

$$
\{\mathbf{e}_\mu\}\ \text{is a coordinate basis} \iff [\mathbf{e}_\alpha,\mathbf{e}_\beta] = 0
$$

Basis fields come from coordinates exactly when all their brackets vanish.

### (33.26) Lie derivative notation · supporting · GA §33.3 p.344 (pdf 361)

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{Z}
$$

Lie derivative of the tensor Z carried along the vector field u; Z is Lie dragged when this vanishes.

**Symbols:** Z any tensor field; u the dragging field

### (33.27) Integral-curve equations · supporting · GA §33.3 p.345 (pdf 362)

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x^\mu(\lambda)}{\mathrm{d}\lambda} = u^\mu\big(x^1(\lambda),\dots,x^n(\lambda)\big)
$$

First-order ODEs whose solutions are the streamlines of u; local existence and uniqueness give a congruence.

### (33.28) Limit definition of the Lie derivative · central · GA §33.3 p.345 (pdf 362)

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{v}(\mathcal{P}) = \lim_{\Delta\lambda\to0}\frac{1}{\Delta\lambda}\left\{\mathbf{v}(\mathcal{Q}) - \mathbf{v}'(\mathcal{Q})\right\}
$$

Compare the field actually at Q with the vector dragged from P to Q along u's flow.

**Symbols:** Q is Delta lambda downstream of P; v' the dragged vector

### (33.29) · derivation-step · GA §33.3 p.345 (pdf 362)

$$
x^{\mu'} = x^\mu + u^\mu\,\mathrm{d}\lambda
$$

Infinitesimal flow map along u, treated as a coordinate transformation.

### (33.30) Component difference quotient · derivation-step · GA §33.3 p.345 (pdf 362)

$$
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{v}(\mathcal{P}))^\beta = \frac{v^\beta(\mathcal{Q}) - v^{\beta'}(\mathcal{Q})}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}
$$

The limit definition in components: the field found at Q minus the vector dragged there from P, divided by the step.

**Symbols:** v^{beta'} components of the dragged vector

### (33.31) Dragged vector · derivation-step · GA §33.3 p.345 (pdf 362)

$$
v^{\beta'}(\mathcal{Q}) = \frac{\partial x^{\beta'}}{\partial x^\alpha}v^\alpha(\mathcal{P}) = v^\beta(\mathcal{P}) + \frac{\partial u^\beta}{\partial x^\alpha}v^\alpha(\mathcal{P})\,\mathrm{d}\lambda
$$

Step I: the flow stretches and rotates v by the gradient of u; the extra piece is the change of u across v.

### (33.32) Field at Q · derivation-step · GA §33.3 p.346 (pdf 363)

$$
v^\beta(\mathcal{Q}) = v^\beta(\mathcal{P}) + \frac{\partial v^\beta}{\partial x^\alpha}u^\alpha(\mathcal{P})\,\mathrm{d}\lambda
$$

Step II: Taylor expansion of v along u.

### (33.33) Covariant form · supporting · GA §33.3 p.346 (pdf 363)

$$
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{v})^\beta = u^\alpha v^\beta{}_{;\alpha} - v^\alpha u^\beta{}_{;\alpha}
$$

Optional rewrite with covariant derivatives (margin note 9). The Christoffel terms cancel only for a symmetric (torsion-free) connection, a condition the note leaves unstated.

### (33.34) · derivation-step · GA §33.3 p.346 (pdf 363)

$$
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{v})^\beta = u^\alpha\frac{\partial v^\beta}{\partial x^\alpha} - v^\alpha\frac{\partial u^\beta}{\partial x^\alpha}
$$

Step III: subtracting (33.31) from (33.32) and dividing by dlambda.

### (33.35) Lie derivative of a vector · central · GA §33.3 p.346 (pdf 363)

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{v} = [\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}]
$$

The Lie derivative of a vector field is the Lie bracket.

### (33.36) Lie derivative of a scalar · central · GA §33.3 p.346 (pdf 363)

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}f = \mathbf{u}[f] = \partial_{\mathbf{u}}f
$$

Scalars are Lie differentiated by the ordinary directional derivative.

### (33.37) · supporting · GA §33.3 p.346 (pdf 363)

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{v} = [\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}] = \left(u^\alpha v^\beta{}_{,\alpha} - v^\alpha u^\beta{}_{,\alpha}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\beta}
$$

Boxed vector rule in comma notation. The printed extra form (d/dlambda) v - (d/dmu) u is schematic shorthand, not a literal identity.

### (33.38) Lie derivative along a basis vector · central · GA §33.3 p.346 (pdf 363)

$$
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{e}_1}\mathbf{W})^\mu = \delta^\nu{}_1\partial_\nu W^\mu - W^\nu\partial_\nu\delta^\mu{}_1 = \frac{\partial W^\mu}{\partial x^1}
$$

Along a coordinate basis vector the Lie derivative is a plain partial derivative. Corrected here: the printed second term has the Kronecker delta mis-indexed as delta^mu_nu.

### (33.39) · supporting · GA §33.4 p.347 (pdf 364)

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{S}\otimes\mathbf{T}) = \mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{S}\otimes\mathbf{T} + \mathbf{S}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{T}
$$

Leibniz rule over tensor products (margin note 10).

### (33.40) · supporting · GA §33.4 p.347 (pdf 364)

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\langle\mathbf{S},\mathbf{T}\rangle = \langle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{S},\mathbf{T}\rangle + \langle\mathbf{S},\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{T}\rangle
$$

Contracted Leibniz rule: the Lie derivative commutes with contraction.

### (33.41) · derivation-step · GA §33.4 p.347 (pdf 364)

$$
\langle\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\tilde\sigma,\mathbf{w}\rangle = \partial_{\mathbf{v}}f - \langle\tilde\sigma,\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{w}\rangle
$$

Defines the action of L_v on a 1-form through its contraction with an arbitrary vector.

**Symbols:** f = <sigma, w>

### (33.42) · derivation-step · GA §33.4 p.347 (pdf 364)

$$
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\tilde\sigma)_\mu w^\mu = v^\nu\partial_\nu(\sigma_\mu w^\mu) - \sigma_\mu\left(v^\nu\partial_\nu w^\mu - w^\nu\partial_\nu v^\mu\right) = \left(v^\nu\partial_\nu\sigma_\mu + \sigma_\nu\partial_\mu v^\nu\right)w^\mu
$$

The derivatives of w cancel. Relabelling the dummy indices in the remaining term shows that w appears only as an overall factor.

**Symbols:** w arbitrary vector field

### (33.43) Lie derivative of a 1-form · central · GA §33.4 p.347 (pdf 364)

$$
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\tilde\sigma)_\mu = v^\nu\frac{\partial\sigma_\mu}{\partial x^\nu} + \sigma_\nu\frac{\partial v^\nu}{\partial x^\mu}
$$

Lower index gets a plus-signed gradient-of-flow correction, opposite in sign to the vector case.

### (33.44) · derivation-step · GA §33.4 p.347 (pdf 364)

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\big[\mathbf{T}(\tilde\sigma,\dots;\mathbf{U},\dots)\big] = (\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{T})(\tilde\sigma,\dots;\mathbf{U},\dots) + \mathbf{T}(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\tilde\sigma,\dots;\mathbf{U},\dots) + \dots + \mathbf{T}(\tilde\sigma,\dots;\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{U},\dots) + \dots
$$

Leibniz rule for a tensor evaluated on its arguments; solving for L_v T gives its components.

### (33.45) Lie derivative of a general tensor · central · GA §33.4 p.347 (pdf 364)

$$
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}T)^{\alpha\beta\dots\kappa}{}_{\nu\xi\dots\omega} = v^\mu\partial_\mu T^{\alpha\beta\dots\kappa}{}_{\nu\xi\dots\omega} - T^{\mu\beta\dots\kappa}{}_{\nu\xi\dots\omega}\partial_\mu v^\alpha - \dots - T^{\alpha\beta\dots\mu}{}_{\nu\xi\dots\omega}\partial_\mu v^\kappa + T^{\alpha\beta\dots\kappa}{}_{\mu\xi\dots\omega}\partial_\nu v^\mu + \dots + T^{\alpha\beta\dots\kappa}{}_{\nu\xi\dots\mu}\partial_\omega v^\mu
$$

Transport term plus one minus term per upper index and one plus term per lower index.

### (33.46) Lie derivative of the metric · central · GA §33.4 p.347 (pdf 364)

$$
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}g)_{\alpha\beta} = v^\gamma\partial_\gamma g_{\alpha\beta} + g_{\gamma\beta}\partial_\alpha v^\gamma + g_{\alpha\gamma}\partial_\beta v^\gamma
$$

How the flow of v changes the metric; zero means the flow is an isometry.

### (33.47) Killing condition · central · GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365)

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol\xi}g = 0
$$

xi generates an isometry: the metric is Lie dragged along its streamlines.

**Symbols:** xi Killing vector

### (33.48) Killing's equation · central · GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365)

$$
\xi_{\alpha;\beta} + \xi_{\beta;\alpha} = 0
$$

Covariant form of the Killing condition, also written xi_(a;b) = 0.

### (33.49) · derivation-step · GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365)

$$
(\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol\xi}g)_{\alpha\beta} = \xi^\gamma\partial_\gamma g_{\alpha\beta} + g_{\gamma\beta}\partial_\alpha\xi^\gamma + g_{\alpha\gamma}\partial_\beta\xi^\gamma = 0
$$

Coordinate statement of the Killing condition, from (33.46).

### (33.50) · derivation-step · GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365)

$$
\xi^\gamma g_{\alpha\beta;\gamma} + g_{\gamma\beta}\xi^\gamma{}_{;\alpha} + g_{\alpha\gamma}\xi^\gamma{}_{;\beta} = 0
$$

Partials replaced by covariant derivatives. This is valid because the Christoffel terms cancel for a symmetric connection.

### (33.51) · derivation-step · GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365)

$$
\xi_{\alpha;\beta} + \xi_{\beta;\alpha} = 0
$$

End of Example 33.9. Once g_{ab;c} = 0 removes the transport term, the metric lowers the index inside each covariant derivative.

### (33.52) Killing vectors by inspection · central · GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366)

$$
(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{e}_1}g)_{\alpha\beta} = \frac{\partial g_{\alpha\beta}}{\partial x^1} = 0
$$

A metric with no x^1 dependence has e_1 as a Killing vector.

### (33.53) · derivation-step · GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366)

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\boldsymbol\xi) = (\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{u})\cdot\boldsymbol\xi + \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}\boldsymbol\xi
$$

Leibniz rule for the rate of change of u.xi along the curve.

### (33.54) · derivation-step · GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366)

$$
\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}\boldsymbol\xi = u^\alpha u^\beta\xi_{\alpha;\beta}
$$

Surviving term after using the geodesic equation.

### (33.55) Killing conservation law · central · GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366)

$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u}\cdot\boldsymbol\xi) = u^\alpha u^\beta\xi_{(\alpha;\beta)} = 0
$$

Symmetric contraction plus Killing's equation: u.xi is constant along any geodesic.

### (33.56) Schwarzschild line element · supporting · GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366)

$$
\mathrm{d}s^2 = -\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)\mathrm{d}t^2 + \left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}\mathrm{d}r^2 + r^2(\mathrm{d}\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi^2)
$$

Metric used to read off Killing vectors. Corrected here: the printed dt^2 coefficient lacks the minus sign required by the book's (-+++) signature.

**Symbols:** M mass in G = c = 1 units

### (33.57) · supporting · GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366)

$$
\mathbf{e}_t = \frac{\partial}{\partial t},\qquad \mathbf{e}_\phi = \frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}
$$

Killing vectors of Schwarzschild: time translation and axial rotation.

### (33.58) · supporting · GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366)

$$
\mathbf{e}_t\cdot\mathbf{u} = u_t,\qquad \mathbf{e}_\phi\cdot\mathbf{u} = u_\phi
$$

Conserved covariant components along Schwarzschild geodesics (energy and angular momentum per unit mass up to sign).

### (33.59) Rindler conserved quantity · supporting · GA §33.5 p.350 (pdf 367)

$$
\mathbf{u}\cdot\boldsymbol\xi = g_{\mu\nu}u^\mu\delta^\nu{}_t = g_{tt}u^t = -x^2u^t
$$

For ds^2 = -x^2 dt^2 + dx^2, e_t is Killing and x^2 dt/dtau is conserved on geodesics.

### (33.61) Closure gap (Exercise 33.1) · supporting · GA p.350 (pdf 367)

$$
\mathcal{P}_4-\mathcal{P}_3 = [\mathbf{u}(\mathcal{P}_0)+\mathbf{v}(\mathcal{P}_1)] - [\mathbf{u}(\mathcal{P}_2)+\mathbf{v}(\mathcal{P}_0)]
$$

Difference of the two routes around the quadrilateral; to leading order it equals [u,v] at P_0.

**Symbols:** P_1 = tip of u from P_0, P_2 = tip of v from P_0

### (33.62) Commutator of Lie derivatives · supporting · GA p.350 (pdf 367)

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}} - \mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{u}} = \mathcal{L}_{[\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}]}
$$

Lie derivatives represent the bracket algebra (Exercise 33.3).

## Worked examples

### Example 33.1 · intro · GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

**Problem:** Compute exterior derivatives of four 1-forms in coordinates (t, chi, theta, phi): dt, a(t) dchi, a(t) sin theta dtheta and a(t) sin chi sin theta dphi.

**Method:** Apply the p = 1 formula dA = partial_mu A_alpha dx^mu wedge dx^alpha to each: differentiate the coefficient with respect to every coordinate, wedge the differential in front, and delete any term in which a differential is wedged with itself.

**Key insight:** Only coefficient dependence on coordinates other than the form's own direction survives; dd = 0 handles pure differentials. The forms are the building blocks of the expanding-Universe metric, so the drill pays off later.

**Result:** d(dt) = 0; d(a dchi) = adot dt^dchi; d(a sin theta dtheta) = adot sin theta dt^dtheta; d(a sin chi sin theta dphi) = adot sin chi sin theta dt^dphi + a cos chi sin theta dchi^dphi + a sin chi cos theta dtheta^dphi (the printed first term omits sin chi).

**Concepts:** exterior derivative, wedge product, nilpotency of the exterior derivative

### Example 33.2 · intro · GA §33.1 p.342 (pdf 359)

**Problem:** Explore what repeated exterior differentiation can build in 3+1 dimensions starting from a function, a 1-form and a 2-form.

**Method:** Form df, dA and d nu, describe each as planes, tubes or cells, and apply d a second time to each using dd = 0; note where degree exceeds the dimension.

**Key insight:** Anything that is already an exterior derivative has vanishing exterior derivative, and form degree cannot exceed the number of dimensions, so the ladder stops at 4-forms.

**Result:** d(df) = 0, d(dA) = 0, d(d nu) = 0; 4-forms are the top degree in spacetime.

**Concepts:** nilpotency of the exterior derivative, geometric picture of forms, vanishing of forms of degree above the dimension

### Example 33.3 · intro · GA §33.2 p.343 (pdf 360)

**Problem:** Evaluate the commutator of two vector fields acting on a test function in coordinates.

**Method:** Expand u^a partial_a(v^b partial_b f) - v^a partial_a(u^b partial_b f) with the product rule; the terms with second derivatives of f cancel because mixed partials commute.

**Key insight:** What remains is a first-order derivative operator, so the commutator of two vector fields is again a vector field, with generally non-zero components.

**Result:** [u,v]^b = u^a partial_a v^b - v^a partial_a u^b.

**Concepts:** Lie bracket of vector fields, vector as directional derivative operator

### Example 33.4 · intro · GA §33.3 p.345 (pdf 362)

**Problem:** Make precise the notion of a congruence and show that a smooth vector field always has integral curves.

**Method:** Define a congruence by a unique curve through each point with tangent equal to u, write the tangency condition as the ODE system dx^mu/dlambda = u^mu(x), and invoke the standard local existence and uniqueness theorem.

**Key insight:** Streamlines are guaranteed to exist locally for any smooth field, so Lie dragging is always well defined near a point.

**Result:** Integral curves solve dx^mu/dlambda = u^mu(x(lambda)) and exist uniquely near P.

**Concepts:** integral curve, congruence of curves

### Example 33.5 · standard · GA §33.3 p.345 (pdf 362)

**Problem:** Derive the component form of the Lie derivative of a vector field v along u from its limit definition.

**Method:** Treat the step P -> Q as the map x^{mu'} = x^mu + u^mu dlambda. Step I: push v(P) forward with the Jacobian delta + (partial u) dlambda. Step II: Taylor expand v from P to Q. Step III: subtract and divide by dlambda.

**Key insight:** The dragged vector differs from v(P) by (v . partial) u because the flow moves the head and tail of v at different speeds; subtracting this from the field's own change (u . partial) v gives exactly the commutator.

**Result:** (L_u v)^b = u^a partial_a v^b - v^a partial_a u^b, i.e. L_u v = [u,v].

**Concepts:** Lie derivative of a vector field, Lie bracket of vector fields, tensor transformation law, Lie dragging

### Example 33.6 · intro · GA §33.3 p.346 (pdf 363)

**Problem:** Find the Lie derivative of a vector field W along a coordinate basis vector e_1.

**Method:** Insert u^nu = delta^nu_1 into the vector formula; the derivative of the constant delta vanishes.

**Key insight:** Along a coordinate basis vector the Lie derivative reduces to an ordinary partial derivative, so it is a coordinate-free generalisation of partial differentiation.

**Result:** (L_{e_1} W)^mu = partial W^mu/partial x^1 (the printed intermediate step mis-indexes the Kronecker delta).

**Concepts:** Lie derivative along a coordinate basis vector, coordinate basis

### Example 33.7 · standard · GA §33.4 p.347 (pdf 364)

**Problem:** Obtain the components of the Lie derivative of a 1-form sigma along v.

**Method:** Apply the Leibniz rule to the scalar sigma_mu w^mu, replace L_v of the scalar by v^nu partial_nu and L_v w by the vector formula, expand, relabel the dummy indices in the derivative-of-v term, and use the arbitrariness of w to strip it off.

**Key insight:** The Lie derivative on 1-forms is fixed by demanding the product rule and consistency with scalars and vectors; the flow-gradient correction enters with a plus sign and index on the derivative.

**Result:** (L_v sigma)_mu = v^nu partial_nu sigma_mu + sigma_nu partial_mu v^nu.

**Concepts:** Lie derivative of a 1-form, Leibniz rule for the Lie derivative

### Example 33.8 · standard · GA §33.4 p.347 (pdf 364)

**Problem:** Specialise the general tensor formula to the metric.

**Method:** Apply eqn 33.45 to the (0,2) tensor g, giving one transport term and two plus-signed lower-index corrections.

**Key insight:** This single formula is all that is needed for Killing vectors; readers may skip the general machinery.

**Result:** (L_v g)_ab = v^c partial_c g_ab + g_cb partial_a v^c + g_ac partial_b v^c.

**Concepts:** Lie derivative of the metric, Lie derivative of a general tensor

### Example 33.9 · standard · GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365)

**Problem:** Prove that L_xi g = 0 implies Killing's equation xi_(a;b) = 0.

**Method:** Write the coordinate form of L_xi g = 0, replace partial derivatives by covariant ones, use g_{ab;c} = 0 to drop the first term and to lower the index inside the covariant derivative in the other two.

**Key insight:** The coordinate-dependent Lie-derivative condition becomes a manifestly tensorial equation once a metric-compatible, torsion-free connection is available. The partial-to-covariant swap is exact here because the Christoffel terms cancel.

**Result:** xi_{a;b} + xi_{b;a} = 0.

**Concepts:** Killing's equation, metric compatibility, comma-to-semicolon rule, Lie derivative of the metric

### Example · intro · GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366)

**Problem:** Show how to recognise Killing vectors directly from the form of a metric.

**Method:** Take xi = e_1 in the Killing condition and use Example 33.6, so L_{e_1} g has components partial_1 g_ab.

**Key insight:** If a coordinate appears nowhere in the metric components, its coordinate basis vector is a Killing vector: the geometric counterpart of a cyclic coordinate in Lagrangian mechanics.

**Result:** partial g_ab/partial x^1 = 0 for all a, b implies e_1 is a Killing vector.

**Concepts:** Killing vectors from metric independence of a coordinate, Lie derivative along a coordinate basis vector

### Example 33.10 · standard · GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366)

**Problem:** Prove that u . xi is constant along a geodesic with tangent u whenever xi is a Killing vector.

**Method:** Differentiate u . xi covariantly along u with the Leibniz rule. The geodesic equation removes the (nabla_u u) . xi term. The remaining u^a u^b xi_{a;b} is contracted with a symmetric pair, so only xi_(a;b) contributes, and Killing's equation sets it to zero.

**Key insight:** Symmetric contraction filters out the antisymmetric part of xi_{a;b}, which is exactly what Killing's equation leaves unconstrained.

**Result:** nabla_u(u . xi) = 0 along geodesics.

**Concepts:** conserved quantity along a geodesic from a Killing vector, Killing's equation, geodesic

### Example 33.11 · intro · GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366)

**Problem:** Identify Killing vectors and conserved quantities for Schwarzschild geodesics.

**Method:** Observe that no metric component depends on t or phi, take e_t and e_phi as Killing vectors, and dot each with the geodesic tangent.

**Key insight:** The energy-like and angular-momentum-like constants used for orbits in Chapter 22 are instances of the general Killing theorem.

**Result:** Killing vectors e_t, e_phi; conserved u_t and u_phi. (The printed line element lacks the minus sign on the dt^2 term.)

**Concepts:** Schwarzschild conserved energy and angular momentum, Killing vectors from metric independence of a coordinate

### Example 33.12 · intro · GA §33.5 p.350 (pdf 367)

**Problem:** Find the Killing vector read off from the Rindler metric ds^2 = -x^2 dt^2 + dx^2 and its conserved quantity.

**Method:** Note that t is absent from the metric, take xi = e_t, and evaluate g_{mu nu} u^mu xi^nu with xi^nu = delta^nu_t.

**Key insight:** Conserved quantities come out as covariant components; in Rindler coordinates the conserved -x^2 u^t is the boost charge of a free particle in flat spacetime.

**Result:** u . xi = g_tt u^t = -x^2 u^t is constant along geodesics.

**Concepts:** Rindler spacetime, Killing vectors from metric independence of a coordinate, conserved quantity along a geodesic from a Killing vector


## Analogies and intuitions

### Drifting down a river while holding an arrow sampled from a second field, then comparing it with the arrow found at the new place → Lie derivative · strong · GA §33.3 p.344 (pdf 361)

Motivates why a second vector field is needed: the current u supplies the rule for moving from point to point, and the Lie derivative is the mismatch between what was carried and what is found.

**Where it breaks down:** A physical boat carries a rigid arrow; a Lie-dragged vector is stretched and rotated by velocity gradients of the current (its head and tail drift separately). Learners also tend to think the answer depends only on u at the point.

**App idea:** River-drift sandbox where a carried arrow is drawn as a stretchy segment whose ends ride separate tracer particles, versus a rigid arrow, both compared with the local field.

### Trying to walk a parallelogram using two direction fields and finding the corners do not meet → Lie bracket of vector fields · strong · GA §33.2 p.343 (pdf 360)

The commutator is read as the missing side needed to close a quadrilateral built by following u then v versus v then u, and reused for the Lie derivative.

**Where it breaks down:** The picture is valid only to second order in step size; for finite steps higher-order terms appear. It can also be confused with curvature holonomy, which involves parallel transport rather than following fields.

**App idea:** Commutator-closure explorer with a shrinking step and a live gap/epsilon^2 readout.

### A thread stretched between two corks floating on neighbouring streamlines → Lie dragging · useful · GA §33.3 p.345 (pdf 362)

Fig. 33.4 shows that a dragged vector keeps joining the same pair of flow lines, a property later used for geodesic separation vectors.

**Where it breaks down:** The cork picture is our paraphrase of the book's figure; it holds only for infinitesimally close streamlines, and real threads have length constraints that a dragged vector does not.

**App idea:** Rubber-band connector animation between two beads riding a flow, with a toggle to a frozen-components arrow that drifts off.

### Forms as stacked sheets, tubes and cells; d as adding a new family of sheets → exterior derivative · useful · GA §33.1 p.340 (pdf 357)

Fig. 33.1 and Example 33.2 visualise d as converting a stack of planes into a lattice of tubes and tubes into cells, and explain why the ladder stops in four dimensions.

**Where it breaks down:** The picture struggles with orientation and sign, with non-integer densities, and with degrees above 3, which cannot be drawn; the 'circulating field' language is heuristic.

**App idea:** 3D form-stack builder that generates dx sheets from a varying coefficient and highlights resulting tubes.

### The Lie derivative as a partial derivative that has forgotten its coordinates → Lie derivative along a coordinate basis vector · useful · GA §33.3 p.346 (pdf 363)

Example 33.6 shows L along e_1 is partial/partial x^1, suggesting the Lie derivative is what partial differentiation looks like when the direction is supplied by a field rather than a coordinate grid.

**Where it breaks down:** Only true when u is a coordinate basis vector; generally extra gradient-of-u terms appear, and two Lie derivatives need not commute.

**App idea:** Toggle a flow between a straight coordinate field and a curved one, showing the extra term in L_u v switch on.

### Sliding a rigid pattern of points along a flow without changing any distances → Killing vector field · strong · GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365)

An isometry is pictured as moving points along the streamlines of xi with all separations preserved; the generator of such a motion is a Killing vector.

**Where it breaks down:** In spacetime, 'distance' includes timelike intervals and the flow can be a boost, which does not look like rigid motion in a Euclidean picture.

**App idea:** Sphere and plane with a sprinkle of points linked by measured edges; the learner picks a candidate field and watches edge lengths stay fixed (Killing) or change (not Killing).

### Symmetry implies conservation (Noether) and cyclic coordinates in Lagrangian mechanics → Killing vectors from metric independence of a coordinate · useful · GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365)

The book cites Noether's theorem as the mechanical parallel. Our addition is the cyclic-coordinate link: a coordinate absent from the Lagrangian gives a conserved momentum, just as a coordinate absent from the metric gives the conserved u_k.

**Where it breaks down:** Hidden symmetries not manifest in the coordinates are missed by inspection, and Killing tensors (e.g. the Carter constant in Kerr) give conserved quantities beyond Killing vectors.

**App idea:** Side-by-side panel: a Lagrangian with a highlighted missing coordinate and conserved momentum, next to a metric with a highlighted missing coordinate and conserved u_k along plotted geodesics.

### Everyone in the Universe being carried along by the cosmic fluid → comoving observers and cosmological fluid · weak · GA §33.3 p.344 (pdf 361)

A margin note justifies the effort spent on Lie derivatives by noting that cosmological observers ride the matter flow, so changes they measure are Lie derivatives along its velocity.

**Where it breaks down:** Only suggestive here; the actual cosmological use appears in Chapter 39 exercises.


## Misconceptions addressed

### You can differentiate a vector field by subtracting its components at two nearby points and dividing by the separation. · GA p.340 (pdf 357)

**Correction:** Components at different points refer to different bases, so the difference is not a tensor. Some rule for moving one vector to the other point is needed: a connection (parallel transport) or an auxiliary flow (Lie dragging).

**Why tempting:** In Cartesian coordinates in flat space this naive difference works, and learners carry the habit over.

### Taking any derivative of a tensor in a curved space requires a metric or a connection. · GA §33.3 p.346 (pdf 363)

**Correction:** The exterior derivative of forms and the Lie derivative of any tensor are defined without either; the covariant form of L_u v is optional.

**Why tempting:** Earlier chapters introduced the covariant derivative as the derivative of GR, with Christoffel symbols built from the metric.

### The Lie derivative L_u v is just the directional derivative of v's components along u. · GA §33.3 p.345 (pdf 362)

**Correction:** That is only the first term. Dragging also deforms the vector through the gradient of u, giving the extra -v . partial u; the two terms together form the bracket. They coincide only when u has constant components, e.g. a coordinate basis vector.

**Why tempting:** For scalars the Lie derivative is exactly the directional derivative, and Example 33.6 shows the same thing along a basis vector.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Since the Lie derivative exists without extra structure, it can replace the covariant derivative for describing curvature. · GA §33.3 p.346 (pdf 363)

**Correction:** The Lie derivative at a point depends on the dragging field's behaviour nearby, not just on a direction at the point, so it does not define a unique rate of change in a given direction; a connection is still needed.

**Why tempting:** It is coordinate-free, tensorial in its output and simpler to define.

### Partial derivatives commute, so the commutator of two vector fields must vanish. · GA §33.2 p.343 (pdf 360)

**Correction:** The second-derivative parts do cancel, but derivatives acting on the field components do not, leaving the non-zero vector u.partial v - v.partial u.

**Why tempting:** Mixed partial derivatives of functions are symmetric, and coordinate basis vectors do commute.

### Any smooth set of basis vectors, such as the orthonormal polar unit vectors, can be generated by some coordinate system. · GA §33.2 p.344 (pdf 361)

**Correction:** A basis is a coordinate basis only if all brackets vanish; the orthonormal frame e_r, (1/r) e_theta has a non-zero bracket, so no coordinates produce it.

**Why tempting:** Coordinate bases are used almost everywhere and unit vectors look like natural coordinate directions.

### When computing d of a(t) sin theta dtheta, the derivative of sin theta contributes a term. · GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358)

**Correction:** Differentiating with respect to theta produces dtheta wedge dtheta, which vanishes by antisymmetry; only derivatives along other coordinates survive.

**Why tempting:** Ordinary product-rule instincts suggest every factor gets differentiated.

### Applying d repeatedly keeps producing higher and higher forms. · GA §33.1 p.342 (pdf 359)

**Correction:** d of an exact form is zero, and in n dimensions forms of degree above n are identically zero; spacetime stops at 4-forms.

**Why tempting:** The operator is described as always raising degree by one.

### u . xi is conserved along any world line if xi is a Killing vector. · GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366)

**Correction:** The proof uses nabla_u u = 0, so conservation holds on geodesics; forces or rockets change u . xi.

**Why tempting:** Conservation laws are often stated without their conditions, and Killing's equation alone looks sufficient.

### All Killing vectors of a metric can be found by looking for coordinates that are missing from it. · GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366)

**Correction:** Inspection finds only symmetries aligned with coordinate directions. The round sphere has three rotational Killing vectors but only partial_phi is manifest in (theta, phi); the others require solving Killing's equation.

**Why tempting:** The book presents inspection as the most straightforward method and all its examples use it.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The metric tensor is a 2-form. · GA §33.4 p.347 (pdf 364)

**Correction:** Forms are antisymmetric; the metric is a symmetric (0,2) tensor, so exterior-calculus rules do not apply to it directly.

**Why tempting:** Example 33.8 calls it the metric 2-form, and both are rank-2 covariant objects written with basis differentials.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Metric compatibility g_{ab;c} = 0 holds for any affine connection. · GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365)

**Correction:** It holds only for connections chosen to be metric-compatible, such as the Levi-Civita connection of GR; generic affine connections do not preserve the metric.

**Why tempting:** In GR the Levi-Civita connection is the only one used, and the text states the property without qualification.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Floating arrow in a river · GA §33.3 p.344 (pdf 361)

**Setup:** An observer drifting with a current u picks up a copy of the local arrow of another field v, drifts for a small parameter interval, and holds the carried arrow beside the arrow of v found at the new location.

**Lesson:** The rate at which these two arrows differ is the Lie derivative. It requires no notion of parallelism, only the flow, and it vanishes when v is carried consistently by the current.

**App idea:** River-drift sandbox with drop-a-boat interaction and a live difference arrow.

### Two routes around a would-be parallelogram · GA §33.2 p.343 (pdf 360)

**Setup:** From one point, walk a short distance along u and then along the v found there. Separately walk along v and then along the u found there. Compare the two endpoints.

**Lesson:** The endpoints differ by the commutator (times step squared); zero gap for all small steps means the fields can serve as coordinate directions.

**App idea:** Commutator-closure explorer with presets for Cartesian, polar coordinate and polar orthonormal fields.

### Sliding a geometry along itself · GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365)

**Setup:** Mark a cloud of points in a space, record all their mutual distances, then move every point the same parameter distance along the streamlines of a candidate field xi and re-measure.

**Lesson:** If no distance changes for any cloud, xi generates an isometry and is a Killing vector; its dot product with geodesic tangents is then conserved.

**App idea:** Isometry tester on a sphere, plane, cylinder and Schwarzschild spatial slice, with edge lengths coloured by fractional change.


## Applications and observations

- **Constants of motion for orbits around a spherical star or black hole** (astrophysical-system, GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366)): Time-translation and axial-rotation Killing vectors of Schwarzschild give conserved u_t and u_phi, the energy and angular momentum per unit mass used to reduce geodesic motion to an effective-potential problem (Chapter 22). Key numbers: Killing vectors e_t, e_phi; conserved u_t, u_phi (G = c = 1).
- **Binding energy of circular Schwarzschild orbits from the Killing energy (our own estimate)** (numerical-estimate, GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366)): Our estimate, not in the book: for circular geodesics, E = -u_t = (1 - 2M/r)/sqrt(1 - 3M/r) and L = u_phi = sqrt(M r)/sqrt(1 - 3M/r). The fraction of rest mass released by spiralling in to a given orbit is 1 - E, which sets the radiative efficiency of thin accretion discs. Key numbers: Our estimate: r = 10M gives E about 0.956 (4.4% binding); innermost stable circular orbit r = 6M gives E = sqrt(8/9) about 0.943 (5.7% efficiency) and L = 2 sqrt(3) M about 3.46M.
- **Conserved quantity for free particles seen by uniformly accelerated observers** (numerical-estimate, GA §33.5 p.350 (pdf 367)): In Rindler coordinates ds^2 = -x^2 dt^2 + dx^2 the metric lacks t, so -x^2 dt/dtau is conserved along geodesics. Our gloss: e_t is the Minkowski boost generator, so this is the boost charge of an inertial particle, which fixes how its Rindler-time rate varies with position. Key numbers: u . xi = -x^2 u^t = constant.
- **Comoving observers in cosmology and the separation of freely falling particles** (astrophysical-system, GA §33.4 p.348 (pdf 365)): The Lie derivative along the cosmic fluid velocity describes changes measured by comoving observers. The separation vector between neighbouring geodesics is Lie dragged by their tangent field, the starting point for the geodesic-deviation (tidal) equation in Chapter 35.

## Historical notes

- **Marius Sophus Lie (1842-1899):** Developed the theory of continuous transformation groups, now called Lie groups, which are central in quantum mechanics; the Lie bracket and Lie derivative carry his name. The note also says Elie Cartan was his doctoral student and gives the pronunciation 'Lee'. — Places the Lie derivative within the mathematics of continuous symmetries, which Killing vectors later realise geometrically. (GA §33.1 p.342 (pdf 359))
- **Wilhelm Killing, Sophus Lie (1847-1923):** Killing invented Lie algebras independently of Lie, who dismissed his work. The modest Killing aimed at more generality and left conjectures that were later confirmed. — Humanises the naming of Killing vectors and hints that Killing fields form a Lie algebra (Exercise 33.4). (GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365))
- **Emmy Noether:** Noether's theorem linking symmetries to conserved quantities is invoked (by name of the theorem only) as the mechanics counterpart of Killing-vector conservation. — Connects the geometric conservation law to the familiar variational one, treated in Chapter 40. (GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365))

## Notation and conventions

- **Forms and the exterior derivative:** Forms are bold with a tilde (A-tilde, sigma-tilde); the operator d and basis 1-forms dx^mu are bold; a p-form is expanded with a 1/p! prefactor and unrestricted sums. — The ordered-index alternative uses vertical bars |ab...s| and drops the 1/p! (margin note 1). (GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358))
- **Vectors as operators:** Vector fields are written u = d/dlambda and act on functions as u[f]; the contraction of a 1-form and vector is written with angle brackets <sigma, w>. (GA §33.2 p.342 (pdf 359))
- **Commutator / Lie bracket:** [u,v] = uv - vu, with components u^a partial_a v^b - v^a partial_a u^b. — Sign order matters: L_u v = [u,v] = -L_v u. (GA §33.2 p.342 (pdf 359))
- **Lie derivative symbol:** A script L with the dragging field as subscript, L_u Z; the book likens the glyph to the pound sign. (GA §33.3 p.344 (pdf 361))
- **Derivative index notation:** Comma denotes partial derivative (v^b_{,a}), semicolon denotes covariant derivative (v^b_{;a}); parentheses on indices denote symmetrisation, xi_(a;b). — Symmetrisation convention (with or without 1/2) is not spelled out; it does not matter for the vanishing statement. (GA §33.3 p.346 (pdf 363))
- **Primed indices for the dragged vector:** In Example 33.5, x^{mu'} labels coordinates of the point Q reached along u and v^{beta'} the dragged components; the flow step is written as a coordinate transformation. — Easily confused with a passive change of coordinates at a single point. (GA §33.3 p.345 (pdf 362))
- **Metric signature and units:** Signature (-,+,+,+) as in the Rindler metric -x^2 dt^2 + dx^2; geometrised units G = c = 1 in the Schwarzschild metric. — The printed Schwarzschild dt^2 term is missing its minus sign. (GA §33.5 p.349 (pdf 366))
- **Basis vectors and dual basis:** Coordinate basis vectors e_mu = partial/partial x^mu; basis 1-forms omega^mu with <omega^mu, e_nu> = delta^mu_nu. — Dual basis notation appears in Exercise 33.2. (GA p.350 (pdf 367))
- **Killing vector symbol:** Killing vectors are denoted by bold xi; geodesic tangents by u; the conserved quantity is written u . xi. (GA §33.5 p.348 (pdf 365))
- **Coordinates in Example 33.1:** (t, chi, theta, phi) are hyperspherical coordinates of a closed Robertson-Walker space with scale factor a(t). (GA §33.1 p.341 (pdf 358))

## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Printed Schwarzschild line element (33.56) has +(1 - 2M/r) dt^2, missing the minus sign required by the book's signature (the Rindler example uses -x^2 dt^2).** — Learners may think the sign is irrelevant to Killing analysis, or get the wrong sign for the conserved energy u_t. *Suggestion:* Show the corrected metric and point out that inspection-based Killing vectors do not depend on signs, but the physical energy E = -u_t does.
- **Eqn 33.15 omits the factor sin chi in the dt wedge dphi term; eqn 33.38 writes the Kronecker delta as delta^mu_nu instead of delta^mu_1.** — Careful readers checking the algebra will think they have made an error. *Suggestion:* Flag both slips and ask the learner to find them as a check of understanding.
- **Example 33.9 says g_{ab;c} = 0 for any affine connection and swaps commas for semicolons without justification. Similarly, the covariant rewrite (33.33) silently needs a symmetric connection for the Christoffel terms to cancel.** — Learners may believe all connections are metric-compatible and that comma-to-semicolon is always legal, which fails for torsionful connections and in general equations containing second derivatives or curvature. *Suggestion:* Show explicitly that the Gamma terms cancel in L_xi g when Gamma is symmetric, and state that metric compatibility plus zero torsion single out the Levi-Civita connection (Chapter 34).
- **The explanation of why the Lie derivative cannot serve as the curvature derivative is imprecise. It contrasts with partial derivatives, which also use neighbouring values, rather than stating that L_u v depends on derivatives of u, not just u at the point.** — The key conceptual distinction between Lie and covariant derivatives stays fuzzy. *Suggestion:* Demonstrate with two fields u and f u, equal at P when f(P) = 1. Their Lie derivatives of v differ by -v[f] u, whereas nabla_{f u} v = f nabla_u v; hence the covariant derivative is tensorial in its direction slot and the Lie derivative is not.
- **Step I of Example 33.5 describes the drag as transforming components into the coordinate frame at Q, blending an active push-forward with a passive coordinate change.** — Novices may not see why a coordinate transformation moves a vector to another point, or which sign convention is in use. *Suggestion:* Present the push-forward directly: advect the tail and head of an infinitesimal v along u and read off the new arrow; then note that the Jacobian of the flow map is the same matrix.
- **Example 33.2 presents dd = 0 as constraining which objects fit in 3+1 dimensions, mixing it with the separate fact that forms of degree above the dimension vanish. Closed versus exact forms and the Poincare lemma are not named.** — Learners may attribute the 4-form ceiling to dd = 0 and miss the physically vital idea that a closed form is locally exact (e.g. dF = 0 allows F = dA). *Suggestion:* Separate the two facts, name closed and exact forms, and connect to grad-curl-div identities and to Maxwell's homogeneous equations.
- **Example 33.8 calls the metric a '2-form'.** — Confuses the symmetric metric with antisymmetric forms, inviting misuse of wedge-product rules. *Suggestion:* Call g a symmetric (0,2) tensor and contrast it with 2-forms explicitly.
- **The paragraph under eqn 33.25 describes the bracket as the comparison of the routes v-then-u and u-then-v without saying which endpoint is subtracted from which. Its caption also names the route v-then-u 'vu', while as operators uv[f] applies v first.** — Learners can easily get the sign or direction of the closing arrow backwards. *Suggestion:* Write the convention as an equation, [u,v] = end(u then v) - end(v then u) to second order, and let the learner check it on u = partial_x, v = x partial_y, which gives [u,v] = partial_y.
- **Killing vectors are found only by inspection; Killing's equation is never solved, hidden symmetries are not mentioned, and no example shows a non-coordinate Killing vector.** — Learners may underestimate the symmetry content of metrics (e.g. three rotations of the sphere, boosts of Minkowski) and misapply the inspection rule as complete. *Suggestion:* Add a short exercise solving Killing's equation on the 2-sphere or the Euclidean plane, and connect with Exercise 33.4 on the bracket closure of rotations.
- **Example 33.1's 1-form a(t) sin theta dtheta does not match the closed Robertson-Walker basis form a(t) sin chi dtheta, though the text says all the chosen forms reappear in the expanding-Universe metric.** — Minor confusion when learners later compare with the RW orthonormal 1-forms. *Suggestion:* Use the RW forms a dchi, a sin chi dtheta and a sin chi sin theta dphi consistently when drilling.
- **Example 33.12 writes the contraction as g_{mu nu} u^mu delta^t_nu, with the delta's index placement mismatched, and gives no physical interpretation of the conserved quantity.** — Index-placement sloppiness undermines the careful up/down conventions of Part V; the Rindler constant feels unmotivated. *Suggestion:* Write xi^nu = delta^nu_t and interpret -x^2 u^t as the boost charge of an inertial particle.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'You have a vector here and another one a metre away. How would you decide whether the field changed?' Let the learner find that some rule for moving vectors is needed, then offer parallel transport (later) and being carried by a flow (today).
- Check prerequisites quickly: can the learner compute a wedge product of 1-forms, explain dx wedge dx = 0, and act with a vector on a function as u^a partial_a f? If not, detour to Chapters 31-32 first.
- Suggested order: d on functions -> d on 1-forms with a picture -> component formula -> dd = 0 ladder -> commutator in components -> parallelogram picture -> coordinate-basis test -> river and Lie dragging -> three-step derivation of L_u v -> Leibniz extension to 1-forms and metric -> Killing condition -> inspection rule -> conservation proof -> Schwarzschild and Rindler.
- For the exterior derivative, have the learner do d(x dy) and d(y dx) by hand in the plane and compare signs; then connect d(A_x dx + A_y dy) to the curl's z-component so the formula is anchored in familiar vector calculus.
- Check for understanding of the bracket with u = partial_x and v = x partial_y (answer partial_y), then with polar orthonormal vectors e_r and (1/r) partial_theta (non-zero bracket), and ask what each result says about coordinates.
- When deriving L_u v, insist the learner explain in words why the dragged vector picks up (v . partial) u: its tail and head ride currents of slightly different velocity. This is the most common sticking point.
- Common learner question: 'Isn't the Lie derivative just the covariant derivative without Christoffel symbols?' Answer via the u versus f u test: the Lie derivative changes when the flow is rescaled away from P, the covariant derivative does not.
- Common learner question: 'Why does the metric not depending on t mean energy is conserved?' Walk through L_{e_t} g = partial_t g, then the three-line proof, then relate -u_t to E in the Newtonian limit.
- Point out the three printed slips (sin chi in 33.15, delta index in 33.38, sign of dt^2 in 33.56) before the learner trips over them; reframe them as spot-the-error checks for strong learners.
- For advanced learners, preview Exercise 33.3-33.4 as the statement that Killing fields form a Lie algebra, and show the rotation algebra of the sphere as an example of symmetries not visible by inspection.
- Link forward explicitly: the Lie-dragged separation vector becomes the geodesic deviation equation in Chapter 35, and d of basis 1-forms is the engine of Cartan's curvature method in Chapter 36.
- For beginners, the Section 33.4 general formula can be deferred: the book itself allows skipping it if eqn 33.46 for L_v g is accepted.
