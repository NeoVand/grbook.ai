# GA ch09 · Geodesic equations and connection coefficients

> Packages the variational geodesic calculation of Chapter 8 into a five-step recipe that turns a line element into equations of motion and then into connection coefficients, drills it on the unit sphere, a paraboloid and the Poincare half plane, and finally runs the same recipe on the general free-particle action to derive the Christoffel formula, which expresses the connection as half of a signed sum of three first derivatives of the metric.

**Pages:** printed 101–107 · pdf 118–124 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 2/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

Conceptually light (one idea: metric determines connection) and explicitly optional, but algebraically dense. Section 9.1 is repetitive calculus that most readers can follow; Example 9.4 requires fluent dummy-index relabelling and silently skips the index-raising step. Several printed slips can derail careful readers: the missing square in 9.9, the wrong derivative variable in 9.23, the wrong index in 9.28, u for a in 9.36, and a citation of Exercise 8.1 that should point to Example 8.6. The 1/L notation in 9.21-9.22 is inconsistent with L containing -m.

## Role in the book

A short, explicitly skippable computational interlude in Part II. Chapter 7 defined connection coefficients through the changing basis vectors and only promised (in a footnote, eqn 7.37) that they come from metric derivatives; Chapter 8 found geodesics by extremizing the interval. This chapter closes the loop metric -> line element -> geodesic equation -> Gamma, so that the reader has a reliable way to compute the connection before Chapter 11 builds the Riemann tensor out of it. Its exercises plant material used much later: the general static spherically symmetric connection (Exercise 9.7) feeds Exercise 11.1 and the Schwarzschild chapters, Rindler spacetime returns in Chapter 26, and the Poincare half plane previews the hyperbolic geometry of Chapters 16, 19 and 36.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can write the parametrized interval functional for any line element, choosing the sign under the square root so that it gives proper length for spacelike curves or proper time for timelike ones.
- Reader can run Steps I-V (Lagrangian, partial derivatives, set L = 1, Euler-Lagrange, read off) to obtain geodesic equations and connection coefficients for a diagonal 2D or 4D metric.
- Reader can read connection coefficients off an equation of motion, halving the coefficient of every mixed velocity product because Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} and Gamma^mu_{beta alpha} both contribute.
- Reader can explain why the normalization L = 1 is imposed only after the first partial derivatives of L have been taken.
- Reader can check a set of computed coefficients with a limiting case, e.g. recovering flat plane-polar coefficients from the paraboloid when a -> 0.
- Reader can derive the all-lower-index Christoffel formula (9.29) from the free-particle action using the product rule and a symmetrizing relabelling of dummy indices, and raise the first index with the inverse metric.
- Reader can apply eqn 9.29 directly to a Lorentzian, coordinate-dependent metric such as ds^2 = (dx^2 - dt^2)/t^2, including the raising step with g^{tt} and g^{xx}.
- Reader can use the diagonal-metric shortcuts (9.32), the identity writing each metric derivative as a sum of two lowered coefficients (9.30), and the trace identity Gamma^alpha_{beta alpha} = d_beta ln sqrt(-g).
- Reader can integrate the Poincare half-plane geodesic equations to show that geodesics are semicircles centred on the boundary and that the boundary lies at infinite distance.
- Reader can argue that replacing the square-root Lagrangian by any monotonic function of it, in particular (1/2) g_{mu nu} xdot^mu xdot^nu, leaves the geodesics unchanged.
- Reader can interpret the Rindler equation of motion in terms of the acceleration 1/x needed to stay at fixed x, which blows up as x -> 0.

## Teaching approach

A recipe chapter. The authors state one thesis (the metric, via the line element, generates the geodesics and hence the connection), draw it as a three-box arrow diagram, and then codify the Chapter 8 calculations as a boxed Steps I-V algorithm with a margin of practical tips. Each worked example is annotated with the step being executed, and the examples climb from a familiar surface (sphere) to a curved surface with a tunable parameter (paraboloid, checked by letting a -> 0) to an exotic one (Poincare half plane). The second section turns the algorithm on the general massive particle action, framing it as a derivation whose answer is already known, so that the payoff is the Christoffel formula promised in Chapter 7. Physical interpretation (Rindler acceleration, rulers in the half plane, Lagrangian as kinetic energy) is pushed into italic commentary inside the exercises, and readers in a hurry are told they may skip everything after the opening.

**Style:** computational-recipe, example-driven, derivation-heavy, conversational-informal

**Narrative arc**

1. Hook: Synge's epigraph recasts a fatal fall as abandoning a curved world line for a geodesic, so gravity is about world-line geometry, not force.
2. Recall the role of Gamma in the geodesic equation and its link to curvature; state the goal as metric -> line element -> connection (Fig. 9.1) and box the chapter's central claim.
3. Model: write the interval as an integral with an absolute value so one expression covers proper length and proper time (margin eqns 9.1, 9.2).
4. Formalism: turn the Chapter 8 procedure into Steps I-V; margin tips give dot notation, the chain rule, the factor-of-two rule for mixed terms and L = 1 as an extra constraint; an aside says the rest can be skipped on first reading.
5. Drill 1: unit sphere, reusing earlier equations of motion and reading off two coefficients.
6. Drill 2: paraboloid of revolution worked in full, with a check that a = 0 gives flat polar coefficients.
7. Drill 3: Poincare half plane worked in full; the shape of its geodesics is deferred to Exercise 9.8 and Fig. 9.2.
8. Generalize: point out the drills were all Riemannian and apply the recipe to the massive-particle action in an arbitrary spacetime (Example 9.4).
9. Derivation: product rule on the momentum term, symmetrize the metric-derivative term by swapping dummy indices, recognize the lowered-index connection, obtain eqn 9.29.
10. Check of generality: stress that 9.29 is geometric and not tied to timelike curves; margin lists shortcut identities 9.30-9.32.
11. Application: use the formula on the Lorentzian metric (dx^2 - dt^2)/t^2 and raise indices (Example 9.5).
12. Consolidate: two-line summary; eleven exercises extend to torus, non-diagonal metric, Rindler, rotating frame, Schwarzschild-type metric, half-plane geodesics, freedom of Lagrangian, moving coordinates, and a geodesic test.

**Signature moves**

- A numbered algorithm (Steps I-V) with every example line labelled by step, so the calculation becomes a checklist rather than an improvisation.
- Reading connection coefficients off Euler-Lagrange output by pattern matching against the geodesic equation, with a margin rule for the factor of two on mixed terms.
- Deferring the choice L = 1 until after the first derivatives so that all the 1/L factors collapse.
- Validating a new result by switching off a parameter (a -> 0) and landing on a known flat-space answer.
- Escalating strangeness of examples (sphere, paraboloid, hyperbolic half plane) to show the recipe does not care what the space is.
- Re-deriving a known equation from an action to extract a reusable general formula as a by-product, cashing a promise made in Chapter 7's footnote.
- Symmetrizing a contracted expression by relabelling dummy indices to expose the three-term Christoffel combination.
- Physical interpretation delivered through italic running commentary inside exercises (Rindler acceleration, ruler picture, kinetic-energy Lagrangian).
- Explicit permission to skip the chapter on first reading, marking it as tool-building rather than core physics.

## Section by section

### Chapter opening: the metric generates the connection — p.101 (pdf 118)

Synge's epigraph sets the geometric attitude: a fall is blamed on following a geodesic rather than on a gravitational force. The authors recall that connection coefficients, which describe how basis vectors change, sit in the geodesic equation and also carry information about curvature. The chapter's aim is a procedure that takes the metric as input and returns the connection, summarized as a flow diagram and a boxed statement that the line element determines the free-fall paths and thereby the coefficients.

**Concepts:** geodesic principle, connection coefficients, line element, connection coefficients from the metric

**Key moves**
- Epigraph reframes free fall as geodesic motion and support by the ground as world-line curvature.
- Links Gamma to both the geodesic equation and spacetime curvature.
- States the pipeline g => ds^2 => Gamma (Fig. 9.1) as the one idea to keep.

### §9.1 Finding connection coefficients — p.101 (pdf 118)

The interval between two events is written as a parameter integral of the absolute value of g_{mu nu} xdot^mu xdot^nu to the one-half power; margin notes separate the proper-length (spacelike) and proper-time (timelike) versions and recall that length parametrization makes u.u = -1. The Euler-Lagrange equations of this integrand, after setting L = 1, take the form of the geodesic equation, so comparing coefficients yields Gamma. The method is boxed as Steps I-V, with tips in the margin. Example 9.1 reads off the sphere coefficients from equations derived in Chapter 8; Example 9.2 works the paraboloid ds^2 = (1 + a^2 r^2) dr^2 + r^2 dtheta^2 in full and checks the a = 0 limit; Example 9.3 works the Poincare half plane ds^2 = (dr^2 + dx^2)/r^2, whose geodesics (circular arcs) are left to Exercise 9.8.

**Concepts:** proper length of a spacelike curve, proper time, Euler-Lagrange equations for the length functional, length parametrization, five-step recipe for connection coefficients, symmetry of connection coefficients in their lower indices, connection coefficients of the 2-sphere, paraboloid of revolution, Poincare half plane, hyperbolic geometry, non-zero connection coefficients do not imply curvature

**Key moves**
- Absolute value in eqn 9.3 lets one formula serve spacelike and timelike curves.
- Steps I-V: build L, take dL/dxdot and dL/dx, impose L = 1, form the Euler-Lagrange equations, read off Gamma using lower-index symmetry.
- Mixed terms: an equation containing 2F xdot^alpha xdot^beta (alpha != beta) gives Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} = F.
- Sphere: theta equation gives Gamma^theta_{phi phi} = -sin theta cos theta; phi equation gives Gamma^phi_{theta phi} = cot theta.
- Paraboloid: divide the r equation by (1 + a^2 r^2) before reading off; a = 0 returns the flat polar values 1/r and -r.
- Half plane: combine the two rdot^2 terms in the r equation before reading off; every nonzero coefficient is +-1/r.

### §9.2 The geodesic equation from the action — p.104 (pdf 121)

Having only treated positive-definite spatial metrics, the authors apply the recipe once and for all to the action S = -m integral dtau (-g_{mu nu} xdot^mu xdot^nu)^{1/2} of a massive particle in an arbitrary spacetime (Example 9.4). Differentiating, normalizing, expanding the total derivative and symmetrizing the metric-derivative term produces g_{mu nu} xddot^mu plus a three-term combination of metric derivatives contracted with two velocities. Defining Gamma_{lambda mu sigma} = g_{rho lambda} Gamma^rho_{mu sigma} gives the Christoffel formula 9.29, which the authors stress is a purely geometric relation valid beyond timelike geodesics. Margin notes add comma notation, the identity for metric derivatives, the trace identity with ln sqrt(-g), and shortcuts for diagonal metrics. Example 9.5 applies the formula to ds^2 = (dx^2 - dt^2)/t^2 and raises indices to get three coefficients all equal to -1/t.

**Concepts:** action for a free relativistic particle, geodesic equation, connection coefficients from the metric, lowered-index connection coefficients, raising and lowering indices with the metric, contracted connection identity, shortcut formulas for diagonal metrics, metric compatibility of the connection

**Key moves**
- Identify L = -m(-g_{mu nu} xdot^mu xdot^nu)^{1/2}; its coordinate derivative is called a force term.
- Normalize so the square-root factor equals 1 (proper-time parametrization).
- Euler-Lagrange: d/dtau(g_{mu nu} xdot^mu) = (1/2) d_nu g_{mu sigma} xdot^mu xdot^sigma.
- Product rule gives g_{mu nu} xddot^mu + d_sigma g_{mu nu} xdot^sigma xdot^mu.
- Split d_sigma g_{mu nu} xdot^mu xdot^sigma into two halves with mu and sigma swapped (eqn 9.27).
- Collect the three metric derivatives and name them Gamma_{lambda mu sigma} (eqn 9.29); raising with g^{rho lambda} (implicit) recovers the geodesic equation.
- Example 9.5: only t-derivatives survive; Gamma_ttt = 1/t^3 and Gamma_xxt = Gamma_xtx = -Gamma_txx = -1/t^3, then raise to -1/t.

### Chapter summary and exercises — p.105 (pdf 122)

The summary reduces the chapter to two claims: an action-based routine extracts the connection, and the metric determines it. Eleven exercises follow: plane polars, a torus, a non-diagonal metric with variable angle, Rindler spacetime with an interpretive part on the acceleration needed to stay still, a rotating-frame metric in Cartesian and cylindrical coordinates, the general static spherically symmetric (Schwarzschild-type) metric, a guided solution for Poincare half-plane geodesics with the ruler picture of Fig. 9.2, the equivalence of Lagrangians that are monotonic functions of each other, straight-line geodesics in a moving-coordinate metric, and a test of whether a given curve is a geodesic.

**Concepts:** Rindler spacetime, uniformly accelerated observer, rotating-frame metric, static spherically symmetric metric, geodesics of the Poincare half plane, quadratic Lagrangian for geodesics, torus metric, non-diagonal metric with variable angle

**Key moves**
- Torus and non-diagonal metrics practise the recipe where the inverse metric is not trivial.
- Rindler: normalization plus v = dx/dt gives xddot = -x/(x^2 - v^2); at v = 0 the acceleration magnitude is 1/x.
- Half plane: xdot = r^2/a, r = a sin t, dlambda = dt/sin t, x = x_0 - a cos t, so (x - x_0)^2 + r^2 = a^2.
- Length integral of dt/sin t diverges at t = 0 and t = pi, so the boundary is infinitely far; rulers of fixed length have coordinate length proportional to r.
- Any monotonic F of the square-root Lagrangian yields the same Euler-Lagrange paths, licensing L = (1/2) g_{mu nu} xdot^mu xdot^nu.

## Concepts

### connection coefficients

*mathematical-object · core* · also: Christoffel symbols, Gamma^mu_{alpha beta}, the connection

Numbers Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} that give the mu-component of the rate of change of the coordinate basis vector e_alpha along x^beta; for the connection used in general relativity they are symmetric in the lower indices, determined by first derivatives of the metric, and do not transform as tensor components.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapters 7-8 as the objects in the geodesic equation and presented as the output of a machine whose input is the metric; computed in five worked examples and eleven exercises.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, position-dependent basis vectors, geodesic equation

$$
\frac{d^2x^\mu}{d\lambda^2}+\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}\frac{dx^\alpha}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^\beta}{d\lambda}=0
$$

**Notes:** The opening says Gamma tell us about curvature; strictly curvature needs derivatives of Gamma (Riemann tensor, ch11).

**Where:** GA p.101 (pdf 118); GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119); GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122)

### connection coefficients from the metric

*identity · core* · also: Christoffel formula, Levi-Civita connection formula, eqn 9.29

For a symmetric, metric-compatible connection, the lowered-index coefficients are Gamma_{lambda mu sigma} = (1/2)(d_sigma g_{lambda mu} + d_mu g_{lambda sigma} - d_lambda g_{mu sigma}), and Gamma^rho_{mu sigma} = g^{rho lambda} Gamma_{lambda mu sigma}.

**How introduced:** Obtained as a by-product of extremizing the massive-particle action in a general spacetime (Example 9.4): the Euler-Lagrange equation, after product rule and a symmetrizing swap of dummy indices, is matched with the lowered geodesic equation. It fulfils the promise of eqn 7.37.

**Prerequisites:** Euler-Lagrange equations for the length functional, lowered-index connection coefficients, raising and lowering indices with the metric, renaming dummy indices

$$
\Gamma_{\lambda\mu\sigma}=\tfrac12\left(g_{\lambda\mu,\sigma}+g_{\lambda\sigma,\mu}-g_{\mu\sigma,\lambda}\right)
$$

$$
\Gamma^{\rho}_{\mu\sigma}=\tfrac12 g^{\rho\lambda}\left(g_{\lambda\mu,\sigma}+g_{\lambda\sigma,\mu}-g_{\mu\sigma,\lambda}\right)
$$

**Notes:** The authors emphasize that although derived from timelike motion the formula is purely geometric.

**Where:** GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121); GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122)

### lowered-index connection coefficients

*definition · developed* · also: all-down-index connection coefficients, Christoffel symbols of the first kind, Gamma_{lambda mu sigma}

Gamma_{lambda mu sigma} = g_{rho lambda} Gamma^rho_{mu sigma}: the connection with its upper index lowered onto the first slot by the metric; symmetric in its last two indices.

**How introduced:** Defined immediately after eqn 9.28 so that the metric-derivative bracket can be named, giving the compact formula 9.29.

**Prerequisites:** connection coefficients, raising and lowering indices with the metric

$$
\Gamma_{\lambda\mu\sigma}=g_{\rho\lambda}\Gamma^{\rho}_{\mu\sigma}
$$

**Notes:** Lowering an index is a definition here; it does not make Gamma a tensor.

**Where:** GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122)

### five-step recipe for connection coefficients

*technique · core* · also: the method, Steps I-V, standard machine, five-point method

Procedure: (I) write the interval as integral of L dlambda with L from the line element; (II) compute dL/dxdot^mu and dL/dx^mu; (III) impose length parametrization L = 1; (IV) take the lambda-derivative and insert into the Euler-Lagrange equations; (V) compare with the geodesic equation and read off Gamma, remembering lower-index symmetry.

**How introduced:** Stated as a boxed algorithm formalizing the Chapter 8 examples, with margin tips; then each example is labelled step by step.

**Prerequisites:** Euler-Lagrange equations for the length functional, length parametrization, geodesic equation

**Notes:** Footnote 7 calls it the five-point method; the recipe is often faster in practice than evaluating eqn 9.29 term by term.

**Where:** GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119); GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120); GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121)

### symmetry of connection coefficients in their lower indices

*identity · developed* · also: torsion-free connection, Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} = Gamma^mu_{beta alpha}, factor-of-two rule

For the connection of general relativity Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} = Gamma^mu_{beta alpha}; hence in the geodesic equation a mixed product xdot^alpha xdot^beta (alpha != beta) appears twice, and a coefficient 2F on it means Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} = F.

**How introduced:** Invoked in Step V and spelled out in a margin tip; used for the phi equation of the sphere, the theta equation of the paraboloid and the x equation of the half plane.

**Prerequisites:** connection coefficients, summation convention

$$
\ddot x^\mu+2F\dot x^\alpha\dot x^\beta=0\ (\alpha\neq\beta)\Rightarrow\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}=\Gamma^\mu_{\beta\alpha}=F
$$

**Where:** GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119)

### geodesic equation

*law · revisited* · also: equation of motion for free fall

The second-order equation d^2x^mu/dlambda^2 + Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} (dx^alpha/dlambda)(dx^beta/dlambda) = 0 satisfied by an affinely parametrized geodesic; in GR it is the equation of motion of a freely falling particle.

**How introduced:** Recalled in eqn 9.4 as the form the Euler-Lagrange equations take, then rederived for a general spacetime from the action in Example 9.4 (lowered-index form 9.28).

**Prerequisites:** connection coefficients, affine parameter

$$
\frac{d^2x^\mu}{d\lambda^2}+\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}\frac{dx^\alpha}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^\beta}{d\lambda}=0
$$

$$
g_{\mu\nu}\ddot x^\mu+\tfrac12\left(g_{\mu\nu,\sigma}+g_{\sigma\nu,\mu}-g_{\mu\sigma,\nu}\right)\dot x^\mu\dot x^\sigma=0
$$

**Where:** GA §9.1 p.101 (pdf 118); GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121)

### geodesic principle

*principle · mention* · also: free fall follows geodesics, world-line curvature view of gravity

Free particles in general relativity move on geodesics of spacetime; departures from geodesic motion (world-line acceleration) require non-gravitational forces.

**How introduced:** Evoked by the Synge epigraph, which attributes a fatal fall not to a force of gravity but to giving up a curved (supported) world line for a geodesic.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic

**Where:** GA p.101 (pdf 118)

### geodesic

*definition · revisited* · also: extremal curve, straightest path

A curve whose interval (proper length or proper time) is stationary under small variations with fixed endpoints; equivalently, a curve whose tangent is parallel transported along itself.

**How introduced:** Taken from Chapter 8; here geodesics are produced as solutions of the Euler-Lagrange equations of the interval functional for several spaces.

**Prerequisites:** line element, Euler-Lagrange equations for the length functional

**Where:** GA §9.1 p.101 (pdf 118); GA p.106 (pdf 123)

### line element

*definition · revisited* · also: ds^2, metric line element

The quadratic form ds^2 = g_{mu nu} dx^mu dx^nu giving the squared interval between neighbouring points; it encodes all metric components.

**How introduced:** Used as the starting input of the recipe; the chapter's thesis is that it determines the geodesics and the connection.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor

$$
ds^2=g_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu
$$

**Where:** GA p.101 (pdf 118); GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119)

### proper length of a spacelike curve

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: Delta l

For a curve along which ds^2 > 0, the length is the parameter integral of (g_{mu nu} xdot^mu xdot^nu)^{1/2}.

**How introduced:** Given in a margin note to eqn 9.3 alongside the timelike version.

**Prerequisites:** line element

$$
\Delta l=\int_a^b d\lambda\,\left(g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu\right)^{1/2}
$$

**Where:** GA §9.1 p.101 (pdf 118)

### proper time

*physical-quantity · revisited* · also: Delta tau, timelike interval

Time elapsed on a clock carried along a timelike world line (ds^2 < 0): the parameter integral of (-g_{mu nu} xdot^mu xdot^nu)^{1/2}.

**How introduced:** Margin note to eqn 9.3; later the massive-particle action is proportional to it and parametrized by it.

**Prerequisites:** line element

$$
\Delta\tau=\int_a^b d\lambda\,\left(-g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu\right)^{1/2}
$$

**Where:** GA §9.1 p.101 (pdf 118); GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121)

### Euler-Lagrange equations for the length functional

*technique · developed* · also: E-L equations, extremizing the interval

Stationarity of s = integral L(x, xdot) dlambda requires d/dlambda(dL/dxdot^mu) - dL/dx^mu = 0 for each coordinate; applied to the interval Lagrangian these are the geodesic equations.

**How introduced:** Carried over from Chapter 8 and used in Steps II-IV of every example.

**Prerequisites:** stationary action

$$
\frac{d}{d\lambda}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^\mu}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^\mu}=0
$$

**Where:** GA §9.1 p.101 (pdf 118); GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119); GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121)

### length parametrization

*technique · developed* · also: L = 1, proper-length or proper-time parametrization, dlambda = ds

Choosing the curve parameter so that it measures interval along the curve (lambda = s, or lambda = tau for timelike curves), which makes the square-root Lagrangian equal to 1 on the solution and removes the 1/L factors from the equations.

**How introduced:** Step III; margin note 3 says it is also what enforces u.u = -1 for massive particles, and a tip says L = 1 is a handy extra equation when solving (used in Exercises 9.4 and 9.8).

**Prerequisites:** reparametrization invariance of the length functional, Euler-Lagrange equations for the length functional

$$
L=\left|g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu\right|^{1/2}=1
$$

**Notes:** Must be imposed after taking partial derivatives; fails for null curves where L = 0.

**Where:** GA §9.1 p.101 (pdf 118); GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119); GA p.106 (pdf 123)

### affine parameter

*definition · mention* · also: affinely parametrized

A parameter in terms of which the geodesic equation takes its standard form with no term proportional to the tangent; any two such parameters are related by lambda -> a lambda + b.

**How introduced:** Appears only in exercise wording (9.8, 9.11) as the parameter along which dots are taken.

**Prerequisites:** length parametrization

**Where:** GA p.106 (pdf 123); GA p.107 (pdf 124)

### action for a free relativistic particle

*principle · developed* · also: S = -m integral dtau (...)^{1/2}

S = -m times the proper-time integral along the world line; extremizing it gives timelike geodesics as the free-particle trajectories.

**How introduced:** Introduced at the start of §9.2 as the general starting point replacing the special spatial examples; the recipe applied to it is Example 9.4.

**Prerequisites:** proper time, stationary action

$$
S=-m\int d\tau\left(-g_{\mu\nu}\frac{dx^\mu}{d\tau}\frac{dx^\nu}{d\tau}\right)^{1/2}
$$

**Where:** GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121)

### raising and lowering indices with the metric

*operation · revisited* · also: index gymnastics, inverse metric

Components with an index up and down are related by contracting with g_{mu nu} or its inverse g^{mu nu}; for a diagonal metric g^{mu mu} = 1/g_{mu mu}.

**How introduced:** Used implicitly to go from eqn 9.28 to the geodesic equation and explicitly in Example 9.5 with g^{tt} = -t^2, g^{xx} = t^2; Exercise 9.5 asks for a non-diagonal inverse.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor

$$
\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma}=g^{\rho\lambda}\Gamma_{\lambda\mu\sigma}
$$

**Where:** GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122); GA p.106 (pdf 123)

### connection coefficients of the 2-sphere

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: unit sphere Christoffel symbols

For ds^2 = dtheta^2 + sin^2 theta dphi^2 the only nonzero coefficients are Gamma^theta_{phi phi} = -sin theta cos theta and Gamma^phi_{theta phi} = Gamma^phi_{phi theta} = cot theta.

**How introduced:** Example 9.1, the first drill, reusing the variational equations found in Chapter 8.

**Prerequisites:** five-step recipe for connection coefficients, great circles as geodesics of the sphere

$$
\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta,\quad \Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi}=\cot\theta
$$

**Where:** GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119)

### paraboloid of revolution

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: parabolic surface, parabolic space

The 2D surface with line element ds^2 = (1 + a^2 r^2) dr^2 + r^2 dtheta^2 (the induced metric on z = a r^2/2); nonzero coefficients Gamma^r_{rr} = a^2 r/(1 + a^2 r^2), Gamma^r_{theta theta} = -r/(1 + a^2 r^2), Gamma^theta_{r theta} = 1/r.

**How introduced:** Example 9.2, worked in full with every step, then checked by setting a = 0.

**Prerequisites:** five-step recipe for connection coefficients

$$
ds^2=(1+a^2r^2)dr^2+r^2d\theta^2
$$

**Notes:** The embedding z = a r^2/2 is our identification; the book only gives the line element.

**Where:** GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119); GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120)

### Poincare half plane

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: hyperbolic half plane, upper half-plane model

The region r > 0 with ds^2 = (dr^2 + dx^2)/r^2, a model of the hyperbolic plane (constant negative curvature); its connection coefficients are Gamma^x_{xr} = Gamma^r_{rr} = -1/r and Gamma^r_{xx} = 1/r.

**How introduced:** Example 9.3 as the most exotic drill, with a Poincare biography margin note; geodesics and distances examined in Exercise 9.8 and Fig. 9.2.

**Prerequisites:** five-step recipe for connection coefficients, hyperbolic geometry

$$
ds^2=\frac{1}{r^2}\left(dr^2+dx^2\right)
$$

**Where:** GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120); GA p.106 (pdf 123)

### hyperbolic geometry

*definition · mention* · also: Lobachevskian geometry, negatively curved plane

Non-Euclidean geometry of constant negative curvature in which, through a point off a line, many lines fail to meet it and triangle angles sum to less than pi.

**How introduced:** Named only in the Poincare margin note, with pointers to Chapters 16 and 19.

**Prerequisites:** intrinsic curvature

**Where:** GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120)

### geodesics of the Poincare half plane

*phenomenon · developed* · also: semicircular geodesics

Solutions of the half-plane geodesic equations are semicircles (x - x_0)^2 + r^2 = a^2 centred on the boundary r = 0 (plus vertical lines x = constant as a limiting case); their length from boundary to boundary diverges.

**How introduced:** Guided Exercise 9.8: first integral xdot = r^2/a, substitution r = a sin t, the L = 1 condition giving dlambda = dt/sin t, then x = x_0 - a cos t; Fig. 9.2 shows an arc and a ladder of equal-length rulers.

**Prerequisites:** Poincare half plane, length parametrization

$$
\dot x=\frac{r^2}{a}
$$

$$
r=a\sin t,\quad d\lambda=\frac{dt}{\sin t},\quad x=x_0-a\cos t
$$

**Notes:** Vertical-line geodesics are not mentioned in the book.

**Where:** GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120); GA p.106 (pdf 123)

### boundary at infinity of the Poincare half plane

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: infinite distance to r = 0

Although r = 0 looks like a nearby edge in coordinates, the geodesic length to it is infinite, because a segment of fixed length Delta s at height r spans coordinate length Delta x = r Delta s.

**How introduced:** Exercise 9.8(e) and its italic commentary with the ruler ladder of Fig. 9.2.

**Prerequisites:** Poincare half plane, geodesics of the Poincare half plane

$$
\Delta s=\frac{\Delta x}{r}
$$

$$
\int\frac{dt}{\sin t}=\ln\tan\frac{t}{2}
$$

**Where:** GA p.106 (pdf 123)

### non-zero connection coefficients do not imply curvature

*principle · mention* · also: coordinate-induced Gamma

Connection coefficients can be nonzero in flat space purely because of curvilinear or moving coordinates (plane polars, rotating or moving frames); only the Riemann tensor decides whether a space is curved.

**How introduced:** Implicit: the paraboloid reduces at a = 0 to flat-plane coefficients that are still nonzero, Exercise 9.1 computes plane-polar coefficients, and Exercise 9.10 has Gamma in a moving-coordinate flat metric whose geodesics are straight lines.

**Prerequisites:** connection coefficients

**Notes:** Not stated in this chapter; the opening's link between Gamma and curvature could mislead without it.

**Where:** GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120); GA p.105 (pdf 122); GA p.107 (pdf 124)

### metric compatibility of the connection

*principle · mention* · also: nabla g = 0

The covariant derivative of the metric vanishes, which together with lower-index symmetry fixes the connection uniquely in terms of metric derivatives.

**How introduced:** Not named here, but margin identity 9.30, which writes each first derivative of the metric as the sum of two lowered connection coefficients, is exactly this condition in components.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative

$$
g_{\alpha\beta,\gamma}=\Gamma_{\alpha\beta\gamma}+\Gamma_{\beta\alpha\gamma}
$$

**Where:** GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122)

### contracted connection identity

*identity · introduced* · also: trace of the connection, ln sqrt(-g) identity

Contracting the upper index with one lower index gives Gamma^alpha_{beta alpha} = d_beta ln sqrt(-g), with g the metric determinant (use |g| for Riemannian metrics).

**How introduced:** Listed in margin note 9 as a labour-saving identity, without proof.

**Prerequisites:** connection coefficients from the metric, determinant of the metric

$$
\Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\alpha}=\partial_\beta\ln\sqrt{-g}
$$

**Where:** GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122)

### shortcut formulas for diagonal metrics

*technique · introduced* · also: diagonal-metric Christoffel shortcuts

For a diagonal metric (no sum, distinct indices): Gamma^mu_{nu lambda} = 0; Gamma^mu_{lambda lambda} = -(1/(2 g_{mu mu})) d_mu g_{lambda lambda}; Gamma^mu_{mu lambda} = d_lambda ln|g_{mu mu}|^{1/2}; Gamma^mu_{mu mu} = d_mu ln|g_{mu mu}|^{1/2}.

**How introduced:** Margin note 9, eqn 9.32, as a quick alternative to either the recipe or the full formula.

**Prerequisites:** connection coefficients from the metric

$$
\Gamma^\mu_{\lambda\lambda}=-\frac{1}{2g_{\mu\mu}}\partial_\mu g_{\lambda\lambda}
$$

$$
\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\lambda}=\partial_\lambda\ln|g_{\mu\mu}|^{1/2}
$$

**Where:** GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122)

### Rindler spacetime

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: Rindler metric, uniformly accelerated coordinates

The 2D flat spacetime written as ds^2 = -x^2 dt^2 + dx^2, covering a wedge of Minkowski space in coordinates adapted to uniformly accelerated observers; it has a coordinate singularity at x = 0.

**How introduced:** Exercise 9.4: extract equations of motion and coefficients (Gamma^t_{tx} = 1/x, Gamma^x_{tt} = x), then use normalization to interpret the motion.

**Prerequisites:** line element, five-step recipe for connection coefficients

$$
ds^2=-x^2dt^2+dx^2
$$

$$
2\dot x\dot t+x\ddot t=0,\quad \ddot x+x\dot t^2=0
$$

**Notes:** First met in GA Example 5.3 and Exercise 5.2; revisited in ch26.

**Where:** GA p.106 (pdf 123)

### uniformly accelerated observer

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: hovering in Rindler coordinates

An observer holding fixed x in Rindler spacetime needs proper acceleration 1/x (units c = 1) directed away from x = 0; a free particle momentarily at rest there accelerates toward x = 0 at that rate. The required acceleration diverges as x -> 0.

**How introduced:** Exercise 9.4(b): with v = dx/dt the normalized equation gives xddot = -x/(x^2 - v^2); commentary notes the divergence and promises the same feature for Schwarzschild.

**Prerequisites:** Rindler spacetime, length parametrization

$$
\ddot x=-\frac{x}{x^2-v^2}
$$

**Notes:** The book's commentary compresses the free-particle and hovering-observer readings; see gaps.

**Where:** GA p.106 (pdf 123)

### rotating-frame metric

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: rotating coordinates

Minkowski spacetime in coordinates rotating with angular velocity Omega about z: g_tt = -[1 - Omega^2(x^2 + y^2)] with off-diagonal dx dt and dy dt terms proportional to Omega.

**How introduced:** Exercises 9.5-9.6: invert the non-diagonal metric and compute coefficients in Cartesian and cylindrical coordinates.

**Prerequisites:** raising and lowering indices with the metric

$$
ds^2=-\left[1-\Omega^2(x^2+y^2)\right]dt^2+dx^2+dy^2+dz^2-2\Omega y\,dx\,dt+2\Omega x\,dy\,dt
$$

**Where:** GA p.106 (pdf 123)

### static spherically symmetric metric

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: Schwarzschild-type metric with Phi(r), Lambda(r)

Metric with g_tt = -e^{2 Phi(r)}, g_rr = e^{2 Lambda(r)} and the usual r^2 angular part; Schwarzschild is the special case e^{2 Phi} = e^{-2 Lambda} = 1 - 2M/r.

**How introduced:** Exercise 9.7 asks for its connection coefficients, written with the proper-time sign convention inside the square root.

**Prerequisites:** five-step recipe for connection coefficients

$$
s=\int d\lambda\left[e^{2\Phi}\dot t^2-e^{2\Lambda}\dot r^2-r^2\dot\theta^2-r^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2\right]^{1/2}
$$

**Notes:** Results feed GA Exercise 11.1 and the Schwarzschild chapters.

**Where:** GA p.106 (pdf 123)

### quadratic Lagrangian for geodesics

*technique · introduced* · also: energy Lagrangian, L = (1/2) g_{mu nu} xdot^mu xdot^nu

Because Euler-Lagrange paths are unchanged when L is replaced by a monotonic function F(L) (with the parameter chosen affinely), geodesics can be found from L = (1/2) g_{mu nu} xdot^mu xdot^nu, which avoids square roots and also works for null curves.

**How introduced:** Exercise 9.9, with commentary likening it to non-relativistic kinetic energy.

**Prerequisites:** Euler-Lagrange equations for the length functional, length parametrization

$$
L=F\!\left(\sqrt{g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}\right)
$$

$$
L=\tfrac12 g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu
$$

**Notes:** Applicability to null geodesics is our addition; the book does not state it here.

**Where:** GA p.106 (pdf 123)

### torus metric

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: surface of a torus

Line element of a torus with tube radius a and central radius c, whose u-direction scale factor is c + a cos v and v-direction scale factor is a; nonzero coefficients Gamma^v_{uu} = (sin v/a)(c + a cos v), Gamma^u_{uv} = -a sin v/(c + a cos v).

**How introduced:** Exercise 9.2.

**Prerequisites:** five-step recipe for connection coefficients

$$
ds^2=(c+a\cos v)^2du^2+a^2dv^2
$$

**Notes:** Printed 9.36 has u instead of a in the denominator.

**Where:** GA p.105 (pdf 122)

### non-diagonal metric with variable angle

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: Chebyshev net metric

ds^2 = du^2 + dv^2 + 2 cos theta(u, v) du dv, coordinates of unit speed meeting at a variable angle theta; the only nonzero coefficients are Gamma^u_{uu} = cot theta theta_{,u}, Gamma^v_{uu} = -theta_{,u}/sin theta and the same with u and v exchanged.

**How introduced:** Exercise 9.3, practising a case where the inverse metric mixes components.

**Prerequisites:** raising and lowering indices with the metric

$$
ds^2=du^2+dv^2+2\cos\theta(u,v)\,du\,dv
$$

**Notes:** The Chebyshev-net name is our identification.

**Where:** GA p.105 (pdf 122)

### four-velocity

*physical-quantity · mention* · also: velocity 4-vector u, u.u = -1

Tangent to a massive particle's world line parametrized by proper time, normalized so that u.u = -1.

**How introduced:** Margin note 3: length parametrization is what guarantees this normalization.

**Prerequisites:** proper time

$$
\boldsymbol u\cdot\boldsymbol u=-1
$$

**Where:** GA §9.1 p.101 (pdf 118)

## Key equations

### (9.1) Proper length of a spacelike curve · supporting · GA §9.1 p.101 (pdf 118)

$$
\Delta l=\int_a^b d\lambda\left(g_{\mu\nu}\frac{dx^\mu}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^\nu}{d\lambda}\right)^{1/2}
$$

Length measured along a curve whose tangent is spacelike.

**Symbols:** lambda curve parameter; g_{mu nu} metric

### (9.2) Proper time along a timelike curve · supporting · GA §9.1 p.101 (pdf 118)

$$
\Delta\tau=\int_a^b d\lambda\left(-g_{\mu\nu}\frac{dx^\mu}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^\nu}{d\lambda}\right)^{1/2}
$$

Clock time accumulated by an observer on a timelike world line; the minus sign keeps the root real with signature (-+++).

**Symbols:** tau proper time

### (9.3) Parametrized interval · central · GA §9.1 p.101 (pdf 118)

$$
s=\int_a^b d\lambda\left|g_{\mu\nu}\frac{dx^\mu}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^\nu}{d\lambda}\right|^{1/2}
$$

The functional whose extremals are geodesics; the absolute value covers both spacelike and timelike curves.

**Symbols:** s interval between events a and b

### (9.4) Geodesic equation · central · GA §9.1 p.101 (pdf 118)

$$
\frac{d^2x^\mu}{d\lambda^2}+\frac{dx^\alpha}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^\beta}{d\lambda}\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}=0
$$

Target form: once the Euler-Lagrange equations are written like this, the coefficients of the velocity products are the connection coefficients.

**Symbols:** Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} connection coefficients

### Metric-to-connection flow (Fig. 9.1) · supporting · GA p.101 (pdf 118)

$$
g\ \Longrightarrow\ ds^2\ \Longrightarrow\ \Gamma
$$

Conceptual thesis of the chapter: the metric defines the line element, which fixes geodesics and hence Gamma.

### Euler-Lagrange equations (Step IV) · supporting · GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119)

$$
\frac{d}{d\lambda}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^\mu}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^\mu}=0,\qquad L=\left|g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu\right|^{1/2}
$$

Equations applied in every example; not printed explicitly in this chapter (carried over from Chapter 8).

**Symbols:** xdot^mu = dx^mu/dlambda

### Factor-of-two read-off rule (margin tip) · supporting · GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119)

$$
\ddot x^\mu+2F\,\dot x^\alpha\dot x^\beta=0\ \ (\alpha\neq\beta)\ \Rightarrow\ \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}=\Gamma^\mu_{\beta\alpha}=F
$$

A mixed velocity product appears twice in the summed geodesic equation, so its coefficient is twice the connection coefficient.

**Symbols:** F any function of the coordinates

### Chain rule tip · derivation-step · GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119)

$$
\frac{d}{d\lambda}f(x^\mu)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^\mu}\frac{dx^\mu}{d\lambda}
$$

Used in Step IV to differentiate metric components along the curve.

### (9.5) Unit sphere interval · derivation-step · GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119)

$$
s=\int\left(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)^{1/2}=\int d\lambda\left[\left(\frac{d\theta}{d\lambda}\right)^2+\sin^2\theta\left(\frac{d\phi}{d\lambda}\right)^2\right]^{1/2}
$$

Step I for the 2-sphere; g_{theta theta} = 1, g_{phi phi} = sin^2 theta.

### (9.6) Sphere geodesic equations · supporting · GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119)

$$
\frac{d^2\theta}{d\lambda^2}-\sin\theta\cos\theta\left(\frac{d\phi}{d\lambda}\right)^2=0,\qquad \frac{d^2\phi}{d\lambda^2}+2\cot\theta\,\frac{d\phi}{d\lambda}\frac{d\theta}{d\lambda}=0
$$

Equations of motion on the sphere in geodesic form (the second is the expanded conservation of sin^2 theta phidot).

### (9.7) Sphere connection coefficients · supporting · GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119)

$$
\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta,\qquad \Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi}=\Gamma^\phi_{\phi\theta}=\cot\theta
$$

Nonzero coefficients of the unit 2-sphere; the cot theta comes from halving the mixed term.

### (9.8) Paraboloid interval · derivation-step · GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119)

$$
ds^2=(1+a^2r^2)dr^2+r^2d\theta^2,\qquad s=\int d\lambda\left[(1+a^2r^2)\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2\right]^{1/2}
$$

Step I for a paraboloid of revolution; a sets how sharply the surface curves.

**Symbols:** a shape constant; r, theta polar coordinates on the surface

### (9.9) Paraboloid Step II derivatives (corrected) · derivation-step · GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120)

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot r}=\frac{1}{L}(1+a^2r^2)\dot r,\quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\theta}=\frac{1}{L}r^2\dot\theta,\quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial r}=\frac{1}{L}\left[a^2r\,\dot r^2+r\,\dot\theta^2\right],\quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta}=0
$$

Momentum-like and force-like derivatives. The printed dL/dr omits the square on dr/dlambda; the correct term is a^2 r rdot^2, as required to reach eqn 9.10.

### (9.10) Paraboloid radial equation · supporting · GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120)

$$
\ddot r+\frac{a^2r}{1+a^2r^2}\dot r^2-\frac{r}{1+a^2r^2}\dot\theta^2=0
$$

Radial geodesic equation after setting L = 1 and dividing by (1 + a^2 r^2).

### (9.11) Paraboloid angular equation · supporting · GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120)

$$
\ddot\theta+\frac{2}{r}\dot r\dot\theta=0
$$

Angular equation; equivalent to conservation of r^2 thetadot.

### (9.12) Paraboloid connection coefficients · supporting · GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120)

$$
\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta}=\frac1r,\quad \Gamma^r_{rr}=\frac{a^2r}{1+a^2r^2},\quad \Gamma^r_{\theta\theta}=-\frac{r}{1+a^2r^2}
$$

Nonzero coefficients; setting a = 0 gives the flat plane-polar set 1/r, 0, -r.

### (9.13) Poincare half-plane metric · supporting · GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120)

$$
ds^2=\frac{1}{r^2}\left(dr^2+dx^2\right),\qquad r>0
$$

Conformally flat metric of the hyperbolic plane; distances shrink relative to coordinates as r grows.

**Symbols:** r height above boundary; x horizontal coordinate

### (9.14) Half-plane interval · derivation-step · GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120)

$$
s=\int d\lambda\left[\frac{\dot r^2}{r^2}+\frac{\dot x^2}{r^2}\right]^{1/2}
$$

Step I for the half plane.

### Half-plane Step II derivatives · derivation-step · GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120)

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot r}=\frac{1}{L}\frac{\dot r}{r^2},\quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x}=\frac{1}{L}\frac{\dot x}{r^2},\quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial r}=-\frac{1}{L}\frac{\dot r^2+\dot x^2}{r^3},\quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial x}=0
$$

Derivatives before normalization.

### (9.15) Half-plane Step IV derivatives · derivation-step · GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120)

$$
\frac{d}{d\lambda}\left(\frac{\dot r}{r^2}\right)=\frac{\ddot r}{r^2}-\frac{2\dot r^2}{r^3},\qquad \frac{d}{d\lambda}\left(\frac{\dot x}{r^2}\right)=\frac{\ddot x}{r^2}-\frac{2\dot r\dot x}{r^3}
$$

Total lambda-derivatives of the momenta with L = 1.

### (9.16) Half-plane Euler-Lagrange equations · derivation-step · GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120)

$$
\ddot r-\frac{2}{r}\dot r^2=-\frac1r\left(\dot r^2+\dot x^2\right),\qquad \ddot x-\frac{2}{r}\dot r\dot x=0
$$

Raw equations of motion before collecting terms.

### (9.17) Half-plane radial equation · supporting · GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120)

$$
\ddot r-\frac1r\dot r^2+\frac1r\dot x^2=0
$$

Radial equation in geodesic form after combining rdot^2 terms.

### (9.18) Half-plane connection coefficients · supporting · GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120)

$$
\Gamma^x_{xr}=\Gamma^x_{rx}=-\frac1r,\quad \Gamma^r_{rr}=-\frac1r,\quad \Gamma^r_{xx}=\frac1r
$$

All nonzero coefficients of the Poincare half plane.

### (9.19) Free massive-particle action · central · GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121)

$$
S=-m\int d\tau\left(-g_{\mu\nu}\frac{dx^\mu}{d\tau}\frac{dx^\nu}{d\tau}\right)^{1/2}
$$

Action proportional to elapsed proper time; its extremals are timelike geodesics in any spacetime.

**Symbols:** m rest mass; tau proper time

### (9.20) Lagrangian of the free particle · derivation-step · GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121)

$$
L=-m\left(-g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu\right)^{1/2}
$$

Step I of the general derivation.

### (9.21) Canonical momentum · derivation-step · GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121)

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^\mu}=\frac{m\,g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\nu}{\left(-g_{\alpha\beta}\dot x^\alpha\dot x^\beta\right)^{1/2}}
$$

Step II momentum. The book writes the denominator as L; since L contains -m, this must be read as the square-root factor, which is 1 after normalization.

### (9.22) Force term · derivation-step · GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121)

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^\nu}=\frac{m}{2}\frac{\partial_\nu g_{\mu\sigma}\,\dot x^\mu\dot x^\sigma}{\left(-g_{\alpha\beta}\dot x^\alpha\dot x^\beta\right)^{1/2}}
$$

Coordinate derivative of L, which the authors call a force term: gradients of the metric act like generalized forces.

### (9.23) Step IV momentum derivative (corrected indices) · derivation-step · GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121)

$$
\frac{d}{d\tau}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^\nu}=m\frac{d}{d\tau}\left(g_{\mu\nu}\frac{dx^\mu}{d\tau}\right)
$$

Rate of change of momentum after normalization. The print has dL/dx^mu on the left, which should be the velocity derivative with free index nu to match the right-hand side.

### (9.24) Euler-Lagrange equation in momentum form · supporting · GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121)

$$
\frac{d}{d\tau}\left(g_{\mu\nu}\frac{dx^\mu}{d\tau}\right)=\frac12\,\partial_\nu g_{\mu\sigma}\frac{dx^\mu}{d\tau}\frac{dx^\sigma}{d\tau}
$$

Covariant momentum changes only where the metric varies; if g is independent of x^nu, the lowered momentum component is conserved.

### (9.25) Product rule · derivation-step · GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121)

$$
\frac{d}{d\tau}\left(g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\right)=g_{\mu\nu}\ddot x^\mu+\partial_\sigma g_{\mu\nu}\,\dot x^\sigma\dot x^\mu
$$

Expands the total derivative using the chain rule on g.

### (9.26) Combined equation · derivation-step · GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121)

$$
g_{\mu\nu}\ddot x^\mu+\left(\partial_\sigma g_{\mu\nu}-\tfrac12\partial_\nu g_{\mu\sigma}\right)\dot x^\mu\dot x^\sigma=0
$$

Euler-Lagrange equation with all terms on one side.

### (9.27) Symmetrization by dummy-index swap · derivation-step · GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121)

$$
\dot x^\mu\dot x^\sigma g_{\mu\nu,\sigma}=\tfrac12\dot x^\mu\dot x^\sigma\left(g_{\mu\nu,\sigma}+g_{\sigma\nu,\mu}\right)
$$

Because xdot^mu xdot^sigma is symmetric, only the symmetric part of the coefficient survives; this creates the three-term Christoffel structure.

### (9.28) Lowered geodesic equation (corrected) · supporting · GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121)

$$
g_{\mu\nu}\frac{d^2x^\mu}{d\tau^2}+\tfrac12\left(\partial_\sigma g_{\mu\nu}+\partial_\mu g_{\sigma\nu}-\partial_\nu g_{\mu\sigma}\right)\frac{dx^\mu}{d\tau}\frac{dx^\sigma}{d\tau}=0
$$

Geodesic equation with its free index lowered. The printed middle term reads d g_{sigma nu}/dx^nu; it must be the derivative with respect to x^mu, as eqn 9.27 shows.

### Definition of lowered connection · supporting · GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122)

$$
\Gamma_{\lambda\mu\sigma}=g_{\rho\lambda}\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma}
$$

Names the bracket in 9.28 (with nu renamed lambda).

### (9.29) Christoffel formula · central · GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122)

$$
\Gamma_{\lambda\mu\sigma}=\frac12\left(\frac{\partial g_{\lambda\mu}}{\partial x^\sigma}+\frac{\partial g_{\lambda\sigma}}{\partial x^\mu}-\frac{\partial g_{\mu\sigma}}{\partial x^\lambda}\right)
$$

The connection built from first derivatives of the metric; a geometric identity valid for all geodesics, not only timelike ones.

**Symbols:** lambda: lowered index; mu, sigma: symmetric pair

### Raising step (implicit in the text) · derivation-step · GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122)

$$
\frac{d^2x^\rho}{d\tau^2}+\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\sigma=0,\qquad \Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma}=g^{\rho\lambda}\Gamma_{\lambda\mu\sigma}
$$

Multiplying 9.28 by the inverse metric recovers the standard geodesic equation; the book leaves this step to the reader.

### (9.29a) Christoffel formula, comma notation · supporting · GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122)

$$
\Gamma_{\lambda\mu\sigma}=\tfrac12\left(g_{\lambda\mu,\sigma}+g_{\lambda\sigma,\mu}-g_{\mu\sigma,\lambda}\right)
$$

Same result with commas for partial derivatives.

### (9.30) Metric derivative from lowered connection · supporting · GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122)

$$
g_{\alpha\beta,\gamma}=\Gamma_{\alpha\beta\gamma}+\Gamma_{\beta\alpha\gamma}
$$

Inverse relation: each metric derivative is a sum of two lowered coefficients; equivalent to metric compatibility.

### (9.31) Contracted connection · supporting · GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122)

$$
\Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\alpha}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\beta}\ln\sqrt{-g}
$$

Trace of the connection from the metric determinant; handy for divergences. Use |g| for positive-definite metrics.

**Symbols:** g determinant of g_{mu nu}

### (9.32) Diagonal-metric shortcuts · supporting · GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122)

$$
\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\lambda}=0,\quad \Gamma^\mu_{\lambda\lambda}=-\frac{1}{2g_{\mu\mu}}\frac{\partial g_{\lambda\lambda}}{\partial x^\mu},\quad \Gamma^\mu_{\mu\lambda}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\lambda}\ln|g_{\mu\mu}|^{1/2},\quad \Gamma^\mu_{\mu\mu}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}\ln|g_{\mu\mu}|^{1/2}
$$

Every coefficient of a diagonal metric by pattern; no summation, and the displayed indices are distinct.

### (9.33) Example 9.5 metric · supporting · GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122)

$$
ds^2=\frac{1}{t^2}\left(dx^2-dt^2\right)
$$

A 2D Lorentzian metric with g_tt = -1/t^2, g_xx = 1/t^2.

### (9.34) Example 9.5 coefficients · supporting · GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122)

$$
\Gamma_{ttt}=\frac{1}{t^3},\quad \Gamma_{xxt}=\Gamma_{xtx}=-\Gamma_{txx}=-\frac{1}{t^3};\qquad \Gamma^t_{tt}=\Gamma^t_{xx}=\Gamma^x_{xt}=-\frac1t
$$

Lowered coefficients from eqn 9.29 and raised ones using g^{tt} = -t^2, g^{xx} = t^2.

### (9.35) Torus line element · supporting · GA p.105 (pdf 122)

$$
ds^2=(c+a\cos v)^2du^2+a^2dv^2
$$

Exercise 9.2 surface.

## Worked examples

### Example 9.1 · intro · GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119)

**Problem:** Find the connection coefficients of the surface of a unit sphere.

**Method:** Write the interval with g_{theta theta} = 1, g_{phi phi} = sin^2 theta (Step I), reuse the Euler-Lagrange equations derived in Chapter 8 (Steps II-IV), put them in geodesic form and compare coefficients (Step V).

**Key insight:** Once the equations look like the geodesic equation, Gamma is just bookkeeping; the phi equation's coefficient 2 cot theta multiplies a mixed product, so the coefficient itself is cot theta.

**Result:** Gamma^theta_{phi phi} = -sin theta cos theta; Gamma^phi_{theta phi} = Gamma^phi_{phi theta} = cot theta; all others vanish.

**Concepts:** connection coefficients of the 2-sphere, five-step recipe for connection coefficients, symmetry of connection coefficients in their lower indices

### Example 9.2 · standard · GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119)

**Problem:** Find the connection coefficients of a paraboloid with ds^2 = (1 + a^2 r^2) dr^2 + r^2 dtheta^2.

**Method:** Full run of the recipe: partial derivatives of the square-root Lagrangian with their 1/L factors, set L = 1, differentiate the momenta along the curve, divide the radial equation by (1 + a^2 r^2), read off.

**Key insight:** The metric factor in front of rddot must be divided out before reading off; and the a = 0 limit must reproduce the flat plane-polar coefficients, a quick correctness check.

**Result:** Gamma^theta_{r theta} = 1/r, Gamma^r_{rr} = a^2 r/(1 + a^2 r^2), Gamma^r_{theta theta} = -r/(1 + a^2 r^2).

**Concepts:** paraboloid of revolution, length parametrization, non-zero connection coefficients do not imply curvature

### Example 9.3 · standard · GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120)

**Problem:** Find the connection coefficients of the Poincare half plane ds^2 = (dr^2 + dx^2)/r^2, r > 0.

**Method:** Recipe with a conformal factor: derivatives of L, set L = 1, product rule on rdot/r^2 and xdot/r^2, form the Euler-Lagrange equations, collect the two rdot^2 terms in the r equation, then read off, halving the mixed x-equation term.

**Key insight:** Because the metric is 1/r^2 times flat, every nonzero coefficient is plus or minus 1/r; terms on both sides of the raw r equation have to be combined before the geodesic form appears.

**Result:** Gamma^x_{xr} = Gamma^x_{rx} = -1/r, Gamma^r_{rr} = -1/r, Gamma^r_{xx} = 1/r; geodesics are circular arcs (Exercise 9.8).

**Concepts:** Poincare half plane, five-step recipe for connection coefficients, hyperbolic geometry

### Example 9.4 · challenging · GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121)

**Problem:** Derive the equation of motion of a free massive particle in an arbitrary metric from the action S = -m integral dtau (-g_{mu nu} xdot^mu xdot^nu)^{1/2}, and hence a general formula for the connection coefficients.

**Method:** Identify L; compute the momentum and the force term; normalize so the root equals 1; write the Euler-Lagrange equation; expand d/dtau(g_{mu nu} xdot^mu) with the product and chain rules; symmetrize the metric-derivative term by swapping the dummy indices mu and sigma; collect into g_{mu nu} xddot^mu plus a three-term bracket; define Gamma_{lambda mu sigma} to match the lowered geodesic equation.

**Key insight:** Only the part of a coefficient symmetric in the two contracted velocity indices matters, and splitting d_sigma g_{mu nu} into two halves is exactly what creates the plus-plus-minus Christoffel combination.

**Result:** Gamma_{lambda mu sigma} = (1/2)(g_{lambda mu,sigma} + g_{lambda sigma,mu} - g_{mu sigma,lambda}) (eqn 9.29), with the geodesic equation recovered on raising the index.

**Concepts:** action for a free relativistic particle, connection coefficients from the metric, lowered-index connection coefficients, geodesic equation

### Example 9.5 · standard · GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122)

**Problem:** Compute the connection coefficients of the 2D Lorentzian metric ds^2 = (dx^2 - dt^2)/t^2.

**Method:** Apply the Christoffel formula directly: only d_t g_tt = 2/t^3 and d_t g_xx = -2/t^3 are nonzero; evaluate each lowered coefficient; raise with g^{tt} = -t^2, g^{xx} = t^2.

**Key insight:** With the formula the sign of g_tt is handled automatically and no Lagrangian is needed; after raising, the three independent nonzero coefficients coincide at -1/t.

**Result:** Gamma_ttt = 1/t^3, Gamma_xxt = Gamma_xtx = -Gamma_txx = -1/t^3; Gamma^t_{tt} = Gamma^t_{xx} = Gamma^x_{xt} = -1/t.

**Concepts:** connection coefficients from the metric, raising and lowering indices with the metric


## Analogies and intuitions

### Synge's cliff: dying from a fall is not gravity's fault but the result of swapping a safely curved world line for a geodesic. → geodesic principle · useful · GA p.101 (pdf 118)

Epigraph and opening sentence: the geometric view makes world-line curvature (acceleration away from a geodesic), not force, the thing to think about.

**Where it breaks down:** The 'curvature of a world line' here means its acceleration relative to free fall, which is different from the curvature of spacetime itself; learners can conflate the two.

**App idea:** Split-screen: an accelerometer on a person standing at a cliff edge reads 1 g while their world line bends away from the geodesic; after stepping off, the reading drops to zero and the world line coincides with the geodesic.

### A machine with the metric as input and the connection as output → five-step recipe for connection coefficients · strong · GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120)

Fig. 9.1 and the phrase about putting the half plane through the standard machine turn a derivation into a repeatable process.

**Where it breaks down:** Mechanical use hides why the recipe works (stationarity of length, parametrization choice) and fails silently for null curves.

**App idea:** A literal conveyor-belt animation: a metric card enters, each station applies one step, and a Gamma table exits; learners can pause and inspect each station.

### Rulers of equal true length that look longer the higher they sit → boundary at infinity of the Poincare half plane · strong · GA p.106 (pdf 123)

Exercise 9.8 commentary and Fig. 9.2 explain the infinite geodesic length by showing coordinate lengths shrink toward r = 0 for fixed proper length.

**Where it breaks down:** Only horizontal rulers are drawn; vertical distances also scale as 1/r (giving a logarithm), and the picture does not by itself explain why geodesics are circles.

**App idea:** Tile the half plane with equal-area hyperbolic squares that shrink toward the boundary; let the learner count tiles along different paths between two points and discover that the semicircle uses fewest.

### The derivative of L with respect to position as a force term → Euler-Lagrange equations for the length functional · useful · GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121)

Example 9.4 labels dL/dx^nu a force term: metric gradients push on the covariant momentum just as potential gradients do in mechanics.

**Where it breaks down:** No real force acts; the 'force' is coordinate dependent and can be removed locally by choosing a freely falling frame.

**App idea:** Show the half-plane walker with arrows for the momentum rate of change computed from the metric gradient, and let the learner switch to local inertial coordinates where the arrows vanish.

### The quadratic Lagrangian looks like non-relativistic kinetic energy → quadratic Lagrangian for geodesics · useful · GA p.106 (pdf 123)

Exercise 9.9 closes by noting (1/2) g_{mu nu} xdot^mu xdot^nu has the form of a free particle's kinetic energy, making the square-root-free Lagrangian feel natural.

**Where it breaks down:** It equals a constant along affinely parametrized geodesics and is not an energy in any conserved-quantity sense for the relativistic particle; its parameter must be affine.

**App idea:** Integrate geodesics on a surface with both Lagrangians and overlay the paths; then deliberately use a non-affine parameter to show where the equivalence breaks.

### Flattening the paraboloid by turning a down to zero → paraboloid of revolution · useful · GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120)

Example 9.2 checks its answer by noting that the coefficients revert to the flat polar ones when a = 0.

**Where it breaks down:** Reproducing a limit is necessary but not sufficient for correctness.

**App idea:** Slider on a from 0 to large values morphs a 3D paraboloid while the Gamma table and a launched geodesic update continuously.

### Hovering near x = 0 in Rindler space is increasingly hard, like hovering near a black hole → uniformly accelerated observer · useful · GA p.106 (pdf 123)

Exercise 9.4 commentary notes the acceleration 1/x needed to stay put diverges at x = 0 and promises the same behaviour in Schwarzschild geometry.

**Where it breaks down:** Rindler space is flat and x = 0 is only a coordinate horizon; tidal effects near a black hole have no Rindler counterpart.

**App idea:** Rocket-thrust dial: choose a hovering position x and read the required proper acceleration 1/x, with the Minkowski diagram showing the matching hyperbola approaching the light cone.


## Misconceptions addressed

### A falling body is pulled by a gravitational force, and standing on the ground is the natural, unforced state. · GA p.101 (pdf 118)

**Correction:** In GR free fall is geodesic and unforced; the ground exerts the real force that keeps a world line away from a geodesic.

**Why tempting:** Everyday experience feels weight as the norm and Newtonian teaching names gravity a force.

### One can set L = 1 in the Lagrangian before taking derivatives. · GA §9.1 p.101 (pdf 118)

**Correction:** L = 1 is a choice of parametrization on the solution curve, not an identity in the variables x and xdot; it may only be imposed after the first partial derivatives are taken, otherwise every derivative of the constant vanishes and no equations result.

**Why tempting:** The final equations contain no L, so it looks harmless to simplify early.

### The coefficient of a mixed term xdot^alpha xdot^beta in the equation of motion is itself Gamma^mu_{alpha beta}. · GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119)

**Correction:** The summed geodesic equation contains both Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} and Gamma^mu_{beta alpha} times the same product, so the coefficient is twice the connection coefficient.

**Why tempting:** Diagonal terms such as xdot^alpha xdot^alpha need no halving, so pattern matching seems uniform.

### Nonzero connection coefficients show that a space is curved. · GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120)

**Correction:** Flat space in polar, rotating or moving coordinates has nonzero Gamma; curvature is diagnosed by the Riemann tensor built from derivatives of Gamma (Chapter 11).

**Why tempting:** The chapter opens by linking Gamma with curvature, and most examples are curved surfaces.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The Christoffel formula holds only for massive particles because it came from their action. · GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122)

**Correction:** The formula is a geometric relation between metric and connection, valid for spacelike, null and timelike geodesics alike.

**Why tempting:** The derivation used m, proper time and u.u = -1.

### The edge r = 0 of the half plane is a boundary a traveller can reach in finite distance. · GA p.106 (pdf 123)

**Correction:** Geodesic length to the boundary diverges logarithmically; rulers of fixed length span ever smaller coordinate intervals as r decreases.

**Why tempting:** In the coordinate picture r = 0 is just a line a short way below.

### Geodesics should look like straight lines in the coordinate drawing. · GA p.106 (pdf 123)

**Correction:** Half-plane geodesics are semicircles centred on the boundary (and vertical lines); what is straight depends on the metric, not on the chart.

**Why tempting:** In Cartesian flat space coordinate-straight and geodesic coincide.

### A particle placed at rest at fixed Rindler coordinate x stays there on its own. · GA p.106 (pdf 123)

**Correction:** A free particle momentarily at rest accelerates toward x = 0 at rate 1/x; remaining at fixed x needs a sustained proper acceleration 1/x away from x = 0.

**Why tempting:** The metric is time independent, and the exercise text speaks of an observer with v = 0 undergoing acceleration without saying who supplies it.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Lowering the index of Gamma with the metric means the connection coefficients form a tensor. · GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122)

**Correction:** Gamma_{lambda mu sigma} = g_{rho lambda} Gamma^rho_{mu sigma} is just a convenient definition; neither version transforms tensorially.

**Why tempting:** Index lowering is otherwise used only on tensors.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The mass m and the overall minus sign in the action affect the particle's path. · GA p.106 (pdf 123)

**Correction:** Constant factors such as -m cancel out of the Euler-Lagrange equations. Exercise 9.9 goes further: replacing the square-root Lagrangian by any monotonic function of it leaves the geodesics unchanged. The book never discusses the mass factor itself.

**Why tempting:** In Newtonian problems mass appears explicitly in the Lagrangian.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Synge's cliff fall · GA p.101 (pdf 118)

**Setup:** A person stands at a cliff edge, supported by the ground, then steps off.

**Lesson:** Standing is accelerated (non-geodesic) motion; the fall is the unforced geodesic, so responsibility lies with abandoning the supported world line, not with a gravitational force.

**App idea:** Spacetime diagram with Schwarzschild-like light-cone tilting where the learner toggles 'ground on/off' and sees the world line snap from a bent track to the local geodesic, with an accelerometer readout.

### Laying rulers in the half plane · GA p.106 (pdf 123)

**Setup:** Place rulers of the same proper length parallel to the x axis at a range of heights r.

**Lesson:** Their coordinate lengths are proportional to r, so approaching r = 0 requires infinitely many rulers and the boundary is infinitely far.

**App idea:** Drop rulers by clicking; each snaps to its correct coordinate width, and a running total of proper distance along a chosen path is displayed.

### Trying to stay still in Rindler spacetime · GA p.106 (pdf 123)

**Setup:** An observer at coordinate x wants zero velocity dx/dt forever.

**Lesson:** The geodesic equation shows free particles accelerate toward x = 0 at 1/x, so staying still demands thrust whose required size diverges as x -> 0, prefiguring behaviour near a horizon.

**App idea:** Minkowski diagram showing hyperbolae of constant x; slide x toward zero and watch the hyperbola hug the light cone while a gauge of required acceleration climbs.

## Notation and conventions

- **Metric signature:** (-+++): timelike intervals have ds^2 < 0, spacelike ds^2 > 0; proper time uses -g_{mu nu} under the root. — Exercise 9.7 writes the interval with signs flipped inside the root (e^{2 Phi} tdot^2 - ...), i.e. the proper-time form. (GA §9.1 p.101 (pdf 118))
- **Units:** c = 1 throughout; e.g. the Rindler acceleration 1/x and speeds v in the moving-coordinate metric are dimensionless. (GA p.106 (pdf 123))
- **Connection index placement:** Gamma^mu_{alpha beta}: upper index is the output component, lower pair symmetric; the lowered form Gamma_{lambda mu sigma} = g_{rho lambda} Gamma^rho_{mu sigma} puts the lowered index first. (GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122))
- **Dot notation:** A dot denotes derivative with respect to the curve parameter lambda (or tau in §9.2). — Suggested in the margin tips to save writing. (GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119))
- **Comma notation:** g_{lambda mu,sigma} means partial derivative of g_{lambda mu} with respect to x^sigma. (GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122))
- **Length parametrization shorthand:** 'L = 1' denotes choosing lambda so the square-root Lagrangian equals one; for massive particles lambda = tau. — In Example 9.4 L includes the factor -m, so 'L = 1' there really refers to the square-root factor. (GA §9.1 p.101 (pdf 118))
- **Name of the method:** Steps I-V are called 'the method', a 'standard machine', and in footnote 7 the 'five-point method'. (GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121))
- **No summation in shortcut formulas:** In eqn 9.32 repeated indices are not summed and the displayed indices are distinct. (GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122))
- **Absolute value in the interval:** Eqn 9.3 uses |g_{mu nu} xdot^mu xdot^nu|^{1/2} so a single expression serves spacelike and timelike curves. (GA §9.1 p.101 (pdf 118))
- **Half-plane coordinates:** The Poincare half plane uses (x, r) with r the height above the boundary, not a radial distance. (GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120))
- **Symbol reuse in Exercise 9.8:** t is an angle parameter in r = a sin t, not time; a is both the circle radius and the lower limit of the length integral in part (e). — Rename limits (e.g. t_1, t_2) when teaching. (GA p.106 (pdf 123))
- **Metric determinant:** g denotes det g_{mu nu}; for Lorentzian metrics g < 0, hence sqrt(-g) in eqn 9.31. (GA §9.2 p.105 (pdf 122))

## Teaching gems

### State the whole chapter as a three-box pipeline, metric -> line element -> connection, before any calculation. · GA p.101 (pdf 118)

**Why it works:** Gives learners a destination and a reason for each step, so the algebra reads as progress through a known chain.

**App idea:** Persistent pipeline header in every Gamma-computing activity that highlights which stage the learner is on.

### A boxed five-step algorithm, with each worked example annotated by step number. · GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119)

**Why it works:** Converts a derivation into a checklist that novices can execute and experts can debug step by step.

**App idea:** Guided stepper: the learner fills each step for a chosen metric; a symbolic engine checks the entry before unlocking the next step and pinpoints the first wrong step.

### The factor-of-two rule for reading off mixed coefficients. · GA §9.1 p.102 (pdf 119)

**Why it works:** Targets the single most common error when matching equations of motion to the geodesic equation.

**App idea:** Drag-and-drop matching: terms of an equation of motion are dragged into slots of a Gamma table; mixed terms dropped without halving flash and show both symmetric slots filling.

### Check a result by switching off a parameter (paraboloid with a -> 0 gives flat polar coefficients). · GA §9.1 p.103 (pdf 120)

**Why it works:** Builds the habit of sanity checks and shows that flat space can still have nonzero Gamma.

**App idea:** 3D paraboloid with slider a; Gamma entries animate toward their polar limits while a geodesic straightens into a coordinate-curved but geometrically straight line.

### Equal-length rulers that shrink toward the boundary explain why the half plane's edge is infinitely far and why geodesics arch upward. · GA p.106 (pdf 123)

**Why it works:** A single picture replaces an integral and gives a physical sense of a hyperbolic metric.

**App idea:** Half-plane walker with ruler ladder, semicircle geodesics through dragged points, and a divergent distance counter near r = 0.

### Derive the general formula by running the same recipe on the action, whose answer is already known, with the symmetrizing dummy-index swap as the key move. · GA §9.2 p.104 (pdf 121)

**Why it works:** Learners see that the Christoffel formula is not a new postulate but a compressed version of the recipe they have already used.

**App idea:** Animated index board: the term d_sigma g_{mu nu} xdot^mu xdot^sigma splits into two copies, one with mu and sigma swapped via colour-coded tiles, and the three-term bracket assembles itself.

### Any monotonic function of the length Lagrangian gives the same geodesics, so use the kinetic-energy-like (1/2) g xdot xdot. · GA p.106 (pdf 123)

**Why it works:** Removes square roots from all later calculations and connects to familiar mechanics.

**App idea:** Numerical geodesic integrator with a Lagrangian selector; paths overlay exactly with affine parameters and drift apart when the learner picks a non-affine parameter.

### Synge's cliff epigraph: blame the geodesic, not gravity. · GA p.101 (pdf 118)

**Why it works:** Memorable reversal of intuition that motivates caring about geodesics at all.

**App idea:** Accelerometer split-screen of standing versus falling world lines on a spacetime diagram.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Example 9.1 attributes the sphere equations of motion to Exercise 8.1, but that exercise concerns shortest paths in flat space in cylindrical polar coordinates; the sphere equations are derived in Example 8.6 (eqns 8.19-8.20).** — A reader who looks back finds the wrong problem and may doubt the equations. *Suggestion:* Cite Example 8.6 and show the expansion of d/dlambda(sin^2 theta phidot) = 0 into the second of eqns 9.6.
- **In eqn 9.9 the printed dL/dr has a^2 r (dr/dlambda) without the square.** — Following the print leads to a dimensionally inconsistent radial equation that does not match 9.10. *Suggestion:* Use a^2 r rdot^2 + r thetadot^2 inside the bracket.
- **Eqn 9.23 shows d/dtau of dL/dx^mu where the velocity derivative with free index nu is meant; eqn 9.21-9.22 write 1/L although L contains the factor -m.** — Index and sign mismatches in the one general derivation of the chapter undermine confidence in index manipulation. *Suggestion:* Write the denominator explicitly as (-g_{alpha beta} xdot^alpha xdot^beta)^{1/2}, normalize that factor to 1, and keep nu as the free index throughout.
- **Eqn 9.28's middle term is printed as d g_{sigma nu}/dx^nu instead of d g_{sigma nu}/dx^mu.** — Copying it gives a wrong Christoffel formula with a doubled index. *Suggestion:* Use the comma form of 9.27 and check against the symmetric result 9.29.
- **The raising step from eqn 9.28 to the standard geodesic equation (contract with g^{rho nu}) is never written, yet Example 9.5 depends on it.** — Learners may not see how 9.29 gives Gamma with an upper index. *Suggestion:* Display the raised-index version explicitly: contract the inverse metric with the three-term bracket of eqn 9.29 to get the upper-index coefficients.
- **Exercise 9.2's answer 9.36 prints sin v divided by u instead of by a.** — Correct work appears to disagree with the book. *Suggestion:* Give Gamma^v_{uu} = (sin v/a)(c + a cos v) and verify with the diagonal shortcut 9.32.
- **The recipe relies on L = 1, which is impossible for null curves (L = 0); photons are not addressed here and the fix appears only implicitly in Exercise 9.9.** — Learners may try to apply Steps I-V to light and get division by zero. *Suggestion:* Introduce the quadratic Lagrangian (1/2) g xdot xdot with an affine parameter as the universal route, noting it covers null geodesics.
- **Why L = 1 may be imposed after differentiation is asserted rather than explained (reparametrization invariance means the Euler-Lagrange system is underdetermined until a parameter is chosen).** — The step feels like a trick and invites the error of normalizing too early. *Suggestion:* Show explicitly that the square-root Lagrangian is invariant under lambda -> f(lambda) and that the extra terms in the equations are proportional to dL/dlambda, which vanish for L = constant.
- **Half-plane geodesics include vertical lines x = constant (the xdot = 0 case, or radius a -> infinity), which the exercise and figure omit.** — Learners may think two points directly above one another have no geodesic. *Suggestion:* Show the vertical line as the limit of ever larger semicircles in the interactive.
- **Exercise 9.4 commentary describes an observer with v = 0 'undergoing' acceleration -1/x without distinguishing free-fall acceleration from the thrust needed to hover; Exercise 9.8 reuses a and t for different meanings.** — Confusion over whether the Rindler observer is free or accelerated, and over symbols in the length integral. *Suggestion:* State that a free particle momentarily at rest falls toward x = 0 at 1/x while a hovering observer needs proper acceleration 1/x outward; rename integration limits t_1, t_2.
- **The opening links Gamma to curvature without warning that nonzero Gamma can arise from coordinates alone.** — Learners conclude that polar coordinates or rotating frames describe curved space. *Suggestion:* Pair Example 9.2's a = 0 limit and Exercise 9.10 with an explicit statement that curvature needs the Riemann tensor.
- **Minor inconsistencies: Example 9.5 says it uses eqn 9.28 though it applies 9.29; footnote 7 calls the recipe a five-point method; eqn 9.31 uses sqrt(-g) although the preceding examples are Riemannian.** — Small frictions when cross-referencing or applying 9.31 to a sphere. *Suggestion:* Refer to 9.29, call it the five-step recipe, and write sqrt|g| for general metrics.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'If all you know about a surface is how to measure short distances on it, can you predict how a straight-ahead path will look in your coordinates?' Then reveal that the answer is the pipeline metric -> line element -> Gamma.
- Before running the recipe, ask the learner to predict which Gamma are nonzero by noting which metric components depend on which coordinates; confirm afterwards.
- Suggested order: Euler-Lagrange refresher -> plane polars (Exercise 9.1) as warm-up -> sphere -> paraboloid with the a -> 0 check -> half plane -> Example 9.4 derivation -> Example 9.5 using the formula -> diagonal shortcuts -> quadratic Lagrangian.
- Check the factor of two: give phiddot + 2 cot theta thetadot phidot = 0 and ask for Gamma^phi_{theta phi}; the answer is cot theta, not 2 cot theta.
- Common question 'Why can we set L = 1 only after differentiating?': the length Lagrangian does not care how the curve is parametrized, so its equations leave the parameter free; choosing lambda = s is fixing that freedom on the solution, and doing it before differentiation destroys the information in the derivatives.
- Common question 'Does nonzero Gamma mean gravity or curvature?': no; show plane polar or rotating-frame coefficients in flat space and point ahead to the Riemann tensor in Chapter 11.
- Common question 'What about light?': the square-root Lagrangian vanishes on null curves; switch to (1/2) g_{mu nu} xdot^mu xdot^nu with an affine parameter (Exercise 9.9).
- When a learner reproduces the book's derivations, pre-empt the printed slips: square missing in 9.9, dL/dx should be dL/dxdot in 9.23, derivative index in 9.28 should be mu, and 9.36 should divide by a.
- For novices, treat §9.2 as optional and let them use 9.29 or the diagonal shortcuts as a tool; for advanced learners, derive 9.29 a second way from metric compatibility plus symmetry (Chapter 7 route) and compare.
- Rindler exercise: make sure the learner separates free fall (accelerates toward x = 0 at 1/x) from hovering (needs thrust 1/x); a vivid number is that 1 g places you about one light-year from the Rindler horizon.
- Half plane: after Exercise 9.8, ask what happens as the semicircle radius grows without bound (vertical geodesics) and why the boundary cannot be reached; the length is ln tan(t/2) up to endpoints.
- Exercise 9.11 check: Gamma^r_{rr} = 1/(2(1 + r)); the given curve has (1 + r) rdot^2 = 1 and satisfies rddot + Gamma^r_{rr} rdot^2 = 0, so it is a geodesic.
- Example 9.5 extension for curious learners: the metric (dx^2 - dt^2)/t^2 is 2D de Sitter space in conformal time (our identification, not stated in the book), linking forward to cosmology chapters.
