# GA ch21 · The Schwarzschild geometry

> States the Schwarzschild line element up front, then justifies it: a static, spherically symmetric, asymptotically flat ansatz with two unknown functions Phi(r) and Lambda(r) is fed through the Einstein equation using orthonormal-frame curvature components; the tt equation defines an enclosed-mass function m(r), the rr equation fixes Phi, the exterior reduces to 1-2M/r, the same machinery yields the TOV equations of relativistic stellar structure, and finally the coordinates r and t are given operational meaning (circumferential radius, clock at infinity).

**Pages:** printed 229–236 · pdf 246–253 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

The logical path (ansatz, two field equations, integrate) is short and cleanly motivated, and the physical naming of Phi and m(r) helps a lot. But the curvature components are quoted rather than derived, the sum rules and orthonormal-frame index moves require comfort with ch10-ch11, and the TOV example compresses conservation of T into a pointer to ch39. Two printed errors (missing minus sign in 21.28, missing square root in 21.35), the omission of 'vacuum' from the Birkhoff summary, and several notational collisions (R, exponent conventions in Exercise 21.7) can trip careful readers.

## Role in the book

Opening GR chapter of Part IV (Orbits, stars, and black holes), placed directly after the purely Newtonian orbit chapter 20. It cashes in the Part II toolkit (connection coefficients from Exercise 9.7, orthonormal frames and vielbeins from ch10, Riemann components from the ch11 exercises, the perfect-fluid energy-momentum tensor of ch12 and the field equation of ch13) on the first exact solution, and supplies the metric that ch22-ch29 use for motion, orbits, light bending, black holes, Kruskal coordinates and Hawking radiation. The light-cone structure at r = 2M is explicitly deferred to the black-hole chapters, the curvature computation itself is deferred to Cartan's method in Part V (ch36), and conservation of T for the fluid is deferred to ch39, consistent with the book's spiral design.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can write down the Schwarzschild line element in geometrized and SI units and convert between them with M -> GM/c^2, t -> ct.
- Reader can list the three structural properties of the metric (static, asymptotically flat, ill-behaved at r = 0 and r = 2M) and state Birkhoff's theorem with its vacuum proviso.
- Reader can justify the general static spherically symmetric ansatz with two functions Phi(r), Lambda(r), including why a third function multiplying dOmega^2 can be absorbed by relabelling r.
- Reader can identify Phi with the Newtonian potential by expanding exp(2Phi) and comparing with the weak-field g_tt.
- Reader can compute Einstein-tensor components from orthonormal-frame Riemann components using the sectional-curvature sum rules.
- Reader can derive m(r) = integral of 4 pi r^2 rho from the tt field equation and interpret it as the mass enclosed within circumferential radius r.
- Reader can derive dPhi/dr = (m + 4 pi r^3 p)/[r(r - 2m)] from the rr equation and integrate it in vacuum to obtain exp(2Phi) = 1 - 2M/r.
- Reader can explain why the Einstein tensor vanishes outside the star while tidal Riemann components of order M/r^3 survive.
- Reader can assemble the TOV equation, recognise it as Newtonian hydrostatics times three relativistic correction factors, and describe how it is integrated outward to find a star's radius and mass.
- Reader can show that r gives circumferences 2 pi r but that radial proper distance exceeds the coordinate difference, and that static clocks tick at sqrt(1 - 2M/r) times the rate of coordinate time t (the clock at infinity).

## Teaching approach

Answer-first chapter: the metric is displayed on the opening page with its notable features and Birkhoff's theorem, and the rest of the chapter works backwards to earn it. The derivation is an educated guess followed by constraint: begin from flat space in spherical polars, promote each coefficient to a function of r, strip one function by relabelling, and read off physical meaning (Newtonian potential, enclosed mass) at each stage rather than only at the end. Heavy algebra (Riemann components) is quoted from earlier exercises and later chapters so the reader sees the logic of the field equation rather than the index grind. The interior problem is kept alive long enough to produce the TOV equations as a bonus, and the chapter closes by turning coordinates into measurements (tape round a circle, ruler along a radius, clock at infinity), preparing for orbits and black holes.

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, components-first, derivation-heavy, example-driven, spiral-revisit, conversational-informal

**Narrative arc**

1. Historical hook: Einstein doubted exact solutions existed; Schwarzschild found one within weeks while at the Russian front.
2. Answer first: display the line element, list static / asymptotically flat / problematic at r = 0 and 2M, invoke Birkhoff.
3. State assumptions (static, spherically symmetric, asymptotically flat) and start from flat spacetime in spherical polars.
4. Generalize coefficients to functions of r, eliminate one by relabelling the radial coordinate, fix boundary values at infinity.
5. Physical anchor: weak-field comparison identifies Phi with the Newtonian potential.
6. Geometric side: orthonormal-frame Riemann components E, E-bar, F, F-bar and Einstein components via sum rules.
7. Physical side: static perfect fluid confined within radius R; tt equation defines the mass function m(r).
8. rr equation gives the relativistic potential gradient; in vacuum it integrates to Phi = (1/2) ln(1 - 2M/r), completing the metric.
9. Check: substitute back, obtain Riemann components of order M/r^3 and confirm G = 0 in vacuum.
10. Bonus application: conservation of T plus the rr equation gives the TOV equation; interpret correction factors; stellar-evolution aside.
11. Interpretation: r as circumferential radius, radial proper distance larger than dr, t as the time of a clock at infinity.
12. Forward pointer to motion (ch22) and orbits (ch23).

**Signature moves**

- Give the final metric on page one and then justify it, so the derivation is a verification the reader can anticipate.
- Name integration functions so they already carry physical meaning: exp(2Phi) where Phi becomes the Newtonian potential, and 2m(r) chosen so it becomes enclosed mass.
- Work in the orthonormal frame with four shorthand curvature scalars E, E-bar, F, F-bar and compact sum rules for G, turning a tensor calculation into bookkeeping.
- Solve the interior problem in general, then specialise to vacuum by setting p = 0 and m = M, so the exterior metric and the stellar-structure equation come from the same two equations.
- Rewrite the TOV equation as the Newtonian result multiplied by bracketed correction factors so the relativistic content can be read term by term.
- Demote coordinates to labels, then rehabilitate them operationally by computing circumferences, radial distances and clock intervals in Example 21.4.
- Park large side topics (stellar evolution, connection coefficients, index-lowering rules) in margin notes to keep the main line short.

## Section by section

### Chapter opening: the answer and its features — p.229 (pdf 246)

Recounts that Einstein expected no exact solution and used an approximation for Mercury, and that Schwarzschild sent one from the war front in late 1915. Defines the Schwarzschild solution as the exterior field of a spherically symmetric mass, warns that the interior solution differs, displays the line element in G = c = 1, and lists its features: independence from t, flatness at large r, and misbehaviour at r = 0 and r = 2M. Birkhoff's theorem is quoted to say that spherical symmetry in vacuum forces this form even without assuming staticity. A margin box repeats the metric with G and c restored and remarks that these coordinates are tied to the rest frame of the central mass.

**Concepts:** Schwarzschild metric, static spacetime, asymptotic flatness, Birkhoff's theorem, geometrized units, exterior versus interior solution

**Key moves**
- Historical framing: exact solution arrived unexpectedly fast
- Metric stated before any derivation
- Three structural features read off by inspection
- Birkhoff's theorem makes the solution unique, not just convenient
- Light-cone behaviour at r = 2M postponed to the black-hole chapters

### §21.1 Justifying the solution — p.230 (pdf 247)

Lists the assumptions (metric components independent of t, spherical symmetry with static worldlines orthogonal to constant-t slices, flatness at infinity) and begins from Minkowski space in spherical polars. Each coefficient is replaced by an unknown function of r, giving three functions; the one multiplying the angular part is absorbed by redefining the radial label, leaving exp(2Phi) and exp(2Lambda) with both exponents vanishing at infinity. Expanding exp(2Phi) to first order and comparing with the weak-field metric shows Phi plays the role of the Newtonian potential, and a margin note turns this into a weak-field criterion.

**Concepts:** general static spherically symmetric metric, static spacetime, spherical symmetry, asymptotic flatness, coordinate freedom, weak-field limit

**Key moves**
- Start from flat spacetime obeying the same symmetries
- Promote g_tt, g_rr, g_thetatheta to functions of r only
- Relabel R(r) -> r to reduce three unknowns to two
- Impose Phi(infinity) = Lambda(infinity) = 0
- Match exp(2Phi) ~ 1 + 2Phi to the weak-field g_tt

### §21.2 Components of the Riemann tensor — p.231 (pdf 248)

Builds the geometric side of the field equation in the static observers' orthonormal frame (vielbeins exp(Phi), exp(Lambda), r, r sin theta). Example 21.1 quotes the six non-zero Riemann components in terms of four functions E, E-bar, F, F-bar of Phi, Lambda and their r-derivatives (derivations deferred to the ch11 exercises and ch36), and combines them into the diagonal Einstein components using sum rules given in a margin note. The section stresses that G vanishes in vacuum even though R need not.

**Concepts:** orthonormal frame of static observers, Riemann tensor components for static spherical symmetry, Einstein tensor from sectional curvatures, vacuum Einstein equation

**Key moves**
- Choose the orthonormal frame so indices move with eta
- Package curvature into E, E-bar, F, F-bar
- Use sum rules: G^0_0 is minus the sum of the three spatial-plane components
- Distinguish vanishing G from vanishing R

### §21.3 A gravitating object — p.232 (pdf 249)

Supplies the physical side: a static perfect-fluid ball of total mass M and radius R. The tt field equation becomes a total derivative, motivating the definition r(1 - exp(-2Lambda)) = 2m(r); then dm/dr = 4 pi r^2 rho, so m(r) is the enclosed mass. The rr equation gives the relativistic potential gradient (m + 4 pi r^3 p)/[r(r - 2m)], which reduces to Newton's m/r^2 in the weak limit. Outside the star p = 0 and m = M, so Phi integrates to (1/2) ln(1 - 2M/r) and the Schwarzschild metric follows. Example 21.2 substitutes back to get Riemann components proportional to M/r^3 and a vanishing G.

**Concepts:** perfect fluid energy-momentum tensor, mass function m(r), relativistic potential gradient, Schwarzschild metric, tidal curvature of Schwarzschild spacetime, vacuum Einstein equation, Newtonian limit

**Key moves**
- Write G_tt = 8 pi rho as (1/r^2) d/dr[r(1 - exp(-2Lambda))]
- Define 2m(r) as the bracket and integrate to enclosed mass
- Use G_rr = 8 pi p with exp(-2Lambda) = 1 - 2m/r to isolate dPhi/dr
- Specialise to r > R: p = 0, m = M
- Integrate with Phi(infinity) = 0 to get g_tt = -(1 - 2M/r) and g_rr = 1/g_tt magnitude
- Verify by computing E, E-bar, F, F-bar = 2M/r^3, -M/r^3, 2M/r^3, -M/r^3

### Example 21.3: Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff stellar structure — p.233 (pdf 250)

Reuses the interior equations: conservation of the perfect-fluid T in this geometry gives dp/dr = -(p + rho) dPhi/dr, and inserting the relativistic dPhi/dr produces the TOV pressure equation, paired with the enclosed-mass integral with m(0) = 0. With an equation of state the pair is integrated outward until pressure vanishes, marking the surface R and total mass m(R). Rewritten per shell, the pressure equation is the Newtonian balance times three brackets; these are negligible when pressure is tiny compared with baryonic density but steepen the pressure gradient in massive stars, shrinking them relative to Newtonian expectations.

**Concepts:** Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff equation, relativistic hydrostatic equilibrium, equation of state, Newtonian hydrostatic equilibrium, mass function m(r)

**Key moves**
- Divergence-free T gives the pressure-gradient balance with inertia (rho + p)
- Combine with eqn 21.19
- Fix m(0) = 0 and integrate outward to p(R) = 0
- Factor into Newtonian term times three correction brackets
- Interpret: pressure and compactness make gravity effectively stronger

### Margin essay: a sketch of stellar evolution — p.234 (pdf 251)

A long margin note gives the life-cycle picture later chapters rely on: gravitational collapse halted by fusion, core hydrogen exhaustion and a red-giant phase, collapse to a white dwarf supported by electron degeneracy (Pauli exclusion forcing electrons into ever higher levels), and, above roughly 1.5 solar masses, collapse to a neutron star supported by neutron degeneracy or to a black hole. Pulsars, found by Jocelyn Bell Burnell in 1967, are identified with spinning neutron stars.

**Concepts:** stellar evolution endpoints, degeneracy pressure

**Key moves**
- Each stage is an equilibrium between gravity and a different pressure source
- Degeneracy pressure explained via filled quantum levels
- Mass threshold decides white dwarf versus neutron star or black hole

### §21.4 The meaning of the coordinates — p.234 (pdf 251)

Having warned that coordinates are only labels, the section attaches measurements to them. Example 21.4 integrates the metric round an equatorial circle at fixed t to get circumference 2 pi r, so r is a circumferential radius, but shows that proper distance along a radial line is an integral of (1 - 2M/r)^(-1/2) dr and so exceeds the coordinate difference; r is therefore not distance from the centre. For time, the proper interval of a clock at fixed position shrinks relative to Delta t and matches it as r -> infinity, so t is the time kept by a distant static observer. Points ahead to motion and orbits.

**Concepts:** circumferential radial coordinate, proper radial distance, gravitational time dilation, observer at infinity, coordinate freedom

**Key moves**
- Set dt = 0, theta = pi/2, integrate r dphi to get 2 pi r
- Set dt = dtheta = dphi = 0 and integrate sqrt(g_rr) dr
- Compare static-clock proper time with Delta t and take r -> infinity

### Chapter summary and exercises — p.235 (pdf 252)

Four-bullet summary restating the metric, its three features and Birkhoff's theorem. Eight exercises: perfect-fluid T in coordinate basis, verifying the Einstein components and their vacuum vanishing, proving the Riemann-to-Einstein sum rules, the rr-equation algebra and its Newtonian limit, area and circumference at fixed r, Newtonian hydrostatic equilibrium, a guided proof of Birkhoff's theorem from a time-dependent ansatz, and a vacuum-energy universe whose static line element turns out to be de Sitter space.

**Concepts:** Birkhoff's theorem, Newtonian hydrostatic equilibrium, de Sitter spacetime in static coordinates, Einstein tensor from sectional curvatures

**Key moves**
- Birkhoff proof: remove dr dt cross term, show G_rt = 0 forces Lambda time-independent, absorb f(t) by rescaling t
- Vacuum energy rho = -p gives Phi = -Lambda and 1 - H^2 r^2 coefficients

## Concepts

### Schwarzschild metric

*solution-or-model · core* · also: Schwarzschild solution, Schwarzschild line element, Schwarzschild geometry

The unique vacuum solution of the Einstein equation outside a spherically symmetric mass M: g_tt = -(1 - 2M/r), g_rr = (1 - 2M/r)^(-1), with the angular part of flat space r^2 dOmega^2, in units G = c = 1.

**How introduced:** Stated on the first page as the answer, then re-derived in §21.1-21.3 from a two-function ansatz fed through G = 8 pi T with vacuum conditions p = rho = 0 and m = M beyond the stellar surface.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein field equation, general static spherically symmetric metric, asymptotic flatness

$$
ds^2 = -\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)dt^2 + \left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}dr^2 + r^2 d\Omega^2
$$

**Notes:** Valid only outside the matter (r > R). Equation appears three times: 21.1, 21.23, 21.36.

**Where:** GA p.229 (pdf 246); GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247); GA §21.3 p.233 (pdf 250); GA p.235 (pdf 252)

### static spacetime

*definition · developed* · also: static metric

A spacetime admitting a time coordinate in which no metric component depends on t and there are no dt dx^i cross terms, so the constant-t slices are orthogonal to the worldlines of observers at fixed spatial coordinates; intervals between such observers do not change in time.

**How introduced:** Listed as feature (i) of the metric and then imposed as an assumption in §21.1, phrased as time-independent components plus fixed-(r, theta, phi) worldlines orthogonal to constant-t hypersurfaces.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, hypersurface

$$
\partial_t g_{\alpha\beta}=0,\quad g_{ti}=0
$$

**Notes:** The book attributes the orthogonality to spherical symmetry; strictly it is the extra condition that distinguishes static from merely stationary.

**Where:** GA p.229 (pdf 246); GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247)

### spherical symmetry

*definition · developed*

Invariance of the geometry under rotations about a centre, so that spacetime is foliated by 2-spheres whose metric is (areal radius)^2 dOmega^2 and all metric functions depend only on a radial label (and possibly t).

**How introduced:** Used to restrict the unknowns: the flat metric's coefficients are allowed to vary only with r, and the angular part keeps its round-sphere form multiplied by R(r)^2.

**Prerequisites:** line element, spherical polar coordinates

$$
d\Omega^2 = d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2
$$

**Where:** GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247)

### asymptotic flatness

*definition · developed* · also: flat at infinity

The requirement that the metric approaches the Minkowski metric as r -> infinity, so gravitational effects vanish far from the source; here it fixes the integration constants Phi(infinity) = Lambda(infinity) = 0.

**How introduced:** Feature (ii) of the metric on the opening page; then imposed in §21.1 as a boundary condition on the two metric functions and used when integrating dPhi/dr.

**Prerequisites:** Minkowski metric

$$
\Phi(\infty)=\Lambda(\infty)=0
$$

**Where:** GA p.229 (pdf 246); GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247); GA §21.3 p.233 (pdf 250)

### Birkhoff's theorem

*theorem · introduced*

Any spherically symmetric solution of the vacuum Einstein equation is (locally) the Schwarzschild solution; in particular the exterior of a spherically symmetric body is static and asymptotically flat even if the body itself pulsates or collapses.

**How introduced:** Quoted on the opening page as the reason the solution is so special, restated in the chapter summary, and proved step by step in Exercise 21.7 from a time-dependent ansatz.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild metric, spherical symmetry, vacuum Einstein equation

$$
\Phi(r,t) = -\Lambda(r) + f(t),\quad \Lambda = -\tfrac12\ln\left|1-\tfrac{2M}{r}\right|
$$

**Notes:** The chapter summary and Exercise 21.7(e) omit the word vacuum; staticity holds only where r > 2M.

**Where:** GA p.229 (pdf 246); GA p.235 (pdf 252); GA p.236 (pdf 253)

### general static spherically symmetric metric

*technique · core* · also: Phi-Lambda ansatz, spherically symmetric line element

The most general static, spherically symmetric line element written with the areal radius as coordinate: ds^2 = -exp(2Phi(r)) dt^2 + exp(2Lambda(r)) dr^2 + r^2 dOmega^2, the exponential form guaranteeing the signs of g_tt and g_rr.

**How introduced:** Obtained by taking flat spacetime in spherical polars, letting the three independent coefficients become functions of r, and absorbing the angular function by relabelling the radial coordinate.

**Prerequisites:** spherical symmetry, static spacetime, coordinate freedom

$$
ds^2 = -e^{2\Phi(r)}dt^2 + e^{2\Lambda(r)}dr^2 + r^2 d\Omega^2
$$

**Notes:** Same form used for the stellar interior; also the starting point of ch11 Exercise 11.1 and ch9 Exercise 9.7.

**Where:** GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247); GA §21.2 p.231 (pdf 248)

### coordinate freedom

*principle · revisited* · also: coordinates have no intrinsic metric significance, relabelling coordinates

Coordinates are arbitrary labels on events; physical meaning comes only from intervals computed with the metric, so any smooth invertible relabelling (such as replacing a radial label by the areal radius) describes the same geometry.

**How introduced:** Invoked to justify naming the coordinates t, r, theta, phi only provisionally, to absorb R(r) into r (margin note 6), and again at the start of §21.4 before operational meanings are extracted.

**Prerequisites:** line element

$$
R(r)\to r
$$

**Where:** GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247); GA §21.4 p.234 (pdf 251)

### weak-field limit

*principle · revisited* · also: Newtonian limit

The regime where the metric deviates slightly from Minkowski, |2Phi/c^2| << 1, in which g_tt = -(1 + 2Phi) with Phi the Newtonian potential and the field equation reduces to Newtonian gravity.

**How introduced:** Used in §21.1 to interpret Phi by expanding exp(2Phi), in margin note 7 as an operational weak-field criterion, and in margin note 13 where the relativistic potential gradient reduces to m/r^2.

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian gravitational potential, general static spherically symmetric metric

$$
-e^{2\Phi}\approx-(1+2\Phi)
$$

$$
\frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\ll1
$$

$$
\frac{d\Phi}{dr}\approx\frac{m}{r^2}
$$

**Where:** GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247); GA §21.3 p.232 (pdf 249)

### geometrized units

*convention · revisited* · also: G = c = 1 units, natural units

Units in which G = c = 1, so mass, time and length all carry the dimension of length; a mass M corresponds to the length GM/c^2 and a time t to the length ct.

**How introduced:** Declared in margin note 2 for Part IV; a margin box then gives the SI form of the metric with explicit G and c.

$$
M\to GM/c^2,\quad t\to ct
$$

**Where:** GA p.229 (pdf 246); GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247)

### Schwarzschild coordinates

*convention · introduced* · also: static coordinates

The coordinates (t, r, theta, phi) of the Schwarzschild line element: t is the static time adapted to the rest frame of the central mass, r is the areal radius, and theta, phi are the usual angles.

**How introduced:** A margin box on pdf 247 contrasts them with the comoving coordinates of the cosmology part, justifying the difference by the r-dependence of g_tt and by the fact that they single out the rest frame of the central mass. (The physical gloss that fixed-r observers are not freely falling is ours, not the book's.)

**Prerequisites:** static spacetime, circumferential radial coordinate

**Where:** GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247); GA §21.4 p.234 (pdf 251)

### orthonormal frame of static observers

*technique · revisited* · also: static tetrad, stationary orthonormal frame, vielbein for Schwarzschild

The local Lorentz frame carried by an observer at fixed (r, theta, phi), with basis vectors obtained by normalising the coordinate basis; its vielbein components are exp(Phi), exp(Lambda), r and r sin theta, and hatted indices are raised and lowered with eta.

**How introduced:** Chosen in §21.2 to simplify the curvature calculation; margin note 8 lists the vielbein components and justifies calling these observers stationary because they are at rest relative to the mass.

**Prerequisites:** vielbein, Minkowski metric

$$
(e_t)^{\hat t}=e^{\Phi},\ (e_r)^{\hat r}=e^{\Lambda},\ (e_\theta)^{\hat\theta}=r,\ (e_\phi)^{\hat\phi}=r\sin\theta
$$

**Where:** GA §21.2 p.231 (pdf 248)

### Riemann tensor components for static spherical symmetry

*mathematical-object · core* · also: E, E-bar, F, F-bar curvature functions

In the static orthonormal frame the only independent non-zero Riemann components are those in the six coordinate planes, expressible via four functions: E (t-r plane), E-bar (t-angular planes), F (theta-phi plane) and F-bar (r-angular planes).

**How introduced:** Quoted in Example 21.1 for the Phi-Lambda metric (derivation delegated to ch11 exercises and ch36), then evaluated for Schwarzschild in Example 21.2.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann curvature tensor, orthonormal frame of static observers, general static spherically symmetric metric

$$
E=-e^{-2\Lambda}(\Phi''+\Phi'^2-\Phi'\Lambda')
$$

$$
\bar E=-\tfrac1r e^{-2\Lambda}\Phi'
$$

$$
F=\tfrac1{r^2}(1-e^{-2\Lambda})
$$

$$
\bar F=\tfrac1r e^{-2\Lambda}\Lambda'
$$

**Where:** GA §21.2 p.231 (pdf 248); GA §21.3 p.233 (pdf 250)

### Einstein tensor from sectional curvatures

*identity · developed* · also: sum rules for G, Sidenote 10 rules

In an orthonormal frame the diagonal Einstein components are minus the sum of the Riemann components R^{ij}_{ij} for the three coordinate planes not containing that index, and off-diagonal components are sums of mixed components; for instance the time-time Einstein component is the negative of the summed curvatures of the x-y, y-z and z-x planes.

**How introduced:** Offered in margin note 10 as the fastest way from Riemann to Einstein components, with the proof set as Exercise 21.3.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein tensor, Ricci tensor, Riemann curvature tensor

$$
G^0{}_0=-(R^{12}{}_{12}+R^{23}{}_{23}+R^{31}{}_{31})
$$

$$
G^0{}_1=R^{02}{}_{12}+R^{03}{}_{13}
$$

**Notes:** G_00 is the sum of spatial sectional curvatures, a useful geometric reading not stated in the book.

**Where:** GA §21.2 p.231 (pdf 248); GA p.235 (pdf 252)

### Einstein field equation

*law · revisited* · also: Einstein equation, G = 8 pi T

G_{mu nu} = 8 pi T_{mu nu} (G = c = 1): the Einstein tensor built from curvature equals 8 pi times the energy-momentum tensor.

**How introduced:** Used as a machine: the candidate metric provides G (left side) and a static perfect fluid provides T (right side); component by component this yields ODEs for Lambda and Phi.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein tensor, energy-momentum tensor

$$
\mathbf G = 8\pi\mathbf T
$$

**Where:** GA §21.1 p.231 (pdf 248); GA §21.3 p.232 (pdf 249)

### vacuum Einstein equation

*principle · developed* · also: G = 0 in vacuum, Ricci-flat

Where T = 0 the field equation demands G = 0 (equivalently Ricci = 0), but the Riemann tensor, which encodes tidal effects, can still be non-zero, so empty space around a mass is curved.

**How introduced:** Stressed after eqn 21.10 and confirmed in Example 21.2, where all Einstein components vanish while Riemann components of order M/r^3 remain.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein field equation, Riemann curvature tensor

$$
\mathbf T = 0 \Rightarrow \mathbf G = 0,\ \mathbf R\neq 0
$$

**Where:** GA §21.2 p.231 (pdf 248); GA §21.3 p.233 (pdf 250)

### tidal curvature of Schwarzschild spacetime

*phenomenon · developed* · also: Schwarzschild Riemann components

Outside a spherical mass the orthonormal Riemann components are of size M/r^3: R_{trtr} = -2M/r^3 (radial stretching), R_{t theta t theta} = R_{t phi t phi} = M/r^3 (transverse squeezing), with spatial components 2M/r^3 and -M/r^3; they match the Newtonian tidal tensor GM/r^3 diag(-2, 1, 1).

**How introduced:** Obtained in Example 21.2 by substituting the exterior Phi and Lambda into E, E-bar, F, F-bar and lowering indices with eta (margin note 16).

**Prerequisites:** Riemann tensor components for static spherical symmetry, tidal forces

$$
R_{\hat t\hat r\hat t\hat r}=-\frac{2M}{r^3},\ R_{\hat t\hat\theta\hat t\hat\theta}=\frac{M}{r^3}
$$

**Notes:** Link to the Newtonian tidal tensor is from ch11, not re-made here.

**Where:** GA §21.3 p.233 (pdf 250)

### perfect fluid energy-momentum tensor

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: static perfect fluid

For a fluid at rest in an orthonormal frame, T^{mu nu} = diag(rho, p, p, p) with rho the energy (mass) density and p the isotropic pressure; no shear stresses or heat flow.

**How introduced:** Used as the source for a spherical star; given as a matrix in margin note 11 and converted to coordinate components in Exercise 21.1.

**Prerequisites:** energy-momentum tensor, orthonormal frame of static observers

$$
T^{\hat\mu\hat\nu}=\mathrm{diag}(\rho,p,p,p)
$$

**Where:** GA §21.3 p.232 (pdf 249); GA p.235 (pdf 252)

### mass function m(r)

*physical-quantity · core* · also: enclosed mass, Misner-Sharp mass (for static case)

The function defined by r(1 - exp(-2Lambda)) = 2m(r), satisfying dm/dr = 4 pi r^2 rho; it equals the total mass-energy within areal radius r and becomes the constant M outside the matter.

**How introduced:** Named in §21.3 because the tt field equation is a total derivative; integrating it yields the Newtonian-looking shell integral, justifying the name. Regularity fixes m(0) = 0 in Example 21.3.

**Prerequisites:** general static spherically symmetric metric, Einstein field equation, perfect fluid energy-momentum tensor

$$
r(1-e^{-2\Lambda})=2m(r)
$$

$$
m(r)=\int_0^r 4\pi r'^2\rho\,dr'
$$

$$
e^{2\Lambda}=\left(1-\frac{2m}{r}\right)^{-1}
$$

**Notes:** The integral uses 4 pi r^2 dr rather than the proper volume element 4 pi r^2 exp(Lambda) dr, so m includes gravitational binding energy; the book does not comment.

**Where:** GA §21.3 p.232 (pdf 249); GA §21.3 p.233 (pdf 250)

### relativistic potential gradient

*law · developed* · also: dPhi/dr equation

Inside a static perfect-fluid star the rr field equation gives dPhi/dr = (m + 4 pi r^3 p)/[r(r - 2m)]; compared with Newton's m/r^2, pressure adds to the source and the factor 1/(1 - 2m/r) strengthens the field.

**How introduced:** Derived by substituting exp(-2Lambda) = 1 - 2m/r into G_rr = 8 pi p; in vacuum it reduces to M/[r(r - 2M)] and integrates to the Schwarzschild g_tt.

**Prerequisites:** mass function m(r), Einstein field equation, weak-field limit

$$
\frac{d\Phi}{dr}=\frac{m(r)+4\pi r^3p}{r[r-2m(r)]}
$$

**Where:** GA §21.3 p.232 (pdf 249)

### relativistic hydrostatic equilibrium

*law · introduced* · also: pressure-gradient equation, Euler equation in static spacetime

For a static perfect fluid, conservation of T gives dp/dr = -(rho + p) dPhi/dr: the pressure gradient supports fluid whose effective inertial and gravitational mass density is rho + p.

**How introduced:** Quoted in Example 21.3 as a consequence of the divergence of T vanishing in this geometry, with the proof deferred to Exercise 39.5.

**Prerequisites:** perfect fluid energy-momentum tensor, conservation of energy-momentum

$$
\frac{\partial p}{\partial r}+(p+\rho)\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial r}=0
$$

**Where:** GA §21.3 p.233 (pdf 250)

### Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff equation

*law · introduced* · also: TOV equations, relativistic stellar structure equations

The equations of relativistic hydrostatic equilibrium for a static spherical perfect-fluid star: dp/dr = -(rho + p)(m + 4 pi r^3 p)/[r^2(1 - 2m/r)] together with dm/dr = 4 pi r^2 rho, closed by an equation of state p(rho).

**How introduced:** Built in Example 21.3 by combining the fluid pressure balance with the relativistic potential gradient; the book then rewrites it per shell as Newtonian hydrostatics times three correction brackets.

**Prerequisites:** relativistic hydrostatic equilibrium, relativistic potential gradient, mass function m(r), equation of state

$$
\frac{dp}{dr}=-\frac{(\rho+p)(m+4\pi r^3 p)}{r^2(1-2m/r)}
$$

**Notes:** Printed eqn 21.28 omits the minus sign; eqn 21.30 has it.

**Where:** GA §21.3 p.233 (pdf 250); GA §21.3 p.234 (pdf 251)

### Newtonian hydrostatic equilibrium

*law · introduced* · also: Pascal's law generalised

In Newtonian gravity each spherical shell is supported by the pressure difference across it: 4 pi r^2 dp = -G m dm / r^2 with dm = 4 pi r^2 rho dr, i.e. dp/dr = -G m rho / r^2.

**How introduced:** Given in margin note 18 as the leading factor of the rewritten TOV equation, and set as Exercise 21.6 by generalising the uniform-field hydrostatic pressure law.

**Prerequisites:** mass function m(r)

### exterior versus interior solution

*definition · introduced* · also: interior metric, matching at the stellar surface

The Schwarzschild metric describes only the vacuum region outside the matter; inside, Lambda is fixed by the enclosed mass m(r) and Phi by the TOV-coupled equation, and the two regions join continuously at the surface r = R.

**How introduced:** Signalled on the opening page and realised in §21.3 by solving the equations with general rho and p before specialising to r > R.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild metric, mass function m(r)

### circumferential radial coordinate

*definition · core* · also: areal radius, Schwarzschild r, reduced circumference

The radial coordinate r of the Schwarzschild metric, defined by the property that a circle at fixed r has proper circumference 2 pi r (and a sphere has area 4 pi r^2); it is not the proper distance from the centre.

**How introduced:** Example 21.4 integrates the metric around an equatorial circle at an instant; Exercise 21.5 extends this to the area of a sphere via the metric determinant.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild metric, coordinate freedom

C=\int_0^{2\pi}r\,d\phi=2\pi r

### proper radial distance

*physical-quantity · developed*

The distance measured by rulers laid along a radial line at fixed t: integral of (1 - 2M/r)^(-1/2) dr, which is always larger than the difference in r outside r = 2M.

**How introduced:** Contrasted with the circumference result in Example 21.4 to warn that r is not a distance from the origin.

**Where:** GA §21.4 p.234 (pdf 251)

### gravitational time dilation

*phenomenon · developed* · also: static clock rate

A clock held at fixed r in the Schwarzschild geometry records proper time dtau = sqrt(1 - 2M/r) dt, so it runs slow relative to coordinate time; the effect vanishes at large r and becomes extreme as r -> 2M.

**How introduced:** Derived in §21.4 by setting dr = dtheta = dphi = 0 in the line element (the printed result drops the square root).

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild metric, proper time

$$
\Delta\tau=\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{1/2}\Delta t
$$

**Notes:** Developed physically as gravitational redshift in ch22 §22.2.

**Where:** GA §21.4 p.234 (pdf 251)

A static observer at arbitrarily large r, whose proper time equals the Schwarzschild coordinate t; used as the standard reference for comparing clock rates and energies.

### Schwarzschild radius

The radius r = 2M (2GM/c^2 in SI units) at which g_tt vanishes and g_rr diverges in Schwarzschild coordinates; later shown to be a horizon and a coordinate rather than curvature singularity.

### stellar evolution endpoints

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: white dwarf, neutron star, red giant, pulsar, planetary nebula

The sequence by which a star exhausts nuclear fuel and settles into a compact remnant supported by degeneracy pressure (white dwarf, neutron star) or collapses to a black hole when no pressure can halt gravity.

**How introduced:** Margin essay on pdf 251 giving later chapters a simple picture; pulsars are cited as observed neutron stars.

**Prerequisites:** degeneracy pressure, Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff equation

**Where:** GA §21.3 p.234 (pdf 251)

### degeneracy pressure

*phenomenon · mention* · also: electron degeneracy, neutron degeneracy

Pressure of a dense fermion gas arising because the Pauli principle forces particles into high-momentum states once low levels are filled; it persists at zero temperature and supports white dwarfs (electrons) and neutron stars (neutrons).

**How introduced:** The stellar-evolution margin note explains it qualitatively: once every low-lying quantum level is taken, the Pauli principle pushes further electrons (or neutrons) to higher energies, so squeezing the matter costs energy and it resists compression.

**Prerequisites:** Pauli exclusion principle

**Where:** GA §21.3 p.234 (pdf 251)

### de Sitter spacetime in static coordinates

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: static de Sitter patch

The vacuum-energy solution ds^2 = -(1 - H^2 r^2) dt^2 + (1 - H^2 r^2)^(-1) dr^2 + r^2 dOmega^2, with H^2 = 8 pi zeta/3 for energy density zeta, equivalent to the exponentially expanding Universe 1 of ch15.

**Where:** GA p.236 (pdf 253)

$$

### Schwarzschild metric components (margin note 3) · supporting · GA p.229 (pdf 246)

Non-zero components read off from the line element; the metric is diagonal.

### (21.2) Schwarzschild metric in SI units · supporting · GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247)

$$
ds^{2}=-\left(1-\frac{2GM}{c^{2}r}\right)c^{2}dt^{2}+\left(1-\frac{2GM}{c^{2}r}\right)^{-1}dr^{2}+r^{2}\left(d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,d\phi^{2}\right)

Same metric with G and c restored; the dimensionless combination 2GM/(c^2 r) measures field strength.

### (21.3) Flat spacetime in spherical polars · derivation-step · GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247)

$$
ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2},\quad d\Omega^2=d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2

### (21.4) Three-function ansatz · derivation-step · GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247)

$$
ds^{2}=-e^{2\Phi(r)}dt^{2}+e^{2\Lambda(r)}dr^{2}+R(r)^{2}d\Omega^{2}

Each flat coefficient replaced by a function of r only, as allowed by staticity and spherical symmetry.

**Symbols:** Phi, Lambda, R unknown functions (R here is not the stellar radius)

### (21.5) General static spherically symmetric line element · central · GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247)

$$
ds^{2}=-e^{2\Phi(r)}dt^{2}+e^{2\Lambda(r)}dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2},\quad \Phi(\infty)=\Lambda(\infty)=0

After relabelling R(r) as r, two unknown functions remain; boundary conditions encode asymptotic flatness. Restated as (21.7) in Example 21.1.

**Symbols:** Phi(r), Lambda(r) metric functions

### (21.6) Weak-field metric (time part) in spherical coordinates · supporting · GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247)

$$
ds^{2}=-(1+2\Phi)dt^{2}+dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2}

Comparing with exp(2Phi) ~ 1 + 2Phi identifies the metric function Phi with the Newtonian potential in weak fields.

**Symbols:** Phi Newtonian gravitational potential

$$
\frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\ll 1

Operational definition of a weak field: the expansion of exp(2Phi) is valid when the potential is small compared with c^2 (in magnitude).

### Static-observer vielbein components (margin note 8) · supporting · GA §21.2 p.231 (pdf 248)

$$
(e_t)^{\hat t}=e^{\Phi},\quad (e_r)^{\hat r}=e^{\Lambda},\quad (e_\theta)^{\hat\theta}=r,\quad (e_\phi)^{\hat\phi}=r\sin\theta

Converts coordinate components to the orthonormal frame of observers at rest relative to the mass.

**Symbols:** hats denote orthonormal-frame indices

### (21.8) Orthonormal Riemann components · central · GA §21.2 p.231 (pdf 248)

$$
R^{\hat t\hat r}{}_{\hat t\hat r}=E,\ R^{\hat t\hat\theta}{}_{\hat t\hat\theta}=R^{\hat t\hat\phi}{}_{\hat t\hat\phi}=\bar E,\ R^{\hat\theta\hat\phi}{}_{\hat\theta\hat\phi}=F,\ R^{\hat r\hat\theta}{}_{\hat r\hat\theta}=\bar F,\ R^{\hat\phi\hat r}{}_{\hat r\hat\phi}=-\bar F

All curvature of a static spherically symmetric metric lies in the six coordinate planes and is captured by four functions.

### (21.9) Curvature functions · central · GA §21.2 p.231 (pdf 248)

$$
E=-e^{-2\Lambda}\left(\Phi''+\Phi'^{2}-\Phi'\Lambda'\right),\ \bar E=-\frac{1}{r}e^{-2\Lambda}\Phi',\ F=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\left(1-e^{-2\Lambda}\right),\ \bar F=\frac{1}{r}e^{-2\Lambda}\Lambda'

Explicit dependence of the curvature on the metric functions; F depends only on Lambda (spatial geometry) while E involves the second derivative of Phi.

**Symbols:** prime = d/dr

### (21.10) Orthonormal Einstein components · central · GA §21.2 p.231 (pdf 248)

$$
G^{\hat t}{}_{\hat t}=-G_{\hat t\hat t}=-(F+2\bar F),\quad G^{\hat r}{}_{\hat r}=G_{\hat r\hat r}=-(F+2\bar E),\quad G^{\hat\theta}{}_{\hat\theta}=G^{\hat\phi}{}_{\hat\phi}=-(E+\bar E+\bar F)

Diagonal Einstein tensor built from the curvature functions; these are the left-hand sides of the field equations.

ds^{2}=-e^{\Phi(r,t)}dt^{2}+e^{\Lambda(r,t)}dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2}

After removing the cross term. The printed exponents lack the factor 2, whereas (21.40) is written for exp(2Phi), exp(2Lambda).

### (21.40) Einstein components for time-dependent spherical symmetry · supporting · GA p.235 (pdf 252)

G_{\hat t\hat t}=r^{-2}(1-e^{-2\Lambda})+\frac{2\Lambda_{,r}}{r}e^{-2\Lambda},\ G_{\hat r\hat t}=\frac{2\Lambda_{,t}}{r}e^{-(\Lambda+\Phi)},\ G_{\hat r\hat r}=\frac{2\Phi_{,r}}{r}e^{-2\Lambda}+r^{-2}(e^{-2\Lambda}-1),\ G_{\hat\theta\hat\theta}=G_{\hat\phi\hat\phi}=\left(\Phi_{,rr}+\Phi_{,r}^{2}-\Phi_{,r}\Lambda_{,r}+\frac{\Phi_{,r}-\Lambda_{,r}}{r}\right)e^{-2\Lambda}-\left(\Lambda_{,tt}+\Lambda_{,t}^{2}-\Phi_{,t}\Lambda_{,t}\right)e^{-2\Phi}
$$

Quoted from Exercise 36.8; the G_rt component forces Lambda to be time-independent in vacuum, the key step of Birkhoff's theorem.

### (21.41) Vacuum Lambda from Birkhoff argument · supporting · GA p.236 (pdf 253)

\Lambda=-\frac{1}{2}\ln\left|1-\frac{2M}{r}\right|

\Phi(r,t)=-\Lambda(r)+f(t)

## Worked examples

### Example 21.1 · challenging · GA §21.2 p.231 (pdf 248)

**Problem:** Obtain the orthonormal-frame curvature and Einstein tensor for the general static spherically symmetric metric with functions Phi(r), Lambda(r).

**Method:** Quote the six plane-type Riemann components in the static observers' frame, packaged as E, E-bar, F, F-bar; combine them into diagonal Einstein components using the sum rules of margin note 10.

**Key insight:** Symmetry confines all curvature to the six coordinate planes, so four scalar functions suffice, and each Einstein component is a sum over the planes excluding its own direction.

**Result:** G^t_t = -(F + 2F-bar), G^r_r = -(F + 2E-bar), G^theta_theta = G^phi_phi = -(E + E-bar + F-bar); G vanishes wherever T does even if R does not.

**Concepts:** Riemann tensor components for static spherical symmetry, Einstein tensor from sectional curvatures, orthonormal frame of static observers

### Example · standard · GA §21.3 p.232 (pdf 249)

**Problem:** Use the tt component of the field equation for a static perfect-fluid star to determine Lambda(r).

**Method:** Recognise F + 2F-bar as (1/r^2) d/dr[r(1 - exp(-2Lambda))], define that bracket as 2m(r), and integrate dm/dr = 4 pi r^2 rho from the centre.

**Key insight:** Choosing the name of the integration function to match Newtonian enclosed mass makes the geometry readable: g_rr at radius r depends only on the mass inside r.

**Result:** exp(2Lambda) = (1 - 2m(r)/r)^(-1) with m(r) = integral of 4 pi r^2 rho dr + m(0).

**Concepts:** mass function m(r), Einstein field equation, perfect fluid energy-momentum tensor

### Example · standard · GA §21.3 p.232 (pdf 249)

**Problem:** Use the rr component to find Phi(r), first inside the star and then in the vacuum exterior, and assemble the Schwarzschild metric.

**Method:** Substitute exp(-2Lambda) = 1 - 2m/r into G_rr = 8 pi p, solve algebraically for dPhi/dr; outside set p = 0 and m = M, integrate with Phi(infinity) = 0.

**Key insight:** The same equation that later gives stellar structure collapses in vacuum to an elementary integral, and the exterior result satisfies Phi = -Lambda.

**Result:** dPhi/dr = (m + 4 pi r^3 p)/[r(r - 2m)]; exterior Phi = (1/2) ln(1 - 2M/r), giving the Schwarzschild line element (21.23).

**Concepts:** relativistic potential gradient, Schwarzschild metric, asymptotic flatness, weak-field limit

### Example 21.2 · standard · GA §21.3 p.233 (pdf 250)

**Problem:** Evaluate the curvature of the exterior Schwarzschild solution and check that it satisfies the vacuum field equation.

**Method:** Differentiate the exterior Phi and Lambda, substitute into the four curvature functions, lower indices with eta (a minus sign per hatted t), and form the Einstein combinations.

**Key insight:** Every Einstein combination cancels identically while individual Riemann components survive at order M/r^3, the relativistic tidal field.

**Result:** E = F = 2M/r^3, E-bar = F-bar = -M/r^3; R_trtr = -2M/r^3, R_t theta t theta = M/r^3, R_theta phi theta phi = 2M/r^3, R_r theta r theta = -M/r^3; G = 0.

**Concepts:** tidal curvature of Schwarzschild spacetime, vacuum Einstein equation, Riemann tensor components for static spherical symmetry

### Example 21.3 · challenging · GA §21.3 p.233 (pdf 250)

**Problem:** Derive the Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff equations for a static spherical perfect-fluid star and interpret the relativistic corrections.

**Method:** Take the radial part of the conservation law for T (proof deferred to Exercise 39.5) to get dp/dr = -(p + rho) Phi', insert the relativistic Phi', pair with the mass integral (m(0) = 0), and factor the result per mass shell as the Newtonian balance times three brackets.

**Key insight:** Relativity strengthens gravity inside stars in three distinct ways (pressure adds inertia, pressure is a source, compactness amplifies), all negligible when p/rho is tiny, as in ordinary stars supported by electron pressure while baryons carry the mass.

**Result:** dp/dr = -(rho + p)(m + 4 pi r^3 p)/[r^2(1 - 2m/r)] with dm/dr = 4 pi r^2 rho; integrate outward to p(R) = 0 given an equation of state; massive stars come out smaller than Newtonian predictions.

**Concepts:** Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff equation, relativistic hydrostatic equilibrium, Newtonian hydrostatic equilibrium, equation of state

### Example 21.4 · intro · GA §21.4 p.234 (pdf 251)

**Problem:** Give operational meaning to the Schwarzschild radial coordinate by measuring a circle and a radial segment at fixed time.

**Method:** For theta = pi/2, dt = 0, dr = 0 integrate r dphi round the circle; for fixed t, theta, phi integrate sqrt(g_rr) dr between r_A and r_B.

**Key insight:** r is the radius that makes circumferences come out Euclidean, but radial rulers read more than Delta r, so the space is not Euclidean and r is not a distance from the centre.

**Result:** C = 2 pi r; Delta s = integral of (1 - 2M/r)^(-1/2) dr > r_B - r_A.

**Concepts:** circumferential radial coordinate, proper radial distance, coordinate freedom

### Example · intro · GA §21.4 p.234 (pdf 251)

**Problem:** Interpret the Schwarzschild time coordinate t.

**Method:** Set dr = dtheta = dphi = 0 in the line element to get the proper time of a static clock, then take r -> infinity.

**Key insight:** Coordinate time equals proper time only for a static observer infinitely far away; clocks nearer the mass run slow relative to it.

**Result:** Delta tau = (1 - 2M/r)^(1/2) Delta t (the book prints the factor without the square root), reducing to Delta tau = Delta t at infinity.

**Concepts:** gravitational time dilation, observer at infinity


## Analogies and intuitions

### Expanding exp(2Phi) to 1 + 2Phi and recognising the weak-field metric → weak-field limit · strong · GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247)

The exponent in g_tt is named Phi so that for small values it literally is the Newtonian potential, letting readers carry Newtonian intuition (deeper potential well, slower clocks) into the exact solution.

**Where it breaks down:** Only the tt component matches; the exact g_rr = (1 - 2M/r)^(-1) is not the isotropic (1 - 2Phi) spatial factor of the full weak-field metric, and Phi in the exterior is (1/2) ln(1 - 2M/r), not -M/r, once fields are strong.

**App idea:** Plot exp(2Phi_exact) = 1 - 2M/r against 1 + 2Phi_Newton = 1 - 2M/r and -M/r against (1/2) ln(1 - 2M/r) as the learner slides r/M toward 2; highlight where the curves separate by more than 1%.

### m(r) as the Newtonian mass inside a sphere → mass function m(r) · strong · GA §21.3 p.232 (pdf 249)

The integration function in g_rr is chosen so its derivative is 4 pi r^2 rho, exactly the shell-by-shell mass accumulation of Newtonian gravity, so the reader can read g_rr as 'determined by what lies inside'.

**Where it breaks down:** The integration measure ignores the proper-volume factor exp(Lambda), so m counts total energy including (negative) gravitational binding, not the sum of rest masses; the analogy hides this.

**App idea:** Onion-layer star: learner drags a probe radius through a density profile; enclosed mass bar and the local g_rr factor update together, with a toggle revealing proper volume versus coordinate volume.

### TOV as Newtonian hydrostatics with three correction knobs → Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff equation · strong · GA §21.3 p.234 (pdf 251)

Writing the equation as the familiar shell balance times [1 + p/rho][1 + 4 pi r^3 p/m][1 - 2m/r]^(-1) lets each relativistic effect be switched on and judged separately.

**Where it breaks down:** The factors are not independent (p and m feed each other through the integration), and the per-shell form hides that in GR even the notion of shell mass is metric-dependent.

**App idea:** Star builder with three toggle switches for the correction brackets and a polytropic equation of state; integrating outward shows radius and mass for each combination, and a mass-radius curve reveals a maximum mass only when all corrections are on.

### Tape measure round a circle versus ruler laid inward → circumferential radial coordinate · useful · GA §21.4 p.234 (pdf 251)

Example 21.4 measures a circle to legitimise calling r a radius, then lays a ruler along the radius to show distances do not match coordinate differences.

**Where it breaks down:** Cannot be carried out down to r = 2M with static rulers, and the extra radial length is a property of a spatial slice, not something a single freely falling observer sees.

**App idea:** Flamm paraboloid embedding with draggable concentric circles: circumference labels stay 2 pi r while the along-surface radial gap between neighbours grows near 2M.

### The distant observer's clock as master clock → observer at infinity · useful · GA §21.4 p.234 (pdf 251)

Coordinate time t is identified with the proper time of a static clock far away, providing a common reference against which all local clocks are compared.

**Where it breaks down:** No observer actually sits at infinity, and the comparison requires a specified signalling procedure (light signals), treated only in ch22.

**App idea:** Row of clocks at different r ticking at sqrt(1 - 2M/r) of the far clock's rate, with a slider for M; learner predicts which clock has aged least after a fixed coordinate time.

### Electrons stacking into filled energy levels → degeneracy pressure · useful · GA §21.3 p.234 (pdf 251)

The stellar-evolution margin note explains white-dwarf support as fermions forced upward in energy because lower levels are full, making compression costly.

**Where it breaks down:** Qualitative only; gives no scaling with density, no relativistic softening, and no derivation of the mass limit.

**App idea:** Fermi-sea box: squeeze a box of fermions and watch levels fill higher and the pressure gauge rise, then switch to relativistic electrons to see the gauge's growth slow.

### Observers at rest relative to the mass as the natural lab frame → orthonormal frame of static observers · useful · GA §21.2 p.231 (pdf 248)

Margin note 8 lets readers picture the orthonormal frame as a small laboratory hovering at fixed r, where special-relativistic index gymnastics (lower a t index, flip a sign) apply.

**Where it breaks down:** Such observers must fire rockets to stay put; they are not inertial, and the frame ceases to exist at and inside r = 2M.


## Misconceptions addressed

### The Schwarzschild r is the distance from the centre of the star or black hole. · GA §21.4 p.234 (pdf 251)

**Correction:** r is defined by circumferences (2 pi r) and areas (4 pi r^2); radial proper distance is the integral of (1 - 2M/r)^(-1/2) dr and exceeds Delta r.

**Why tempting:** The symbol r and the angular part r^2 dOmega^2 look exactly like flat-space spherical polars.

### The coordinate t is the time shown by any clock in the spacetime. · GA §21.4 p.234 (pdf 251)

**Correction:** t equals proper time only for static observers at infinity; a static clock at r records sqrt(1 - 2M/r) dt.

**Why tempting:** In special relativity t is the time of a family of inertial observers everywhere.

### Empty space around a star is flat because the field equation gives zero there. · GA §21.2 p.231 (pdf 248)

**Correction:** Vacuum forces the Einstein (Ricci) part of curvature to vanish, but Riemann components of size M/r^3 remain and produce tides.

**Why tempting:** Newtonian intuition that sources are needed for 'something', plus conflating G = 0 with R = 0.

### The Schwarzschild metric describes the whole star, including its interior. · GA p.229 (pdf 246)

**Correction:** It holds only where T = 0; inside, g_rr depends on m(r) and g_tt on the TOV-coupled potential, matching the exterior at the surface.

**Why tempting:** The metric is often quoted without the r > R restriction.

### Birkhoff's theorem relies on assuming the source is static. · GA p.229 (pdf 246)

**Correction:** Spherical symmetry plus vacuum suffices: a pulsating or collapsing spherical star still has the static Schwarzschild exterior (no monopole gravitational waves).

**Why tempting:** The derivation in the chapter itself assumes staticity from the outset.

### Birkhoff's theorem says every spherically symmetric spacetime is Schwarzschild. · GA p.235 (pdf 252)

**Correction:** It applies only to vacuum regions; spherically symmetric matter (stars, FRW cosmologies, de Sitter with vacuum energy as in Exercise 21.8) gives other metrics.

**Why tempting:** The chapter summary and Exercise 21.7(e) state the theorem without the word vacuum.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Schwarzschild coordinates are comoving, like the Robertson-Walker coordinates of cosmology. · GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247)

**Correction:** The book says these coordinates are not comoving because they are adapted to the rest frame of the central mass (it cites g_tt depending on r). A clearer physical statement (our addition): observers at fixed r must be held in place and are not freely falling, unlike comoving cosmological observers.

**Why tempting:** Both metrics are diagonal and written with t, r, theta, phi.

### Relativistic effects make compact stars puffier because pressure pushes outward harder. · GA §21.3 p.234 (pdf 251)

**Correction:** In the TOV equation pressure appears as extra gravitating inertia and extra source, steepening the required pressure gradient, so stars end up smaller than Newtonian estimates.

**Why tempting:** Everyday experience associates pressure only with outward push.

### Because g_rr blows up at r = 2M, spacetime is infinitely curved there. · GA p.229 (pdf 246)

**Correction:** The curvature components at r = 2M are finite (of order 1/(4M^2)); the divergence is a failure of the static coordinates, analysed in later chapters.

**Why tempting:** The book lists r = 2M alongside r = 0 as a place where the metric is badly behaved.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The metric function Phi(r) in the exterior is exactly the Newtonian potential -M/r. · GA §21.3 p.233 (pdf 250)

**Correction:** Only at large r; exactly Phi = (1/2) ln(1 - 2M/r), which diverges at r = 2M.

**Why tempting:** The weak-field comparison in §21.1 invites a one-to-one identification.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Measuring a circle, then pacing out its radius · GA §21.4 p.234 (pdf 251)

**Setup:** At one instant, a surveyor lays a tape round an equatorial circle about a mass, then lays rulers end to end along a radial line between two such circles.

**Lesson:** The circle gives 2 pi r but the radial count exceeds the difference in r, revealing curved space and fixing what r means.

**App idea:** Surveyor mini-game on an embedding surface: learner chooses two radii, predicts ruler count, then watches rulers placed along the curved profile.

### Hovering clocks compared with a far-away clock · GA §21.4 p.234 (pdf 251)

**Setup:** Identical clocks held at rest at several radii outside a mass are compared with a clock at very large distance over the same coordinate interval.

**Lesson:** Lower clocks accumulate less proper time; t is the far clock's reading.

**App idea:** Animated clock tower down a gravity well with adjustable compactness 2M/R, showing tick counts diverging.

### Building a star from the centre outward · GA §21.3 p.233 (pdf 250)

**Setup:** Choose a central pressure and an equation of state, then add shells one at a time, updating enclosed mass and pressure with the TOV equation until the pressure reaches zero.

**Lesson:** Radius and mass are outputs of the integration; relativistic terms steepen the pressure fall and shrink the star.

**App idea:** Live numerical integrator plotting p(r) and m(r) side by side for Newtonian versus TOV, with the surface marked where p hits zero.

### A spherical star that pulsates · GA p.229 (pdf 246)

**Setup:** A spherically symmetric star oscillates in radius while an observer outside monitors the gravitational field.

**Lesson:** By Birkhoff's theorem the exterior metric does not change at all; spherical motions radiate no gravitational waves.

**App idea:** Breathing star with a field probe outside that stays constant, contrasted with a non-spherical wobbling star whose exterior field ripples.


## Applications and observations

- **Relativistic stellar structure (TOV integration)** (astrophysical-system, GA §21.3 p.234 (pdf 251)): TOV plus an equation of state is integrated from the centre until the pressure vanishes, giving stellar radius and mass; relativistic corrections are negligible for ordinary stars (pressure from electrons, density from baryons) but make massive compact stars smaller than Newtonian predictions. Key numbers: Corrections scale with p/rho, 4 pi r^3 p/m and 2m/r; all << 1 for Sun-like stars.
- **Stellar evolution and compact remnants** (astrophysical-system, GA §21.3 p.234 (pdf 251)): Main sequence to red giant to white dwarf (electron degeneracy) for roughly solar-mass stars; above a threshold the remnant becomes a neutron star (neutron degeneracy) or a black hole. Key numbers: Threshold quoted in the book as about 1.5 solar masses (the standard Chandrasekhar limit is about 1.4 solar masses; our note).
- **Pulsars as neutron stars** (detection-event, GA §21.3 p.234 (pdf 251)): Pulsars discovered in 1967 by Jocelyn Bell Burnell and later interpreted as rapidly rotating neutron stars, the objects Oppenheimer and Volkoff predicted. Key numbers: Discovery year 1967.
- **How strong is the field? Compactness 2GM/(c^2 R) (our estimates)** (numerical-estimate, GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247)): Applying the weak-field criterion of margin note 7 at the surface of various bodies (our own estimates, not in the book). Key numbers: Earth 1.4e-9; Sun 4.2e-6; 0.6 solar-mass white dwarf with R = 7000 km about 2.5e-4; 1.4 solar-mass neutron star with R = 12 km about 0.34.
- **Schwarzschild radius of familiar masses (our estimates)** (numerical-estimate, GA p.229 (pdf 246)): r_s = 2GM/c^2 converts the geometrized M to a length (our own estimates). Key numbers: Sun about 2.95 km; Earth about 8.9 mm.
- **Static clock rates (our estimates using the corrected eqn 21.35)** (numerical-estimate, GA §21.4 p.234 (pdf 251)): Fractional slowing 1 - sqrt(1 - 2GM/(c^2 r)) for clocks at rest on various surfaces relative to a distant clock (our own estimates). Key numbers: Earth surface 7.0e-10; Sun surface 2.1e-6; neutron star (1.4 solar masses, 12 km) clock runs at about 0.81 of the far rate.
- **Extra radial distance near a black hole (our estimate)** (numerical-estimate, GA §21.4 p.234 (pdf 251)): Integrating eqn 21.34 exactly: the proper distance between r = 4M and r = 10M, and between r = 2M and r = 3M (our own computation). Key numbers: 4M to 10M: 7.24M versus coordinate difference 6M; 2M to 3M: 3.05M versus 1M.

## Historical notes

- **Karl Schwarzschild (1915):** The chapter epigraph is taken from Schwarzschild's 1915 letter to Einstein, in which he remarks wryly that the war, despite the artillery, had left him free to explore Einstein's theory. — Humanises the first exact solution: it was worked out by an astronomer in his forties serving on the Russian front. (GA p.229 (pdf 246))
- **Albert Einstein (1915):** Einstein treated Mercury's perihelion with an approximate solution, believing the field equation too complex for exact solutions. — Sets up the surprise of an exact solution and shows approximation methods came first. (GA p.229 (pdf 246))
- **Karl Schwarzschild (1915):** While serving as a soldier on the Russian front, Schwarzschild sent Einstein an exact exterior solution in a letter at the end of 1915; he died in 1916 of an illness (pemphigus) contracted around then. — First exact solution of GR, the basis of orbits, light bending and black holes. A margin note also corrects pronunciation and notes the name means black shield. (GA p.229 (pdf 246))
- **Albert Einstein, Karl Schwarzschild (1916):** Einstein wrote back admiring both how compactly the exact solution could be expressed and the quality of Schwarzschild's mathematical handling of the problem. — Illustrates that even Einstein underestimated the tractability of his equation under symmetry. (GA §21.3 p.233 (pdf 250))
- **George David Birkhoff:** Birkhoff's theorem: spherically symmetric vacuum solutions are necessarily the static Schwarzschild solution. — Makes the Schwarzschild exterior universal for any spherical body, static or not. (GA p.229 (pdf 246))
- **Richard C. Tolman, J. Robert Oppenheimer, George Volkoff:** Oppenheimer and Volkoff built on Tolman's relativistic fluid-sphere work to formulate relativistic stellar structure and predict neutron stars. — The TOV equations underpin maximum-mass limits for neutron stars and the case for black-hole formation. Our caveat: the book credits Oppenheimer and Volkoff with predicting neutron stars; the idea was proposed earlier (Baade and Zwicky, 1934), and their 1939 contribution was the relativistic structure and a maximum mass. (GA §21.3 p.233 (pdf 250))
- **Jocelyn Bell Burnell (1967):** Discovery of pulsars, later identified as rapidly spinning neutron stars. — Observational confirmation that neutron-degenerate stars exist. (GA §21.3 p.234 (pdf 251))

## Notation and conventions

- **Units:** G = c = 1 throughout Part IV; restore with M -> GM/c^2 and t -> ct. — Mass M has dimensions of length. (GA p.229 (pdf 246))
- **Metric signature:** (-, +, +, +): g_tt negative outside r = 2M. (GA p.229 (pdf 246))
- **Metric functions:** g_tt = -exp(2Phi(r)), g_rr = exp(2Lambda(r)), with Phi(infinity) = Lambda(infinity) = 0; a prime denotes d/dr. — Exercise 21.7 switches to exp(Phi), exp(Lambda) in (21.39) but uses exp(-2Lambda) in (21.40); comma subscripts denote partial derivatives there. (GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247))
- **Angular element:** dOmega^2 = dtheta^2 + sin^2 theta dphi^2. (GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247))
- **Symbol R:** R(r) in (21.4) is a temporary areal-radius function; R later denotes the star's outer radius; bold R is the Riemann tensor. — Three meanings within two pages; flag for learners. (GA §21.3 p.232 (pdf 249))
- **Hatted indices:** Hats label components in the orthonormal frame of static observers; raise and lower with eta, so lowering a t-hat index introduces a minus sign and other indices are unchanged. (GA §21.2 p.231 (pdf 248))
- **Vielbein notation:** (e_mu)^{nu-hat} gives the orthonormal component of the coordinate basis vector e_mu, e.g. (e_t)^{t-hat} = exp(Phi). (GA §21.2 p.231 (pdf 248))
- **Curvature shorthand:** E, E-bar, F, F-bar denote the t-r, t-angular, theta-phi and r-angular plane Riemann components respectively; mixed-position R^{ab}_{ab} form. — Riemann sign convention as in ch11 (MTW-like), giving R_trtr = -2M/r^3. (GA §21.2 p.231 (pdf 248))
- **Fluid variables:** rho is (mass-)energy density, p pressure, m(r) enclosed mass function, M total mass; energy-momentum tensor diag(rho, p, p, p) in the orthonormal frame. (GA §21.3 p.232 (pdf 249))
- **Partial versus total derivatives:** Example 21.3 writes partial p / partial r for what are ordinary radial derivatives in a static configuration; §21.1 writes d g/d t for partial time derivatives. (GA §21.3 p.233 (pdf 250))

## Teaching gems

### Guess-and-constrain construction of the ansatz: start with flat space in spherical polars, let each coefficient depend on r, then use coordinate freedom to eliminate the angular function. · GA §21.1 p.230 (pdf 247)

**Why it works:** Learners see that the general form is forced by symmetry and relabelling, not pulled from a hat, and that only two functions carry physics.

**App idea:** Metric sculptor: three sliders/curves for g_tt(r), g_rr(r) and the angular factor; a 'relabel r' button redraws the geometry with the angular factor straightened to r^2 while the embedded surface and clocks stay unchanged.

### Name integration functions for their future meaning: exp(2Phi) so Phi is the Newtonian potential, and r(1 - exp(-2Lambda)) = 2m(r) so m is enclosed mass. · GA §21.3 p.232 (pdf 249)

**Why it works:** Each new function arrives with an intuitive handle, so the final metric is readable term by term (g_tt from potential, g_rr from mass inside).

**App idea:** Two linked panels for a model star: left shows density and pressure profiles, right shows exp(2Phi) and exp(2Lambda) computed live, with hover labels explaining which physical quantity drives each curve.

### Solve the interior problem generally, then set p = 0 and m = M to get the exterior: one pair of equations produces both the Schwarzschild metric and the TOV equations. · GA §21.3 p.232 (pdf 249)

**Why it works:** Shows the vacuum solution as a special case of a physical star, and makes the surface matching intuitive.

**App idea:** Radial scrubber through a star and out into space: equations panel greys out the p and rho terms once the probe crosses R, and the metric curves continue smoothly into the 1 - 2M/r form.

### Vacuum does not mean flat: all Einstein combinations cancel while Riemann components of order M/r^3 survive. · GA §21.3 p.233 (pdf 250)

**Why it works:** Directly confronts the conflation of 'no source' with 'no curvature' and ties the numbers to tides.

**App idea:** Curvature ledger: six plane components shown as coloured tiles with values vs r; the three Einstein sums are drawn as brackets over tiles and visibly total zero, while a dust ball beside the table stretches radially and squeezes sideways.

### Rewrite TOV as Newtonian hydrostatics times three bracketed corrections and argue which dominate for ordinary versus compact stars. · GA §21.3 p.234 (pdf 251)

**Why it works:** Converts an opaque ODE into a physical story about why relativistic stars are smaller and why a maximum mass can exist.

**App idea:** Star builder with toggles for each bracket and an adjustable stiffness of equation of state; outputs a mass-radius curve comparing Newtonian and relativistic families.

### Give coordinates meaning by measurement: tape round a circle gives r; rulers along a radius give more than Delta r; a static clock gives dtau < dt with equality only at infinity. · GA §21.4 p.234 (pdf 251)

**Why it works:** Replaces the abstract slogan with concrete operations learners can imagine performing, preparing for redshift and black-hole horizons.

**App idea:** Measurement lab on a Flamm paraboloid: tools for circumference, radial distance and clock comparison, each reporting both the coordinate value and the measured value as M is varied.

### Orthonormal-frame curvature packaged into four functions plus sum rules for the Einstein tensor. · GA §21.2 p.231 (pdf 248)

**Why it works:** Reduces a many-index computation to a small table and teaches the geometric fact that Einstein components are sums over planes.

**App idea:** Interactive plane selector: a cube whose six faces represent coordinate planes; choosing an Einstein component highlights the three faces that contribute, showing their current values for a user-defined Phi and Lambda.

## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Printed eqn 21.28 (TOV) lacks the leading minus sign; eqns 21.27 and 21.30 imply it.** — A reader copying 21.28 will integrate an outward-increasing pressure and never find a stellar surface. *Suggestion:* Display the corrected sign and have learners check it against dp/dr = -(rho + p) Phi' with Phi' > 0.
- **Printed eqn 21.35 gives Delta tau = (1 - 2M/r) Delta t without the square root.** — Squares the time-dilation factor, doubling weak-field redshift estimates and conflicting with ch22. *Suggestion:* Derive Delta tau = sqrt(-g_tt) Delta t live from the line element and note the misprint.
- **The chapter summary and Exercise 21.7(e) state Birkhoff's theorem without restricting to vacuum; margin note 4 says static without noting this holds only for r > 2M.** — Learners may think every spherically symmetric spacetime (including FRW or stellar interiors) is Schwarzschild. *Suggestion:* Always say 'spherically symmetric vacuum region'; use Exercise 21.8 (de Sitter) as a counterexample with a source.
- **Staticity is described partly as a consequence of spherical symmetry (orthogonality of fixed-position worldlines to t slices), and the time independence is written as a total derivative.** — Blurs the distinction between spherical symmetry, stationarity and staticity, which matters for rotating (Kerr) spacetimes later. *Suggestion:* State separately: symmetry restricts dependence to r; staticity means time-independent and no dt dr cross term; mention Birkhoff makes the latter automatic in vacuum.
- **Absorbing R(r) into r silently assumes R is monotonic with non-zero derivative.** — Hides the fact that some geometries (e.g. wormhole throats, or regions inside horizons) cannot use the areal radius as a good static coordinate. *Suggestion:* Add a one-line caveat and a picture of an embedding where R(r) has a minimum.
- **The weak-field comparison uses a metric with flat spatial part, while ch5 eqn 5.24 has (1 - 2Phi) spatial factors in isotropic form; Schwarzschild g_rr ~ 1 + 2M/r is not isotropic.** — Learners comparing chapters may think the spatial parts contradict each other. *Suggestion:* Explain that the identification uses g_tt only, and that isotropic and Schwarzschild radial coordinates differ at first order in M/r.
- **Margin box justifies 'not comoving' by g_tt,r being non-zero, which is not the comoving criterion used in ch16.** — Confusing test; learners may try to apply it to other metrics. *Suggestion:* Say instead that fixed-r worldlines are not geodesics (static observers need thrust), which is the physical point.
- **The Riemann components and sum rules are quoted, not derived, and fluid conservation is deferred to ch39.** — The chapter can feel like assembling results on trust; learners who skipped the ch11 exercises lack the check. *Suggestion:* Offer a symbolic-algebra notebook or step-through that computes Gamma, Riemann and G for the ansatz so learners can verify every quoted line.
- **m(r) is integrated with coordinate volume 4 pi r^2 dr rather than proper volume, but no comment is made on binding energy.** — Misses a deep point: the gravitating mass is less than the sum of rest masses of the constituents. *Suggestion:* Add a sidebar comparing M with the proper-volume integral for a uniform-density star.
- **Interior Phi and matching at r = R are not discussed; only the exterior integration constant is fixed.** — Learners may not realise that Phi inside is set by continuity with the exterior value at the surface. *Suggestion:* Show the uniform-density (Schwarzschild interior) solution as an optional worked example with continuity of g_tt and g_rr at R.
- **Exercise 21.8 writes the source as T_tt = 8 pi zeta and T_ii = -8 pi zeta, but rho = -p = zeta is intended (the 8 pi belongs to G); Exercise 21.6 writes GM for the enclosed mass; eqn 21.39 drops factors of 2 in the exponents.** — Students attempting the exercises get wrong constants or inconsistent equations. *Suggestion:* Provide corrected statements: T_tt = zeta, T_ii = -zeta; G m(r) dm; exp(2Phi), exp(2Lambda).
- **The stellar-evolution note is simplified and partly dated: main-sequence support is described as radiation pressure (thermal gas pressure dominates in Sun-like stars) and the white-dwarf limit is given as about 1.5 solar masses (Chandrasekhar limit about 1.4, applying to the remnant core mass).** — Learners may carry imprecise astrophysics into later black-hole discussions. *Suggestion:* Clarify thermal versus radiation pressure, quote 1.4 solar masses for the core, and mention the neutron-star maximum mass of roughly 2-2.5 solar masses from TOV with realistic equations of state.
- **The finiteness of curvature at r = 2M is not remarked, although Example 21.2 makes it evident.** — Learners leave thinking r = 2M is a physical singularity until ch25-ch27. *Suggestion:* Point out that R components at r = 2M equal about 1/(4M^2), foreshadowing the coordinate nature of the horizon.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'If a star is perfectly round and not moving, how many unknown functions do you think its spacetime metric needs?' Let the learner reason from flat spherical polars before revealing the two-function ansatz.
- Order of explanation that works: metric and its features -> symmetry assumptions -> ansatz and relabelling -> Phi as Newtonian potential -> tt equation gives m(r) -> rr equation gives Phi -> exterior solution -> meaning of r and t -> TOV as bonus.
- For beginners, skip the E, E-bar, F, F-bar table and state the two field equations (21.12, 21.17) as given; for advanced learners, have them verify the sum rules and Example 21.2 cancellation.
- Quick check: ask what circumference and what radial distance a learner would measure between r = 4M and r = 10M; expected answer 2 pi r for circles and about 7.24M, not 6M, for the radial gap.
- Quick check: 'Is space outside the Sun flat, since T = 0 there?' Expect the learner to separate Einstein/Ricci from Riemann and mention tides.
- When deriving time dilation, have the learner produce Delta tau = sqrt(1 - 2M/r) Delta t themselves and point out that the printed eqn 21.35 omits the square root; similarly flag the missing minus in 21.28.
- Common learner question: 'Why is g_rr the reciprocal of g_tt?' Answer: in vacuum the tt and rr equations together force Phi' = -Lambda', and both vanish at infinity, so Phi = -Lambda; inside matter this fails.
- Common learner question: 'What happens at r = 2M?' Acknowledge the coordinate breakdown, note curvature stays finite, and defer details to the black-hole chapters rather than hand-waving.
- Common learner question: 'Does Birkhoff mean a collapsing star has no effect outside?' Clarify that the exterior metric depends only on M, so spherical collapse changes nothing outside until the surface passes a given radius; no spherical gravitational waves.
- Use SI conversions early (r_s of Sun about 3 km, Earth about 9 mm) so learners calibrate how weak everyday fields are; the compactness of a neutron star (about 0.34) shows where GR corrections become order unity.
- For stellar-structure-minded learners, turn Example 21.3 into a numerical activity: integrate TOV with a simple polytrope and compare with Newtonian results to see the radius shrink.
- Keep the vacuum proviso on Birkhoff in every statement; test with 'Is de Sitter space, which is spherically symmetric, a Schwarzschild spacetime?'
