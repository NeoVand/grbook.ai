# GA ch06 · Finding a theory of gravitation

> Opens Part II by extracting a programme for relativistic gravity from two principles, equivalence and general covariance: free fall erases gravity locally, tidal effects betray it, physics in curved spacetime follows from special-relativistic tensor laws by swapping eta for g, the metric becomes a field obeying a field equation, local flatness makes this precise, and gravitational time dilation and redshift are the first payoff.

**Pages:** printed 67–80 · pdf 84–97 · **Difficulty:** math 2/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 4/5

Main text uses only Newtonian kinematics, a Galilean transformation, index matching, one metric component and a binomial expansion; conceptual load comes from separating local from global statements, frames from coordinates, and from accepting deferred claims (local flatness, Riemann). A few statements are loose or wrong (timelike curves called geodesics, light-bending factor of three) and could mislead careful readers. Exercises 6.5 and 6.6 jump to general stationary metrics and are markedly harder than the chapter.

## Role in the book

Part I built special relativity, vectors, 1-forms, tensors and the metric in flat or merely curvilinear settings. This short, largely qualitative chapter is the hinge into Part II: it supplies the physical reasons why gravity should be geometry and sets the agenda (parallel transport and covariant derivatives in ch7, geodesics in ch8-9, measurements in orthonormal frames in ch10, Riemann in ch11, energy-momentum in ch12, the field equation in ch13). Its six numbered 'Lessons' are the contract the rest of the book fulfils, and its hand-waving estimates (light bending, clock rates) are explicitly redone properly in Part IV (ch21-24) and the local flatness claim is proved with Riemann normal coordinates in Part V (ch35).

## Learning objectives

- Reader can explain why Newton's instantaneous action at a distance is incompatible with the relativity of simultaneity.
- Reader can state the weak and strong equivalence principles, say what separates them, and connect the weak form to m_i = m_g.
- Reader can show by the Galilean coordinate change x' = x - g t^2/2 that a uniform gravitational field disappears from the equations of motion of an interacting particle cloud.
- Reader can explain why only tidal (inhomogeneous) effects distinguish genuine gravity from an accelerating frame, and design a two-particle test for it.
- Reader can define a local inertial frame and explain in what sense it is 'local' in space and time.
- Reader can use the accelerating-rocket argument to predict that light bends in a gravitational field, estimate the solar deflection to order of magnitude, and say why this argument misses part of the full answer.
- Reader can state the principle of general covariance and use index matching to spot a law that singles out a preferred basis or uses a non-scalar source.
- Reader can explain why a Lorentz-invariant wave-equation upgrade of Poisson's equation with rho as source fails.
- Reader can apply the eta -> g rule to promote a special-relativistic tensor law to one valid in the presence of gravity.
- Reader can explain why the metric must satisfy a differential field equation and what role curvature plays in separating gravity from coordinate artefacts.
- Reader can state the local flatness theorem (g = eta and first derivatives zero at a point, second derivatives not removable) and link second derivatives to curvature.
- Reader can derive d tau = (1 - 2GM/r)^{1/2} dt from g_00 for a static clock, rewrite it in terms of escape speed or Schwarzschild radius, and expand it in the weak-field limit.
- Reader can estimate gravitational redshift for Earth, Sun, white dwarf and neutron star surfaces and explain why GPS needs both SR and GR clock corrections.
- Reader can distinguish Doppler, gravitational and cosmological redshift by their physical origin.

## Teaching approach

A conceptual chapter built around two guiding claims (you cannot feel your own weight in free fall; only the metric carries the influence of spacetime) and distilled into six numbered Lessons that act as a checklist for the whole of Part II. The authors alternate short boxed Examples with prose: an explicit Galilean coordinate change, a springs-in-a-box accelerometer, a spaceship tidal test, an accelerating rocket for light bending, a Machian interlude, a deliberately failed theory, and a clock-drop argument. The mathematics stays at the level of Part I (coordinate changes, index matching, one metric component), and rigorous versions are deferred with pointers to ch11, ch24 and ch35. Estimates are made with numerical factors thrown away openly, and the authors are candid when a heuristic under-counts (light bending). The chapter ends with a concrete, measurable consequence (gravitational time dilation, GPS) so that the abstract principles pay off.

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, thought-experiment, conversational-informal, example-driven, historical-narrative, spiral-revisit, survey-overview, analogy-driven

**Narrative arc**

1. Part II roadmap: what chapters 6-14 will build towards (curvature on the left, energy-momentum on the right of Einstein's equation)
2. Motivating problem: Newtonian gravity acts instantaneously and so defines a universal 'now', which relativity forbids
3. Two guiding claims announced: equivalence (no felt weight in free fall) and general covariance (spacetime enters physics only through the metric)
4. Model: m_i = m_g makes gravitational acceleration a property of the coordinates; weak equivalence principle
5. Formal check: a Galilean change to a falling frame removes a uniform g from an interacting N-particle cloud (Example 6.1); accelerometer box (Example 6.2)
6. Generalization to the strong equivalence principle covering all non-gravitational laws; consequence that free fall follows timelike geodesics (Example 6.3)
7. Caveat and escape: a uniform field is indistinguishable from no field, but real fields are inhomogeneous, so tidal effects reveal gravity (Example 6.4, Fig. 6.2)
8. Lessons 1-3 codified: free fall along geodesics, local inertial frames, tidal detection over finite regions
9. Quick payoff: rocket thought experiment shows light must bend (Example 6.5); honest admission that the local argument misses the spatial-curvature part; crude solar estimate eqn (6.5)
10. Historical interlude: Mach's principle, inertial forces from distant matter, and why nonlinearity pushes us from particle interactions to local fields (Example 6.6)
11. General covariance stated; no preferred bases; a failed Lorentz-covariant scalar theory shows what goes wrong (Example 6.7)
12. Synthesis: a valid tensor law without gravity remains valid with gravity once eta is replaced by g (Lesson 4)
13. The metric as a field: invariant geometry requires g at different points to be related by a differential equation built from curvature (Riemann tensor); correspondence with Newton and SR (Fig. 6.4, Lesson 5)
14. Local flatness theorem: g = eta and dg = 0 achievable at a point, second derivatives not; local orthonormal frames as the place where measurements happen (Lesson 6)
15. Application: static clock rate from g_00, rederived by a dropped-clock argument; gravitational redshift, neutron stars, GPS; three kinds of redshift contrasted

**Signature moves**

- Numbered 'Lessons' (1-6) that compress each conceptual step into a portable rule the later chapters cite
- Turning a principle into an explicit coordinate transformation so the reader watches g vanish from an equation (Example 6.1)
- Deliberately constructing a plausible but wrong theory (Poisson upgraded to a wave equation) and diagnosing its failure by transformation properties
- Order-of-magnitude estimates that openly discard factors and then state the true factor (light bending off by 4)
- Candid admission of where the equivalence-principle heuristic breaks: the photon samples enough space to feel spatial curvature
- Reusing everyday settings as test beds for relativity: a car driving through vertical rain, an accelerometer made of springs, waking up in a spaceship
- Deriving the same result twice by different routes (metric component g_00 and a falling-clock plus SR time dilation argument)
- Anchoring the abstract to technology and precision measurement (LEP Earth tides, GPS, a 2022 strontium lattice clock exercise)
- Commuting-limits diagram (Fig. 6.4) to place GR relative to Newtonian gravity, SR and Newtonian mechanics

## Section by section

### Part II opener: roadmap to the field equation — p.67 (pdf 84)

A one-page overview of Part II listing what each of chapters 6-14 contributes: equivalence principle, parallel transport and connection coefficients, geodesics, making local measurements, Riemann and Ricci curvature as the left side of Einstein's equation, the energy-momentum tensor as the right side, the field equation itself, and a tour of its successes.

**Concepts:** Riemann curvature tensor, Einstein field equation (preview), energy-momentum tensor

**Key moves**
- Frames the field equation as curvature (geometry) balanced against energy-momentum (matter)
- Signals that later Parts will unpack chapter 14's successes in detail

### Chapter opening: why Newtonian gravity must be replaced — p.68 (pdf 85)

After an Einstein epigraph linking equal inertial and gravitational mass to composition-independent free fall, the authors point out that Newton's instantaneous interaction singles out a universal time, contradicting relativity of simultaneity. Two guiding claims are set out: a falling person feels no weight (equivalence), and spacetime influences physics only through its metric (general covariance).

**Concepts:** instantaneous action at a distance, equivalence of inertial and gravitational mass, principle of general covariance

**Key moves**
- Identify the conflict between instantaneous gravity and relative simultaneity
- Announce equivalence and covariance as the two search principles and hint that they are linked

### §6.1 Free fall and the equivalence principle — p.68 (pdf 85)

Setting m_i = m_g in Newton's equation makes the gravitational field equal to a coordinate acceleration, so a falling observer sees no gravity. The weak principle (mechanics only) is illustrated by transforming a uniformly accelerated interacting particle cloud into a falling frame and by a spring accelerometer that cannot tell acceleration from weight. The strong principle extends this to all laws in a small enough region, implying that free particles follow timelike geodesics. Because a uniform field can be transformed away completely, gravity is only detectable through inhomogeneity, i.e. tidal effects, as in the spaceship test. Lessons 1-3 summarize. The rocket argument then shows light must bend, gives the angle gx/c^2, and the authors admit this is only part of the full photon deflection before estimating the solar deflection as GM/(R c^2).

**Concepts:** equivalence of inertial and gravitational mass, free fall, weak equivalence principle, strong equivalence principle, uniformly accelerating reference frame, tidal force, local inertial frame, timelike geodesic as free-fall world line, gravitational deflection of light

**Key moves**
- m_i = m_g turns g(x) into a coordinate acceleration
- Galilean change x' = x - g t^2/2 removes g from an N-body equation of motion while leaving pair forces (which depend on differences) untouched
- Promote from mechanics (weak) to all laws within a small region (strong)
- Particle at rest in SR moves along the time axis, so by SEP free fall is along timelike geodesics
- Uniform field = no field; inhomogeneity (tides) is the only local signature of real gravity
- Lessons 1-3: geodesic free fall, LIFs obey SR, tidal detection over extended regions
- Rocket crossing: straight path in inertial frame becomes parabola y = y0 - g x^2/(2v^2) in rocket frame; set v -> c to get bending angle gx/c^2
- Crude solar estimate: g ~ GM/R^2 acting over length ~ R gives theta ~ GM/(R c^2), off from the true value by a factor 4

### Interlude: Mach's principle and the case for fields (Example 6.6) — p.72 (pdf 89)

A historical detour: inertial forces in non-inertial frames raise the question of what they are relative to. Mach's answer, acceleration relative to the average matter of the Universe, would make inertial forces real interactions between masses, with an electromagnetism-like static inverse-square part and a 1/r part for relative acceleration. Equivalence identifies the static part with gravity. The authors then argue that treating gravity as particle-particle interactions is hopeless because mass-energy, including interaction energy, is itself a source: the theory is nonlinear, so a local field description is needed.

**Concepts:** Mach's principle, inertial forces, nonlinearity of gravitation, field description of gravity

**Key moves**
- Pose the question of defining inertial frames without absolute space
- Mach: judge acceleration relative to the 'fixed stars'
- Model inertial force by analogy with electrostatics (1/r^2) and radiation (1/r)
- Equivalence principle identifies the static inertial term with gravity
- Nonlinearity (interaction energy gravitates) forces a switch from particle interactions to locally defined fields; contrast with linear electromagnetism

### §6.2 Why general relativity? — p.73 (pdf 90)

General covariance is stated as the requirement that physical laws keep their form under arbitrary coordinate transformations, which rules out laws that single out particular components or bases. A tempting relativistic upgrade of Poisson's equation to a wave equation with mass density as source fails because density is not a scalar. Combining the equivalence principle (SR laws hold in a LIF) with covariance (form preserved in any coordinates) gives the key rule: a valid tensor equation without gravity remains valid with gravity, with geometry, via the replacement of eta by a position-dependent g, being the only change (Lesson 4).

**Concepts:** principle of general covariance, manifestly covariant tensor equation, failed scalar theory of gravity, minimal coupling via eta to g replacement, Minkowski metric

**Key moves**
- State general covariance and interpret it as a ban on preferred bases
- Illustrate with F = dp/dt (geometric) versus a component-picking pseudo-law
- Build d'Alembertian version of Poisson's equation, eqn (6.6), and reject it because rho transforms as part of a tensor
- Syllogism: SR law holds in LIF (equivalence) + form holds in all coordinates (covariance) => law holds with gravity
- Lesson 4: gravity enters tensor laws only through eta -> g

### §6.3 A differential equation to describe gravity — p.75 (pdf 92)

The metric computes intervals along curves, eqn (6.7). Because coordinate-independent geometric facts (circle ratios, triangle angle sums) must come out the same whichever coordinates are used, the values of g at neighbouring points cannot be arbitrary: g is a field obeying a differential field equation. That equation involves a curvature tensor (Riemann) built from g and its derivatives, and curvature, unlike coordinate artefacts, is the true signature of gravity. The theory must reduce to Newtonian gravity for weak fields and slow motion and to SR when gravity vanishes (Fig. 6.4). A margin note distinguishes a general LIF from the freely falling frame, which stays inertial along its geodesic for long proper times. Lesson 5: GR is a field theory of the metric.

**Concepts:** line element and interval along a curve, metric as a field, field equation for the metric, Riemann curvature tensor, correspondence limits of general relativity, frame of reference versus coordinate system, freely falling frame

**Key moves**
- Metric as a rulebook for intervals, eqn (6.7)
- Coordinate-invariant geometric answers require g at different points to be related, hence a differential equation
- Field viewpoint: input a spacetime point, output the tensor g there
- Curvature (Riemann) as the part that no coordinate choice can remove
- Constraint from correspondence: Newtonian limit and SR limit (Fig. 6.4)
- Lesson 5: GR is a field theory of g(x)

### §6.4 Local flatness — p.76 (pdf 93)

Minkowski spacetime is flat. By the equivalence principle every observer can treat spacetime as flat locally. The local flatness theorem says coordinates exist in which the metric equals eta at a chosen point (possible since a symmetric matrix can be diagonalized and normalized) and, more strongly, its first derivatives vanish there; second derivatives cannot generally be removed because they encode curvature. A LIF is a frame meeting both conditions at a point; the freely falling frame is the key example. Observers build orthonormal bases where they are, and all measurements are interpreted there with the Minkowski metric (Lesson 6).

**Concepts:** flat spacetime, local flatness theorem, local inertial frame, Riemann normal coordinates, local orthonormal frame, Lorentz signature

**Key moves**
- Equivalence principle => locally everyone can use flat spacetime
- Condition 1: g_{mu nu}(P) = eta_{mu nu} by diagonalizing and rescaling
- Condition 2: partial derivatives of g vanish at P, making flatness hold in an infinitesimal neighbourhood
- Second derivatives are not removable and relate to curvature
- Lesson 6: measurements are made in a local orthonormal frame using eta

### §6.5 Time dilation in a gravitational field — p.77 (pdf 94)

For a static clock, only g_00 contributes, so proper tick interval is (-g_00)^{1/2} times coordinate interval. With the weak-field metric g_00 = -(1 - 2GM/r) this gives gravitational time dilation, rewritten via escape speed and Schwarzschild radius. A second derivation drops a clock from far away: it matches the distant clock at release, arrives at radius r with v^2 = 2GM/r, and the SR gamma factor between it and a static clock reproduces the same factor. Recast for atomic transitions this is gravitational redshift. The authors note the weak-field approximation 1 - GM/R, negligible effects on Earth, large effects on neutron stars, the GPS need for both SR and GR corrections, and contrast Doppler, gravitational and cosmological redshifts.

**Concepts:** gravitational time dilation, gravitational redshift, proper time, escape velocity, Schwarzschild radius, Newtonian limit of the metric, Doppler versus gravitational versus cosmological redshift, Global Positioning System relativistic corrections

**Key moves**
- Static clock: -d tau^2 = g_00 dt^2
- Insert g_00 from eqn (5.22) with Phi = -GM/r
- Identify 2GM/r = v_esc^2 and r_S = 2GM/c^2
- Alternative route: free-falling clock is locally inertial; energy conservation gives its speed; SR gamma supplies the factor
- Clocks -> atomic frequencies -> redshift
- Binomial expansion for weak fields and survey of magnitudes (Earth, neutron star, GPS)
- Classify redshifts by origin: velocity (Doppler) versus change of metric (gravitational, cosmological)

### Chapter summary and exercises — p.79 (pdf 96)

A three-point summary (equivalence principle in LIFs, general covariance, gravitational time dilation) and six exercises ranging from redshift estimates and a real optical-lattice clock measurement to satellite clock rates, Schwarzschild clock comparisons, radar-defined spatial distance in a general stationary metric, clock synchronization and the Sagnac effect.

**Concepts:** gravitational redshift, spatial metric of a stationary spacetime, clock synchronization in stationary spacetimes, Sagnac effect

**Key moves**
- Consolidate with numerical estimates
- Extend the g_00 idea to metrics with g_0i cross terms via light-signal constructions

## Concepts

### instantaneous action at a distance

*historical-idea · mention* · also: Newtonian instantaneous gravity

The Newtonian assumption that the gravitational force between bodies depends on their simultaneous positions, so a change in one source is felt everywhere at once; it presupposes an absolute simultaneity that special relativity denies.

**How introduced:** The opening paragraph uses it as the reason a new theory is needed: instantaneous interaction picks out a universal time for all observers.

**Prerequisites:** relativity of simultaneity

**Where:** GA p.68 (pdf 85)

### equivalence of inertial and gravitational mass

*principle · core* · also: m_i = m_g, universality of free fall

The experimental fact that the mass resisting acceleration (inertial mass) equals the mass coupling to gravity (gravitational mass), so all bodies at a given place fall with the same acceleration regardless of composition.

**How introduced:** Via the Einstein epigraph and eqn (6.1): cancelling m_i against m_g shows the field g(x) equals the particle's coordinate acceleration, suggesting gravity is a feature of the coordinates.

**Prerequisites:** Newton's second law

$$
m_i \ddot{\vec x} = m_g \vec g(\vec x)
$$

**Where:** GA §6.1 p.68 (pdf 85)

### free fall

*definition · core* · also: freely falling body, freely falling observer

Motion under the influence of gravity alone, with no non-gravitational forces acting; a freely falling observer carries no felt weight.

**How introduced:** Defined in a margin note and dramatized by the astronaut who, seeing no change in her rest-frame coordinates, concludes there is no gravity.

**Where:** GA §6.1 p.68 (pdf 85); GA §6.1 p.71 (pdf 88)

### weak equivalence principle

*principle · core* · also: WEP, principle of weak equivalence

The statement that inertial and gravitational mass are identical, so that for purely mechanical motion one can choose coordinates in which a freely falling particle obeys the same laws as in unaccelerated Cartesian coordinates without gravity.

**How introduced:** Introduced right after eqn (6.1); illustrated by transforming away a uniform field (Example 6.1) and by the spring accelerometer (Example 6.2). A margin note jokes that its weakness is that it covers only mechanics.

**Prerequisites:** equivalence of inertial and gravitational mass, free fall

**Where:** GA §6.1 p.68 (pdf 85); GA §6.1 p.69 (pdf 86)

### strong equivalence principle

*principle · core* · also: Einstein equivalence principle, SEP, principle of equivalence

At any event in any gravitational field one can choose local coordinates such that, in a small enough neighbourhood, all laws of physics (not only mechanics) take their special-relativistic form without gravity.

**How introduced:** Presented as a strengthening of the weak principle, boxed, and immediately used to deduce geodesic free fall (Example 6.3). The chapter summary phrases it as non-gravitational laws taking SR form in every LIF.

**Prerequisites:** weak equivalence principle

**Notes:** Modern literature usually calls this (non-gravitational laws) the Einstein equivalence principle and reserves 'strong' for a version that includes gravitational self-energy and gravitational experiments; the book's usage differs.

**Where:** GA §6.1 p.69 (pdf 86); GA §6.1 p.70 (pdf 87); GA p.79 (pdf 96)

### uniformly accelerating reference frame

*technique · developed* · also: accelerated coordinates, falling frame transformation

A frame whose origin accelerates with constant acceleration relative to an inertial frame; in Newtonian kinematics reached by x' = x - (1/2) a t^2, t' = t, which adds a uniform fictitious acceleration -a to every body.

**How introduced:** Example 6.1 applies it with a = g to an interacting N-particle cloud: the uniform gravitational term cancels while the pair forces, depending only on separations, are unchanged.

**Prerequisites:** free fall

$$
\vec x' = \vec x - \tfrac12 \vec g t^2,\ t'=t
$$

**Where:** GA §6.1 p.69 (pdf 86)

### homogeneous gravitational field equivalence

*principle · developed* · also: uniform field is equivalent to no field

A spatially uniform, constant gravitational field can be removed everywhere by passing to a single uniformly accelerating frame, so no experiment can distinguish it from acceleration; such a field carries no invariant gravitational content.

**How introduced:** Emphasized after Example 6.3 as the apparently discouraging conclusion that seems to make gravity undetectable, before being rescued by the inhomogeneity of real fields.

**Prerequisites:** uniformly accelerating reference frame, strong equivalence principle

**Where:** GA §6.1 p.70 (pdf 87)

### tidal force

*phenomenon · core* · also: tidal acceleration, relative acceleration of nearby free particles

The relative acceleration between nearby freely falling bodies caused by the variation of the gravitational field across the region they occupy; it cannot be transformed away by any choice of frame and is the local signature of genuine gravity (spacetime curvature).

**How introduced:** Arises as the escape from the uniform-field paradox; Example 6.4 has an astronaut release two parallel test particles and watch whether their separation changes; a margin note connects to ocean tides and the Earth tide stretching the LEP ring.

**Prerequisites:** homogeneous gravitational field equivalence, free fall

**Notes:** Formalized as geodesic deviation and the Riemann tensor in GA ch11 and ch35.

**Where:** GA §6.1 p.70 (pdf 87); GA §6.1 p.71 (pdf 88)

### local inertial frame

*definition · core* · also: LIF, freely falling laboratory

A frame, set up around an event, in which the metric takes the Minkowski form and its first derivatives vanish at that event, so that special-relativistic physics holds to first order in a sufficiently small neighbourhood; a freely falling, non-rotating laboratory realizes one.

**How introduced:** Lesson 2 introduces it physically as the falling observer's laboratory; section 6.4 later gives the mathematical conditions (6.8) and (6.9).

**Prerequisites:** strong equivalence principle, local flatness theorem

$$
g_{\mu\nu}(\mathcal P)=\eta_{\mu\nu},\ \partial_\alpha g_{\mu\nu}(\mathcal P)=0
$$

**Where:** GA §6.1 p.71 (pdf 88); GA §6.4 p.76 (pdf 93)

### local Lorentz frame

*definition · developed* · also: local orthonormal frame, orthonormal LIF

A local inertial frame described with an orthonormal basis, so the metric components at the observer are exactly diag(-1,1,1,1); it is the frame in which an observer actually interprets measured components.

**How introduced:** Named in a margin note on Lesson 2 and returned to as Lesson 6 with the picture of an observer's lab built from short rigid rods.

**Prerequisites:** local inertial frame, Minkowski metric

**Notes:** Developed operationally in GA ch10.

**Where:** GA §6.1 p.71 (pdf 88); GA §6.4 p.76 (pdf 93); GA §6.4 p.77 (pdf 94)

### timelike geodesic as free-fall world line

*principle · introduced* · also: geodesic motion, geodesic

Freely falling particles move on timelike geodesics: world lines whose tangent is everywhere timelike and which are locally straight, i.e. straight time-axis world lines in each local inertial frame.

**How introduced:** Example 6.3 argues that since a particle at rest in SR moves along the time axis, the strong equivalence principle implies free-fall paths are timelike curves (the book loosely calls such curves geodesics); Lesson 1 and a margin note contrast this with a Newtonian force from the Sun.

**Prerequisites:** strong equivalence principle, timelike vector

**Notes:** The book's wording can suggest every timelike curve is a geodesic; only the unaccelerated ones are. Geodesics are developed in GA ch8-9.

**Where:** GA §6.1 p.70 (pdf 87); GA §6.1 p.71 (pdf 88)

### gravitational deflection of light

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: light bending, bending of starlight

The change in direction of a light ray passing a massive body. The equivalence principle alone forces some bending; full GR predicts 4GM/(b c^2) for impact parameter b, twice the value obtained from the equivalence principle or Newtonian corpuscles, the extra half coming from spatial curvature.

**How introduced:** Example 6.5 has a particle cross an accelerating rocket; in the rocket frame its path is a parabola, and replacing the particle by a photon and acceleration by gravity implies bending by angle about gx/c^2. A crude solar estimate follows, eqn (6.5).

**Prerequisites:** strong equivalence principle, uniformly accelerating reference frame

$$
\theta \approx gx/c^2
$$

$$
\theta \approx GM_\odot/(R_\odot c^2)
$$

**Notes:** The book states the full photon effect is three times the heuristic value; the standard result is a factor of two (see gaps_and_pitfalls). Done properly in GA ch24 §24.1.

**Where:** GA §6.1 p.71 (pdf 88); GA §6.1 p.72 (pdf 89)

### Mach's principle

*historical-idea · introduced* · also: fixed stars define inertia

The idea that inertial frames, and hence inertial forces, are determined by acceleration relative to the averaged distribution of all matter in the Universe rather than relative to absolute space.

**How introduced:** Example 6.6 poses how inertial frames can be defined without absolute space, gives Mach's answer, and sketches a speculative force law between inertial masses modelled on electromagnetism.

**Prerequisites:** inertial forces

$$
F_{\text{static}} \propto \alpha m_i m_j / r^2
$$

$$
F_{\text{accel}} \propto \beta m_1 m_2 / r
$$

**Notes:** Presented as an influence on Einstein; the chapter does not discuss how far GR actually realizes Mach's principle.

**Where:** GA §6.1 p.72 (pdf 89); GA §6.1 p.73 (pdf 90)

### inertial forces

*phenomenon · mention* · also: fictitious forces, non-inertial forces

Apparent forces proportional to mass, such as centrifugal force, that appear in Newton's laws written in accelerating or rotating frames.

**How introduced:** Motivates the Mach interlude; a margin note gives centrifugal force as the example and points to ch8.

**Prerequisites:** uniformly accelerating reference frame

**Notes:** Treated in GA ch8 §8.3.

**Where:** GA §6.1 p.73 (pdf 90)

### nonlinearity of gravitation

*principle · introduced* · also: gravity gravitates, self-interaction of the gravitational field

Because all mass-energy sources gravity, including interaction energy and the energy of the gravitational field itself, gravitational fields do not superpose linearly; for example gravitational waves scatter off one another, unlike light waves in vacuum.

**How introduced:** Example 6.6 uses it to explain why describing gravity as pairwise particle interactions becomes intractable; a margin note contrasts linear electromagnetism, whose waves are uncharged and pass through each other.

**Prerequisites:** mass-energy equivalence

**Where:** GA §6.1 p.73 (pdf 90)

### field description of gravity

*principle · introduced* · also: local field viewpoint

Describing an interaction through a field defined at each spacetime point, which each particle both sources and responds to locally, instead of direct influences between distant particles.

**How introduced:** Offered as the escape from the nonlinear many-body bookkeeping of the Machian approach; later sharpened by the claim that the field in GR is the metric itself.

**Prerequisites:** nonlinearity of gravitation

**Where:** GA §6.1 p.73 (pdf 90); GA §6.3 p.75 (pdf 92)

### principle of general covariance

*principle · core* · also: general covariance

Physical laws must be expressible in a form that is unchanged under arbitrary smooth coordinate transformations, which in practice means writing them as tensor equations with no preferred basis or components.

**How introduced:** Stated in the opening (spacetime acts only through the metric) and boxed in section 6.2 as the reason for the name 'general' relativity; illustrated by a law picking out the y-component and by the failed theory of Example 6.7.

**Prerequisites:** tensor, coordinate transformation

**Notes:** The book calls it a restatement of the equivalence principle; the physical content of general covariance by itself is debated (any theory can be written covariantly), a point not raised here.

**Where:** GA p.68 (pdf 85); GA §6.2 p.73 (pdf 90); GA §6.2 p.74 (pdf 91)

### manifestly covariant tensor equation

*definition · developed* · also: valid tensor equation, manifest covariance

An equation written entirely in terms of tensors with the same free indices on both sides, so that if it holds in one coordinate system it holds in all.

**How introduced:** Margin note to the Lesson 4 argument; motivated by contrasting F = dp/dt with a law that singles out one component.

**Prerequisites:** tensor, index notation

$$
F = \frac{dp}{dt}
$$

**Where:** GA §6.2 p.74 (pdf 91)

### failed scalar theory of gravity

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: relativistic Poisson equation, d'Alembertian Newtonian potential

The guess that relativistic gravity is a scalar potential obeying the wave equation box Phi = 4 pi G rho. It fails because the source rho (mass density) is not a Lorentz scalar: it is one component of the energy-momentum tensor, so the equation cannot hold in every inertial frame.

**How introduced:** Example 6.7 constructs it by replacing the Laplacian in Poisson's equation with the 4D d'Alembertian, then rejects it on transformation grounds; a margin note says the correct equation has curvature on the left and the energy-momentum tensor on the right.

**Prerequisites:** Poisson's equation for gravity, energy-momentum tensor, principle of general covariance

$$
\partial^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho
$$

**Notes:** Our addition, not the book's: scalar theories of this type (e.g. Nordstrom's) also fail observationally, predicting no light bending. The book rejects the equation on transformation grounds alone.

**Where:** GA §6.2 p.74 (pdf 91)

### minimal coupling via eta to g replacement

*technique · core* · also: Lesson 4, eta -> g rule, comma-goes-to-semicolon rule (partial)

The recipe for including gravity in non-gravitational physics: take a tensor law valid in special relativity and replace the Minkowski metric eta by the general metric g (and, as later chapters add, partial derivatives by covariant ones).

**How introduced:** Derived as a syllogism: SR laws hold in a LIF by equivalence, and a tensor law keeps its form in every coordinate system by covariance, so the only trace of gravity is the altered geometry encoded in g.

**Prerequisites:** strong equivalence principle, principle of general covariance, metric tensor

$$
\eta_{\mu\nu} \to g_{\mu\nu}(x)
$$

**Notes:** The need to replace derivatives with covariant derivatives is not mentioned here; it is the subject of GA ch7.

**Where:** GA §6.2 p.74 (pdf 91); GA §6.3 p.75 (pdf 92); GA §6.4 p.76 (pdf 93)

### Minkowski metric

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: eta, flat metric

The metric of flat spacetime with components diag(-1,1,1,1) in inertial Cartesian coordinates; described as the box of clocks and rulers of special relativity.

**How introduced:** Recalled in section 6.2 as the metric used in a LIF, to be upgraded to g.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor

$$
\eta_{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,1,1)
$$

**Where:** GA §6.2 p.74 (pdf 91); GA §6.4 p.76 (pdf 93)

### line element and interval along a curve

*definition · revisited* · also: path length, spacetime interval

The invariant length of a curve x^mu(lambda) computed by integrating the square root of the absolute value of g_{mu nu} (dx^mu/d lambda)(dx^nu/d lambda) along it.

**How introduced:** Section 6.3 presents the metric as a rulebook for distances and angles and writes the integral (6.7).

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor

$$
s=\int_a^b d\lambda\,\left|g_{\mu\nu}\frac{dx^\mu}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^\nu}{d\lambda}\right|^{1/2}
$$

**Where:** GA §6.3 p.75 (pdf 92)

### metric as a field

*principle · core* · also: metric field g(x), gravitational field is the metric

In GR the metric tensor is a dynamical field on spacetime, assigning a tensor g(x) to each event, and it is this field (not a force field) that carries gravity.

**How introduced:** Described as a machine that takes a spacetime point and returns the tensor there; a margin note warns against picturing gravity as a force field, and Lesson 5 codifies it.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, field description of gravity

**Where:** GA §6.3 p.75 (pdf 92); GA §6.3 p.76 (pdf 93)

### field equation for the metric

*law · introduced* · also: gravitational field equation, Einstein field equation (preview)

A differential equation that determines how the metric varies from point to point in terms of its sources, separating true gravitational effects from coordinate choices.

**How introduced:** Argued for because coordinate-independent geometric statements (circumference/diameter = pi, triangle angle sums) require the values of g at different points to be related; its form is deferred to ch13.

**Prerequisites:** metric as a field, Riemann curvature tensor

**Where:** GA p.67 (pdf 84); GA §6.3 p.75 (pdf 92)

### Riemann curvature tensor

*mathematical-object · mention* · also: Riemann tensor, curvature tensor R

A tensor built from the metric and its first and second derivatives whose non-vanishing signals intrinsic curvature of spacetime, which no coordinate change can remove.

**How introduced:** Named as the tensor that the field equation is built from and identified with true gravitation; definition deferred to ch11.

**Prerequisites:** metric as a field

**Where:** GA p.67 (pdf 84); GA §6.3 p.75 (pdf 92)

### correspondence limits of general relativity

*principle · introduced* · also: Newtonian limit, special-relativistic limit

Any relativistic theory of gravity must reproduce Newtonian gravity for weak fields and slow motion and reduce to special relativity when gravity vanishes; Newtonian mechanics sits at the corner where both limits are taken.

**How introduced:** Given as the one remaining clue for building the theory and displayed as a square of limits in Fig. 6.4.

**Prerequisites:** Minkowski metric

**Where:** GA §6.3 p.75 (pdf 92); GA §6.3 p.76 (pdf 93)

### frame of reference versus coordinate system

*definition · mention* · also: frame vs coordinates

A frame is a set of basis vectors defined at points, existing independently of any coordinate grid; coordinates are labels of events. Different frames can share coordinates and vice versa.

**How introduced:** A margin note in section 6.4 draws the distinction and contrasts a general LIF (coordinate conditions at a point, achievable by Riemann normal coordinates) with the freely falling frame (basis e_t tangent to a geodesic).

**Prerequisites:** basis vectors

**Where:** GA §6.4 p.76 (pdf 93)

### freely falling frame

*definition · introduced* · also: Fermi-type falling frame

A local frame whose time basis vector is the tangent to a free-fall geodesic; it remains locally inertial for the whole of that world line, so it is flat only in a small spatial region but for long stretches of proper time.

**How introduced:** A margin note separates it from a general LIF defined at a single event; the main text calls it the key example of a LIF.

**Prerequisites:** local inertial frame, timelike geodesic as free-fall world line

**Notes:** Developed in GA ch10 §10.4.

**Where:** GA §6.4 p.76 (pdf 93)

### flat spacetime

*definition · revisited* · also: Minkowski spacetime, flatness

Spacetime with no curvature, in which global coordinates exist making the metric equal to eta everywhere; used by observers not subject to gravity.

**How introduced:** Opening sentence of section 6.4.

**Prerequisites:** Minkowski metric

**Where:** GA §6.4 p.76 (pdf 93)

### local flatness theorem

*theorem · core* · also: local flatness, existence of local inertial coordinates

At any point P of a spacetime with Lorentzian metric one can choose coordinates such that g_{mu nu}(P) = eta_{mu nu} and all first partial derivatives of g_{mu nu} vanish at P; the second derivatives cannot in general all be set to zero, their residue being curvature.

**How introduced:** Motivated by the equivalence principle; the first condition justified by diagonalizing a symmetric matrix, the second asserted with a pointer to Riemann normal coordinates in ch35.

**Prerequisites:** local inertial frame, metric tensor, Riemann curvature tensor

$$
g_{\mu\nu}(x^\alpha(\mathcal P))=\eta_{\mu\nu}
$$

$$
\partial g_{\mu\nu}/\partial x^\alpha|_{\mathcal P}=0
$$

**Notes:** No counting argument (40 first derivatives vs 40 free second-order coordinate coefficients; 100 second derivatives vs 80, leaving 20 curvature components) is given here.

**Where:** GA §6.4 p.76 (pdf 93)

### Riemann normal coordinates

*technique · mention* · also: RNC

A coordinate system built from geodesics radiating from a point, in which the metric is Minkowskian and its first derivatives vanish at that point.

**How introduced:** Named in a margin note as the construction that realizes a general LIF; deferred to ch35.

**Prerequisites:** local flatness theorem

**Where:** GA §6.4 p.76 (pdf 93)

### Lorentz signature

*convention · mention* · also: signature (-,+,+,+)

The pattern of one negative and three positive eigenvalues of the metric, which is preserved by any coordinate change and must be matched when normalizing a local frame to eta.

**How introduced:** Margin note to the local flatness theorem.

**Prerequisites:** Minkowski metric

**Where:** GA §6.4 p.76 (pdf 93)

### proper time

*physical-quantity · revisited* · also: tau, clock time

The time recorded by an ideal clock along its world line, given by -d tau^2 = g_{alpha beta} dx^alpha dx^beta; for a clock at rest in coordinates it is (-g_00)^{1/2} dt.

**How introduced:** Example 6.8 upgrades the SR relation between proper and coordinate time to the curved metric.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor

$$
-d\tau^2 = g_{\alpha\beta}dx^\alpha dx^\beta
$$

**Where:** GA §6.5 p.77 (pdf 94)

### Newtonian limit of the metric

*solution-or-model · revisited* · also: weak-field metric, g_00 = -(1+2Phi)

For weak static fields the metric has g_00 = -(1 + 2 Phi) with Phi = -GM/r for a point mass (and g_ij = (1 - 2 Phi) delta_ij), valid when GM/(r c^2) << 1.

**How introduced:** Imported from eqn (5.22) to evaluate g_00 near a star in Example 6.8; the authors caution the result assumes the Newtonian limit.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, gravitational potential

$$
g_{00} = -(1-2GM/r)
$$

**Where:** GA §6.5 p.77 (pdf 94); GA §6.5 p.78 (pdf 95)

### gravitational time dilation

*phenomenon · core* · also: gravitational time-dilation factor, clocks run slow in a potential well

A clock held at rest deeper in a gravitational potential ticks more slowly, as compared by exchange of signals, than an identical clock far away; for a static clock outside a spherical mass d tau = (1 - 2GM/(r c^2))^{1/2} dt.

**How introduced:** Derived twice in section 6.5: from g_00 of the weak-field metric, and by dropping a third clock from far away and applying SR time dilation at speed v = sqrt(2GM/r).

**Prerequisites:** proper time, Newtonian limit of the metric, strong equivalence principle

$$
d\tau = (1-2GM/r)^{1/2}dt
$$

$$
d\tau = dt\sqrt{1-r_S/r}
$$

$$
d\tau = dt\sqrt{1-(v_{esc}/c)^2}
$$

**Notes:** Formula (6.11) is actually exact for static observers in Schwarzschild spacetime, eqn (6.14), even though here it is obtained in the weak-field limit.

**Where:** GA §6.5 p.77 (pdf 94); GA §6.5 p.78 (pdf 95)

### escape velocity

*physical-quantity · mention* · also: v_esc

The minimum speed at distance r from a mass M that lets a projectile reach infinity, v_esc^2 = 2GM/r in Newtonian mechanics.

**How introduced:** Used to reinterpret the time-dilation factor as the SR gamma for the escape speed, and in the clock-drop derivation as the speed of a clock falling from rest at infinity.

$$
v_{esc}^2 = 2GM/r
$$

**Where:** GA §6.5 p.77 (pdf 94); GA §6.5 p.78 (pdf 95)

### Schwarzschild radius

*physical-quantity · mention* · also: r_S, gravitational radius 2GM/c^2

The radius r_S = 2GM/c^2 associated with a mass M; the static time-dilation factor is sqrt(1 - r_S/r).

**How introduced:** Introduced as a convenient rewriting of eqn (6.11), with a pointer to Part IV.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational time dilation

$$
r_S = 2GM/c^2
$$

**Where:** GA §6.5 p.77 (pdf 94)

### gravitational redshift

*phenomenon · core* · also: gravitational frequency shift, Einstein shift

Light emitted by a source deeper in a gravitational potential is received by a higher observer at lower frequency (longer wavelength), by the same factor as gravitational time dilation.

**How introduced:** Obtained by reinterpreting the clock argument in terms of atomic transition frequencies; applied to Earth, neutron stars and GPS, and to a millimetre-scale strontium clock experiment in Exercise 6.2.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational time dilation

$$
\nu_\infty/\nu_r = (1-2GM/(rc^2))^{1/2}
$$

**Where:** GA §6.5 p.77 (pdf 94); GA §6.5 p.78 (pdf 95); GA p.79 (pdf 96)

### Doppler versus gravitational versus cosmological redshift

*definition · introduced* · also: three kinds of redshift

Doppler redshift comes from relative velocity of source and observer in flat spacetime; gravitational redshift from differences in the metric between emission and reception points in a static field; cosmological redshift from the expansion of space between emission and reception.

**How introduced:** A closing paragraph of section 6.5 lists the three and groups the latter two as metric effects.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational redshift

**Where:** GA §6.5 p.78 (pdf 95)

### Global Positioning System relativistic corrections

*experiment-or-observation · introduced* · also: GPS clock corrections

Satellite navigation requires correcting orbiting clocks for special-relativistic time dilation due to their motion (clocks run slow) and for gravitational blueshift due to their weaker gravitational potential (clocks run fast).

**How introduced:** Cited in section 6.5 as the everyday technology where tiny gravitational redshift matters; Exercise 6.3 asks for the combined factor for a satellite.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational time dilation, time dilation

**Where:** GA §6.5 p.78 (pdf 95); GA p.79 (pdf 96)

### spatial metric of a stationary spacetime

*technique · introduced* · also: radar distance, measured length in a metric with g_0i

In a metric with time-space cross terms, the distance measured by bouncing light between neighbouring points is dl^2 = (g_ij - g_0i g_0j / g_00) dx^i dx^j.

**How introduced:** Exercise 6.5 builds it from the round-trip light time between A and B (Fig. 6.6).

**Prerequisites:** line element and interval along a curve, proper time

$$
dl^2=\left(g_{ij}-\frac{g_{0i}g_{0j}}{g_{00}}\right)dx^idx^j
$$

**Where:** GA p.79 (pdf 96); GA p.80 (pdf 97)

### clock synchronization in stationary spacetimes

*technique · introduced* · also: Einstein synchronization with g_0i

Defining simultaneity with light signals in a metric with g_0i gives a time shift -g_0i dx^i/g_00 between neighbouring clocks; around a closed loop the shifts need not cancel, so global synchronization can fail (e.g. on a rotating disc).

**How introduced:** Exercise 6.6 develops it and applies it to the rotating frame metric.

**Prerequisites:** spatial metric of a stationary spacetime

$$
x^0-\int \frac{g_{0i}}{g_{00}}dx^i
$$

**Where:** GA p.80 (pdf 97)

### Sagnac effect

*phenomenon · mention* · also: Sagnac interference shift

Counter-propagating light beams around a rotating closed loop acquire a travel-time difference proportional to the rotation rate and enclosed area, giving an interference fringe shift of 4 pi Omega r^2/(lambda c) for a circular loop.

**How introduced:** Final part of Exercise 6.6, derived from the synchronization discrepancy.

**Prerequisites:** clock synchronization in stationary spacetimes

$$
\Delta N = 4\pi\Omega r^2/\lambda
$$

**Where:** GA p.80 (pdf 97)

## Key equations

### (6.1) Newtonian equation of motion with two masses · central · GA §6.1 p.68 (pdf 85)

$$
m_i \ddot{\vec x} = m_g \vec g(\vec x)
$$

Distinguishes the mass that resists acceleration from the mass that couples to gravity; equality makes the acceleration independent of the body.

**Symbols:** m_i inertial mass; m_g gravitational mass; g(x) gravitational field

### (6.2) Equation of motion of one particle in an interacting cloud · derivation-step · GA §6.1 p.69 (pdf 86)

$$
m\frac{d^2\vec x}{dt^2} = m\vec g + \sum_{p=1}^{N-1}\vec F(\vec x-\vec x_p)
$$

One test particle feels the uniform gravitational field plus non-gravitational forces from the other N-1 particles that depend only on separations.

**Symbols:** x_p positions of the other particles; F pair force

### (6.3) Transformation to the freely falling frame · derivation-step · GA §6.1 p.69 (pdf 86)

$$
\vec x' = \vec x - \tfrac12\vec g t^2,\qquad t=t'
$$

Galilean change to coordinates whose origin falls with acceleration g.

**Symbols:** primed quantities refer to the falling observer O'

### (6.4) Equation of motion in the falling frame · central · GA §6.1 p.69 (pdf 86)

$$
m\frac{d^2\vec x'}{dt'^2} = \sum_{p=1}^{N-1}\vec F(\vec x'-\vec x_p')
$$

The uniform field has disappeared; the particle obeys the gravity-free law, demonstrating the (weak) equivalence principle.

### Parabolic path in the rocket frame · derivation-step · GA §6.1 p.71 (pdf 88)

$$
x = x_0 + vt,\quad y = y_0 - \tfrac12 g t^2\ \Rightarrow\ y = y_0 - \frac{g x^2}{2v^2}
$$

A particle moving straight in the inertial frame traces a downward parabola in a rocket accelerating upward at g.

**Symbols:** v horizontal speed; (x_0,y_0) entry point

### Local light-bending angle · supporting · GA §6.1 p.71 (pdf 88)

$$
\theta \approx \left|\frac{dy}{dx}\right| = \frac{g x}{c^2}
$$

Setting v = c: after crossing a horizontal distance x in an effective field g, a light ray tilts by this angle.

**Symbols:** g acceleration or field; x horizontal distance

### (6.5) Crude deflection of starlight by the Sun · central · GA §6.1 p.72 (pdf 89)

$$
\theta \approx \frac{g R_\odot}{c^2} = \frac{G M_\odot}{R_\odot c^2}
$$

Order-of-magnitude deflection taking field GM/R^2 acting over a path length of order R; the true GR value is four times this.

**Symbols:** M_sun, R_sun solar mass and radius

### Machian inertial force ansatz · supporting · GA §6.1 p.73 (pdf 90)

$$
F \sim \frac{\alpha m_i m_j}{r^2} + \frac{\beta m_1 m_2}{r} + \dots
$$

Speculative electromagnetism-inspired form: a static inverse-square part identified with gravity plus a 1/r part for relative acceleration.

**Symbols:** alpha, beta constants

### Geometric form of Newton's second law · supporting · GA §6.2 p.74 (pdf 91)

$$
\boldsymbol F = \frac{d\boldsymbol p}{dt}
$$

Relation between vectors with no preferred basis; contrasted with a non-covariant law that singles out the y-component.

### d'Alembertian · supporting · GA §6.2 p.74 (pdf 91)

$$
\partial^2 = \partial_\mu\partial^\mu = -\partial_t^2 + \vec\nabla^2
$$

Lorentz-invariant generalization of the Laplacian used to build the failed theory.

**Symbols:** c = 1 units

### (6.6) Failed relativistic Poisson equation · central · GA §6.2 p.74 (pdf 91)

$$
\partial^2\Phi = -\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\Phi}{\partial t^2} + \vec\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho
$$

Tempting wave-equation upgrade of Newtonian gravity; rejected because rho is not a scalar under Lorentz transformations.

**Symbols:** Phi potential; rho mass density

### Lesson 4 replacement rule · central · GA §6.2 p.74 (pdf 91)

$$
\eta_{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,1,1)\ \longrightarrow\ g_{\mu\nu}(x)
$$

Gravity enters tensor laws by replacing the constant Minkowski metric with a position-dependent metric.

### (6.7) Interval along a curve · supporting · GA §6.3 p.75 (pdf 92)

$$
s = \int_a^b d\lambda\,\left|g_{\mu\nu}\frac{dx^\mu}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^\nu}{d\lambda}\right|^{1/2}
$$

The metric turns a parametrized curve into an invariant length or proper time.

**Symbols:** lambda curve parameter

### (6.8) Local flatness condition 1 · central · GA §6.4 p.76 (pdf 93)

$$
g_{\mu\nu}\big(x = x^\alpha(\mathcal P)\big) = \eta_{\mu\nu}
$$

At any single point, coordinates can be chosen to make the metric Minkowskian.

**Symbols:** P chosen event

### (6.9) Local flatness condition 2 · central · GA §6.4 p.76 (pdf 93)

$$
\left.\frac{\partial g_{\mu\nu}}{\partial x^\alpha}\right|_{x=x^\alpha(\mathcal P)} = 0
$$

First derivatives of the metric can also be made to vanish at that point; second derivatives cannot, and encode curvature.

### (6.10) Proper time of a static clock · derivation-step · GA §6.5 p.77 (pdf 94)

$$
-d\tau^2 = g_{\alpha\beta}\,dx^\alpha dx^\beta = g_{00}\,dt^2
$$

For a clock at rest in the coordinates only the time-time metric component contributes.

**Symbols:** tau proper time; t coordinate time

### (6.11) Gravitational time dilation · central · GA §6.5 p.77 (pdf 94)

$$
d\tau = (-g_{00})^{1/2}dt = \left(1-\frac{2GM}{r}\right)^{1/2}dt
$$

A static clock at radius r ticks slower than coordinate (far-away) time; in SI units the factor is [1 - 2GM/(c^2 r)]^{1/2}.

**Symbols:** c = 1; M mass of the star; r radial distance; g_00 time-time metric component

### Escape-velocity form · supporting · GA §6.5 p.77 (pdf 94)

$$
d\tau = dt\sqrt{1-(v_{esc}/c)^2},\qquad v_{esc}^2 = 2GM/r
$$

The time-dilation factor equals the SR gamma factor evaluated at the escape speed.

### (6.12) Schwarzschild-radius form · supporting · GA §6.5 p.77 (pdf 94)

$$
d\tau = dt\sqrt{1-\frac{r_S}{r}},\qquad r_S = \frac{2GM}{c^2}
$$

Same factor expressed with the Schwarzschild radius.

**Symbols:** r_S Schwarzschild radius

### SR time dilation in the clock-drop argument · derivation-step · GA §6.5 p.78 (pdf 95)

$$
dt = \gamma\,d\tau,\qquad \gamma = (1-v^2)^{-1/2},\qquad v^2 = 2GM/r
$$

Kinetic energy gained in falling from rest at infinity fixes the speed; the SR gamma between falling and static clocks supplies the factor.

**Symbols:** c = 1

### (6.13) Time dilation rederived · supporting · GA §6.5 p.78 (pdf 95)

$$
d\tau = \left(1-\frac{2GM}{r}\right)^{1/2}dt
$$

Result of the falling-clock argument, identical to (6.11).

### Weak-field expansion · supporting · GA §6.5 p.78 (pdf 95)

$$
\left(1-\frac{2GM}{Rc^2}\right)^{1/2} \approx 1-\frac{GM}{Rc^2}
$$

Binomial approximation of the time-dilation factor when GM/(Rc^2) << 1 (the book writes 1 - GM/R in c = 1 units).

### (6.14) Schwarzschild line element · supporting · GA p.79 (pdf 96)

$$
ds^2 = -\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)dt^2 + \left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}dr^2 + r^2\left(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)
$$

Metric outside a spherically symmetric star, given in Exercise 6.4 for comparing clocks at two radii.

**Symbols:** G = c = 1

### Satellite versus polar clock rate (Exercise 6.3) · supporting · GA p.79 (pdf 96)

$$
\frac{d\tau_{sat}}{d\tau_{pole}} \approx 1 + \frac{GM}{c^2}\left[\frac1R - \frac{3}{2r}\right]
$$

Combines the gravitational blueshift of the higher clock with the SR slowing due to orbital motion (v^2 = GM/r for a circular orbit); the two rates agree when r = 3R/2.

**Symbols:** R planet radius; r orbit radius

### (6.15) Coordinate time for a light signal in a general metric · derivation-step · GA p.80 (pdf 97)

$$
dt = \frac{1}{g_{00}}\left\{-g_{0i}dx^i \pm \left[(g_{0i}g_{0j}-g_{ij}g_{00})dx^idx^j\right]^{1/2}\right\}
$$

Solving ds^2 = 0 for dt gives the two coordinate-time intervals for a null displacement dx^i (future- and past-directed).

**Symbols:** i, j spatial indices

### (6.16) Round-trip time for a radar signal · derivation-step · GA p.80 (pdf 97)

$$
d\tau = -\frac{2}{g_{00}}\left[(g_{0i}g_{0j}-g_{ij}g_{00})dx^idx^j\right]^{1/2}
$$

Round-trip interval for light from A to B and back. As printed this is the coordinate-time round trip; the proper time at A needs an extra factor (-g_00)^{1/2}, i.e. 2[...]^{1/2}/(-g_00)^{1/2}, which is what yields (6.17).

### (6.17) Spatial metric from radar ranging · supporting · GA p.80 (pdf 97)

$$
dl^2 = \left(g_{ij}-\frac{g_{0i}g_{0j}}{g_{00}}\right)dx^idx^j
$$

Physical distance between neighbouring points in a stationary spacetime, as measured by light round trips.

### (6.18) Synchronized time on A's world line · supporting · GA p.80 (pdf 97)

$$
x^0 - \int \frac{g_{0i}}{g_{00}}\,dx^i
$$

Time on A simultaneous with event x^0 on B, defined as midway between emission and reception.

### (6.19) Synchronization discrepancy around a rotating loop · supporting · GA p.80 (pdf 97)

$$
\Delta t = \oint \frac{\Omega r^2}{1-\Omega^2 r^2}\,d\phi \approx 2\pi\Omega r^2
$$

Going once around a circle in a rotating frame, the synchronization offsets accumulate to a non-zero total (the printed integral omits d phi).

**Symbols:** Omega angular velocity; r radius; c = 1

### (6.20) Sagnac fringe shift · supporting · GA p.80 (pdf 97)

$$
\Delta N = \frac{4\pi\Omega r^2}{\lambda}
$$

Interference shift between counter-propagating beams in a rotating circular fibre (divide by c in SI units).

**Symbols:** lambda wavelength

## Worked examples

### Example 6.1 · intro · GA §6.1 p.69 (pdf 86)

**Problem:** Show that a uniform gravitational field can be removed from the equations of motion of a particle in a cloud of N interacting test particles by moving to a freely falling frame.

**Method:** Write Newton's law with a uniform gravity term plus pairwise forces depending on separations; apply the Galilean accelerating transformation x' = x - g t^2/2; the second time derivative subtracts g while separations are unchanged.

**Key insight:** Because gravity gives every particle the same acceleration (m_i = m_g), it can be absorbed into the frame; interactions that depend only on relative positions are blind to the change.

**Result:** Equation (6.4): the particle obeys the gravity-free equation with only interparticle forces.

**Concepts:** weak equivalence principle, uniformly accelerating reference frame

### Example 6.2 · intro · GA §6.1 p.69 (pdf 86)

**Problem:** Use a mass suspended by springs inside a box (Fig. 6.1) to illustrate the equivalence principle.

**Method:** Describe how acceleration in +x shifts the mass towards -x, and note that a gravitational field produces an identical displacement.

**Key insight:** An accelerometer measures the combination of acceleration and gravity, not either separately; in free fall it reads zero.

**Concepts:** weak equivalence principle

### Example 6.3 · intro · GA §6.1 p.70 (pdf 87)

**Problem:** Deduce from special relativity what path freely falling particles follow in the presence of gravity.

**Method:** In SR a particle at rest moves along its time axis, a timelike straight line; by the strong equivalence principle the same holds in the falling LIF, so globally free fall traces timelike curves that are locally straight.

**Key insight:** The equivalence principle lets a gravity-free SR fact become a statement about gravity: free fall is geodesic motion.

**Result:** Free-falling particles follow timelike geodesics.

**Concepts:** strong equivalence principle, timelike geodesic as free-fall world line

### Example 6.4 · intro · GA §6.1 p.70 (pdf 87)

**Problem:** An astronaut feels pressed into bed on waking: is the ship accelerating or near a planet?

**Method:** Release two test particles moving initially parallel; watch whether their separation changes.

**Key insight:** A planet's field is inhomogeneous, so tidal convergence or divergence reveals gravity, while a pure acceleration leaves the separation fixed.

**Result:** Any change in separation signals a tidal force and hence real gravity.

**Concepts:** tidal force, homogeneous gravitational field equivalence

### Example 6.5 · standard · GA §6.1 p.71 (pdf 88)

**Problem:** Show using an accelerating rocket that a gravitational field bends light.

**Method:** In the inertial frame a particle crosses the rocket in a straight line; transform to the rocket frame to get the parabola y = y0 - g x^2/(2v^2); invoke equivalence to replace acceleration by a gravitational field; set v = c to get a tilt angle |dy/dx| = g x/c^2.

**Key insight:** Anything that moves straight in an inertial frame curves in an accelerated one, and equivalence transfers this to gravity, so even light must bend; the local argument, however, captures only the time-part of curvature.

**Result:** Bending angle about g x/c^2; authors state the full effect near a spherical mass is larger because spatial curvature also contributes.

**Concepts:** gravitational deflection of light, uniformly accelerating reference frame, strong equivalence principle

### Example · intro · GA §6.1 p.72 (pdf 89)

**Problem:** Crude estimate of how much the Sun deflects starlight that grazes its limb. In the printed layout this is still inside the Example 6.5 box, which closes after the factor-of-4 remark.

**Method:** Take the field at the surface g = GM/R^2, assume it acts over a path of order R, insert into theta ~ g x/c^2 and discard numerical factors.

**Key insight:** Dimensional reasoning with the equivalence-principle result already captures the scaling GM/(R c^2); only the numerical factor needs full GR.

**Result:** theta ~ GM_sun/(R_sun c^2), eqn (6.5), about 2.1e-6 rad or 0.44 arcsec (our evaluation); the true value is four times larger, about 1.75 arcsec.

**Concepts:** gravitational deflection of light

### Example 6.6 · standard · GA §6.1 p.73 (pdf 90)

**Problem:** How can inertial frames be defined without absolute space, and what would a Machian theory of inertial forces look like?

**Method:** Adopt Mach's criterion of non-acceleration relative to distant matter; model inertial interactions by analogy with electromagnetism (static inverse-square term, 1/r term for relative acceleration); identify the static part with gravity via equivalence; then observe that mass-energy including interaction energy gravitates, making a pairwise description nonlinear and unmanageable.

**Key insight:** Nonlinearity is what pushes gravitational theory from action between particles to local fields.

**Concepts:** Mach's principle, inertial forces, nonlinearity of gravitation, field description of gravity

### Example 6.7 · standard · GA §6.2 p.74 (pdf 91)

**Problem:** Test whether upgrading Poisson's equation to a Lorentz-invariant wave equation gives an acceptable theory of gravity.

**Method:** Replace the Laplacian with the d'Alembertian to get eqn (6.6), then check transformation properties of each side.

**Key insight:** The left side transforms as a scalar but the mass density rho is a component of the energy-momentum tensor, so the equation holds only in one frame; a correct theory needs a tensor source and a tensorial left side involving curvature.

**Result:** The equation is rejected, though it points towards a tensor equation with energy-momentum as source.

**Concepts:** failed scalar theory of gravity, principle of general covariance, manifestly covariant tensor equation

### Example 6.8 · intro · GA §6.5 p.77 (pdf 94)

**Problem:** Find how the ticks of a clock at rest at distance r from a star compare with coordinate time.

**Method:** Write -d tau^2 = g_00 dt^2 for a static clock; use g_00 = -(1 - 2GM/r) from the weak-field metric; take the square root; interpret the factor using escape velocity and the Schwarzschild radius.

**Key insight:** Gravitational time dilation follows from nothing more than one metric component: the metric is what connects coordinate labels to what clocks read.

**Result:** d tau = (1 - 2GM/r)^{1/2} dt = dt sqrt(1 - r_S/r), eqns (6.11)-(6.12).

**Concepts:** gravitational time dilation, proper time, Newtonian limit of the metric, Schwarzschild radius, escape velocity

### Example · standard · GA §6.5 p.78 (pdf 95)

**Problem:** Rederive gravitational time dilation without the metric, using three identical clocks and a star (Fig. 6.5).

**Method:** Clock 3 is released at rest next to distant clock 1 so they agree; it falls freely and is locally inertial so its tick interval is unchanged; Newtonian energy conservation gives v^2 = 2GM/r when it passes static clock 2; SR time dilation with gamma(v) relates the two, giving (6.13).

**Key insight:** Equivalence lets a freely falling clock act as an unbiased intermediary, converting a gravitational comparison into an SR velocity comparison.

**Result:** d tau = (1 - 2GM/r)^{1/2} dt, eqn (6.13).

**Concepts:** gravitational time dilation, local inertial frame, escape velocity


## Analogies and intuitions

### Springs-in-a-box accelerometer → weak equivalence principle · strong · GA §6.1 p.69 (pdf 86)

A mass held by springs is displaced equally by acceleration and by gravity, so a box observer cannot separate them.

**Where it breaks down:** It illustrates only mechanics; in free fall it reads zero even near a planet, and a large box with two masses would detect tides, which the analogy does not show.

**App idea:** Pair the box demo with a two-box version separated vertically that shows differing readings in a planetary field.

### A car accelerating through vertical rain → gravitational deflection of light · useful · GA §6.1 p.71 (pdf 88)

Rain falling straight down appears to slant and curve on the windows of an accelerating car, the same kinematics as the rocket crossing rotated by 90 degrees.

**Where it breaks down:** Nothing relativistic or gravitational is involved; it conveys only the frame-dependence of trajectories, not why light obeys it.

**App idea:** Short animation morphing the rain-on-car view into the rocket-and-photon view by a 90-degree rotation.

### Metric as a box of clocks and rulers / a rulebook → metric as a field · useful · GA §6.2 p.74 (pdf 91)

The metric is what tells an observer how to convert coordinate displacements into measured times, lengths and angles.

**Where it breaks down:** Suggests a rigid measuring apparatus; in curved spacetime there is no global set of rulers, only local ones, and coordinates themselves carry no metric meaning.

### A field as a machine: put in a point, get out a tensor → metric as a field · useful · GA §6.3 p.75 (pdf 92)

Clarifies that g(x) is an assignment of a tensor to each spacetime point, with a field equation constraining how the outputs vary.

**Where it breaks down:** Does not convey that the field is dynamical and self-interacting.

**App idea:** Hover-probe over a 2D spacetime patch returning the local metric matrix and drawing the corresponding light cone.

### Electromagnetism: Coulomb's law and radiation from accelerated charges → Mach's principle · weak · GA §6.1 p.73 (pdf 90)

Suggests a static inverse-square inertial interaction plus a 1/r term for relative acceleration, as with charges.

**Where it breaks down:** Gravity is not a vector-like force theory, and GR does not straightforwardly implement Mach's principle; the analogy is speculative.

### Linear electromagnetism versus nonlinear gravity → nonlinearity of gravitation · strong · GA §6.1 p.73 (pdf 90)

Light waves pass through each other because they carry no charge, while gravitational waves carry energy and therefore source further gravity.

**Where it breaks down:** Photon-photon scattering exists quantum mechanically; the contrast is classical.

**App idea:** Side-by-side wave-packet collision: EM packets superpose and pass; gravitational packets pick up a small scattered component as amplitude increases.

### Newton's Sun pulling the Earth versus the Earth falling freely along a geodesic → timelike geodesic as free-fall world line · strong · GA §6.1 p.71 (pdf 88)

Reframes orbital motion as force-free free fall along a spacetime path that happens to circle the Sun.

**Where it breaks down:** The spatial orbit is not itself a geodesic of space; the geodesic is the helical world line in spacetime.

**App idea:** 3D spacetime helix of Earth's world line around the Sun's world line, with a toggle showing the Newtonian force arrow disappear in the geodesic view.

### A laboratory built from short rigid rods → local Lorentz frame · useful · GA §6.4 p.77 (pdf 94)

Pictures measurement as done with a small orthonormal frame at the observer's location.

**Where it breaks down:** Rigid rods are an idealization and must be short relative to the curvature scale.

### Escape speed as a stand-in for gravitational time dilation → gravitational time dilation · useful · GA §6.5 p.77 (pdf 94)

The gravitational factor equals the SR factor at the speed needed to escape to infinity, linking gravity to familiar kinematic dilation.

**Where it breaks down:** The coincidence is special to Schwarzschild static observers; it is not a general rule for arbitrary metrics.

### A law that singles out the y-component 'smells a rat' → principle of general covariance · useful · GA §6.2 p.74 (pdf 91)

An equation privileging one axis would look different after a rotation, showing why laws must be built from geometric objects.

**Where it breaks down:** Rotational covariance is a much weaker requirement than covariance under arbitrary coordinate changes.


## Misconceptions addressed

### Gravity and acceleration are exactly the same thing everywhere, so gravity is 'just' a choice of frame. · GA §6.1 p.70 (pdf 87)

**Correction:** The equivalence holds only in a small enough region; real fields vary in space and their tidal effects cannot be removed by any frame, which is what curvature captures.

**Why tempting:** The chapter first stresses sameness (Einstein's insight that gravity and an accelerating coordinate system are the same thing, the falling astronaut, the spring accelerometer) and only afterwards brings in the tidal caveat.

### A uniform gravitational field is a physically real gravitational field detectable by local experiments. · GA §6.1 p.70 (pdf 87)

**Correction:** A strictly uniform field can be transformed away everywhere at once; it has no invariant content.

**Why tempting:** Textbook problems routinely treat g as uniform near Earth's surface.

### The rocket/equivalence argument gives the full deflection of starlight by the Sun. · GA §6.1 p.71 (pdf 88)

**Correction:** It gives only the contribution from the time-time part of the metric; spatial curvature adds an equal amount, so the observed deflection is twice the equivalence-principle value (the book says three times, which is incorrect).

**Why tempting:** The argument is clean and yields the correct scaling GM/(bc^2).

### Light bending in the accelerating rocket is a special-relativistic effect. · GA §6.1 p.71 (pdf 88)

**Correction:** The rocket is barely moving; the bending is pure kinematics of an accelerated frame, as with rain seen from an accelerating car.

**Why tempting:** Light and relativity are strongly associated in learners' minds.

### 'General relativity' is a generalization of the relativity principle to a broader class of relative motions. · GA §6.2 p.73 (pdf 90)

**Correction:** The name reflects general covariance; the theory is a theory of gravity, not a more general relativity of motion.

**Why tempting:** The names 'special' and 'general' suggest a hierarchy of relativity principles.

### Making Poisson's equation Lorentz-invariant with a d'Alembertian is enough for relativistic gravity. · GA §6.2 p.74 (pdf 91)

**Correction:** Mass density is a component of the energy-momentum tensor, so the source is not a scalar; a tensor equation with curvature is needed.

**Why tempting:** The same trick works for electromagnetic potentials in Lorenz gauge.

### Gravity is a force field permeating spacetime, like an electric field. · GA §6.3 p.75 (pdf 92)

**Correction:** In GR the gravitational field is the metric itself; free fall involves no force.

**Why tempting:** Newtonian intuition and the name 'gravitational field'.

### With a clever enough coordinate choice you can make spacetime flat around a point to all orders. · GA §6.4 p.76 (pdf 93)

**Correction:** Only the metric and its first derivatives can be matched to Minkowski at a point; second derivatives carry curvature that no coordinate change removes.

**Why tempting:** The equivalence principle seems to say gravity can always be transformed away.

### A frame of reference and a coordinate system are the same thing. · GA §6.4 p.76 (pdf 93)

**Correction:** A frame is a choice of basis vectors at points; coordinates label events; they are independent choices.

**Why tempting:** In SR inertial frames come with their own Cartesian coordinates, blurring the distinction.

### Gravitational redshift is a kind of Doppler shift. · GA §6.5 p.78 (pdf 95)

**Correction:** Doppler redshift comes from relative velocity; gravitational (and cosmological) redshift arises from the metric differing between emission and reception, with source and receiver at rest.

**Why tempting:** Both show up as frequency shifts of spectral lines and can mimic each other in data.

### Astronauts in orbit are weightless because gravity is negligible at their altitude. · GA §6.1 p.68 (pdf 85)

**Correction:** Gravity there is only about 10 percent weaker than at the surface; they feel weightless because they are in free fall.

**Why tempting:** Everyday language of 'zero gravity' and the word 'weightless'.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Every timelike curve is a geodesic. · GA §6.1 p.70 (pdf 87)

**Correction:** Timelike curves include accelerated world lines; only those with zero 4-acceleration (locally straight) are geodesics.

**Why tempting:** Example 6.3 phrases the conclusion in a way that seems to equate the two.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### A clock near a massive body 'feels' its time slow down locally. · GA §6.5 p.77 (pdf 94)

**Correction:** Locally every clock ticks at its normal proper rate; time dilation is a comparison between separated observers via signals.

**Why tempting:** Popular accounts describe time 'flowing slower' near a mass as if it were a local sensation.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### GPS satellite clocks run slow because of their orbital speed, so the GR correction is minor. · GA §6.5 p.78 (pdf 95)

**Correction:** The gravitational blueshift (clocks fast by about 45 microseconds per day) outweighs the velocity effect (slow by about 7), for a net gain of about 38 microseconds per day (our figures, not the book's).

**Why tempting:** Special-relativistic time dilation is the better-known effect.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Falling astronaut who cannot feel her weight · GA §6.1 p.68 (pdf 85)

**Setup:** An astronaut in free fall watches her own coordinates in her rest frame and notices no change.

**Lesson:** Free fall removes the local sensation and local evidence of gravity.

**App idea:** First-person falling-lab view where released objects float; a toggle reveals the external view with the lab and objects falling together.

### Waking up in a spaceship · GA §6.1 p.70 (pdf 87)

### Particle crossing an accelerating rocket · GA §6.1 p.71 (pdf 88)

**Setup:** A particle or photon enters one window of a rocket accelerating at g and exits lower on the opposite wall.

**Setup:** Passengers thrown by a jerking train are imagined as being acted on by the distant stars relative to which the train accelerates.

**Lesson:** Mach's view makes inertial forces an interaction with distant matter, motivating Einstein.

### Dropping a clock from far away · GA §6.5 p.78 (pdf 95)

**Setup:** Three identical clocks: one far from a star, one held at radius r, and one released from beside the first to fall past the second.

**Lesson:** A freely falling clock links a gravitational rate comparison to an SR velocity comparison, reproducing the time-dilation factor.

**Setup:** Observer A bounces a light signal off B and uses the round-trip time for distance and the midpoint for simultaneity; repeated around a rotating loop.

- **Ocean tides and the Earth tide at CERN's LEP collider** (observation, GA §6.1 p.70 (pdf 87)): Tidal forces from Sun and Moon raise ocean tides and also slightly deform the solid Earth; the Earth tide changed the circumference of the Large Electron-Positron collider by millimetres, which had to be corrected for. Key numbers: LEP circumference about 27 km; stretching of a few mm (book's figures)
- **Deflection of starlight by the Sun** (numerical-estimate, GA §6.1 p.72 (pdf 89)): Crude equivalence-principle estimate theta ~ GM/(R c^2) from eqn (6.5), stated to be low by a factor of 4 relative to GR. Key numbers: Our evaluation: GM_sun/(R_sun c^2) = 2.1e-6 rad = 0.44 arcsec; GR value 4 times this = 1.75 arcsec
- **Gravitational redshift at Earth's surface and on a neutron star** (numerical-estimate, GA §6.5 p.78 (pdf 95)): Negligible for an Earth-surface clock relative to deep space, but very large at the surface of a neutron star of about 1.4 solar masses and 10 km radius. Key numbers: Our evaluation: Earth GM/(Rc^2) ~ 7e-10; neutron star 2GM/(Rc^2) ~ 0.41, rate factor ~ 0.77, redshift z ~ 0.31
- **GPS satellite navigation** (technology, GA §6.5 p.78 (pdf 95)): Positioning depends on precise timing; satellite clocks need an SR correction for orbital motion and a GR correction because they sit higher in Earth's potential. Key numbers: Standard values (not in the book): gravitational +45 microseconds/day, velocity -7 microseconds/day, net about +38 microseconds/day
- **Surface redshift of Earth, Sun and a white dwarf (Exercise 6.1)** (numerical-estimate, GA p.79 (pdf 96)): Students estimate the fractional clock slowdown on three bodies. Key numbers: Our evaluation of GM/(Rc^2): Earth 7.0e-10; Sun 2.1e-6; 1 solar-mass white dwarf with R = 1000 km 1.5e-3
- **Millimetre-scale redshift in a strontium lattice clock (Exercise 6.2)** (experiment, GA p.79 (pdf 96)): Ultracold 87Sr atoms (about 100 nK) in an optical lattice resolve a frequency gradient across the sample; students compare with g/c^2. Key numbers: Measured gradient about -1.0(2)e-19 per mm; our evaluation g/c^2 = 1.09e-19 per mm, consistent
- **Satellite versus ground clock rates (Exercise 6.3)** (numerical-estimate, GA p.79 (pdf 96)): Combining gravitational and orbital-speed effects gives a net rate difference that vanishes for an orbit of radius 3R/2 and is positive for higher orbits. Key numbers: Our evaluation for geostationary orbit (r = 42,164 km, R = 6,371 km): satellite clock fast by about 5.4e-10 (roughly 46 microseconds/day)
- **Sagnac effect in a rotating fibre loop (Exercise 6.6)** (technology, GA p.80 (pdf 97)): Counter-propagating beams in a rotating circular fibre shift in phase by an amount proportional to rotation rate and enclosed area, the principle of fibre-optic and ring-laser gyroscopes. Key numbers: Delta N = 4 pi Omega r^2/(lambda c) in SI units

## Teaching gems

### Transform gravity out of an interacting many-particle equation with x' = x - g t^2/2, so the learner sees the g term cancel while pair forces survive. · GA §6.1 p.69 (pdf 86)

**Why it works:** Turns an abstract principle into a two-line calculation and highlights why universality of free fall (m_i = m_g) is essential.

**App idea:** Equation morph: the Newtonian equation animates term by term under the transformation, with a live particle cloud whose centre-of-mass motion disappears when the frame switch is toggled.

### A uniform field is equivalent to no field, so the only honest evidence for gravity is tidal: release two particles and watch their separation. · GA §6.1 p.70 (pdf 87)

**Why it works:** Resolves the apparent paradox of the equivalence principle and plants the seed of curvature as relative acceleration.

**App idea:** Unlabeled-cabin challenge where the learner must detect a planet using test particles; the minimum detectable cabin size scales with planet radius.

### Accelerating-rocket crossing shows light must bend, followed by an honest admission that locality fails for the full photon path and spatial curvature supplies more. · GA §6.1 p.71 (pdf 88)

**Why it works:** Delivers a real prediction from almost no mathematics while modelling scientific candour about the limits of a heuristic.

**App idea:** Two-part demo: rocket frame bending, then a 'full GR' overlay showing the time-part and space-part contributions to solar deflection as equal halves.

### Build a plausible Lorentz-invariant theory of gravity (wave-equation Poisson) and show it fails because density is not a scalar. · GA §6.2 p.74 (pdf 91)

**Why it works:** Learning why a natural guess fails makes the tensor source and curvature feel necessary rather than arbitrary.

**App idea:** Boost slider applied to a dust cloud: rho grows by gamma^2 while a scalar would stay fixed; the learner sees the mismatch between the two sides of the equation.

### Equivalence plus covariance syllogism: a tensor law true without gravity stays true with gravity after eta -> g. · GA §6.2 p.74 (pdf 91)

**Why it works:** Gives a practical recipe that the learner can immediately apply to Maxwell's equations or conservation laws.

**App idea:** Recipe card interaction: drag an SR equation onto a 'curved spacetime' zone; the app substitutes eta -> g and flags where partial derivatives still need upgrading (bridge to ch7).

### Local flatness as a hierarchy: value and slope of the metric can be matched to Minkowski at a point, curvature (second derivatives) cannot. · GA §6.4 p.76 (pdf 93)

**Why it works:** Maps onto the familiar Taylor-expansion picture of a tangent plane touching a curved surface.

**App idea:** Sphere with a tangent plane at a draggable point; show the metric error growing quadratically with distance, with coefficient given by curvature.

### Derive gravitational time dilation twice: from g_00 and from a falling clock that uses SR gamma at escape speed. · GA §6.5 p.78 (pdf 95)

**Why it works:** Two independent routes to the same factor build confidence and link GR clocks to familiar SR dilation and Newtonian energy.

**App idea:** Clock-drop animation with dual readout of (1 - 2GM/rc^2)^{1/2} and 1/gamma(v_fall), including a finite-height release mode revealing the heuristic's assumption.

### Place GR in a square of limits with SR, Newtonian gravity and Newtonian mechanics. · GA §6.3 p.76 (pdf 93)

**Why it works:** A one-glance map that tells learners what any new theory must reproduce and where each approximation lives.

**App idea:** Interactive regime map (v/c versus GM/rc^2) with real systems plotted and a probe showing the size of neglected terms.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **The text states that the full deflection of light by a spherical mass is three times the equivalence-principle estimate and that the argument accounts for one third; the standard result is that the time-part (equivalence) and space-part contributions are equal, so the full value is twice the heuristic (4GM/(bc^2) versus 2GM/(bc^2)).** — Learners may memorize the wrong ratio and be confused when ch24 or other sources give a factor of two; it also clashes with the later 'factor of 4' remark relative to the crude estimate. *Suggestion:* Teach: equivalence principle (or Newtonian corpuscle) gives 2GM/(bc^2); spatial curvature doubles it to 4GM/(bc^2) = 1.75 arcsec at the solar limb; the crude (6.5) misses a further factor 2 from integrating along the whole path.
- **Example 6.3 says timelike curves 'are called geodesics', implying any timelike curve is a geodesic.** — Blurs the distinction between accelerated world lines and free fall, which ch8-9 rely on. *Suggestion:* Say: free-fall world lines are timelike and locally straight (zero 4-acceleration); those special timelike curves are geodesics.
- **The clock-drop rederivation is heuristic: it assumes the falling clock's rate stays equal to the far clock's, uses reciprocal SR time dilation without specifying which frame measures which clock, and its dt/d tau labelling is inverted relative to (6.11).** — Attentive learners notice SR dilation is symmetric and may conclude the argument proves either direction; the exact agreement with Schwarzschild looks deeper than it is. *Suggestion:* Present it as a plausibility argument; for a rigorous route use g_00 or photon energy conservation (Pound-Rebka style climb) and later the Killing-energy argument in Schwarzschild.
- **Exercise 6.5 eqn (6.16) is labelled the proper time at A but equals the coordinate-time round trip; without a factor (-g_00)^{1/2} it does not lead to (6.17).** — Students attempting part (d) cannot reproduce the stated spatial metric and may doubt their algebra. *Suggestion:* Use d tau = 2[(g_0i g_0j - g_ij g_00)dx^i dx^j]^{1/2}/(-g_00)^{1/2} and dl = d tau/2 (c = 1).
- **Minor printed slips in exercises: Exercise 6.3 names the planet mass m but the result uses M; eqn (6.19) omits d phi inside the integral; (6.20) is in c = 1 units.** — Small but can stall a novice checking dimensions. *Suggestion:* Tutor should silently correct: mass M, integrand with d phi, and Delta N = 4 pi Omega r^2/(lambda c) in SI.
- **The local flatness theorem's first condition is justified only by 'a symmetric matrix can be diagonalized', and the vanishing of first derivatives is asserted without the counting argument.** — Readers may think diagonalizing at a point is trivially the whole story and not see why second derivatives cannot vanish (20 leftover degrees of freedom = Riemann components). *Suggestion:* Add the Taylor-expansion counting: 10 metric values vs 16 Jacobian entries, 40 first derivatives vs 40 second-order coefficients, 100 second derivatives vs 80 third-order coefficients; defer proof to ch35.
- **Fig. 6.4 labels the GR-to-Newtonian-gravity limit only as v -> 0, but the Newtonian limit also requires weak fields (GM/rc^2 << 1) and stresses small compared with energy density; G -> 0 for SR is also more naturally 'curvature negligible'.** — Learners may think slow motion near a black hole is Newtonian. *Suggestion:* Relabel axes as v/c and GM/(rc^2) (or c -> infinity) and emphasize both conditions for the Newtonian limit.
- **General covariance is presented as having strong physical content and as a restatement of the equivalence principle; the Kretschmann objection (any theory can be written covariantly) is not mentioned.** — Advanced learners may later find the principle's status contested and feel misled. *Suggestion:* Frame covariance as a powerful guiding heuristic that, combined with 'no prior geometry' and minimal coupling, constrains theories; mention the debate briefly for advanced learners.
- **The eta -> g rule (Lesson 4) omits that derivatives must also become covariant derivatives, and that curvature-coupling ambiguities can arise for second-derivative laws.** — A learner applying the rule literally to partial_mu T^{mu nu} = 0 gets a non-tensorial equation. *Suggestion:* State the full minimal-coupling rule (eta -> g, partial -> nabla) as a preview and point to ch7.
- **The authors say eqn (6.11) requires the Newtonian limit, but with g_00 = -(1 - 2GM/r) it is exact for static observers in Schwarzschild spacetime (6.14), valid down to r = 2GM/c^2.** — Learners may think neutron-star redshift estimates from (6.11) are unreliable, or may later be surprised by its exactness. *Suggestion:* Note that the weak-field derivation happens to produce the exact Schwarzschild g_00; caution only applies to the weak-field metric's other components and to the binomial approximation.
- **Fig. 6.2's caption says particles released on parallel paths approach each other, which is true only for horizontal separation; vertically separated particles in a planet's field drift apart.** — Misses the stretch-and-squeeze pattern of tides central to ch11. *Suggestion:* Show both orientations (Example 6.4 text does mention 'or away from each other').
- **The weak/strong equivalence principle terminology differs from modern usage (WEP, Einstein EP, strong EP including gravitational experiments).** — Confusion when reading papers on tests of the equivalence principle (e.g. MICROSCOPE, lunar laser ranging). *Suggestion:* Tutor gives a three-level ladder: WEP (free-fall universality), EEP (all non-gravitational laws locally SR), SEP (also gravitational phenomena and self-gravitating bodies).

## Tutor notes

- Open with a question: 'If you were in a windowless box, could you tell whether you are resting on Earth or accelerating at 9.8 m/s^2 in deep space?' Then: 'What if the box were very large?' The second question leads directly to tides.
- Order of explanation that works: m_i = m_g and universality of free fall -> transforming away uniform g (Example 6.1) -> accelerometer in free fall reads zero -> strong principle and LIFs -> tides as the invariant remnant -> light bending as a payoff -> covariance and eta -> g -> metric as field and local flatness -> clock rates.
- Check for understanding: ask the learner why an orbiting astronaut is weightless even though gravity at ISS altitude is about 90 percent of surface gravity.
- Check: ask which of two initially side-by-side particles above a planet move together or apart, then repeat for a vertically stacked pair; expect converge and diverge respectively.
- When covering light bending, correct the text's factor: the equivalence-principle argument yields half the GR deflection (not one third); the full solar-limb value is 1.75 arcsec.
- For Example 6.7 have the learner boost a cloud of dust: number density gains a gamma and each particle's energy gains a gamma, so energy density scales as gamma^2, making it clear rho is not a scalar.
- Distinguish 'local inertial frame at an event' (g = eta, dg = 0 there) from 'freely falling frame along a world line'; learners often conflate them.
- For time dilation, emphasize comparison by signals: each clock ticks normally for its owner; the redshift is a relation between separated observers. Use GPS numbers (+45, -7, net +38 microseconds per day) as a memorable anchor.
- Common learner question: 'If gravity is not a force, why do I feel it standing on the ground?' Answer: the ground's normal force pushes you off your geodesic; what you feel is that non-gravitational force.
- Common learner question: 'Why can't we transform away curvature too?' Use the tangent-plane picture: you can match height and slope of a surface at a point but not its bending.
- Common learner question: 'Is general relativity just about accelerated frames?' No: SR already handles accelerated observers; GR is about gravity as curvature, and the name comes from general covariance.
- Adapt by level: for novices stay with elevators, rockets, tides and clock comparisons with numbers; for advanced learners add the derivative-counting argument for local flatness, the EEP/SEP distinction, and the exact Schwarzschild status of (6.11).
