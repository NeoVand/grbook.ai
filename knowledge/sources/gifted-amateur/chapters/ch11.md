# GA ch11 · Riemann curvature and the Ricci tensor

> Introduces intrinsic curvature through two operational tests (geodesic deviation and loop parallel transport), derives the Riemann tensor from the connection by carrying a vector round a tiny parallelogram, lists its symmetries, contracts it to the Ricci tensor and scalar, checks the machinery on the plane and the 2-sphere, and recovers Newtonian tidal stretching from Schwarzschild curvature in a freely falling frame.

**Pages:** printed 120–130 · pdf 137–147 · **Difficulty:** math 4/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

Conceptual framing (intrinsic curvature, tides) is gentle and well motivated, but the loop derivation involves careful sign and index bookkeeping, several results are quoted without proof (deviation equation, component count, Schwarzschild components), and §11.7 relies on vielbein machinery from Chapter 10. The OCR copy damages the hatted equations 11.40-11.42, and printed eqn 11.38 drops a sign.

## Role in the book

This is the hinge of Part II. Chapters 7-10 built the covariant derivative, connection coefficients, geodesics and orthonormal measurement frames; this chapter turns those tools into a coordinate-independent verdict on whether spacetime is genuinely curved. It supplies the raw material (Ricci tensor and scalar) that Chapters 12-13 need to write the field equations, and it deliberately leaves the rigorous geodesic-deviation derivation, the independent-component count and efficient computation methods to Part V (Chapters 30, 34-36), in keeping with the book's spiral structure.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can distinguish intrinsic from extrinsic curvature using the cylinder (unrollable) versus sphere (not unrollable) contrast.
- Reader can describe two operational tests for intrinsic curvature: relative acceleration of initially parallel geodesics, and rotation of a vector parallel transported around a closed loop.
- Reader can derive the Newtonian tidal tensor as the Hessian of the potential and use it to predict stretching along the radial direction and squeezing transverse to it for a falling cloud.
- Reader can explain, via Poisson's equation, why a small cloud falling in vacuum keeps its volume while changing shape.
- Reader can reproduce the parallelogram parallel-transport argument leading to the component formula for the Riemann tensor in terms of Christoffel symbols and their derivatives.
- Reader can use the matrix-commutator mnemonic to remember and evaluate the Riemann formula.
- Reader can show that for a weak static field the Riemann components R^i_{tjt} reduce to second derivatives of the Newtonian potential.
- Reader can state the pair antisymmetries, pair-exchange symmetry and cyclic identity of R_{abcd} and use them to count independent components (0, 1, 6, 20 in 1-4 dimensions).
- Reader can form the Ricci tensor by contracting the first and third indices of Riemann and the Ricci scalar by tracing with the inverse metric.
- Reader can compute Riemann, Ricci and the Ricci scalar for flat polar coordinates (all zero) and for a sphere of radius a (R = 2/a^2).
- Reader can write the geodesic deviation equation in a freely falling orthonormal frame and interpret Riemann components as tidal 'spring constants', recovering the Schwarzschild tidal stretching 2M/r^3 and compression M/r^3.

## Teaching approach

Short, physically motivated chapter that asks how beings trapped inside spacetime can detect its curvature, answers with two operational tests, and then takes the computationally easier of the two (loop parallel transport) to derive the Riemann formula explicitly. Newtonian tidal physics is used as a bridge on both ends: the chapter opens with the Hessian of the potential acting as a tidal tensor and closes by showing Schwarzschild curvature in a freely falling frame gives the same stretch-and-squeeze pattern. Symmetries are read off from a local-inertial-frame formula rather than proved in general, component counting and the cleaner geodesic-deviation derivation are postponed to Part V, and two brute-force examples (plane, sphere) serve as sanity checks.

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, derivation-heavy, example-driven, spiral-revisit, conversational-informal, computational-recipe, diagram-driven

**Narrative arc**

1. Motivating question: we cannot step outside spacetime, so how can its curvature be measured from within? (Earth-surface and Gauss analogy)
2. Classify curvature: Euclid's parallel postulate fails on curved surfaces; cylinder is extrinsically curved, sphere intrinsically; positive curvature converges parallel lines, negative diverges them
3. Two operational tests proposed: geodesic deviation and loop parallel transport, promised to give the same tensor
4. Rule of thumb: curvature needs first and second derivatives of the metric, so it should be expressible via Gamma and dGamma
5. Newtonian bridge: equivalence principle forces us to look at two particles; tidal tensor = Hessian of Phi; falling cloud elongates radially and shrinks sideways
6. State relativistic geodesic deviation equation with R(., u, xi, u) as the relative acceleration; defer its derivation
7. Volume conservation of the falling cloud proved with Poisson's equation (trace of tidal tensor vanishes in vacuum)
8. Formalism: parallel transport a vector around an infinitesimal parallelogram, expand to lowest order, identify the bilinear coefficient as Riemann components
9. Check: weak static field Riemann equals the Newtonian tidal tensor
10. Symmetries: go to a local inertial frame where Gamma vanishes, write Riemann as second metric derivatives, read off antisymmetries, pair symmetry and cyclic identity; count independent components
11. Contraction: build the symmetric Ricci tensor and the Ricci scalar as the physically usable (0,2) and (0,0) pieces
12. Checks by brute force: flat polar coordinates give zero; 2-sphere gives R = 2/a^2
13. Application: geodesic deviation in a freely falling orthonormal frame; Schwarzschild components reproduce Newtonian tidal stretching and squeezing; curvature as spring constant

**Signature moves**

- Frames curvature detection as an insider's problem (no embedding available), using the surface of the Earth and Gauss as the precedent.
- Uses the 'boxed' rule-of-thumb arrows (dg -> Gamma; dg + d2g -> R) to set expectations about what a curvature object must be built from before deriving it.
- Builds the Newtonian tidal tensor R^i_j = d2Phi/dx^i dx^j first, deliberately naming it R so the later relativistic Riemann components are recognized as its generalization.
- Writes the loop change in words-inside-brackets ('change around the loop' = 'measure of curvature' times V times the area) before any calculus, emphasizing that linearity in the loop sides is what makes it a tensor.
- Offers a matrix commutator mnemonic for the Riemann formula, treating Gamma_mu as a matrix in its other two indices.
- Obtains the symmetries from the local-inertial-frame expression built only from second derivatives of the symmetric metric rather than proving them in general.
- Motivates the Ricci tensor on the grounds that laws of physics are written with (0,2) tensors, and hints at its role in volume shrinkage.
- Closes the loop with a spring-constant/simple-harmonic-oscillator reading of geodesic deviation in a freely falling frame, matching the Newtonian example from the start of the chapter.

## Section by section

### Chapter opening: measuring curvature from inside — p.120 (pdf 137)

Sets the problem that observers cannot leave spacetime to inspect its shape, recalls that people deduced the Earth's roundness from within, credits Gauss with intrinsic surface curvature and Riemann (1854) with the higher-dimensional generalization, and announces that a non-vanishing Riemann tensor is the definitive signature of true curvature. A margin note warns that the rubber-sheet picture shows curved space, not curved spacetime.

**Concepts:** intrinsic curvature, Riemann curvature tensor

**Key moves**
- Insider problem: no embedding space is available for spacetime
- Historical lineage Gauss -> Riemann -> Christoffel, Ricci, Levi-Civita -> Einstein
- Claim that R != 0 is the test of genuine curvature

### §11.1 What is curvature? — p.120 (pdf 137)

Uses the failure of Euclid's parallel postulate to define curvature, contrasts the cylinder (curved only as seen from outside, since it unrolls flat) with the sphere (curved intrinsically), classifies surfaces by whether initially parallel geodesics converge (positive), stay parallel (flat) or diverge (negative, saddle), and proposes two equivalent tests: geodesic deviation and loop parallel transport. Ends with the rule of thumb that curvature is built from first and second metric derivatives, hence from Gamma and its derivatives.

**Concepts:** parallel postulate, intrinsic curvature, extrinsic curvature, sign of curvature, geodesic deviation, holonomy of parallel transport around a closed loop, metric as source of curvature

**Key moves**
- Cylinder can be unwrapped into a plane, so parallel lines on it never meet
- Sign of curvature classified by convergence or divergence of parallel geodesics (Fig. 11.1)
- Vector transported around a spherical triangle returns rotated (Fig. 11.2)
- Both tests will yield the same Riemann tensor
- Rule of thumb (11.1): dg + d2g -> R

### §11.2 Tidal forces — p.121 (pdf 138)

Argues that because the equivalence principle hides gravity from a single particle, curvature must be detected through relative motion of neighbouring particles. In Newtonian gravity a falling spherical cloud is stretched radially and squeezed sideways; Example 11.1 derives the separation equation with the Hessian of the potential as a tidal tensor and evaluates it on the z-axis. The relativistic geodesic deviation equation is stated with R(., u, xi, u) as the relative acceleration, with derivation postponed. Example 11.2 uses Gauss's law and Poisson's equation to show the tidal tensor is traceless in vacuum, so the cloud's volume is preserved.

**Concepts:** equivalence principle, tidal forces, Newtonian tidal tensor, separation (deviation) vector, geodesic deviation equation, Poisson's equation for gravity, volume preservation of a freely falling cloud in vacuum

**Key moves**
- Single-particle experiments cannot distinguish curvature from coordinate acceleration; use two particles
- Difference of equations of motion, Taylor-expanded in small separation s, gives d2s^i/dt2 = -R^i_j s^j with R_ij = d2Phi/dx^i dx^j
- On the z-axis the tidal tensor is (GM/z^3) diag(1,1,-2): stretch along z, squeeze in x and y
- Relativistic version: D2 xi/dtau2 + R(., u, xi, u) = 0 with R a (1,3) tensor
- Trace of Hessian equals Laplacian = 4 pi G rho, zero inside a cloud with no mass, so the volume's second time derivative vanishes

### §11.3 Riemann curvature — p.124 (pdf 141)

Stresses that a changing vector under parallel transport along an open path may just reflect awkward coordinates, but net rotation around a closed loop is a smoking gun for curvature. Example 11.3 transports a vector around an infinitesimal coordinate parallelogram with sides delta a e1 and delta b e2, uses the transport condition dV/dx = -Gamma V on each edge, pairs opposite edges and expands to lowest order to obtain a change proportional to delta a delta b, identifying the bracket as the Riemann components (11.19). A margin mnemonic writes the formula with matrix commutators. Example 11.4 checks that for a weak static field the only non-zero components at this order equal the Hessian of Phi, reproducing the Newtonian tidal tensor.

**Concepts:** parallel transport, holonomy of parallel transport around a closed loop, Riemann curvature tensor, Riemann tensor component formula, matrix commutator form of the Riemann tensor, weak-field limit of the Riemann tensor, metric as source of curvature

**Key moves**
- Loop change written schematically as (curvature measure) x V x delta a delta b; linearity in loop sides makes the coefficient a tensor
- Four edge integrals (11.11-11.12), noting constant coordinate on each edge
- Opposite edges pair up into first derivatives across the loop (11.13-11.14)
- Replace derivatives of V with -Gamma V to get dGamma and Gamma Gamma terms (11.15)
- Relabel 1,2 as nu,mu and define R^alpha_{beta mu nu} (11.16, 11.19)
- Weak-field check: Gamma^i_tt = dPhi/dx^i gives R^i_{tjt} = d2Phi/dx^j dx^i (11.20-11.21)

### §11.4 Symmetries of the Riemann tensor — p.126 (pdf 143)

Lowers the first index and, working at a point in a local inertial frame where the connection vanishes but its derivatives do not, writes the all-lower Riemann tensor purely as second derivatives of the metric (11.23). From this form the antisymmetry in each index pair, symmetry under pair exchange and the cyclic identity are evident. These cut the N^4 components down to N^2(N^2-1)/12 (proof deferred to Chapter 35), tabulated as 0, 1, 6, 20 for dimensions 1-4. Example 11.6 notes that a line has no intrinsic curvature and a surface has a single independent component R_1212.

**Concepts:** local flatness theorem, Riemann tensor in a local inertial frame, symmetries of the Riemann tensor, cyclic (first Bianchi) identity, number of independent Riemann components, intrinsic curvature

**Key moves**
- Local inertial frame: g = eta, dg = 0 so Gamma = 0 at P, but d2g != 0
- Riemann components reduce to derivatives of Gamma, hence to second metric derivatives (11.23)
- Symmetry of g and of mixed partials makes the index symmetries manifest (11.24-11.25)
- Counting table 0, 1, 6, 20
- 1D spaces are always isometric to a straight line; 2D surfaces have one curvature number

### §11.5 The Ricci tensor and Ricci scalar — p.127 (pdf 144)

Argues that physical laws use rank-two tensors, so the (1,3) Riemann tensor is contracted on its upper index and middle lower index to give the symmetric Ricci tensor, then traced with the inverse metric to give the Ricci scalar. Margin notes define contraction and trace, explain why the Ricci tensor gets no bold symbol (the Einstein tensor does), and hint that Ricci's averaging captures how curvature makes volumes shrink.

**Concepts:** tensor contraction, Ricci tensor, Ricci scalar, Einstein tensor

**Key moves**
- Motivation: physics lives in (0,2) tensors, not (1,3)
- Symmetries leave essentially one non-trivial contraction: R_{nu beta} = R^mu_{nu mu beta} (11.26)
- Ricci tensor inherits symmetry from pair exchange
- Scalar R = g^{mu nu} R_{mu nu} (11.27), with the metric needed for the trace in curved spacetime

### §11.6 Example computations — p.128 (pdf 145)

Two brute-force evaluations of the Riemann formula (11.28). For the plane in polar coordinates, the non-zero Christoffel symbols conspire so every Riemann component cancels, confirming the formalism does not mistake curvilinear coordinates for curvature. For a sphere of radius a, the components R^theta_{phi theta phi} = sin^2 theta and R^phi_{theta phi theta} = 1 lead to Ricci components R_thetatheta = 1, R_phiphi = sin^2 theta and Ricci scalar 2/a^2. A pointer promises more efficient methods in Part V.

**Concepts:** Riemann tensor component formula, flat space in curvilinear coordinates, curvature of the 2-sphere, Ricci tensor, Ricci scalar

**Key moves**
- Polar coordinates: Gamma^r_thetatheta = -r, Gamma^theta_rtheta = 1/r; derivative and product terms cancel (11.29-11.30)
- Sphere: Gamma^theta_phiphi = -sin theta cos theta, Gamma^phi_thetaphi = cot theta (11.32)
- Riemann components sin^2 theta and 1 (11.33-11.34)
- Ricci scalar 2/a^2: smaller spheres are more curved (11.36)

### §11.7 Geodesic deviation revisited — p.129 (pdf 146)

Returns to geodesic deviation armed with the Riemann tensor and the orthonormal-frame ideas of Chapter 10. In a freely falling local inertial frame the observer's 4-velocity is (1,0,0,0), the covariant second derivative becomes an ordinary one, and deviation reduces to d2 xi^i/dtau2 = -R^i_{0j0} xi^j, a set of oscillator-like equations with curvature components as spring constants. Example 11.9 quotes the Schwarzschild orthonormal-frame components (derived later) and shows radial stretching at rate 2M/r^3 and transverse squeezing at M/r^3, mirroring the Newtonian cloud of Example 11.1. The chapter summary and four exercises follow.

**Concepts:** geodesic deviation equation, freely falling orthonormal frame, vielbein transformation of tensor components, Riemann components as tidal spring constants, Schwarzschild tidal tensor, tidal forces

**Key moves**
- Deviation acceleration expressed as D2 xi/dtau2 = nabla_u nabla_u xi (Jacobi equation)
- u^0 = 1 from u.u = -1 with eta_00 = -1
- Frame components of R obtained from coordinate ones via vielbein factors
- Schwarzschild: d2xi^r/dtau2 = +2M/r^3 xi^r, transverse components -M/r^3
- Explicit link back to Newtonian diag(1,1,-2) pattern

## Concepts

### intrinsic curvature

*definition · core* · also: true curvature, genuine curvature

Curvature detectable by measurements made entirely within a space (distances, parallel transport, behaviour of geodesics), independent of any embedding; it is present exactly when the Riemann tensor is non-zero.

**How introduced:** Posed as the problem faced by beings trapped in spacetime, then made concrete by contrasting a sphere, which cannot be wrapped by flat paper and on which parallel lines meet, with a cylinder that unrolls flat.

**Prerequisites:** parallel postulate, geodesic

**Notes:** A margin note adds that a 1D space can never be intrinsically curved.

**Where:** GA §11.1 p.120 (pdf 137); GA §11.1 p.121 (pdf 138)

### extrinsic curvature

*definition · introduced*

Apparent bending of a space that exists only relative to a higher-dimensional space it is embedded in; it can be removed by unrolling or re-embedding without stretching, and it leaves geodesics and parallel transport unaffected.

**How introduced:** The cylinder, formed by gluing opposite edges of a flat sheet, is the key example; later a curve tied in a bow illustrates that 1D curvature is always of this kind.

**Prerequisites:** intrinsic curvature

**Where:** GA §11.1 p.120 (pdf 137); GA §11.4 p.127 (pdf 144)

### parallel postulate

*principle · mention* · also: Euclid's fifth postulate

Euclid's axiom, equivalent to the statement that lines which start out parallel never meet; it holds only in flat geometry.

**How introduced:** Opens Section 11.1 as the definition of flatness whose failure signals curvature; a sidebar lists all five postulates.

**Where:** GA §11.1 p.120 (pdf 137); GA §11.2 p.121 (pdf 138)

### sign of curvature

*definition · introduced* · also: positive and negative curvature

Classification of a surface by what happens to initially parallel geodesics: they converge on positively curved surfaces (sphere), remain parallel on flat ones, and diverge on negatively curved (saddle-shaped) ones.

**How introduced:** Stated right after the cylinder/sphere discussion and illustrated by two geodesics converging on a dome (Fig. 11.1).

**Prerequisites:** intrinsic curvature, geodesic

**Where:** GA §11.1 p.121 (pdf 138)

### geodesic

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: free-fall world line

A curve whose tangent vector is parallel transported along itself; freely falling test particles follow timelike geodesics.

**How introduced:** Relied on from Chapters 8-9; here used as the straightest lines whose relative behaviour reveals curvature.

**Prerequisites:** parallel transport

$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol u}\boldsymbol u = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §11.1 p.121 (pdf 138); GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146)

### geodesic deviation

*phenomenon · core* · also: relative acceleration of neighbouring geodesics

The relative acceleration of two nearby freely falling particles whose world lines start parallel; it is non-zero only in intrinsically curved spacetime and is the relativistic form of tidal effects.

**How introduced:** First of the two proposed curvature tests, drawn as converging paths on a dome; developed via the Newtonian falling cloud and revisited in a freely falling frame at the end.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic, separation (deviation) vector, equivalence principle

**Where:** GA §11.1 p.121 (pdf 138); GA §11.2 p.123 (pdf 140); GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146)

### geodesic deviation equation

*law · core* · also: Jacobi equation, equation of geodesic deviation

The equation giving the covariant second proper-time derivative of the separation vector between neighbouring geodesics in terms of the Riemann tensor contracted with the fiducial 4-velocity twice and the separation once: (D^2 xi/dtau^2)^a = -R^a_{bcd} u^b xi^c u^d.

**How introduced:** Stated without proof in §11.2 as the relativistic analogue of the Newtonian separation equation, with a pointer to Chapter 35 for justification; reused in §11.7 where a freely falling frame reduces it to oscillator equations.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann curvature tensor, covariant derivative, separation (deviation) vector, 4-velocity

$$
\frac{D^2\xi^\alpha}{d\tau^2} = -R^\alpha{}_{\beta\gamma\delta}u^\beta\xi^\gamma u^\delta
$$

$$
\frac{D^2\boldsymbol\xi}{d\tau^2} = \nabla_{\boldsymbol u}\nabla_{\boldsymbol u}\boldsymbol\xi
$$

**Notes:** Eqn 11.38 as printed omits the minus sign that appears in 11.6 and 11.39.

**Where:** GA §11.2 p.123 (pdf 140); GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146)

### separation (deviation) vector

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: connecting vector, xi, s

A small vector joining a point on a fiducial geodesic to the corresponding point on a neighbouring geodesic; its second derivative measures relative (tidal) acceleration.

**How introduced:** Introduced in the Newtonian setting as s = y - x between two falling particles (Fig. 11.4), then as xi in the relativistic setting.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic

$$
\vec s(t) = \vec y(t) - \vec x(t)
$$

**Where:** GA §11.2 p.122 (pdf 139); GA §11.2 p.123 (pdf 140)

### equivalence principle

*principle · revisited*

Locally, the motion of a single freely falling particle cannot distinguish a gravitational field from acceleration of the reference frame, so gravity must be diagnosed from relative motion of separated particles.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Part II to explain why a static-frame test of gravity fails and why two particles are needed.

**Where:** GA §11.2 p.122 (pdf 139)

### tidal forces

*phenomenon · core* · also: tidal acceleration

Differential gravitational accelerations across an extended body or cloud that, in the body's own freely falling frame, stretch it along the direction to the attracting mass and compress it in the perpendicular directions.

**How introduced:** A spherical cloud of test particles falling toward the Earth is shown in the Earth frame and in the cloud's frame (Fig. 11.3): the near side falls faster, the sides converge.

**Prerequisites:** equivalence principle, Newtonian tidal tensor

**Where:** GA §11.2 p.122 (pdf 139); GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146)

### Newtonian tidal tensor

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: Hessian of the gravitational potential, Newtonian R^i_j

The symmetric matrix of second partial derivatives of the Newtonian potential, R_ij = d^2 Phi/dx^i dx^j, which governs the relative acceleration of nearby particles via d^2 s^i/dt^2 = -R^i_j s^j.

**How introduced:** Derived in Example 11.1 by subtracting two particles' equations of motion and Taylor expanding in their separation; evaluated for a point mass on the z-axis as (GM/z^3) diag(1,1,-2).

**Prerequisites:** separation (deviation) vector, Taylor expansion

$$
R^i{}_j = \frac{\partial^2\Phi}{\partial x^i\partial x^j}
$$

$$
\frac{d^2 s^i}{dt^2} = -R^i{}_j s^j
$$

**Notes:** Named R on purpose to foreshadow the Riemann tensor.

**Where:** GA §11.2 p.122 (pdf 139)

### Poisson's equation for gravity

*law · developed* · also: Gauss's law for gravity

The field equation of Newtonian gravity, nabla^2 Phi = 4 pi G rho, equivalently div g = -4 pi G rho or the flux of g through a closed surface equals -4 pi G times the enclosed mass.

**How introduced:** Built by analogy with Gauss's law in electrostatics in Example 11.2, then used to fix the trace of the tidal tensor.

**Prerequisites:** Newtonian tidal tensor

$$
\oint \vec g\cdot d\vec S = -4\pi G M
$$

$$
\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho
$$

**Where:** GA §11.2 p.123 (pdf 140)

### volume preservation of a freely falling cloud in vacuum

*theorem · developed* · also: shape change without volume change

A small cloud of test particles, released from rest in a region containing no matter, is reshaped by tides into an ellipsoid but keeps its initial volume. The reason is that the volume's second time derivative is set by the trace of the tidal tensor, and that trace equals 4 pi G rho, which is zero.

**How introduced:** Claimed in the tidal-force discussion and proved in Example 11.2; contrasted with a large sphere enclosing the Earth, which contracts.

**Prerequisites:** Poisson's equation for gravity, Newtonian tidal tensor

$$
\mathrm{tr}\,R^i{}_j = \nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho
$$

**Notes:** Foreshadows the Ricci tensor as the part of curvature controlling volume change (margin note 20).

**Where:** GA §11.2 p.122 (pdf 139); GA §11.2 p.123 (pdf 140)

### parallel transport

*operation · revisited*

Moving a vector along a curve so that its covariant derivative along the curve vanishes; component-wise dV^a/dx^mu = -Gamma^a_{mu b} V^b along a coordinate direction.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapter 7; the text stresses that component changes along an open path can be pure coordinate artefacts.

**Prerequisites:** connection coefficients

$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol e_1}\boldsymbol V = 0 \Rightarrow \frac{\partial V^\alpha}{\partial x^1} = -\Gamma^\alpha_{1\beta}V^\beta
$$

**Where:** GA §11.3 p.124 (pdf 141)

### holonomy of parallel transport around a closed loop

*phenomenon · core* · also: loop parallel transport test, vector rotation around a closed loop

The net change of a vector after parallel transport around a closed loop; for a small loop it is proportional to the loop's area and to the Riemann tensor, and it vanishes in flat space regardless of coordinates.

**How introduced:** Second curvature test, shown on a spherical triangle (Fig. 11.2) and then made quantitative with an infinitesimal parallelogram (Fig. 11.5, Example 11.3).

**Prerequisites:** parallel transport, intrinsic curvature

$$
\delta V^\alpha = R^\alpha{}_{\beta\mu\nu}V^\beta\,\delta a\,\delta b
$$

**Notes:** The term holonomy is not used in the chapter. The sign of the formula depends on the loop's orientation: eqn 11.16 goes along e_nu first and then along e_mu; going round the other way flips the sign.

**Where:** GA §11.1 p.121 (pdf 138); GA §11.3 p.124 (pdf 141)

### Riemann curvature tensor

*mathematical-object · core* · also: Riemann tensor, curvature tensor, R( , , , )

The (1,3) tensor built from the connection and its first derivatives (hence from first and second metric derivatives) that gives both the change of a vector transported around an infinitesimal loop and the relative acceleration of neighbouring geodesics; all its components vanish if and only if the space is flat.

**How introduced:** Anticipated by the rule of thumb dg + d2g -> R, glimpsed as the relativistic generalization of the Newtonian tidal tensor, then defined through the loop transport calculation where linearity in the loop sides certifies tensor character.

**Prerequisites:** connection coefficients, parallel transport, holonomy of parallel transport around a closed loop, linear slot machine (tensor)

$$
R^\alpha{}_{\beta\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\Gamma^\alpha_{\nu\beta} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\alpha_{\mu\beta} + \Gamma^\alpha_{\mu\sigma}\Gamma^\sigma_{\nu\beta} - \Gamma^\alpha_{\nu\sigma}\Gamma^\sigma_{\mu\beta}
$$

**Notes:** GA uses the MTW sign convention with signature (-+++).

**Where:** GA p.120 (pdf 137); GA §11.2 p.123 (pdf 140); GA §11.3 p.125 (pdf 142)

### Riemann tensor component formula

*identity · core* · also: Riemann in terms of Christoffel symbols

Explicit expression of R^a_{bmn} as two antisymmetrized derivative-of-Gamma terms plus two antisymmetrized products of Gammas, used for brute-force computation.

**How introduced:** Emerges at the end of the parallelogram calculation (11.15-11.19) and is restated as the working formula for Section 11.6 and in the chapter summary.

**Prerequisites:** connection coefficients, Riemann curvature tensor

$$
R^\mu{}_{\nu\alpha\beta} = \frac{\partial\Gamma^\mu_{\beta\nu}}{\partial x^\alpha} - \frac{\partial\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\nu}}{\partial x^\beta} + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\rho}\Gamma^\rho_{\beta\nu} - \Gamma^\mu_{\beta\rho}\Gamma^\rho_{\alpha\nu}
$$

**Where:** GA §11.3 p.125 (pdf 142); GA §11.6 p.128 (pdf 145); GA p.130 (pdf 147)

### matrix commutator form of the Riemann tensor

*technique · introduced* · also: aide-memoire for Riemann

Regarding Gamma_mu as a matrix with row index up and column index down, the Riemann components form the matrix d_mu Gamma_nu - d_nu Gamma_mu + [Gamma_mu, Gamma_nu], which makes the index sums matrix multiplications.

**How introduced:** Offered in margin note 11 as a memory aid, with the 2x2 matrix layout for two dimensions.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann tensor component formula, commutator

$$
R^{\bullet}{}_{\bullet\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\Gamma_{\nu} - \partial_\nu\Gamma_{\mu} + [\Gamma_{\mu},\Gamma_{\nu}]
$$

**Notes:** Mirrors the field-strength formula of non-abelian gauge theory treated later in the book.

**Where:** GA §11.3 p.125 (pdf 142)

### weak-field limit of the Riemann tensor

*identity · developed*

For the static weak-field metric ds^2 = -(1+2 Phi)dt^2 + dx^2+dy^2+dz^2, to first order in Phi the Riemann components R^i_{tjt} equal d^2 Phi/dx^i dx^j, the Newtonian tidal tensor.

**How introduced:** Example 11.4 plugs the single relevant Christoffel symbol Gamma^i_tt = dPhi/dx^i into the new formula as a consistency check with Example 11.1.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann tensor component formula, Newtonian tidal tensor

$$
R^i{}_{tjt} = \partial_j\Gamma^i_{tt} = \frac{\partial^2\Phi}{\partial x^j\partial x^i}
$$

**Notes:** The text says these are the only non-zero components; strictly this holds to linear order in Phi and up to the antisymmetric partners R^i_{ttj}.

**Where:** GA §11.3 p.125 (pdf 142)

### metric as source of curvature

*principle · developed* · also: rule of thumb dg + d2g -> R

Curvature is determined by the metric: Christoffel symbols come from first metric derivatives and the Riemann tensor from first and second derivatives, so the metric field ultimately encodes curvature.

**How introduced:** Given as boxed arrow rules (11.1) extending the earlier dg -> Gamma rule, and repeated at the end of §11.3.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, connection coefficients

$$
\boxed{\partial g} + \boxed{\partial^2 g} \to \boxed{R}
$$

**Where:** GA §11.1 p.121 (pdf 138); GA §11.3 p.126 (pdf 143)

### local flatness theorem

*theorem · revisited* · also: local inertial frame (LIF)

At any point one can choose coordinates in which the metric equals the Minkowski metric and its first derivatives vanish, so the Christoffel symbols vanish there; second derivatives generally cannot be removed.

**How introduced:** Invoked in Example 11.5 as the shortcut to a simple Riemann expression from which symmetries are visible.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, connection coefficients

$$
g_{\hat\mu\hat\nu}(\mathcal P) = \eta_{\mu\nu},\ \partial g_{\hat\mu\hat\nu}(\mathcal P) = 0 \Rightarrow \Gamma(\mathcal P)=0
$$

**Where:** GA §11.4 p.126 (pdf 143)

### Riemann tensor in a local inertial frame

*identity · developed* · also: Riemann from second metric derivatives

In a local inertial frame the all-lower Riemann tensor is half a signed sum of four second derivatives of the metric; in general coordinates additional Gamma-Gamma terms contracted with the metric appear.

**How introduced:** Derived in Example 11.5 from the vanishing of Gamma at the point; margin note 14 gives the general-coordinate version for reassurance.

**Prerequisites:** local flatness theorem, Riemann tensor component formula

$$
R_{\hat\mu\hat\nu\hat\alpha\hat\beta} = \tfrac12\left(g_{\hat\alpha\hat\nu,\hat\beta\hat\mu} - g_{\hat\alpha\hat\mu,\hat\beta\hat\nu} + g_{\hat\beta\hat\mu,\hat\alpha\hat\nu} - g_{\hat\beta\hat\nu,\hat\alpha\hat\mu}\right)
$$

**Where:** GA §11.4 p.126 (pdf 143)

### symmetries of the Riemann tensor

*identity · core* · also: index symmetries of R_abcd

The all-lower Riemann tensor is antisymmetric under swapping the first two indices, antisymmetric under swapping the last two, and symmetric under exchanging the first pair with the second pair.

**How introduced:** Read off from the local-inertial-frame expression built from the symmetric metric; a margin note translates them into vanishing components with repeated indices in a pair.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann tensor in a local inertial frame, index lowering

$$
R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = -R_{\beta\alpha\gamma\delta} = -R_{\alpha\beta\delta\gamma} = R_{\gamma\delta\alpha\beta}
$$

**Notes:** Being a tensor equation, the result holds in every frame even though derived in a LIF.

**Where:** GA §11.4 p.126 (pdf 143)

### cyclic (first Bianchi) identity

*identity · introduced* · also: cyclic identity

Holding the first index fixed and cyclically permuting the other three, the three Riemann components sum to zero.

**How introduced:** Stated as checkable from the LIF formula alongside the pair symmetries; the name Bianchi is not used here.

**Prerequisites:** symmetries of the Riemann tensor

$$
R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} + R_{\alpha\delta\beta\gamma} + R_{\alpha\gamma\delta\beta} = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §11.4 p.126 (pdf 143)

### number of independent Riemann components

*theorem · introduced* · also: component counting

In N dimensions the symmetries reduce the N^4 components of Riemann to N^2(N^2-1)/12 independent ones: 0, 1, 6 and 20 for N = 1, 2, 3, 4.

**How introduced:** Quoted with proof deferred to Chapter 35 and tabulated; Example 11.6 interprets the 1D and 2D cases.

**Prerequisites:** symmetries of the Riemann tensor, cyclic (first Bianchi) identity

$$
\frac{N^2(N^2-1)}{12}
$$

**Notes:** Margin note 13 cites 256 raw components in 4D.

**Where:** GA §11.4 p.126 (pdf 143); GA §11.4 p.127 (pdf 144)

### tensor contraction

*operation · revisited* · also: trace

Setting an upper index equal to a lower index and summing, which lowers the tensor's rank by two; a trace of a (0,2) tensor in curved spacetime requires the inverse metric.

**How introduced:** Margin notes 17 and 19 define it just when needed to build Ricci objects.

**Prerequisites:** linear slot machine (tensor), metric tensor

$$
A = g^{\mu\nu}A_{\mu\nu}
$$

**Where:** GA §11.5 p.127 (pdf 144)

### Ricci tensor

*mathematical-object · core* · also: R_{mu nu}

The symmetric (0,2) tensor obtained by contracting the upper index of the Riemann tensor with its second lower index (the third slot): R_{nu beta} = R^mu_{nu mu beta}. It captures the average, volume-changing part of curvature.

**How introduced:** Motivated by the observation that physical laws are written with rank-two tensors and that the symmetries leave essentially one useful contraction; interpreted informally as an average over Riemann components.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann curvature tensor, tensor contraction, symmetries of the Riemann tensor

$$
R_{\nu\beta} = R^\mu{}_{\nu\mu\beta}
$$

$$
R_{\mu\nu} = R_{\nu\mu}
$$

**Notes:** Written without bold in GA because only its trace-reversed combination (Einstein tensor) enters the field equation.

**Where:** GA §11.5 p.127 (pdf 144); GA §11.6 p.128 (pdf 145)

### Ricci scalar

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: curvature scalar, scalar curvature, R

The scalar obtained by tracing the Ricci tensor with the inverse metric, R = g^{mu nu} R_{mu nu}; for a 2-sphere of radius a it equals 2/a^2.

**How introduced:** Defined immediately after Ricci as a further contraction, with detailed discussion promised for the following chapters; computed for the sphere in Example 11.8.

**Prerequisites:** Ricci tensor, inverse metric

$$
R = g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}
$$

**Where:** GA §11.5 p.127 (pdf 144); GA §11.6 p.128 (pdf 145)

### Einstein tensor

*mathematical-object · mention* · also: trace-reversed Ricci tensor, G

The symmetric combination G_{mu nu} = R_{mu nu} - (1/2) g_{mu nu} R that appears in Einstein's field equation.

**How introduced:** Only mentioned in a margin note explaining the book's bold-symbol convention; developed in Chapter 13.

**Prerequisites:** Ricci tensor, Ricci scalar

$$
G_{\mu\nu} = R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12 g_{\mu\nu}R
$$

**Notes:** The OCR copy of the margin note shows 1/3; the correct coefficient is 1/2.

**Where:** GA §11.5 p.127 (pdf 144)

### flat space in curvilinear coordinates

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: polar-coordinate plane

The Euclidean plane with ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2 dtheta^2 has non-zero Christoffel symbols but identically vanishing Riemann tensor, showing that non-zero connection coefficients do not imply curvature.

**How introduced:** Example 11.7 as a sanity check: derivative and product terms cancel component by component.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann tensor component formula, connection coefficients

$$
\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = -r,\ \Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = 1/r
$$

**Where:** GA §11.6 p.128 (pdf 145)

### curvature of the 2-sphere

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: spherical space of radius a

The sphere ds^2 = a^2 dtheta^2 + a^2 sin^2 theta dphi^2 has R^theta_{phi theta phi} = sin^2 theta, R^phi_{theta phi theta} = 1, Ricci components R_thetatheta = 1 and R_phiphi = sin^2 theta, and constant Ricci scalar 2/a^2.

**How introduced:** Example 11.8, the non-trivial counterpart to the flat-plane check.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann tensor component formula, Ricci tensor, Ricci scalar

$$
R = \frac{2}{a^2}
$$

**Notes:** Gaussian curvature 1/a^2 equals R/2, a connection not made explicit in the chapter.

**Where:** GA §11.6 p.128 (pdf 145)

### freely falling orthonormal frame

*definition · developed* · also: freely falling local inertial frame, hatted frame

An orthonormal frame carried by a freely falling observer, in which the metric is Minkowskian, the observer's 4-velocity has components (1,0,0,0), and covariant derivatives along the world line reduce to ordinary ones at the observer.

**How introduced:** Chosen in §11.7, building on the measurement frames of Chapter 10, to convert abstract deviation into directly measurable relative accelerations.

**Prerequisites:** local flatness theorem, vielbein transformation of tensor components, 4-velocity

$$
u^{\hat\beta} = (1,0,0,0)
$$

$$
\eta_{\hat 0\hat 0}(u^{\hat 0})^2 = -1
$$

**Where:** GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146)

### vielbein transformation of tensor components

*technique · mention* · also: frame components via basis vectors

Components in an orthonormal frame are obtained by contracting coordinate components with the components of the frame basis vectors (and dual one-forms) for each slot.

**How introduced:** Quoted in §11.7 as the bridge from coordinate Riemann components to frame components, relying on Chapter 10.

**Prerequisites:** freely falling orthonormal frame

$$
R^{\hat\alpha}{}_{\hat\beta\hat\gamma\hat\delta} = R^\mu{}_{\nu\lambda\sigma}(\boldsymbol\omega^{\hat\alpha})_\mu(\boldsymbol e_{\hat\beta})^\nu(\boldsymbol e_{\hat\gamma})^\lambda(\boldsymbol e_{\hat\delta})^\sigma
$$

**Notes:** The printed formula is OCR-damaged; the first factor must be a dual basis one-form.

**Where:** GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146)

### Riemann components as tidal spring constants

*phenomenon · developed* · also: curvature as spring constant

In a freely falling frame the deviation equation d^2 xi^i/dtau^2 = -R^i_{0j0} xi^j has the form of a harmonic oscillator; positive diagonal components act like squared angular frequencies (restoring, converging), negative ones like anti-springs (separating).

**How introduced:** Margin note 25 draws the analogy with x-double-dot = -omega^2 x immediately after reducing the deviation equation.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic deviation equation, freely falling orthonormal frame

$$
\frac{d^2\xi^{\hat\alpha}}{d\tau^2} = -R^{\hat\alpha}{}_{\hat 0\hat\beta\hat 0}\xi^{\hat\beta}
$$

**Where:** GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146)

### Schwarzschild tidal tensor

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: Schwarzschild curvature in a freely falling frame

Outside a spherical mass, the orthonormal-frame Riemann components give radial deviation acceleration +2M/r^3 times separation and transverse acceleration -M/r^3 times separation, the relativistic version of Newtonian tides.

**How introduced:** Example 11.9 quotes components derived in later chapters and exercises and compares the resulting deviation equations with Example 11.1.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic deviation equation, freely falling orthonormal frame

$$
R_{\hat t\hat r\hat t\hat r} = -\frac{2M}{r^3},\ R_{\hat t\hat\theta\hat t\hat\theta} = R_{\hat t\hat\phi\hat t\hat\phi} = \frac{M}{r^3}
$$

**Notes:** Units G = c = 1.

**Where:** GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146)

### connection coefficients

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: Christoffel symbols, Gamma

Coefficients Gamma^a_{bc}, built from first derivatives of the metric, that specify how basis vectors change from point to point and hence how to parallel transport vectors.

**How introduced:** Taken from Chapters 7 and 9 as the raw ingredient; the chapter repeatedly insists that Gamma alone cannot certify curvature.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor

**Where:** GA §11.1 p.121 (pdf 138); GA §11.3 p.124 (pdf 141)

## Key equations

### (11.1) Curvature rule of thumb · supporting · GA §11.1 p.121 (pdf 138)

$$
\boxed{\partial g} + \boxed{\partial^2 g} \to \boxed{R}
$$

Curvature requires first and second derivatives of the metric, so it can also be built from Gamma and its first derivatives.

**Symbols:** g metric components; R Riemann tensor

### (11.2) Newtonian equation of motion · derivation-step · GA §11.2 p.122 (pdf 139)

$$
m\frac{d^2x^i}{dt^2} = -m\frac{\partial\Phi(\vec x)}{\partial x^i}
$$

Each test particle accelerates down the gradient of the gravitational potential.

**Symbols:** Phi = -GM/|x| potential of a point mass

### (11.3) Difference of accelerations · derivation-step · GA §11.2 p.122 (pdf 139)

$$
\frac{d^2y^i}{dt^2} - \frac{d^2x^i}{dt^2} = -\frac{\partial\Phi(\vec x+\vec s)}{\partial x^i} + \frac{\partial\Phi(\vec x)}{\partial x^i} \approx -\frac{\partial^2\Phi(\vec x)}{\partial x^i\partial x^j}s^j
$$

Relative acceleration of two nearby particles is the Hessian of the potential acting on their separation, to first order.

**Symbols:** s = y - x small separation

### (11.4) Newtonian tidal (deviation) equation · central · GA §11.2 p.122 (pdf 139)

$$
\frac{d^2s^i}{dt^2} = -R^i{}_j s^j,\quad R^i{}_j = R_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2\Phi}{\partial x^i\partial x^j}
$$

The Newtonian tidal tensor controls how the separation between free particles accelerates; the template for relativistic geodesic deviation.

**Symbols:** R^i_j Newtonian tidal tensor (indices unbalanced deliberately)

### (11.5) Tidal tensor on the z-axis · central · GA §11.2 p.122 (pdf 139)

$$
R^i{}_j = \frac{GM}{z^3}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&-2\end{pmatrix}
$$

Above a point mass the cloud is stretched along the radial (z) direction and compressed equally in the two transverse directions; the trace vanishes.

**Symbols:** M central mass; z distance from centre

### (11.6) Geodesic deviation equation (abstract form) · central · GA §11.2 p.123 (pdf 140)

$$
\frac{D^2\boldsymbol\xi}{d\tau^2} + \boldsymbol R(\cdot,\boldsymbol u,\boldsymbol\xi,\boldsymbol u) = 0
$$

The covariant relative acceleration of neighbouring geodesics is given by feeding the fiducial 4-velocity (twice) and separation (once) into the Riemann tensor.

**Symbols:** xi separation vector; u 4-velocity of fiducial geodesic; D/dtau covariant derivative along it

### (11.7) Gauss's law for gravity (integral form) · supporting · GA §11.2 p.123 (pdf 140)

$$
\oint \vec g\cdot d\vec S = -4\pi G M
$$

Flux of the gravitational field through a closed surface is fixed by the enclosed mass.

**Symbols:** g gravitational field (F = m g); M enclosed mass

### (11.8) Poisson's equation · supporting · GA §11.2 p.123 (pdf 140)

$$
\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho
$$

The Laplacian of the potential, which is the trace of the tidal tensor, equals 4 pi G times the local density; zero in vacuum, so tidal deformation preserves volume.

**Symbols:** rho mass density

### (11.9) Loop change schematic · supporting · GA §11.3 p.124 (pdf 141)

$$
\delta V^\alpha = \big(\text{curvature measure}\big)^\alpha{}_\mu V^\mu\,\delta a\,\delta b
$$

Transporting V round a small parallelogram changes it by an amount linear in V and in each side length; this linearity is what lets the coefficient be a tensor.

**Symbols:** delta a, delta b side lengths along e_1, e_2

### (11.10) Parallel transport along e_1 · derivation-step · GA §11.3 p.124 (pdf 141)

$$
\frac{\partial V^\alpha}{\partial x^1} = -\Gamma^\alpha_{1\beta}V^\beta
$$

Component form of nabla_{e_1} V = 0.

### (11.11) First leg A to B · derivation-step · GA §11.3 p.124 (pdf 141)

$$
V^\alpha(B) = V^\alpha(A) - \int_{x^2=b}dx^1\,\Gamma^\alpha_{1\beta}V^\beta
$$

Integrating the transport condition along the bottom edge.

**Symbols:** subscript records the coordinate held fixed

### (11.12) Remaining legs B to C to D to A · derivation-step · GA §11.3 p.125 (pdf 142)

$$
V^\alpha(C) = V^\alpha(B) - \int_{x^1=a+\delta a}dx^2\,\Gamma^\alpha_{2\beta}V^\beta,\ V^\alpha(D) = V^\alpha(C) + \int_{x^2=b+\delta b}dx^1\,\Gamma^\alpha_{1\beta}V^\beta,\ V^\alpha(A_{\rm final}) = V^\alpha(D) + \int_{x^1=a}dx^2\,\Gamma^\alpha_{2\beta}V^\beta
$$

Signs flip on the return legs because they run against the coordinate directions.

### (11.13) Net change as paired edge integrals · derivation-step · GA §11.3 p.125 (pdf 142)

$$
\delta V^\alpha = \int_{x^1=a}dx^2\,\Gamma^\alpha_{2\beta}V^\beta - \int_{x^1=a+\delta a}dx^2\,\Gamma^\alpha_{2\beta}V^\beta + \int_{x^2=b+\delta b}dx^1\,\Gamma^\alpha_{1\beta}V^\beta - \int_{x^2=b}dx^1\,\Gamma^\alpha_{1\beta}V^\beta
$$

Opposite sides nearly cancel; only their difference across the loop survives.

### (11.14) Lowest-order expansion · derivation-step · GA §11.3 p.125 (pdf 142)

$$
\delta V^\alpha \approx \delta a\,\delta b\left[-\frac{\partial}{\partial x^1}(\Gamma^\alpha_{2\beta}V^\beta) + \frac{\partial}{\partial x^2}(\Gamma^\alpha_{1\beta}V^\beta)\right]
$$

Differences of integrals across the loop become derivatives times the loop area.

### (11.15) Loop change in terms of Gamma · derivation-step · GA §11.3 p.125 (pdf 142)

$$
\delta V^\alpha \approx \delta a\,\delta b\left[\frac{\partial\Gamma^\alpha_{1\beta}}{\partial x^2} - \frac{\partial\Gamma^\alpha_{2\beta}}{\partial x^1} + \Gamma^\alpha_{2\sigma}\Gamma^\sigma_{1\beta} - \Gamma^\alpha_{1\sigma}\Gamma^\sigma_{2\beta}\right]V^\beta
$$

Product rule plus the transport condition turn derivatives of V into Gamma-Gamma terms.

### (11.16) Loop change for general directions · supporting · GA §11.3 p.125 (pdf 142)

$$
\delta V^\alpha = \left[\partial_\mu\Gamma^\alpha_{\nu\beta} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\alpha_{\mu\beta} + \Gamma^\alpha_{\mu\sigma}\Gamma^\sigma_{\nu\beta} - \Gamma^\alpha_{\nu\sigma}\Gamma^\sigma_{\mu\beta}\right]V^\beta\,\delta a\,\delta b
$$

Same result with the loop going first along e_nu, then e_mu, then back.

### (11.17) Matrix commutator mnemonic · supporting · GA §11.3 p.125 (pdf 142)

$$
R^{\bullet}{}_{\bullet\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\Gamma^{\bullet}_{\nu\bullet} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^{\bullet}_{\mu\bullet} + \left[\Gamma^{\bullet}_{\mu\bullet},\Gamma^{\bullet}_{\nu\bullet}\right]
$$

Treating Gamma_mu as a matrix turns the index sums into matrix products and a commutator.

**Symbols:** [A,B] = AB - BA

### (11.18) Gamma as a 2x2 matrix · supporting · GA §11.3 p.125 (pdf 142)

$$
\Gamma^{\bullet}_{\mu\bullet} = \begin{pmatrix}\Gamma^1_{\mu1}&\Gamma^1_{\mu2}\\\Gamma^2_{\mu1}&\Gamma^2_{\mu2}\end{pmatrix}
$$

Layout of the connection matrix in two dimensions: upper index labels rows, last lower index labels columns.

### (11.19) Riemann tensor components · central · GA §11.3 p.125 (pdf 142)

$$
R^\alpha{}_{\beta\mu\nu} = \frac{\partial\Gamma^\alpha_{\nu\beta}}{\partial x^\mu} - \frac{\partial\Gamma^\alpha_{\mu\beta}}{\partial x^\nu} + \Gamma^\alpha_{\mu\sigma}\Gamma^\sigma_{\nu\beta} - \Gamma^\alpha_{\nu\sigma}\Gamma^\sigma_{\mu\beta}
$$

Definition of the Riemann curvature tensor in a coordinate basis; the working formula of the chapter (restated as 11.28 and 11.43).

**Symbols:** alpha: output index; beta: transported vector; mu, nu: loop plane

### (11.20) Weak-field connection · derivation-step · GA §11.3 p.125 (pdf 142)

$$
\Gamma^i_{tt} = \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x^i}
$$

For ds^2 = -(1+2Phi)dt^2 + dx^2+dy^2+dz^2 this is the relevant connection coefficient at first order.

### (11.21) Weak-field Riemann equals tidal tensor · central · GA §11.3 p.125 (pdf 142)

$$
R^i{}_{tjt} = \frac{\partial\Gamma^i_{tt}}{\partial x^j} = \frac{\partial^2\Phi}{\partial x^j\partial x^i}
$$

Relativistic curvature reduces to the Newtonian tidal tensor in the weak static limit.

### (11.22) All-lower Riemann in general coordinates · supporting · GA §11.4 p.126 (pdf 143)

$$
R_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\left(g_{\alpha\nu,\beta\mu} - g_{\alpha\mu,\beta\nu} + g_{\beta\mu,\alpha\nu} - g_{\beta\nu,\alpha\mu}\right) + g_{\sigma\rho}\left(\Gamma^\sigma_{\beta\mu}\Gamma^\rho_{\alpha\nu} - \Gamma^\sigma_{\beta\nu}\Gamma^\rho_{\alpha\mu}\right)
$$

Riemann directly from metric derivatives plus quadratic connection terms; reduces to 11.23 where Gamma = 0.

**Symbols:** commas denote partial derivatives

### (11.23) Riemann in a local inertial frame · central · GA §11.4 p.126 (pdf 143)

$$
R_{\hat\mu\hat\nu\hat\alpha\hat\beta} = \tfrac12\left(\frac{\partial^2 g_{\hat\alpha\hat\nu}}{\partial x^{\hat\beta}\partial x^{\hat\mu}} - \frac{\partial^2 g_{\hat\alpha\hat\mu}}{\partial x^{\hat\beta}\partial x^{\hat\nu}} + \frac{\partial^2 g_{\hat\beta\hat\mu}}{\partial x^{\hat\alpha}\partial x^{\hat\nu}} - \frac{\partial^2 g_{\hat\beta\hat\nu}}{\partial x^{\hat\alpha}\partial x^{\hat\mu}}\right)
$$

At a point where the connection vanishes, curvature is purely second metric derivatives, making the symmetries manifest.

**Symbols:** hats denote LIF coordinates

### (11.24) Pair symmetries · central · GA §11.4 p.126 (pdf 143)

$$
R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = -R_{\beta\alpha\gamma\delta},\quad R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = -R_{\alpha\beta\delta\gamma},\quad R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = R_{\gamma\delta\alpha\beta}
$$

Antisymmetric within each index pair, symmetric under exchange of the pairs.

### (11.25) Cyclic identity · central · GA §11.4 p.126 (pdf 143)

$$
R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} + R_{\alpha\delta\beta\gamma} + R_{\alpha\gamma\delta\beta} = 0
$$

Cyclic sum over the last three indices vanishes (first Bianchi identity).

### Independent component count · supporting · GA §11.4 p.126 (pdf 143)

$$
N_{\rm indep} = \frac{N^2(N^2-1)}{12}\ \ (0,1,6,20 \text{ for } N=1,2,3,4)
$$

Symmetries reduce N^4 components to this number; 20 in spacetime.

**Symbols:** N dimension

### (11.26) Ricci tensor · central · GA §11.5 p.127 (pdf 144)

$$
R_{\nu\beta} = R^\mu{}_{\nu\mu\beta} = R^0{}_{\nu0\beta} + R^1{}_{\nu1\beta} + R^2{}_{\nu2\beta} + R^3{}_{\nu3\beta}
$$

Contract the upper index with the first index of the loop pair; the result is symmetric.

### (11.27) Ricci scalar · central · GA §11.5 p.127 (pdf 144)

$$
R = g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}
$$

Metric trace of the Ricci tensor; a single number of curvature at each point.

### (11.28) Riemann formula (relabelled) · supporting · GA §11.6 p.128 (pdf 145)

$$
R^\mu{}_{\nu\alpha\beta} = \frac{\partial\Gamma^\mu_{\beta\nu}}{\partial x^\alpha} - \frac{\partial\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\nu}}{\partial x^\beta} + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\rho}\Gamma^\rho_{\beta\nu} - \Gamma^\mu_{\beta\rho}\Gamma^\rho_{\alpha\nu}
$$

Same as 11.19 with renamed indices, used for the brute-force examples.

### (11.29) Flat polar check, first component · derivation-step · GA §11.6 p.128 (pdf 145)

$$
R^\theta{}_{r\theta r} = 0 + \frac{1}{r^2} + 0 - \frac{1}{r}\frac{1}{r} = 0
$$

Derivative and product terms cancel in flat space.

### (11.30) Flat polar check, second component · derivation-step · GA §11.6 p.128 (pdf 145)

$$
R^r{}_{\theta r\theta} = -1 + 0 + 0 + 1 = 0
$$

Again the curvature vanishes despite non-zero Gammas.

### (11.31) 2-sphere line element · supporting · GA §11.6 p.128 (pdf 145)

$$
ds^2 = a^2 d\theta^2 + a^2\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2
$$

Metric of the surface of a sphere of radius a.

### (11.32) 2-sphere connection · derivation-step · GA §11.6 p.128 (pdf 145)

$$
\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi} = -\sin\theta\cos\theta,\quad \Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi} = \cot\theta
$$

Non-zero Christoffel symbols of the sphere.

### (11.33) Sphere Riemann component · derivation-step · GA §11.6 p.128 (pdf 145)

$$
R^\theta{}_{\phi\theta\phi} = \sin^2\theta - \cos^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = \sin^2\theta
$$

Non-zero curvature component of the sphere.

### (11.34) Sphere Riemann component (partner) · derivation-step · GA §11.6 p.128 (pdf 145)

$$
R^\phi{}_{\theta\phi\theta} = \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta} - \frac{\cos^2\theta}{\sin^2\theta} = 1
$$

Second non-zero mixed component; consistent with the single independent R_{theta phi theta phi} = a^2 sin^2 theta.

### (11.35) Sphere Ricci tensor · supporting · GA §11.6 p.128 (pdf 145)

$$
R_{\phi\phi} = \sin^2\theta,\quad R_{\theta\theta} = 1
$$

Ricci components; note R_{mu nu} = g_{mu nu}/a^2.

### (11.36) Sphere Ricci scalar · central · GA §11.6 p.128 (pdf 145)

$$
R = g^{\theta\theta}R_{\theta\theta} + g^{\phi\phi}R_{\phi\phi} = \frac{1}{a^2} + \frac{\sin^2\theta}{a^2\sin^2\theta} = \frac{2}{a^2}
$$

Constant positive scalar curvature that grows as the sphere shrinks.

### (11.37) Deviation acceleration as repeated covariant derivative · supporting · GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146)

$$
\frac{D^2\boldsymbol\xi}{d\tau^2} = \nabla_{\boldsymbol u}\nabla_{\boldsymbol u}\boldsymbol\xi
$$

The D/dtau in the deviation equation is the covariant derivative along the fiducial 4-velocity applied twice.

### (11.38) Geodesic deviation (as printed) · supporting · GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146)

$$
\frac{D^2\boldsymbol\xi}{d\tau^2} = -\boldsymbol R(\cdot,\boldsymbol u,\boldsymbol\xi,\boldsymbol u)
$$

Restatement of 11.6; the printed version lacks the minus sign, which is restored here for consistency with 11.6 and 11.39.

### (11.39) Geodesic deviation in components · central · GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146)

$$
\left(\frac{D^2\boldsymbol\xi}{d\tau^2}\right)^\alpha = -R^\alpha{}_{\beta\gamma\delta}u^\beta\xi^\gamma u^\delta
$$

Component form used for calculation.

### (11.40) Deviation in a freely falling frame · central · GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146)

$$
\frac{d^2\xi^{\hat\alpha}}{d\tau^2} = -R^{\hat\alpha}{}_{\hat 0\hat\beta\hat 0}\,\xi^{\hat\beta}
$$

With u = (1,0,0,0) and ordinary derivatives, curvature components act as a matrix of spring constants. (OCR of the printed hats is damaged; this is the intended form.)

**Symbols:** hatted indices: freely falling orthonormal frame

### (11.41) Schwarzschild Riemann components in a freely falling frame · central · GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146)

$$
R_{\hat t\hat r\hat t\hat r} = -\frac{2M}{r^3},\ R_{\hat\theta\hat\phi\hat\theta\hat\phi} = +\frac{2M}{r^3},\ R_{\hat t\hat\theta\hat t\hat\theta} = R_{\hat t\hat\phi\hat t\hat\phi} = +\frac{M}{r^3},\ R_{\hat r\hat\theta\hat r\hat\theta} = R_{\hat r\hat\phi\hat r\hat\phi} = -\frac{M}{r^3}
$$

Curvature outside a spherical mass as measured by a local observer (index labels reconstructed from the OCR-damaged print using standard results).

**Symbols:** M mass (G = c = 1); r areal radius

### (11.42) Schwarzschild tidal equations · central · GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146)

$$
\frac{d^2\xi^{\hat r}}{d\tau^2} = \frac{2M}{r^3}\xi^{\hat r},\quad \frac{d^2\xi^{\hat\theta}}{d\tau^2} = -\frac{M}{r^3}\xi^{\hat\theta},\quad \frac{d^2\xi^{\hat\phi}}{d\tau^2} = -\frac{M}{r^3}\xi^{\hat\phi}
$$

Radial stretching and transverse squeezing, matching the Newtonian diag(1,1,-2) pattern.

## Worked examples

### Example 11.1 · intro · GA §11.2 p.122 (pdf 139)

**Problem:** Find how the separation between nearby dust particles in an initially spherical cloud evolves as the cloud falls toward a point mass.

**Method:** Write Newton's second law for two particles, subtract, Taylor expand the gradient of Phi about one particle to first order in the separation, define the Hessian as a tidal tensor, and evaluate it on the z-axis for Phi = -GM/r.

**Key insight:** Relative acceleration is linear in separation and set by second derivatives of the potential; the resulting matrix has one negative (stretching) eigenvalue along the radial line and two positive (squeezing) ones transverse to it.

**Result:** d^2 s^i/dt^2 = -R^i_j s^j with R^i_j = (GM/z^3) diag(1,1,-2): stretch along z at 2GM/z^3, compression along x and y at GM/z^3.

**Concepts:** Newtonian tidal tensor, tidal forces, separation (deviation) vector

### Example 11.2 · standard · GA §11.2 p.123 (pdf 140)

**Problem:** Show that the falling cloud changes shape but keeps its volume.

**Method:** Build Gauss's law for gravity by analogy with electrostatics, convert to Poisson's equation, note that the Laplacian of Phi is the trace (sum of eigenvalues) of the tidal matrix, and relate the second time derivative of the cloud's volume to that trace.

**Key insight:** The trace of the tidal tensor measures enclosed mass density; with no mass inside the cloud the trace vanishes, so tides only reshape. A sphere enclosing the Earth would instead shrink.

**Result:** Small vacuum cloud evolves into an ellipsoid of equal volume, to the order considered.

**Concepts:** Poisson's equation for gravity, volume preservation of a freely falling cloud in vacuum, Newtonian tidal tensor

### Example 11.3 · challenging · GA §11.3 p.124 (pdf 141)

**Problem:** Compute the change in a vector's components after parallel transport around an infinitesimal coordinate parallelogram, and extract the Riemann tensor.

**Method:** Integrate the transport condition dV/dx = -Gamma V along each of four edges, collect opposite edges into differences, expand to first order in each side length, apply the product rule and substitute the transport condition again for derivatives of V, then relabel to general directions.

**Key insight:** Opposite edges cancel at zeroth order, leaving a term proportional to the area delta a delta b and linear in V; the coefficient therefore defines a (1,3) tensor built from dGamma and GammaGamma.

**Result:** R^alpha_{beta mu nu} = d_mu Gamma^alpha_{nu beta} - d_nu Gamma^alpha_{mu beta} + Gamma^alpha_{mu sigma} Gamma^sigma_{nu beta} - Gamma^alpha_{nu sigma} Gamma^sigma_{mu beta}.

**Concepts:** holonomy of parallel transport around a closed loop, Riemann tensor component formula, parallel transport

### Example 11.4 · standard · GA §11.3 p.125 (pdf 142)

**Problem:** Evaluate the Riemann tensor for the static weak-field metric and compare with the Newtonian tidal tensor.

**Method:** Use the single relevant connection coefficient Gamma^i_tt = dPhi/dx^i, drop time derivatives (static) and products of Gammas (second order in Phi), and substitute into the Riemann formula.

**Key insight:** The relativistic curvature components with two time slots reduce exactly to the Hessian of Phi, so Riemann generalizes Newtonian tides.

**Result:** R^i_{tjt} = d^2 Phi/dx^j dx^i, identical to the tidal tensor of eqn 11.4.

**Concepts:** weak-field limit of the Riemann tensor, Newtonian tidal tensor

### Example 11.5 · standard · GA §11.4 p.126 (pdf 143)

**Problem:** Find a form of the Riemann tensor that makes its index symmetries evident.

**Method:** Work at a point in a local inertial frame where Gamma vanishes; only dGamma terms survive, and writing Gamma via first metric derivatives gives Riemann as four second derivatives of the metric; use symmetry of g and of mixed partials.

**Key insight:** Choosing coordinates that kill the connection at a point isolates the genuinely curvature-carrying second derivatives, and tensor equations proved in one frame hold in all.

**Result:** Eqn 11.23 and the symmetries R_abcd = -R_bacd = -R_abdc = R_cdab plus the cyclic identity.

**Concepts:** local flatness theorem, Riemann tensor in a local inertial frame, symmetries of the Riemann tensor, cyclic (first Bianchi) identity

### Example 11.6 · intro · GA §11.4 p.127 (pdf 144)

**Problem:** Interpret the independent-component count in one and two dimensions.

**Method:** Apply the counting formula and the symmetries: in 1D all components have repeated indices within a pair; in 2D the only surviving independent combination is R_1212.

**Key insight:** A line can always be reparametrized to Euclidean form, so has no intrinsic curvature; a surface has exactly one curvature number per point.

**Result:** N=1: 0 components; N=2: single component R_1212.

**Concepts:** number of independent Riemann components, intrinsic curvature, extrinsic curvature

### Example 11.7 · intro · GA §11.6 p.128 (pdf 145)

**Problem:** Verify that the Euclidean plane in polar coordinates has vanishing Riemann tensor.

**Method:** Use Gamma^r_thetatheta = -r and Gamma^theta_rtheta = 1/r in the Riemann formula for mixed components R^theta_{r theta r} and R^r_{theta r theta}.

**Key insight:** Non-zero Christoffel symbols from curvilinear coordinates generate derivative and quadratic terms that cancel exactly; the formalism does not confuse coordinates with curvature.

**Result:** R^theta_{r theta r} = 1/r^2 - 1/r^2 = 0 and R^r_{theta r theta} = -1 + 1 = 0; Ricci tensor and scalar also vanish.

**Concepts:** flat space in curvilinear coordinates, Riemann tensor component formula

### Example 11.8 · standard · GA §11.6 p.128 (pdf 145)

**Problem:** Compute the Riemann components, Ricci tensor and Ricci scalar for the surface of a sphere of radius a.

**Method:** From the line element obtain Gamma^theta_phiphi = -sin theta cos theta and Gamma^phi_thetaphi = cot theta; evaluate two mixed Riemann components; contract to Ricci; trace with the inverse metric g^thetatheta = 1/a^2, g^phiphi = 1/(a^2 sin^2 theta).

**Key insight:** Unlike the plane, the terms no longer cancel; the scalar curvature is constant over the sphere and scales as the inverse square of its radius.

**Result:** R^theta_{phi theta phi} = sin^2 theta, R^phi_{theta phi theta} = 1, R_thetatheta = 1, R_phiphi = sin^2 theta, R = 2/a^2.

**Concepts:** curvature of the 2-sphere, Ricci tensor, Ricci scalar

### Example 11.9 · standard · GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146)

**Problem:** Use the freely falling frame form of geodesic deviation to find tidal accelerations near a spherical mass described by the Schwarzschild geometry.

**Method:** Quote the orthonormal-frame Riemann components (derived later), raise indices with eta, and read off R^i_{0j0} for radial and transverse directions.

**Key insight:** Relativistic tides take the same form as Newtonian ones: radial anti-spring of strength 2M/r^3, transverse springs of strength M/r^3.

**Result:** d^2 xi^r/dtau^2 = +(2M/r^3) xi^r; d^2 xi^theta/dtau^2 = -(M/r^3) xi^theta; same for phi.

**Concepts:** Schwarzschild tidal tensor, geodesic deviation equation, freely falling orthonormal frame, Riemann components as tidal spring constants


## Analogies and intuitions

### Deducing the Earth's shape while standing on it → intrinsic curvature · useful · GA p.120 (pdf 137)

Humans confined to the 2D surface of the Earth still found ways to establish it is a ball; spacetime observers face the same insider problem, only harder because there is nothing like watching distant stars change elevation.

**Where it breaks down:** Earth-surface methods can exploit a third spatial dimension (light from stars); for spacetime no such outside reference exists.

**App idea:** Flatland explorer: a 2D creature on a hidden surface performs triangle, circle-circumference and transport experiments; the learner guesses the shape before it is revealed.

### Cylinder made by rolling a flat sheet versus a sphere that cannot be gift-wrapped → extrinsic curvature · strong · GA §11.1 p.120 (pdf 137)

Anything you can unroll into a flat sheet without tearing or stretching only looks curved from outside; the sphere cannot be flattened, so its curvature is real.

**Where it breaks down:** Wrapping is about isometric embedding; learners may think any visibly bent surface is curved, and the torus or cone cases need extra care.

**App idea:** Unroll-or-tear: learner tries to peel a paper mesh off a cylinder, cone and sphere; strain colour shows where stretching is forced.

### Rubber-sheet model of gravity → spacetime curvature (as opposed to spatial curvature) · weak · GA p.120 (pdf 137)

Mentioned in a margin note as a common picture, with a caution that it shows curved space only, while effects like planetary precession depend strongly on the time-time metric component.

**Where it breaks down:** Explicitly flagged by the authors: misses curvature involving time, and relies on an external gravity to pull objects into dents.

### Ant walking on a large ball → holonomy of parallel transport around a closed loop · useful · GA §11.4 p.127 (pdf 144)

An ant can only learn its world is curved by walking a closed loop carrying a direction and comparing on return; such loops are impossible on a line, which is why 1D spaces have no intrinsic curvature.

**Where it breaks down:** Ignores other insider tests (triangle angle sums, circumference ratios).

**App idea:** Ant-with-a-compass-needle walk on a sphere, where the needle is transported without twisting and the final misalignment is displayed.

### Tidal deviation as a harmonic oscillator; curvature as spring constant → Riemann components as tidal spring constants · strong · GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146)

In a freely falling frame, each deviation component obeys an equation like x-double-dot = -omega^2 x, with Riemann components in the role of omega^2; negative values correspond to separating motion.

**Where it breaks down:** Only holds for small separations and in a frame where the equation is diagonal; 'negative spring constants' give exponential rather than oscillatory motion.

**App idea:** Springs-between-particles cartoon: a 3D cross of test masses connected by springs whose stiffness equals the tidal eigenvalues; move closer to a mass and watch springs stiffen or turn into repulsive struts.

### Gauss's law in electrostatics carried over to gravity → Poisson's equation for gravity · useful · GA §11.2 p.123 (pdf 140)

The familiar link between Coulomb's law and div E = rho/epsilon0 is mirrored to write div g = -4 pi G rho and Poisson's equation, which then controls the trace of the tidal tensor.

**Where it breaks down:** Sign differs (like masses attract) and there is no negative mass.

### Ricci as an average of Riemann → Ricci tensor · useful · GA §11.5 p.127 (pdf 144)

Readers are told to think of Ricci informally as averaging over Riemann components, an average that captures volume shrinkage.

**Where it breaks down:** Not literally an average; it is a specific contraction that discards the tidal (Weyl) part, which can be non-zero where Ricci vanishes.

**App idea:** Ball-of-particles volume meter: a cloud in vacuum (Ricci = 0) versus inside matter (Ricci != 0), with shape and volume tracked separately.

### Connection matrices and a commutator → matrix commutator form of the Riemann tensor · useful · GA §11.3 p.125 (pdf 142)

Memory aid: treat each Gamma_mu as a matrix so Riemann reads like derivative-minus-derivative plus a commutator.

**Where it breaks down:** The matrix picture hides which index is which; careless ordering of the lower indices of Gamma gives sign errors.

**App idea:** Symbolic Riemann calculator that displays each Gamma_mu as a coloured matrix and animates the commutator product.


## Misconceptions addressed

### Any visibly curved surface, such as a cylinder, is curved in the sense relevant to geometry and gravity. · GA §11.1 p.120 (pdf 137)

**Correction:** Only intrinsic curvature counts; a cylinder is flat intrinsically because it unrolls into a plane and parallel lines on it never meet.

**Why tempting:** Everyday 'curved' means bent in 3D space, and textbook pictures show surfaces embedded in space.

### Non-zero Christoffel symbols, or a vector's components changing under parallel transport along a path, mean the space is curved. · GA §11.3 p.124 (pdf 141)

**Correction:** Those effects can come purely from curvilinear coordinates; only the net change around a closed loop (non-zero Riemann) signals curvature, as the polar-coordinate plane example shows.

**Why tempting:** Gammas are the first 'gravity-like' objects learners meet, and the geodesic equation makes them look like forces.

### The rubber-sheet picture shows what spacetime curvature is. · GA p.120 (pdf 137)

**Correction:** It depicts curved space only; the time part of the metric often dominates relativistic corrections, so curvature is a genuinely spacetime property.

**Why tempting:** It is the most widely reproduced popular image of general relativity.

### A single falling particle can reveal whether there is a gravitational field. · GA §11.2 p.122 (pdf 139)

**Correction:** By the equivalence principle one particle cannot distinguish curvature from frame acceleration; relative acceleration of two separated particles is required.

**Why tempting:** In Newtonian thinking we detect gravity by releasing an object and watching it fall relative to the ground.

### Tidal forces squash a falling cloud and reduce its volume. · GA §11.2 p.123 (pdf 140)

**Correction:** In vacuum the cloud stretches along the radial line and compresses sideways by amounts that exactly balance, so its volume is unchanged; only enclosed matter shrinks volume.

**Why tempting:** Gravity is attractive, so one expects everything to be pulled together.

### Vanishing Ricci tensor means spacetime is flat. · GA p.130 (pdf 147)

**Correction:** Ricci can vanish (as outside a star, Exercise 11.3) while Riemann does not; tidal stretching persists in vacuum.

**Why tempting:** Ricci is called a curvature tensor and the vacuum field equation sets it to zero.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The Riemann tensor has 256 independent pieces of information in spacetime. · GA §11.4 p.126 (pdf 143)

**Correction:** Its symmetries leave only 20 independent components in four dimensions (one in two dimensions).

**Why tempting:** A four-index object in four dimensions naively has 4^4 components.

### A curve drawn tied in a knot or wound on a cylinder is a curved one-dimensional space. · GA §11.4 p.127 (pdf 144)

**Correction:** Every 1D space is isometric to a straight line; its bending is purely extrinsic.

**Why tempting:** Visually the curve is obviously bent.

### Symmetries derived in a local inertial frame hold only in that frame. · GA §11.4 p.126 (pdf 143)

**Correction:** They are statements about tensor components related by index permutations, so they hold in every frame once true in one.

**Why tempting:** The derivation explicitly used special coordinates where Gamma vanishes.


## Thought experiments

### Two parallel travellers on a curved surface · GA §11.1 p.121 (pdf 138)

**Setup:** Two free particles start moving side by side along parallel geodesics on a sphere, a plane and a saddle.

**Lesson:** Their separation stays constant only on flat surfaces; convergence or divergence measures the sign and size of curvature.

**App idea:** Race two geodesics on switchable surfaces and plot their separation.

### Vector carried around a spherical triangle · GA §11.1 p.121 (pdf 138)

**Setup:** Starting at a point on a sphere, carry a vector without turning it along a closed geodesic triangle and compare on return.

**Lesson:** Net rotation after a closed loop is an unambiguous sign of intrinsic curvature; on a cylinder or plane it would vanish.

**App idea:** Interactive globe with draggable triangle vertices and a live rotation-angle readout.

### Static star frame test for gravity · GA §11.2 p.121 (pdf 138)

**Setup:** In an idealized static geometry around a star, place one particle at rest in the star's frame and see if it accelerates.

**Lesson:** This would detect gravity only if a preferred static frame existed; relativity denies such frames in general, so the test must be replaced by relative acceleration of two particles.

### Falling cloud of dust around an observer · GA §11.2 p.122 (pdf 139)

**Setup:** A spherical cloud of free test particles, with an observer at its centre, drops toward the Earth.

**Lesson:** Seen from the cloud's own free-fall frame, it elongates toward the Earth and narrows sideways at constant volume: tidal forces are the residue of gravity after the equivalence principle removes the common acceleration.

**App idea:** Animated cloud with frame toggle and volume meter.

### Large shell of particles surrounding the Earth · GA §11.2 p.123 (pdf 140)

**Setup:** Instead of a small cloud, imagine a sphere of free particles enclosing the whole Earth, all released from rest.

**Lesson:** Because the shell encloses mass, the volume does shrink, showing that volume change tracks enclosed density (the Ricci part of curvature).

**App idea:** Slider growing the cloud radius past the planet's surface with volume versus time plotted.


## Applications and observations

- **Tidal stretching of a cloud falling toward the Earth** (numerical-estimate, GA §11.2 p.122 (pdf 139)): Newtonian tidal tensor for a point mass gives radial stretching and transverse compression; used as the physical anchor for curvature. Key numbers: Tidal matrix (GM/r^3) diag(1,1,-2); trace zero in vacuum.
- **Tidal accelerations near a spherical star or black hole (Schwarzschild)** (astrophysical-system, GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146)): Freely falling observers see radial separations grow and transverse separations shrink, with rates set by orthonormal Riemann components. Key numbers: Radial +2M/r^3, transverse -M/r^3 (G = c = 1).
- **Planetary orbit precession depends on time-time metric** (observation, GA p.120 (pdf 137)): Margin note cites orbital precession as a case where the timelike metric component dominates the relativistic correction, undermining purely spatial pictures.
- **Curvature of a sphere of radius a** (numerical-estimate, GA §11.6 p.128 (pdf 145)): Scalar curvature of a 2-sphere is constant and scales with inverse radius squared, giving a concrete scale for curvature radius. Key numbers: R = 2/a^2

## Historical notes

- **Carl Friedrich Gauss:** Showed how distance measurements made within a 2D surface determine its curvature objectively. — Established that curvature can be intrinsic, the precondition for curved spacetime without an embedding. (GA p.120 (pdf 137))
- **Bernhard Riemann (1854):** Extended curvature to spaces of any dimension, the description later encoded in the Riemann tensor. — Provided the mathematics Einstein adopted for gravitation. (GA p.120 (pdf 137))
- **Elwin Bruno Christoffel, Gregorio Ricci-Curbastro, Tullio Levi-Civita, Albert Einstein:** Developed Riemann's description into tensor calculus, which Einstein brought into physics. — Lineage from geometry to general relativity. (GA p.120 (pdf 137))
- **Euclid:** Stated five postulates of plane geometry, the fifth being equivalent to the parallel postulate. — Failure of the fifth postulate is the entry point to curved geometry. (GA §11.2 p.121 (pdf 138))
- **Gregorio Ricci-Curbastro:** Margin biography (1853-1925) attached to the Ricci tensor. — Name-giver of the contracted curvature tensor central to the field equations. (GA §11.5 p.127 (pdf 144))

## Notation and conventions

- **Riemann index order:** R^alpha_{beta mu nu}: first lower index is the transported vector, last two label the loop plane; the derivative term with index mu carries a plus sign as in eqn 11.19 (the MTW convention). — Other books order or sign differently; compare before mixing sources. (GA §11.3 p.125 (pdf 142))
- **Ricci contraction:** R_{nu beta} = R^mu_{nu mu beta}: contract upper index with the third slot. — With this choice the sphere has positive Ricci scalar. (GA §11.5 p.127 (pdf 144))
- **Bold symbols:** Tensors are bold (R for Riemann, G for Einstein), but the Ricci tensor is written only in components, not bold. — Other books do give Ricci a bold symbol, for example Ri. (GA §11.5 p.127 (pdf 144))
- **Slot notation:** R( , , , ) with empty slots, and R(., u, xi, u) for a partially filled tensor returning a vector. — Continues the linear slot machine picture of Chapter 4. (GA §11.2 p.123 (pdf 140))
- **Hatted indices:** Hats denote components in a local inertial or freely falling orthonormal frame; indices raised and lowered with eta there. — In the OCR copy hats appear as dots. (GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146))
- **Units and signature:** Signature (-,+,+,+); Schwarzschild results in G = c = 1 units, while Newtonian examples keep G explicit. (GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146))
- **Unbalanced indices in Newtonian work:** In Example 11.1 index balance is temporarily relaxed (R^i_j = R_ij) since the space is Euclidean. — Margin note 12 gives the fussy form with g^{ki}. (GA §11.2 p.122 (pdf 139))
- **Edge-integral subscripts:** In the loop derivation the subscript on an integral names the coordinate held fixed along that edge, not its limits. (GA §11.3 p.125 (pdf 142))
- **Comma derivatives:** g_{alpha nu, beta mu} denotes second partial derivatives with respect to x^beta and x^mu. (GA §11.4 p.126 (pdf 143))
- **Covariant proper-time derivative:** D/dtau denotes nabla_u along the fiducial world line; D^2 xi/dtau^2 = nabla_u nabla_u xi. (GA §11.7 p.129 (pdf 146))

## Teaching gems

### Name the Newtonian Hessian of the potential 'R' before introducing Riemann, then show the weak-field Riemann components equal it. · GA §11.2 p.122 (pdf 139)

**Why it works:** Learners already trust Newtonian tides; the new tensor inherits meaning instead of arriving as an unmotivated formula.

**App idea:** Side-by-side panel: Newtonian tidal matrix computed from Phi and relativistic R^i_{tjt} computed from the weak-field metric, updating together as the learner moves a probe point.

**Why it works:** Connects the tensor-as-linear-machine idea to a physical procedure, so the index structure of Riemann has a reason.

**App idea:** Loop explorer where doubling delta a doubles delta V, and rotating the loop plane changes which components contribute.

**Why it works:** Directly defuses the confusion between coordinate effects and curvature using a familiar space.

**App idea:** Term-by-term cancellation animation: each of the four Riemann terms appears as a coloured bar that sums to zero for the plane and to a non-zero value for the sphere.

### Tidal stretching with constant volume via the trace of the tidal tensor and Poisson's equation. · GA §11.2 p.123 (pdf 140)

**Why it works:** Gives the Ricci/Riemann split a tangible physical meaning (shape change versus volume change) before the field equations.

**App idea:** Deformable particle ball with separate shape and volume gauges, placed in vacuum or inside a uniform-density region.

## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Printed eqn 11.38 lacks the minus sign present in 11.6 and 11.39.** — Sign confusion about whether curvature causes convergence or divergence. *Suggestion:* Always use the component form with the explicit minus sign and check against the Newtonian limit.
- **Geodesic deviation equation is stated, not derived, and the link to loop transport is only asserted.** — The claim that both tests give the same tensor may feel like a leap of faith. *Suggestion:* Offer a short heuristic: deviation arises because transporting u across to the neighbouring geodesic and along it do not commute, which is the loop argument in disguise.
- **The loop derivation treats V inside edge integrals loosely and drops terms without stating the order of approximation.** — Careful readers may worry about missing second-order pieces or sign conventions for loop orientation. *Suggestion:* State explicitly that all terms are kept to order delta a delta b and draw the loop orientation, then verify numerically with a small loop on a sphere.
- **Weak-field example says the listed components are the only non-zero ones, glossing over linear-order truncation and antisymmetric partners.** — Learners may miscount components or think Gamma-Gamma terms vanish exactly. *Suggestion:* Add a caveat: to first order in Phi, and up to R^i_{ttj} = -R^i_{tjt}.
- **Choice of Ricci contraction is justified only as constrained by symmetries; other contractions giving zero or minus Ricci are not shown.** — The index position R^mu_{nu mu beta} looks arbitrary and sign conventions across books confuse. *Suggestion:* Demonstrate that contracting the first two indices gives zero by antisymmetry and contracting with the last index gives minus Ricci.
- **Vielbein transformation and Schwarzschild frame components in §11.7 are quoted from later chapters, and the OCR copy of eqns 11.40-11.42 is garbled.** — Hard to reproduce Example 11.9; index labels are unreadable in the reading copy. *Suggestion:* Present the reconstructed components (t-r: -2M/r^3, t-theta and t-phi: +M/r^3) and show the conversion for one component explicitly.
- **No link is made between the sphere's Ricci scalar 2/a^2 and Gaussian curvature 1/a^2, or to angle excess of the triangle in Fig. 11.2.** — Missed opportunity to connect the formal result to the loop picture quantitatively. *Suggestion:* Show that the rotation angle around a loop equals enclosed area times Gaussian curvature, i.e. area times R/2 in 2D.
- **Margin note on the Einstein tensor shows a coefficient 1/3 in the reading copy.** — Learners may memorize the wrong trace-reversal coefficient. *Suggestion:* Correct to G_mu nu = R_mu nu - (1/2) g_mu nu R.
- **Reading-copy slips in Example 11.1: the stretching equation along z shows the coordinate z where the separation component s^z belongs, and it writes r for the distance that eqn 11.5 calls z.** — It can look as if the tidal acceleration depends on position instead of on the separation, which defeats the point of the tidal tensor. *Suggestion:* Write it as d^2 s^z/dt^2 = +(2GM/z^3) s^z and d^2 s^x/dt^2 = -(GM/z^3) s^x, and say that z and r are the same distance on the axis.
- **The volume argument in Example 11.2 is only sketched: the relation between the trace of the tidal matrix and the volume's second time derivative is asserted, not shown (the reading copy also drops the d^2/dt^2 in m s^i = ...), and constant volume also requires the cloud to start with no expansion rate.** — Learners may not see why the sum of eigenvalues controls volume, or may think volume is conserved for any initial motion. *Suggestion:* For a small ellipsoid with semi-axes s_i, V is proportional to s_1 s_2 s_3. Starting from rest, the fractional second derivative of V equals -tr(R^i_j) = -4 pi G rho. Make the release-from-rest condition explicit.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'If you could never leave the surface of a ball, how would you prove it is curved?' Let the learner propose tests before naming geodesic deviation and loop transport.
- Contrast cylinder and sphere early with a paper-wrapping question; check the learner can say why a cylinder is intrinsically flat.
- Order: tides in Newtonian gravity -> equivalence principle forces two-particle tests -> loop transport derivation -> weak-field check -> symmetries -> Ricci -> sphere example -> free-fall deviation with Schwarzschild.
- Before the loop derivation, ask the learner to predict what the change of V should be proportional to (V, delta a, delta b) and why that makes it a tensor.
- Check for understanding: 'The plane in polar coordinates has non-zero Christoffel symbols. Is it curved? How would you prove it?'
- Check: 'Why does a falling cloud keep its volume, and when would it not?' Expect the trace of the tidal tensor and enclosed density.
- When learners ask why Ricci and not Riemann enters the field equations, point to rank-two tensors on the matter side and the volume-shrinking meaning of Ricci; warn that Ricci = 0 does not mean flat.
- Watch for sign and index-order confusion; fix a convention (MTW, as in GA) and have the learner verify with the sphere result R = 2/a^2 > 0.
- Use the spring-constant picture to interpret signs: a negative R^i_{0i0} component means neighbouring particles separate along that direction.
- Common learner question: 'How many numbers describe curvature?' Answer with 0, 1, 6, 20 and relate 1 in 2D to Gaussian curvature.
- If the learner struggles with the matrix mnemonic, compute Gamma_theta and Gamma_phi for the sphere as explicit 2x2 matrices and evaluate the commutator together.
