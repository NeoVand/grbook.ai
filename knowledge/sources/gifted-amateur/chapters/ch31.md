# GA ch31 · A reintroduction to geometry

> Rebuilds vectors, 1-forms and tensors on a bare smooth manifold without a metric: a vector becomes the derivative d/dlambda along a curve, basis vectors become partial derivatives, basis 1-forms become coordinate differentials dx^alpha, and tensors become multilinear slot machines with a toolkit of operations (gradient, contraction, divergence, transpose, symmetrization, antisymmetrization, wedge product).

**Pages:** printed 322–333 · pdf 339–350 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 4/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

The algebra is light (chain rule, duality delta, permutations), but the conceptual shift from arrows to derivative operators on a metric-free manifold is abstract and moves fast. The heuristic 'remove the point' step and the tip-minus-base pictures help but are not rigorous, and the tensor-operations catalogue is dense, list-like and contains index and bracket slips (31.27 index labels, 31.40 Q_{alpha beta} for Q_{alpha gamma}, square brackets in 31.50). Exercises 31.6 and 31.7 assume Part II connections and Maxwell's equations.

## Role in the book

Second chapter of Part V, the book's rigorous second pass through geometry. Part I (ch2-ch5) had introduced vectors as arrows, 1-forms as stacks of surfaces and tensors as slot machines on flat spacetime with a metric; Part II used connections and curvature. Having reviewed classical extrinsic and intrinsic curvature in ch30, the authors now strip the arena down to a smooth manifold and re-derive the objects in a coordinate-free, derivative-based language. This supplies the vocabulary (tangent space, dual space, vectors as operators, dx^alpha as dual basis, antisymmetrized tensor products) that ch32 needs for p-forms, ch33 for exterior and Lie derivatives, ch34-ch36 for the connection and Cartan's method, and ch37-ch38 for Hodge duality and Stokes' theorem.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can say what makes a space a manifold (locally resembling R^n at every point) and classify examples such as the circle, sphere and torus versus a kinked line, a self-crossing curve, a double cone or a line piercing a plane.
- Reader can explain why the school picture of a vector as an arrow between two points and of the gradient as a vector normal to a surface is inadequate on a general manifold, including the index-placement mismatch in the components of grad h.
- Reader can obtain a tangent vector as the derivative of a parametrized curve and, using the chain rule, write it as v = (dx^alpha/dlambda) partial/partial x^alpha.
- Reader can identify components v^alpha = dx^alpha/dlambda and coordinate basis vectors e_alpha = partial/partial x^alpha, and apply a vector to a function to get its directional derivative v[f].
- Reader can show that the tangent vectors at a point form a vector space by combining the derivative operators of two curves into the tangent of a third curve.
- Reader can define a congruence of curves and explain how it generates a vector field, and why vectors at different points cannot be compared without extra structure (a connection or the Lie derivative).
- Reader can state the duality between vectors and 1-forms, use linearity of the pairing <sigma, v>, and interpret it as the number of 1-form surfaces a vector pierces.
- Reader can identify the coordinate basis 1-forms with differentials dx^alpha, verify <dx^mu, partial/partial x^nu> = delta^mu_nu, and show that <df, u> reproduces u[f].
- Reader can define an (n, m) tensor as a multilinear machine, build tensors with the tensor product, and extract components by feeding in basis 1-forms and basis vectors, then reconstruct the tensor from components.
- Reader can write the metric as the (0,2) tensor g = g_{mu nu} dx^mu tensor dx^nu.
- Reader can carry out and interpret gradient, contraction, divergence, transpose, symmetrization T^{(mu nu)}, antisymmetrization T^{[mu nu]} and the wedge product u wedge v, in slot and component language.

## Teaching approach

A deliberate second pass (spiral revisit) over objects the reader already uses, now stripped of the metric so that only smoothness remains. The chapter pivots on one slogan, that every vector is a derivative, reached by gradually removing the point from the expression dP/dlambda until only the operator d/dlambda is left. Everything else is built on that pivot: components and basis vectors fall out of the chain rule, 1-forms become differentials by duality, and the two 'slots' of a vector (square brackets for functions, round brackets for 1-forms) are reconciled. The tensor material is then a brisk, MTW-style catalogue of slot operations delivered inside one long example. Voice is informal, with a Samuel Johnson epigraph and heavy reliance on the slot-machine metaphor from ch4; proofs are light and several notational slips survive in print.

**Style:** spiral-revisit, index-free-abstract, geometric-first, conversational-informal, example-driven, analogy-driven, diagram-driven

**Narrative arc**

1. Declare the arena: a smooth manifold with no metric, no connection, no curvature; illustrate with manifold and non-manifold sketches.
2. Review the old technology (arrow between two points, components on a basis, gradient as a normal vector, directional derivative v.grad h) and flag that it will not survive, notably because grad h has mismatched index placement.
3. Parametrize the straight segment between two points and note that its lambda-derivative returns the arrow, so the vector can be recovered from a derivative.
4. Generalize to a curved path: the tangent vector is dP/dlambda; bring in coordinates and the chain rule.
5. Strip the point off to obtain the operator d/dlambda = (dx^alpha/dlambda) partial_alpha; read off components and coordinate basis vectors; box the result.
6. Feed functions rather than points into the operator to recover the directional derivative; state the slogan linking geometry and analysis.
7. Show closure: a linear combination of tangent operators of two curves through P is the tangent operator of a third curve, so tangent vectors at P form a vector space.
8. Extend from one point to all points via a congruence of non-intersecting curves, giving a vector field; warn that comparing vectors at different points needs a connection (or, later, the Lie derivative).
9. Reintroduce 1-forms as the dual partner of vectors (tangent space, dual space, real line), with linearity and the piercing-surfaces picture.
10. Identify basis 1-forms with coordinate differentials dx^alpha, check the duality delta, and show <df, u> = u[f], retiring the separate function slot.
11. Generalize to (n, m) tensors as slot machines built from tensor products; extract components by inserting basis objects; write the metric as a (0,2) tensor.
12. Catalogue tensor operations on a sample (0,3) tensor: gradient, contraction, divergence, transpose, symmetry types, (anti)symmetrization, wedge product and bivectors.
13. Summarize and point forward to differential forms in ch32.

**Signature moves**

- Removing the point: dP/dlambda is turned into the bare operator d/dlambda[ ], so the vector stops being a displacement and becomes a machine waiting for input.
- Two kinds of vector slot, marked by bracket shape: square brackets eat functions (directional derivative), round brackets eat 1-forms (dual pairing); the chapter then shows they are the same thing via <df, u> = u[f].
- Reading components and basis vectors directly off the chain rule: dx^alpha/dlambda are components, partial/partial x^alpha are basis vectors.
- Manifold versus non-manifold pictures judged by the 'zoom in until it looks flat' test.
- Tip-minus-base intuition reused for directional derivatives and even for the gradient of a tensor (eqn 31.36).
- Three-level space diagram (tangent space, real line, dual space) making the vector/1-form duality a pair of maps onto numbers.
- Tensor operations presented as manipulations of slots (insert, swap, contract, add a slot) with component translations alongside, following MTW.
- Penrose diagrammatic notation offered as an exercise to visualize index contractions and (anti)symmetrizations.

## Section by section

### Chapter opening: the smooth manifold as a stripped-down arena — p.322 (pdf 339)

States the chapter's aim of a coordinate-free geometry resting on the idea that each vector is a derivative. Introduces the manifold as a space that is locally like R^n and has only smoothness, with metric, vector-space and topological structure deliberately set aside. Points, coordinate patches, monotonically parametrized curves and differentiation along curves are declared well defined. Example 31.1 sorts spaces into manifolds (R^n, circle, sphere, torus) and non-manifolds (line jutting from a plane, double cone, kinked line, self-crossing line). Vectors are said to live in a tangent space and 1-forms in a dual space rather than in the manifold, and the reader is asked to forget the metric, covariant derivative and curvature for now.

**Concepts:** smooth manifold, tangent space, dual space

**Key moves**
- Strip R^n of everything except smoothness to define the arena.
- Use the zoom-until-flat criterion on pictures to separate manifolds from non-manifolds.
- Announce that vectors and 1-forms live in spaces attached to a point, not in the manifold itself.
- Explicitly suspend the metric, connection and curvature.

### §31.1 Old notions of vectors and gradients — p.323 (pdf 340)

Example 31.2 recalls the elementary kit: a vector as a directed segment from A to B expanded on a basis, an orthonormal basis with e.e = delta, a surface z = f(x,y) recast as h(x,y,z) = 0 whose gradient is a vector normal to the tangent plane, and the directional derivative v.grad h = v^alpha partial h/partial x^alpha giving the change in h from base to tip of v. The authors then declare this toolkit unfit for their purposes, noting in a footnote that grad h written as h_{,alpha} e_alpha breaks the up-down summation rule, and promise that 1-forms will remove the forced, non-covariant idea of a gradient as a normal vector.

**Concepts:** gradient as a normal vector, directional derivative, basis vectors and components

**Key moves**
- Write grad h = (partial h/partial x^alpha) e_alpha and point out the two lower indices.
- Define the directional derivative as a number: the change of h from base to tip of v.
- Signal that the gradient really wants to be a 1-form, not a vector.

### §31.2 Vectors and vector fields — p.324 (pdf 341)

Replaces two-point vectors with a vector at one point. Parametrizing the segment P(lambda) = A + lambda(B - A) shows its derivative is B - A; for a general curve the tangent vector is dP/dlambda (Cartan's reading: the motion of the point). Introducing coordinates x^alpha(lambda) and the chain rule gives v = (dx^alpha/dlambda) partial P/partial x^alpha; stripping off P leaves the operator d/dlambda = (dx^alpha/dlambda) partial/partial x^alpha, from which components v^alpha = dx^alpha/dlambda and basis vectors e_alpha = partial/partial x^alpha are read off (boxed eqn 31.11). Feeding functions into the operator gives v[f] = partial_v f, the directional derivative, and the slogan that every vector is a derivative ties geometry to analysis. Example 31.3 shows a linear combination av + bu of tangent operators of two curves through P is the tangent operator d/dphi of a third curve, so tangent vectors at P form a vector space. Finally a congruence of non-crossing space-filling curves defines a vector field, with the caution that vectors at different points cannot be compared without a connection; the Lie derivative is flagged for later.

**Concepts:** tangent vector as derivative operator, coordinate basis vectors, directional derivative, tangent space, vector space, congruence of curves, vector field, parallel transport and connection

**Key moves**
- d/dlambda of A + lambda(B - A) returns B - A, so a derivative can reproduce the arrow.
- Chain rule: dP/dlambda = (dx^alpha/dlambda)(partial P/partial x^alpha).
- Delete the point P to obtain a pure operator acting on whatever is supplied.
- Match v = v^alpha e_alpha against the operator to identify v^alpha = dx^alpha/dlambda and e_alpha = partial_alpha.
- Insert a function f: v[f] = v^alpha partial_alpha f is the directional derivative.
- Closure: a d/dlambda + b d/dmu = d/dphi for some third curve through P.
- Congruence gives a unique tangent at every point, hence a vector field; comparison across points requires parallel transport.

### §31.3 Linear slot machines again — p.327 (pdf 344)

Brings back 1-forms, the first members of the family of differential forms that occupies the rest of Part V, and argues that forms deserve the same undergraduate status as vectors. Besides its function slot, a vector has a round-bracket slot that accepts a 1-form and returns a number, written symmetrically as v(sigma) = sigma(v) = <sigma, v>. Vectors, 1-forms and numbers are pictured in a tangent space, a dual space and the real line, with the pairing mapping between them; linearity in each argument is stated. The ch4 picture of a 1-form as evenly spaced surfaces returns, with the pairing counting surfaces pierced; Example 31.4 notes that the surface spacing along x is 1/A_1 and scaling the form scales the density. Basis 1-forms are defined by <omega^beta, e_alpha> = delta^beta_alpha and, in a coordinate basis, identified with differentials dx^alpha, checked via partial x^mu/partial x^nu = delta. The differential df = (partial_alpha f) dx^alpha is the simplest 1-form, and <df, u> = u^alpha partial_alpha f shows that acting on a function and pairing with its differential are the same, letting the function slot be retired. 1-form fields and the resulting scalar fields close the section.

**Concepts:** 1-form, dual space, duality pairing of vectors and 1-forms, basis 1-forms as coordinate differentials, differential of a function as a 1-form, 1-form field, directional derivative

**Key moves**
- Give the vector a second slot, for 1-forms, and make the pairing symmetric.
- Picture three spaces (tangent, dual, real line) linked by the pairing.
- Count pierced surfaces to interpret <A, X>; spacing is the reciprocal of the component.
- Define dual basis by <omega^beta, e_alpha> = delta^beta_alpha.
- Identify omega^alpha = dx^alpha because <dx^mu, partial_nu> = partial x^mu/partial x^nu.
- Compute <df, u> in components and find it equals u[f], unifying the two vector slots.

### §31.4 Tensors again — p.329 (pdf 346)

Generalizes to tensors as slot machines: an (n, m) tensor takes n 1-forms and m vectors multilinearly to a number. The tensor product of two vectors, u tensor v, is a (2,0) tensor whose slots take 1-forms; Example 31.5 extracts its components T^{alpha beta} = u^alpha v^beta by inserting basis 1-forms and rebuilds T = u^alpha v^beta e_alpha tensor e_beta. Example 31.6 writes the metric as the (0,2) tensor g = g_{mu nu} dx^mu tensor dx^nu (also called ds^2) that eats two vectors. General (n, m) tensors are tensor products of n vectors and m 1-forms; components come from inserting n basis 1-forms and m basis vectors, and the tensor is rebuilt from components on the tensor-product basis. Order within the vector factors and within the 1-form factors matters, but the two groups may be listed in either order. Tensor fields assign such a tensor to every point. A footnote sketches the history of tensor calculus through Ricci-Curbastro and Levi-Civita.

**Concepts:** tensor as multilinear map, tensor valence (n, m), tensor product, tensor components by slot insertion, metric tensor as a (0,2) tensor, tensor field

**Key moves**
- Define an (n, m) tensor by what it eats and what it returns.
- Build a (2,0) tensor from two vectors with the tensor product.
- Feed basis 1-forms to extract T^{alpha beta} = u^alpha v^beta and reconstruct the tensor on e_alpha tensor e_beta.
- Recast the metric line element as a tensor product of basis 1-forms.
- General component extraction and reconstruction formulas for (n, m) tensors.
- Order rule: keep order among vector factors and among 1-form factors separately.

### §31.5 Examples of tensor operations — p.330 (pdf 347)

Example 31.7, modelled on MTW, runs a catalogue of operations on a (0,3) tensor P and a (1,3) tensor S. The gradient adds one vector slot and is read as the change of P from base to tip of the new vector, with components P_{alpha beta gamma, mu} in flat space. Contraction inserts a basis 1-form and matching basis vector into two slots and sums, lowering the rank by two (S^mu_{alpha mu gamma}). Divergence contracts the new gradient slot with an existing upper slot. Transpose swaps two slots and defines symmetric, totally symmetric, antisymmetric and totally antisymmetric tensors. Symmetrization and antisymmetrization average over permutations of indices with 1/n! and with or without sign, extracting the symmetric or antisymmetric part. The wedge product u wedge v = u tensor v - v tensor u builds a bivector with components u^mu v^nu - v^mu u^nu. The section closes with a summary that geometry can be done with coordinate-free objects or their components, and a pointer to differential forms and curvature in ch32.

**Concepts:** gradient of a tensor, tensor contraction, divergence of a tensor, transpose of tensor slots, symmetric and antisymmetric tensors, symmetrization, antisymmetrization, wedge product, bivector, comma notation for partial derivatives

**Key moves**
- Filling all three slots of P with vectors gives P_{alpha beta gamma} u^alpha v^beta w^gamma.
- Gradient: an extra vector slot measuring tip-minus-base change, P_{alpha beta gamma, mu} in flat space.
- Contraction as inserting omega^mu and e_mu in two slots and summing.
- Divergence as contraction of the gradient slot with an upper slot.
- Symmetry types defined by behaviour under slot swaps.
- Symmetrizer and antisymmetrizer with 1/n! normalization.
- Wedge product as the antisymmetrized tensor product; bivector components.

## Concepts

### smooth manifold

*definition · introduced* · also: manifold M

A space in which a neighbourhood of every point can be smoothly matched to a region of flat R^n, so that coordinates can be laid on patches and curves and differentiation are well defined, without assuming any metric, connection or global vector-space structure.

**How introduced:** Set up as the minimal arena for the chapter by removing metric, vector-space and topological structure from R^n and keeping only smoothness; made concrete by Example 31.1 and Fig. 31.1 contrasting shapes that look flat under magnification with shapes that have a defective point.

**Prerequisites:** Euclidean space R^n, coordinates

**Notes:** The book defers the full definition (charts, atlases) to Appendix C; the tutor should not over-formalize here.

**Where:** GA p.322 (pdf 339); GA p.323 (pdf 340)

### non-manifold (singular point) examples

*definition · introduced* · also: spaces not smooth enough to be manifolds

Spaces containing at least one point whose neighbourhood never resembles R^n however closely it is examined, such as a kinked line, a self-crossing curve, the apex of a double cone, or a line sticking out of a plane.

**How introduced:** Example 31.1 lists them as the more illuminating half of the classification and Fig. 31.1 draws them next to good manifolds.

**Prerequisites:** smooth manifold

**Notes:** Strictly a kinked line is topologically a manifold but not a smooth embedded one; the book's criterion is the informal smooth-look test.

**Where:** GA p.322 (pdf 339)

### gradient as a normal vector

*definition · revisited* · also: gradient vector of a surface, old gradient

The elementary construction grad h = (partial h/partial x^alpha) e_alpha, a vector perpendicular to the level surface h = 0; it relies on a metric (to turn derivatives into a vector) and its components carry a lower index despite being attached to vector basis elements.

**How introduced:** Recalled in Example 31.2 as part of the old technology, then criticized as forced and non-covariant, with a footnote pointing to the index mismatch.

**Prerequisites:** partial derivative, basis vectors and components

$$
\nabla h = \frac{\partial h}{\partial x^{\alpha}}\boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha}
$$

**Notes:** Motivates the replacement of the gradient vector by the 1-form dh.

**Where:** GA §31.1 p.323 (pdf 340)

### basis vectors and components

*convention · revisited* · also: vector expansion u = u^alpha e_alpha

Any vector can be written as a sum of basis vectors e_alpha weighted by component numbers u^alpha, with the index on the components written up and summed against the lower index on the basis.

**How introduced:** Recalled from earlier chapters in Example 31.2 with an orthonormal basis e_alpha . e_beta = delta_{alpha beta}; later re-derived from the chain rule.

**Prerequisites:** vector space

$$
\boldsymbol{u} = u^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha}
$$

**Where:** GA §31.1 p.323 (pdf 340); GA §31.2 p.324 (pdf 341)

### directional derivative

*operation · core* · also: partial_v f, v[f], derivative of f along v

The rate of change of a scalar function f along a vector v, given in components by v^alpha partial f/partial x^alpha; heuristically the value of f at the tip of v minus its value at the base.

**How introduced:** First as v . grad h in the old toolkit, then reinterpreted as the action of the vector operator on a function, and finally as the pairing of the vector with the 1-form df.

**Prerequisites:** partial derivative, tangent vector as derivative operator

$$
\partial_{\boldsymbol{v}} h = \boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla h = v^{\alpha}\frac{\partial h}{\partial x^{\alpha}}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{u}[f] = \partial_{\boldsymbol{u}} f = \langle \boldsymbol{d}f, \boldsymbol{u}\rangle
$$

**Notes:** Appears three times in three guises; the unifying thread of the chapter.

**Where:** GA §31.1 p.323 (pdf 340); GA §31.2 p.325 (pdf 342); GA §31.3 p.328 (pdf 345)

### tangent vector to a parametrized curve

*definition · core* · also: velocity vector dP/dlambda, tangent vector

For a curve P(lambda) through a manifold, the tangent vector at a point is the derivative of the curve with respect to its parameter at that point; for a straight segment A + lambda(B - A) it reproduces B - A.

**How introduced:** Obtained by parametrizing the straight line between two points and differentiating, then generalized to a curved path (Fig. 31.3), with Cartan's reading as the motion of the point.

**Prerequisites:** parametrized curve, derivative

$$
\frac{d\mathcal{P}(\lambda)}{d\lambda} = \mathcal{B} - \mathcal{A}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{v} = \frac{d\mathcal{P}(\lambda)}{d\lambda}
$$

**Notes:** On a genuine manifold 'P(lambda) = A + lambda(B - A)' only makes sense in a coordinate patch or flat space; the book uses it as a stepping stone.

**Where:** GA §31.2 p.324 (pdf 341)

### tangent vector as derivative operator

*principle · core* · also: every vector is a derivative, v = d/dlambda, vector operator v[ ]

A tangent vector at a point is identified with the first-order differential operator d/dlambda along a curve through that point; acting on any function it returns the function's rate of change along the curve, and in coordinates it equals (dx^alpha/dlambda) partial/partial x^alpha.

**How introduced:** The chain rule is applied to dP/dlambda in coordinates and the point P is then removed, leaving an operator with an empty slot; the result is boxed and sloganized, and Fig. 31.5 links vector with derivative and geometry with analysis.

**Prerequisites:** tangent vector to a parametrized curve, chain rule, directional derivative

$$
\boldsymbol{v}[\ ] = \frac{d}{d\lambda} = \frac{dx^{\alpha}}{d\lambda}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\alpha}} = v^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{v}[f] = v^{\alpha}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{\alpha}}
$$

**Notes:** The key idea of the chapter; it makes vectors intrinsic to the manifold without needing displacements between points.

**Where:** GA §31.2 p.324 (pdf 341); GA §31.2 p.325 (pdf 342)

### coordinate basis vectors

*definition · core* · also: e_alpha = partial/partial x^alpha, holonomic basis, partial_alpha

In a coordinate system x^alpha the basis vectors at a point are the partial derivative operators partial/partial x^alpha, i.e. the tangent vectors to the coordinate lines, and the components of a curve's tangent are dx^alpha/dlambda.

**How introduced:** Read off by comparing the operator form of a tangent vector with v = v^alpha e_alpha (eqn 31.9); a footnote explains components as how coordinates change with the parameter and basis vectors as rates of change with respect to coordinates.

**Prerequisites:** tangent vector as derivative operator, coordinates

$$
v^{\alpha} = \frac{dx^{\alpha}}{d\lambda}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\alpha}}
$$

**Notes:** Non-coordinate (e.g. orthonormal) frames are not discussed here.

**Where:** GA §31.2 p.324 (pdf 341); GA §31.2 p.325 (pdf 342)

### tangent space

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: T_P M, space of vectors at P

The vector space of all tangent vectors (directional derivative operators) at a single point P of a manifold; it has the same dimension as the manifold and is distinct from the manifold itself.

**How introduced:** Named in the opening as the home of vectors, then justified in Example 31.3 by showing linear combinations of tangents to curves through P are tangents to other curves through P; drawn as a plane in Fig. 31.8.

**Prerequisites:** tangent vector as derivative operator, vector space

$$
\frac{d}{d\phi} = a\frac{d}{d\lambda} + b\frac{d}{d\mu}
$$

**Notes:** Appendix C gives the formal treatment.

**Where:** GA p.322 (pdf 339); GA §31.2 p.325 (pdf 342); GA §31.2 p.326 (pdf 343); GA §31.3 p.327 (pdf 344)

### vector space

*definition · mention* · also: linear space

A set closed under addition and scalar multiplication obeying the usual axioms (zero element, inverses, associativity, commutativity, distributivity), possessing a basis of linearly independent elements whose number is the dimension.

**How introduced:** Summarized in a margin note when showing that tangent operators at P are closed under linear combination.

**Where:** GA §31.2 p.326 (pdf 343)

### congruence of curves

*definition · introduced* · also: congruence, space-filling family of non-intersecting curves

A family of curves such that exactly one curve passes through each point of a region, so that the curves fill the region and never cross; the parameter values on different curves need not correspond to equal distances.

**How introduced:** Introduced via Fig. 31.7 as the device that turns single tangent vectors into a field; a margin note previews its use as fluid streamlines in ch39.

**Prerequisites:** tangent vector as derivative operator

**Notes:** Mathematically these are the integral curves of the vector field.

**Where:** GA §31.2 p.326 (pdf 343)

### vector field

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: field of tangent vectors

An assignment of one tangent vector to every point of a region of the manifold; equivalently the tangents d/dlambda|_Q to the curves of a congruence at each point Q.

**How introduced:** Built from a congruence: each point lies on one curve whose tangent is the vector there, described as a machine that takes a point and returns a vector.

**Prerequisites:** congruence of curves, tangent space

$$
\boldsymbol{v} = \left.\frac{d}{d\lambda}\right|_{\mathcal{Q}}
$$

**Where:** GA §31.2 p.326 (pdf 343)

### comparing vectors at different points requires a connection

*principle · introduced* · also: no natural comparison across tangent spaces

Because vectors at different points belong to different tangent spaces, a bare manifold gives no way to say whether they are equal or to subtract them; extra structure such as a connection (parallel transport) or a flow (Lie derivative) is needed.

**How introduced:** Stated after the congruence discussion as a caution, recalling the connection and covariant derivative of earlier chapters and previewing the Lie derivative.

**Prerequisites:** tangent space, vector field

**Where:** GA §31.2 p.326 (pdf 343)

### parallel transport and connection

*definition · mention* · also: connection, covariant derivative

The extra structure that specifies how to carry a vector from one point to another so that it counts as unchanged, giving a notion of parallelism and a derivative of vector fields between points.

**How introduced:** Recalled briefly from earlier chapters as what would be needed to compare vectors at different points; otherwise explicitly set aside.

**Prerequisites:** vector field

**Notes:** Treated geometrically in ch34.

**Where:** GA §31.2 p.326 (pdf 343)

### Lie derivative

*operation · mention*

A derivative that compares tensor fields at neighbouring points by dragging them along the flow of a vector field, requiring no connection or metric.

**How introduced:** Only named in a margin note as an alternative way to compare vector fields at different points.

**Prerequisites:** vector field, congruence of curves

**Notes:** Developed in ch33.

**Where:** GA §31.2 p.326 (pdf 343)

### 1-form

*mathematical-object · core* · also: covector, one-form, linear slot machine for vectors, differential form of degree 1

A linear map from vectors at a point to real numbers; pictured as a stack of evenly spaced surfaces whose value on a vector is the number of surfaces the vector crosses.

**How introduced:** Returned to from ch4 as the first member of the family of differential forms; motivated by giving vectors a slot that accepts a 1-form, with the three-space picture of Fig. 31.8 and the stacked-sheet picture of Fig. 31.9.

**Prerequisites:** tangent space, linear map

$$
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}} = \sigma_{\alpha}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\alpha}
$$

**Notes:** The authors argue forms are no harder than vectors and deserve equal standing in undergraduate teaching.

**Where:** GA §31.3 p.327 (pdf 344); GA §31.3 p.328 (pdf 345)

### dual space

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: cotangent space, T*_P M

The vector space of all linear maps from the tangent space at a point to the real numbers, i.e. the space where 1-forms at that point live; it has the same dimension as the tangent space.

**How introduced:** Named in the opening and drawn in Fig. 31.8 as a second plane, mirrored across the real line from the tangent space.

**Prerequisites:** tangent space, 1-form

**Where:** GA p.323 (pdf 340); GA §31.3 p.327 (pdf 344)

### duality pairing of vectors and 1-forms

*operation · core* · also: inner product <sigma, v>, contraction of a 1-form with a vector, sigma . v

The bilinear rule that combines a 1-form and a vector at the same point into a number, written <sigma, v> = sigma(v) = v(sigma); it is linear in each argument and requires no metric.

**How introduced:** Presented as a two-way mapping: a 1-form eats a vector and a vector eats a 1-form, both landing on the real line; linearity is stated and the surface-piercing count gives the picture.

**Prerequisites:** 1-form, tangent space

$$
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v} \equiv \langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle = \boldsymbol{v}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}) = \tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{v})
$$

$$
\langle a\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}} + b\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}, \boldsymbol{v}\rangle = a\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle + b\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle
$$

**Notes:** The book calls this an inner product; it is not a metric scalar product.

**Where:** GA §31.3 p.327 (pdf 344)

### dual basis

*definition · developed* · also: basis 1-forms omega^alpha

Given basis vectors e_alpha, the basis 1-forms omega^beta are defined so that each returns 1 on its own partner and 0 on the others: <omega^beta, e_alpha> = delta^beta_alpha.

**How introduced:** Imposed as the rule that lets components be mapped to numbers, right after expanding a 1-form on basis 1-forms.

**Prerequisites:** 1-form, basis vectors and components

$$
\langle\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\beta},\boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha}\rangle = \delta^{\beta}_{\alpha}
$$

**Where:** GA §31.3 p.328 (pdf 345)

### basis 1-forms as coordinate differentials

*identity · core* · also: omega^alpha = dx^alpha, coordinate dual basis

In a coordinate basis the dual basis 1-forms are the differentials of the coordinate functions, dx^alpha, because applying partial/partial x^nu to x^mu gives delta^mu_nu.

**How introduced:** Asked as a question (if vectors are derivatives, what operation is a 1-form?) and answered with the dual operation, the differential; verified in one line (eqn 31.22).

**Prerequisites:** dual basis, coordinate basis vectors, total differential

$$
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\alpha} = \boldsymbol{d}x^{\alpha}
$$

$$
\left\langle \boldsymbol{d}x^{\mu}, \frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\nu}}\right\rangle = \frac{\partial x^{\mu}}{\partial x^{\nu}} = \delta^{\mu}_{\nu}
$$

**Notes:** Bold d marks the exterior derivative, explained in ch33.

**Where:** GA §31.3 p.328 (pdf 345)

### differential of a function as a 1-form

*mathematical-object · core* · also: df, gradient 1-form

The 1-form df = (partial f/partial x^alpha) dx^alpha associated with a scalar function; its value on any vector u is the directional derivative u[f].

**How introduced:** Offered as the simplest 1-form, recalling the calculus total differential in a margin note, and then used to reconcile the function slot and the 1-form slot of a vector (eqn 31.23).

**Prerequisites:** basis 1-forms as coordinate differentials, directional derivative

$$
\boldsymbol{d}f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{\alpha}}\boldsymbol{d}x^{\alpha}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{u}[f] \equiv \langle\boldsymbol{d}f,\boldsymbol{u}\rangle = u^{\alpha}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{\alpha}}
$$

**Notes:** This is the covariant replacement of the old gradient vector criticized in 31.1, though the book does not make the link explicit at this point.

**Where:** GA §31.3 p.328 (pdf 345)

### two slots of a vector

*convention · developed* · also: square-bracket slot and round-bracket slot, v[f] versus v(sigma)

Book-specific notation in which a vector acting on a function is written with square brackets, v[f], and a vector acting on a 1-form with round brackets, v(sigma); the identity v[f] = <df, v> shows both are the same operation.

**How introduced:** Introduced in a margin note when functions are first fed to vectors, then resolved in 31.3 where the function slot is said to be retired.

**Prerequisites:** tangent vector as derivative operator, differential of a function as a 1-form

**Notes:** The book itself slips from this convention in eqn 31.50.

**Where:** GA §31.2 p.325 (pdf 342); GA §31.3 p.328 (pdf 345)

### 1-form field

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: covector field

An assignment of a 1-form to every point; pairing it with a vector field point by point produces a scalar field. Pairings only make sense between a vector and a 1-form attached to the same point.

**How introduced:** Stated in parallel with vector fields at the end of 31.3.

**Prerequisites:** 1-form, vector field

**Notes:** The chapter summary stresses the same-point restriction, echoing the earlier warning that vectors at different points cannot be compared without extra structure.

**Where:** GA §31.3 p.328 (pdf 345); GA p.332 (pdf 349)

### tensor as multilinear map

*definition · core* · also: slot machine, generalized slot machine

A tensor at a point is a machine that is linear in each of its inputs and turns a fixed ordered list of 1-forms and vectors into a number.

**How introduced:** Revived from ch4's slot-machine metaphor as the natural generalization of vectors and 1-forms; defining rule written as T(sigma^1..sigma^n, v^1..v^m) = number.

**Prerequisites:** 1-form, tangent space, duality pairing of vectors and 1-forms

$$
\boldsymbol{T}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^1,\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^n,\boldsymbol{v}^1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}^m) = \text{(number)}
$$

**Notes:** Multilinearity is implicit in the text rather than stated.

**Where:** GA §31.4 p.329 (pdf 346)

### tensor valence (n, m)

*convention · developed* · also: rank (n, m), type (n, m)

An (n, m) tensor has n slots for 1-forms (n upper indices on its components) and m slots for vectors (m lower indices).

**How introduced:** Stated with the defining rule of a tensor and used throughout the examples.

**Prerequisites:** tensor as multilinear map

**Notes:** The book uses 'rank' and 'valence' interchangeably, and 'rank' also for the total n + m when saying contraction reduces rank by two.

**Where:** GA §31.4 p.329 (pdf 346)

### tensor product

*operation · developed* · also: outer product, otimes

The operation combining tensors into a higher-valence tensor whose value on a list of inputs is the product of the factors' values on their respective inputs, keeping the order of slots.

**How introduced:** Used to build a (2,0) tensor from two vectors, then the metric from two basis 1-forms, then an arbitrary (n, m) tensor; a margin note says the symbol just preserves slot order.

**Prerequisites:** tensor as multilinear map

$$
\boldsymbol{T}(\ ,\ ) = \boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{u}(\ )\otimes\boldsymbol{v}(\ )
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{T} = \boldsymbol{u}^1\otimes\ldots\otimes\boldsymbol{u}^n\otimes\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^1\otimes\ldots\otimes\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^m
$$

**Notes:** Not every tensor is a single product of vectors and 1-forms; general tensors are sums of such products (the book's 'arbitrary tensor can be built' needs this qualification).

**Where:** GA §31.4 p.329 (pdf 346); GA §31.4 p.330 (pdf 347)

### tensor components by slot insertion

*technique · core* · also: extracting components with basis objects

The components of a tensor are the numbers obtained by feeding basis 1-forms into its upper slots and basis vectors into its lower slots; the tensor is recovered by multiplying components with the tensor product of the corresponding basis elements and summing.

**How introduced:** Worked for u tensor v in Example 31.5, then written for a general (n, m) tensor in eqns 31.32-31.33.

**Prerequisites:** dual basis, tensor product

$$
T^{\alpha\beta} = \boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\beta}) = u^{\alpha}v^{\beta}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{T} = T^{\mu_1\ldots\mu_n}{}_{\nu_1\ldots\nu_m}\,\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu_1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu_n}\otimes\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\nu_1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\nu_m}
$$

**Where:** GA §31.4 p.329 (pdf 346); GA §31.4 p.330 (pdf 347)

### metric tensor as a (0,2) tensor

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: g, ds^2, line element as a tensor

The metric is the symmetric (0,2) tensor g = g_{mu nu} dx^mu tensor dx^nu, a two-slot machine that takes two vectors and returns their scalar product.

**How introduced:** Example 31.6 uses the tensor product of basis 1-forms to rewrite the familiar line element as a tensor, even though the metric is otherwise being kept out of the chapter.

**Prerequisites:** tensor product, basis 1-forms as coordinate differentials

$$
\boldsymbol{g} = g_{\mu\nu}\,\boldsymbol{d}x^{\mu}\otimes\boldsymbol{d}x^{\nu} = g_{\mu\nu}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\mu}\otimes\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\nu}
$$

**Where:** GA §31.4 p.329 (pdf 346)

### tensor field

*mathematical-object · introduced*

An assignment of a tensor of fixed valence to each point, which combines with vectors and 1-forms at the same point to give numbers.

**How introduced:** Stated by analogy with vector and 1-form fields at the end of 31.4.

**Prerequisites:** tensor as multilinear map, vector field, 1-form field

**Where:** GA §31.4 p.330 (pdf 347)

### gradient of a tensor

*operation · introduced* · also: nabla P, covariant derivative of a tensor (flat-space version)

An operation that turns an (n, m) tensor field into an (n, m+1) tensor by adding one vector slot; filled with a vector z, it gives the change of the original tensor's output from the base to the tip of z. In flat Cartesian coordinates its components are the partial derivatives of the components.

**How introduced:** First item in Example 31.7's list, with the tip-minus-base heuristic of eqn 31.36 and comma notation in 31.37.

**Prerequisites:** tensor field, directional derivative

$$
\nabla\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{z}) = P_{\alpha\beta\gamma,\mu}u^{\alpha}v^{\beta}w^{\gamma}z^{\mu}
$$

**Notes:** On a curved manifold the partial derivative must become a covariant derivative; the book states the flat-space restriction but does not revisit it here.

**Where:** GA §31.5 p.330 (pdf 347)

### comma notation for partial derivatives

*convention · mention* · also: P_{alpha beta gamma, mu}, h_{,alpha}

A comma before an index denotes partial differentiation with respect to that coordinate.

**How introduced:** Used without comment in a margin note of 31.1 and in the gradient and divergence formulas of 31.5.

$$
P_{\alpha\beta\gamma,\mu} = \frac{\partial P_{\alpha\beta\gamma}}{\partial x^{\mu}}
$$

**Where:** GA §31.1 p.323 (pdf 340); GA §31.5 p.330 (pdf 347)

### tensor contraction

*operation · developed* · also: contracting an index, trace over a slot pair

Inserting a basis 1-form into one upper slot and the matching basis vector into one lower slot and summing over the basis index; it produces a tensor with one fewer upper and one fewer lower slot, in components by setting an upper and a lower index equal and summing.

**How introduced:** Demonstrated on a (1,3) tensor S to make a (0,2) tensor Q in Example 31.7.

**Prerequisites:** tensor components by slot insertion, dual basis

$$
\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}) = \boldsymbol{S}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\mu},\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu},\boldsymbol{v})
$$

$$
Q_{\alpha\gamma} = S^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\mu\gamma}
$$

**Notes:** Result is basis-independent because omega^mu and e_mu are dual; worth pointing out, the book does not.

**Where:** GA §31.5 p.330 (pdf 347); GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

### divergence of a tensor

*operation · introduced* · also: nabla . S

The contraction of the extra slot added by the gradient with one of the tensor's upper (1-form) slots; for a (1,3) tensor in flat space its components are S^mu_{alpha beta gamma, mu}.

**How introduced:** Defined in Example 31.7 as gradient followed by contraction.

**Prerequisites:** gradient of a tensor, tensor contraction

$$
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{S}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}) = \nabla\boldsymbol{S}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\mu},\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}) = S^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\beta\gamma,\mu}u^{\alpha}v^{\beta}w^{\gamma}
$$

**Where:** GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

### transpose of tensor slots

*operation · introduced* · also: slot swap

Exchanging the inputs of two slots of the same kind to produce a new tensor of the same valence.

**How introduced:** Listed in Example 31.7 as the operation used to define symmetry types.

**Prerequisites:** tensor as multilinear map

$$
\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\rightarrow\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w})
$$

**Where:** GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

### symmetric and antisymmetric tensors

*definition · developed* · also: totally symmetric, totally antisymmetric, alternating tensor

A tensor is symmetric in two slots if swapping them leaves its output unchanged and antisymmetric if the swap flips the sign; it is totally (anti)symmetric if this holds for every pair of slots, so that any odd permutation of inputs flips the sign in the antisymmetric case.

**How introduced:** Defined through transposition in Example 31.7, with (2,0) component versions T^{mu nu} = T^{nu mu} and A^{mu nu} = -A^{nu mu} in margin notes.

**Prerequisites:** transpose of tensor slots

$$
\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}) = -\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}) = +\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})
$$

**Notes:** Totally antisymmetric tensors of 1-form type are the p-forms of ch32.

**Where:** GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

### symmetrization

*operation · developed* · also: T^{(mu nu)}, round-bracket index notation

The projection that averages a tensor's components over all permutations of a chosen set of n indices, with weight 1/n!, yielding the symmetric part; it leaves symmetric tensors unchanged and kills antisymmetric ones.

**How introduced:** Given for a (2,0) tensor and then as a general verbal rule in Example 31.7.

**Prerequisites:** symmetric and antisymmetric tensors, permutations

$$
T^{(\mu\nu)} = \frac{1}{2!}(T^{\mu\nu} + T^{\nu\mu})
$$

**Where:** GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

### antisymmetrization

*operation · developed* · also: T^{[mu nu]}, square-bracket index notation, alternating sum

The projection that forms the signed average of a tensor's components over all permutations of n chosen indices (even permutations with +, odd with -), weighted by 1/n!, extracting the antisymmetric part.

**How introduced:** Presented alongside symmetrization in Example 31.7, both for (2,0) tensors and as a general rule.

**Prerequisites:** symmetric and antisymmetric tensors, permutations

$$
T^{[\mu\nu]} = \frac{1}{2!}(T^{\mu\nu} - T^{\nu\mu})
$$

**Notes:** Any rank-2 tensor splits as T = T^{(mu nu)} + T^{[mu nu]}; exploited in Exercise 31.4.

**Where:** GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

### wedge product

*operation · introduced* · also: exterior product, u wedge v

The antisymmetrized tensor product; for two vectors u wedge v = u tensor v - v tensor u, a totally antisymmetric (2,0) tensor.

**How introduced:** Final item of Example 31.7, as preparation for the differential forms of the next chapter.

**Prerequisites:** tensor product, antisymmetrization

$$
\boldsymbol{u}\wedge\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{v} - \boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{u}
$$

**Notes:** Normalization conventions differ between books (with or without 1/2); this book uses no 1/2 factor here.

**Where:** GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

### bivector

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: 2-vector

An antisymmetric (2,0) tensor such as u wedge v, with components b^{mu nu} = u^mu v^nu - v^mu u^nu; geometrically an oriented plane element spanned by two vectors.

**How introduced:** Built as the example of the wedge product in Example 31.7.

**Prerequisites:** wedge product

$$
b^{\mu\nu} = u^{\mu}v^{\nu} - v^{\mu}u^{\nu}
$$

**Notes:** p-vectors are developed in ch32 (32.3).

**Where:** GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

### Penrose graphical tensor notation

*convention · mention* · also: tensor diagrams, birdtracks

A diagrammatic notation in which a tensor is a shape with one line per index (upper indices on top, lower on the bottom), joined lines are contracted indices, bars across lines denote symmetrization or antisymmetrization, and a circle around a shape denotes covariant differentiation.

**How introduced:** Only in Exercise 31.7 and Fig. 31.10, as an entertaining way to visualize contractions and symmetry operations.

**Prerequisites:** tensor contraction, antisymmetrization, symmetrization

**Notes:** Left-to-right position of line ends records index order; the exercise notes that the numerical 1/n! factors of (anti)symmetrization are conventionally left off the bars and must be supplied by hand (thick bar = antisymmetrize, wiggly bar = symmetrize). Excellent basis for an interactive index-bookkeeping tool.

**Where:** GA p.333 (pdf 350)

## Key equations

### (31.1) Vector on a basis · supporting · GA §31.1 p.323 (pdf 340)

$$
\boldsymbol{u} = u^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha} = u^{0}\boldsymbol{e}_{0} + u^{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{1} + u^{2}\boldsymbol{e}_{2} + u^{3}\boldsymbol{e}_{3} + \dots
$$

Elementary expansion of a vector as components times basis vectors, recalled as the old picture.

**Symbols:** u^alpha components; e_alpha basis vectors

### (31.2) Gradient vector in Cartesian 3D · supporting · GA §31.1 p.323 (pdf 340)

$$
\nabla h(x,y,z) = \frac{\partial h}{\partial x}\boldsymbol{e}_{x} + \frac{\partial h}{\partial y}\boldsymbol{e}_{y} + \frac{\partial h}{\partial z}\boldsymbol{e}_{z}
$$

Gradient of the function h whose zero set is a surface; points normal to the tangent plane.

**Symbols:** h(x,y,z) = 0 defines the surface z = f(x,y)

### (31.3) Gradient vector, index form · supporting · GA §31.1 p.323 (pdf 340)

$$
\nabla h = \frac{\partial h}{\partial x^{\alpha}}\boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha}
$$

General-coordinate version of the gradient vector; the footnote notes both indices are down, violating the summation convention, which foreshadows that the gradient is really a 1-form.

**Symbols:** h_{,alpha} = partial h/partial x^alpha

### (31.4) Directional derivative (old form) · supporting · GA §31.1 p.323 (pdf 340)

$$
\partial_{\boldsymbol{v}}h = \boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla h = v^{\alpha}\frac{\partial h}{\partial x^{\alpha}}
$$

Rate of change of h along v, a number: roughly h at the tip of v minus h at its base.

**Symbols:** v^alpha components of v

### (31.5) Straight-line vector as a derivative · derivation-step · GA §31.2 p.324 (pdf 341)

$$
\frac{d\mathcal{P}(\lambda)}{d\lambda} = \mathcal{B} - \mathcal{A}
$$

Differentiating the parametrized segment P(lambda) = A + lambda(B - A) returns the arrow from A to B, the first hint that vectors are derivatives.

**Symbols:** A, B points; lambda parameter

### (31.6) Tangent vector to a curve · supporting · GA §31.2 p.324 (pdf 341)

$$
\boldsymbol{v} = \frac{d\mathcal{P}(\lambda)}{d\lambda}
$$

Defines the tangent vector at a point of a general parametrized curve.

**Symbols:** P(lambda) curve

### (31.7) Tangent vector via the chain rule · derivation-step · GA §31.2 p.324 (pdf 341)

$$
\boldsymbol{v} = \frac{d\mathcal{P}}{d\lambda} = \frac{dx^{\alpha}}{d\lambda}\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial x^{\alpha}}
$$

Expresses the tangent through the coordinates of the curve x^alpha(lambda).

**Symbols:** x^alpha coordinates

### (31.8) Vector operator with the point removed · central · GA §31.2 p.324 (pdf 341)

$$
\boldsymbol{v}[\ ] = \frac{d}{d\lambda} = \frac{dx^{\alpha}}{d\lambda}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\alpha}}
$$

Stripping P from 31.7 leaves a differential operator with an empty slot: the vector is identified with d/dlambda.

**Symbols:** [ ] empty input slot

### (31.9) Components and coordinate basis vectors identified · central · GA §31.2 p.324 (pdf 341)

$$
\frac{dx^{\alpha}}{d\lambda} = v^{\alpha}, \quad \frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\alpha}} = \boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha}
$$

Comparing 31.8 with v = v^alpha e_alpha: components are coordinate rates along the curve and basis vectors are partial derivative operators.

### (31.10) Vector as directional-derivative operator · supporting · GA §31.2 p.325 (pdf 342)

$$
\boldsymbol{v}[\ ] \equiv \frac{d}{d\lambda} = v^{\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\alpha}} = \partial_{\boldsymbol{v}}
$$

The vector is the directional derivative operator partial_v.

**Symbols:** partial_v directional derivative operator

### (31.11) Boxed definition: vector as derivative · central · GA §31.2 p.325 (pdf 342)

$$
\boldsymbol{v}[\ ] = \frac{d}{d\lambda} = \frac{dx^{\alpha}}{d\lambda}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\alpha}} = v^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha}
$$

Summary box: a tangent vector to a curve is the derivative along it, with components dx^alpha/dlambda and basis vectors partial/partial x^alpha.

**Symbols:** lambda curve parameter

### (31.12) Vector acting on a function · central · GA §31.2 p.325 (pdf 342)

$$
\boldsymbol{v}[f(x)] = \frac{df(x)}{d\lambda} = \frac{dx^{\alpha}}{d\lambda}\frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x^{\alpha}} = v^{\alpha}\frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x^{\alpha}} = \partial_{\boldsymbol{v}}f(x)
$$

Feeding a function into the vector operator yields its directional derivative along v.

**Symbols:** f scalar function

### (31.13) Second tangent vector · derivation-step · GA §31.2 p.325 (pdf 342)

$$
\boldsymbol{u} = \frac{d}{d\mu} = \frac{dx^{\alpha}}{d\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\alpha}}
$$

Tangent operator of another curve through the same point, parametrized by mu.

**Symbols:** mu second curve's parameter

### (31.14) Linear combination of tangent operators · derivation-step · GA §31.2 p.325 (pdf 342)

$$
a\boldsymbol{v} + b\boldsymbol{u} = a\frac{d}{d\lambda} + b\frac{d}{d\mu} = \left(a\frac{dx^{\alpha}}{d\lambda} + b\frac{dx^{\alpha}}{d\mu}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\alpha}}
$$

Referring both vectors to the same coordinate basis, the combination has components that are the combined coordinate rates.

**Symbols:** a, b constants

### (31.15) Combination is tangent to a third curve · derivation-step · GA §31.2 p.326 (pdf 343)

$$
\boldsymbol{w} = \frac{d}{d\phi} = \left(a\frac{dx^{\alpha}}{d\lambda} + b\frac{dx^{\alpha}}{d\mu}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\alpha}} = w^{\alpha}\boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha}
$$

The new operator is the derivative along some other curve through P with parameter phi.

**Symbols:** phi parameter of the third curve

### (31.16) Closure of tangent vectors at a point · central · GA §31.2 p.326 (pdf 343)

$$
\frac{d}{d\phi} = a\frac{d}{d\lambda} + b\frac{d}{d\mu}
$$

Directional derivatives at P are closed under linear combination, so they form a vector space (the tangent space).

### (31.17) Symmetric vector/1-form pairing · central · GA §31.3 p.327 (pdf 344)

$$
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v} \equiv \langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle = \boldsymbol{v}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}) = \tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{v}) = (\text{number})
$$

A vector eats a 1-form and a 1-form eats a vector, with the same numerical result; angle brackets avoid confusion with the metric dot product.

**Symbols:** sigma-tilde 1-form; v vector

### (31.18) Linearity of the pairing · supporting · GA §31.3 p.327 (pdf 344)

$$
\langle(a\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}} + b\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}),\boldsymbol{v}\rangle = a\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle + b\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle, \quad \langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},(n\boldsymbol{v} + m\boldsymbol{u})\rangle = n\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle + m\langle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\boldsymbol{u}\rangle
$$

The pairing is linear in the 1-form and in the vector (the second identity is stated untagged in the text).

**Symbols:** a, b, n, m numbers

### (31.19) Dual basis condition · central · GA §31.3 p.328 (pdf 345)

$$
\langle\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\beta},\boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha}\rangle = \delta^{\beta}_{\alpha}
$$

Defines basis 1-forms as dual to basis vectors.

**Symbols:** omega^beta basis 1-forms; delta Kronecker delta

### (31.20) Total differential (margin note) · supporting · GA §31.3 p.328 (pdf 345)

$$
df = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)_{y}dx + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)_{x}dy
$$

Calculus reminder motivating the identification of 1-forms with differentials.

**Symbols:** f(x,y)

### (31.21) Basis 1-forms are coordinate differentials · central · GA §31.3 p.328 (pdf 345)

$$
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\alpha} = \boldsymbol{d}x^{\alpha}
$$

The operation dual to taking a derivative is taking a differential; bold d is the exterior derivative.

**Symbols:** bold d differential (exterior derivative)

### (31.22) Check of coordinate duality · central · GA §31.3 p.328 (pdf 345)

$$
\langle\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\mu},\boldsymbol{e}_{\nu}\rangle = \left\langle\boldsymbol{d}x^{\mu},\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\nu}}\right\rangle = \frac{\partial x^{\mu}}{\partial x^{\nu}} = \delta^{\mu}_{\nu}
$$

Applying the basis vector (a derivative) to the coordinate function gives the Kronecker delta, confirming 31.19 for dx and partial.

### Differential of a function as a 1-form · central · GA §31.3 p.328 (pdf 345)

$$
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}} = \boldsymbol{d}f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{\alpha}}\boldsymbol{d}x^{\alpha} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{\alpha}}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\alpha}
$$

The simplest 1-form; its components are the partial derivatives of f with lower indices, correctly matched with upper-index basis 1-forms.

**Symbols:** f scalar function

### (31.23) Pairing df with a vector · derivation-step · GA §31.3 p.328 (pdf 345)

$$
\boldsymbol{u}[f] \equiv \langle\boldsymbol{d}f,\boldsymbol{u}\rangle = \left\langle\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{\alpha}}\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\alpha},u^{\beta}\boldsymbol{e}_{\beta}\right\rangle = u^{\beta}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{\alpha}}\langle\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\alpha},\boldsymbol{e}_{\beta}\rangle = u^{\beta}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{\alpha}}\delta^{\alpha}_{\beta} = u^{\alpha}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{\alpha}}
$$

Expanding both objects and using linearity and duality shows the pairing reproduces the directional derivative.

### (31.24) Function slot equals 1-form slot · central · GA §31.3 p.328 (pdf 345)

$$
\boldsymbol{u}[f] = \partial_{\boldsymbol{u}}f = \langle\boldsymbol{d}f,\boldsymbol{u}\rangle
$$

Boxed: acting with a vector on a function equals pairing the vector with the function's differential.

### (31.25) Defining rule of an (n, m) tensor · central · GA §31.4 p.329 (pdf 346)

$$
\boldsymbol{T}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{1},\ldots,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{n},\boldsymbol{v}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{v}^{m}) = (\text{number})
$$

A tensor takes n 1-forms and m vectors to a number (linearly in each slot).

**Symbols:** n 1-form slots, m vector slots

### (31.26) Tensor product of two vectors · supporting · GA §31.4 p.329 (pdf 346)

$$
\boldsymbol{T}(\ ,\ ) = \boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{u}(\ )\otimes\boldsymbol{v}(\ )
$$

A (2,0) tensor with two empty 1-form slots, the first fed to u and the second to v.

**Symbols:** otimes tensor product

### (31.27) Components of u tensor v · supporting · GA §31.4 p.329 (pdf 346)

$$
T^{\alpha\beta} = \boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\beta}) = \boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\alpha})\,\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\beta}) = \langle\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\alpha},\boldsymbol{u}\rangle\langle\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\beta},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle = u^{\alpha}v^{\beta}
$$

Insert basis 1-forms to extract components.

### (31.28) Reconstruction of u tensor v · supporting · GA §31.4 p.329 (pdf 346)

$$
\boldsymbol{T} = u^{\alpha}v^{\beta}\,\boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha}\otimes\boldsymbol{e}_{\beta}
$$

Components times tensor products of basis vectors rebuild the tensor.

### (31.29) Reconstruction with slots shown · derivation-step · GA §31.4 p.329 (pdf 346)

$$
\boldsymbol{T}(\ ,\ ) = u^{\alpha}v^{\beta}\,\boldsymbol{e}_{\alpha}(\ )\otimes\boldsymbol{e}_{\beta}(\ )
$$

Same as 31.28 with the empty 1-form slots made explicit.

### (31.30) Metric as a (0,2) tensor · central · GA §31.4 p.329 (pdf 346)

$$
\boldsymbol{g} = g_{\mu\nu}\,\boldsymbol{d}x^{\mu}\otimes\boldsymbol{d}x^{\nu} = g_{\mu\nu}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\mu}\otimes\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\nu}
$$

The line element ds^2 recast as a tensor product of basis 1-forms; a two-slot machine for vectors.

**Symbols:** g_{mu nu} metric components

### (31.31) Building an (n, m) tensor · supporting · GA §31.4 p.329 (pdf 346)

$$
\boldsymbol{T} = \boldsymbol{u}^{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\boldsymbol{u}^{n}\otimes\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{m}
$$

Tensor product of n vectors and m 1-forms gives an (n, m) tensor; general tensors are sums of such products.

### (31.32) Components of an (n, m) tensor · central · GA §31.4 p.330 (pdf 347)

$$
T^{\mu_1\ldots\mu_n}{}_{\nu_1\ldots\nu_m} = \boldsymbol{u}^{1}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\mu_1})\ldots\boldsymbol{u}^{n}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\mu_n})\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{1}(\boldsymbol{e}_{\nu_1})\ldots\tilde{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{m}(\boldsymbol{e}_{\nu_m})
$$

Insert n basis 1-forms and m basis vectors to read off components.

### (31.33) Expansion of an (n, m) tensor on the basis · central · GA §31.4 p.330 (pdf 347)

$$
\boldsymbol{T} = T^{\mu_1\ldots\mu_n}{}_{\nu_1\ldots\nu_m}\left(\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu_1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu_n}\otimes\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\nu_1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\nu_m}\right)
$$

Components times tensor-product basis rebuild the tensor.

### (31.34) A (0,3) tensor · supporting · GA §31.5 p.330 (pdf 347)

$$
\boldsymbol{P}(\ ,\ ,\ ) = P_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\alpha}\otimes\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\beta}\otimes\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\gamma}
$$

Sample tensor with three vector slots used for the operations catalogue.

### (31.35) Filling all slots · supporting · GA §31.5 p.330 (pdf 347)

$$
\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}) = P_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\beta}(\boldsymbol{v})\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\gamma}(\boldsymbol{w}) = P_{\alpha\beta\gamma}u^{\alpha}v^{\beta}w^{\gamma}
$$

A fully fed tensor is a scalar obtained by contracting components with input components.

### (31.36) Gradient of a tensor, tip-minus-base picture · supporting · GA §31.5 p.330 (pdf 347)

$$
\nabla\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{z}) \approx \boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\big|_{\text{tip of }\boldsymbol{z}} - \boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})\big|_{\text{base of }\boldsymbol{z}}
$$

The extra slot of the gradient measures the change of the tensor's output across the vector z (valid for small z).

**Symbols:** z vector in the new slot

### (31.37) Gradient components in flat space · supporting · GA §31.5 p.330 (pdf 347)

$$
\frac{\partial P_{\alpha\beta\gamma}}{\partial x^{\mu}}u^{\alpha}v^{\beta}w^{\gamma}z^{\mu} = P_{\alpha\beta\gamma,\mu}u^{\alpha}v^{\beta}w^{\gamma}z^{\mu}
$$

In flat space (Cartesian-like coordinates) the gradient's components are ordinary partial derivatives.

**Symbols:** comma denotes partial derivative

### (31.38) A (1,3) tensor · supporting · GA §31.5 p.330 (pdf 347)

$$
\boldsymbol{S} = S^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}\otimes\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\alpha}\otimes\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\beta}\otimes\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\gamma}
$$

Sample tensor with the index structure of the Riemann tensor, used to illustrate contraction and divergence.

### (31.39) Contraction in slot language · central · GA §31.5 p.330 (pdf 347)

$$
\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}) = \boldsymbol{S}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\mu},\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu},\boldsymbol{v})
$$

Contracting the first and third slots by inserting a dual pair and summing gives a (0,2) tensor.

**Symbols:** sum over mu implied

### (31.40) Contraction in components · central · GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

$$
\boldsymbol{Q}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}) = Q_{\alpha\gamma}u^{\alpha}v^{\gamma} = S^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\mu\gamma}u^{\alpha}v^{\gamma}
$$

Setting an upper and a lower index equal and summing: Q_{alpha gamma} = S^mu_{alpha mu gamma}. The printed equation writes Q_{alpha beta} u^alpha v^gamma, an index typo corrected here.

### (31.41) Divergence of a (1,3) tensor · supporting · GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

$$
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{S}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}) = \nabla\boldsymbol{S}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\mu},\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{e}_{\mu}) = \frac{\partial S^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}}{\partial x^{\mu}}u^{\alpha}v^{\beta}w^{\gamma} = S^{\mu}{}_{\alpha\beta\gamma,\mu}u^{\alpha}v^{\beta}w^{\gamma}
$$

Contract the gradient slot with the upper slot; component form valid in flat space.

### (31.42) Transpose · supporting · GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

$$
\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}) \rightarrow \boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w})
$$

Swap the contents of two slots.

### (31.43) Totally symmetric tensor · supporting · GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

$$
\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}) = \boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}) = \boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}) = \ldots
$$

Output unchanged by any permutation of inputs.

### (31.44) Totally antisymmetric tensor · supporting · GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

$$
\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}) = -\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}) = +\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}) = \ldots
$$

Each swap of two inputs flips the sign, so cyclic (even) permutations keep it.

### (31.45) Symmetrization of a (2,0) tensor · central · GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

$$
T^{(\mu\nu)} = \frac{1}{2!}\left(T^{\mu\nu} + T^{\nu\mu}\right)
$$

Extracts the symmetric part; equals T for symmetric tensors and vanishes for antisymmetric ones.

### (31.46) General symmetrization rule · supporting · GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

$$
T^{(\alpha\ldots\kappa)}{}_{\mu\ldots\sigma} = \frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in S_n} T^{\pi(\alpha\ldots\kappa)}{}_{\mu\ldots\sigma}
$$

Average over all permutations of the n bracketed indices (the book states the sum in words).

**Symbols:** pi permutation of the bracketed indices

### (31.47) Antisymmetrization of a (2,0) tensor · central · GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

$$
T^{[\mu\nu]} = \frac{1}{2!}\left(T^{\mu\nu} - T^{\nu\mu}\right)
$$

Extracts the antisymmetric part; vanishes for symmetric tensors and equals A for antisymmetric ones.

### (31.48) General antisymmetrization rule · supporting · GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

$$
T^{[\alpha\ldots\kappa]}{}_{\mu\ldots\sigma} = \frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in S_n}\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\,T^{\pi(\alpha\ldots\kappa)}{}_{\mu\ldots\sigma}
$$

Signed average over permutations of the n bracketed indices (stated in words in the book).

**Symbols:** sgn(pi) = +1 for even, -1 for odd permutations

### (31.49) Wedge product of two vectors · central · GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

$$
\boldsymbol{b} = \boldsymbol{u}\wedge\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{v} - \boldsymbol{v}\otimes\boldsymbol{u}
$$

The antisymmetrized tensor product builds a bivector.

**Symbols:** wedge exterior product

### (31.50) Bivector components · supporting · GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348)

$$
b^{\mu\nu} = \boldsymbol{b}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\mu},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\nu}) = \boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\mu})\,\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\nu}) - \boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\mu})\,\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\nu}) = u^{\mu}v^{\nu} - v^{\mu}u^{\nu}
$$

Components of u wedge v are antisymmetric in mu, nu. The printed version uses square brackets for the 1-form slots, contrary to the book's own bracket convention; round brackets used here.

### (31.51) Exercise 31.5(b) identity · supporting · GA p.332 (pdf 349)

$$
F^{\mu\nu}\eta_{\mu\alpha}\eta_{\nu\beta}u^{\alpha}u^{\beta} = 0
$$

Contracting an antisymmetric tensor with the symmetric product of a vector with itself gives zero.

**Symbols:** F^{mu nu} antisymmetric; eta Minkowski metric

### (31.52) Exercise 31.6 starting expression · supporting · GA p.332 (pdf 349)

$$
\frac{\partial^{2}\xi^{\mu}}{\partial x^{\lambda}\partial x^{\sigma}} = \Gamma^{\nu}{}_{\lambda\sigma}\frac{\partial\xi^{\mu}}{\partial x^{\nu}}
$$

Relation between second derivatives of local inertial coordinates and connection coefficients (from Part II).

**Symbols:** xi^mu locally inertial coordinates; Gamma connection coefficients

### (31.53) Derivative of basis vectors (Exercise 31.6 target) · supporting · GA p.333 (pdf 350)

$$
\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{e}_{\sigma}}{\partial x^{\lambda}} = \Gamma^{\nu}{}_{\lambda\sigma}\boldsymbol{e}_{\nu}
$$

Removing the 'point' xi from 31.52, as basis vectors were obtained from partial P/partial x, gives the familiar statement that connection coefficients measure how basis vectors change.

## Worked examples

### Example 31.1 · intro · GA p.322 (pdf 339)

**Problem:** Decide which of a list of spaces qualify as manifolds.

**Method:** Apply the informal test that every point must have a neighbourhood that looks like flat R^n under enough magnification; check lines, planes, circle, sphere, torus, then a line through a plane, a double cone, a kinked line and a self-intersecting line.

**Key insight:** Failure is local: one point where branches meet, a cone tip, a corner or a crossing is enough to spoil the manifold property.

**Result:** R^n, S^1, S^2 and the torus are manifolds; the four defective shapes are not.

**Concepts:** smooth manifold, non-manifold (singular point) examples

### Example 31.2 · intro · GA §31.1 p.323 (pdf 340)

**Problem:** Review the elementary tools: vectors as arrows on a basis, gradient of a surface function, and directional derivative.

**Method:** Expand u on a basis, rewrite z = f(x,y) as h = 0, compute grad h in Cartesian and index form, and dot it with v.

**Key insight:** The directional derivative is a number computed from derivatives of h and components of v, which is exactly what a 1-form acting on a vector will later deliver; the gradient-as-vector step is the unnatural part.

**Result:** partial_v h = v^alpha partial h/partial x^alpha.

**Concepts:** gradient as a normal vector, directional derivative, basis vectors and components

### Example 31.3 · standard · GA §31.2 p.325 (pdf 342)

**Problem:** Show that tangent vectors at a point, defined as derivative operators along curves, form a vector space.

**Method:** Write u = d/dmu and v = d/dlambda in the common coordinate basis partial_alpha, form av + bu, collect components, and argue that the resulting operator is d/dphi along some other curve through P.

**Key insight:** Linearity of the chain rule: coordinate rates add, so the sum of two directional derivatives is itself a directional derivative.

**Result:** d/dphi = a d/dlambda + b d/dmu, i.e. w = av + bu with w^alpha = a dx^alpha/dlambda + b dx^alpha/dmu.

**Concepts:** tangent space, vector space, coordinate basis vectors

### Example 31.4 · intro · GA §31.3 p.327 (pdf 344)

**Problem:** Interpret a component of a 1-form in the stacked-surface picture.

**Method:** Pair the 1-form A with the unit x-direction basis vector to get A_1, read that as the number of surfaces crossed per unit length, and invert to get the spacing.

**Key insight:** Large components mean closely packed surfaces; scaling the form by F packs the surfaces F times more densely.

**Result:** Surface spacing along x is 1/A_1; multiplying the form by F multiplies surface density by F.

**Concepts:** 1-form, duality pairing of vectors and 1-forms

### Example · standard · GA §31.3 p.328 (pdf 345)

**Problem:** Show that pairing the differential df with a vector u gives the directional derivative u[f] (eqn 31.23).

**Method:** Expand df on basis 1-forms and u on basis vectors, pull out components by linearity, apply the duality delta, and relabel.

**Key insight:** The function slot of a vector is redundant: once 1-forms are differentials, acting on f is the same as eating df.

**Result:** <df, u> = u^alpha partial_alpha f = u[f].

**Concepts:** differential of a function as a 1-form, dual basis, directional derivative

### Example 31.5 · intro · GA §31.4 p.329 (pdf 346)

**Problem:** Extract the components of the (2,0) tensor u tensor v and reconstruct it.

**Method:** Insert basis 1-forms into both slots, factor into two pairings, use duality to get components, then multiply by e_alpha tensor e_beta and sum.

**Key insight:** Components are just the tensor's outputs on basis inputs; the same recipe works for any valence.

**Result:** T^{alpha beta} = u^alpha v^beta and T = u^alpha v^beta e_alpha tensor e_beta.

**Concepts:** tensor product, tensor components by slot insertion

### Example 31.6 · intro · GA §31.4 p.329 (pdf 346)

**Problem:** Express the metric as a tensor built from basis 1-forms.

**Method:** Take the tensor product of coordinate differentials and weight by g_{mu nu}.

**Key insight:** The familiar line element ds^2 is a (0,2) machine eating two vectors.

**Result:** g = g_{mu nu} dx^mu tensor dx^nu.

**Concepts:** metric tensor as a (0,2) tensor, tensor product

### Example 31.7 · standard · GA §31.5 p.330 (pdf 347)

**Problem:** Survey the standard operations on tensors using a (0,3) tensor P and a (1,3) tensor S.

**Method:** Fill slots of P to get a scalar; add a gradient slot and read it as tip-minus-base change; contract S by inserting omega^mu and e_mu; define divergence as gradient plus contraction; swap slots to define symmetry classes; write symmetrizers and antisymmetrizers with 1/n!; build a bivector via the wedge product and compute its components.

**Key insight:** Every operation is a statement about slots: add one, pair two, swap two, or average over permutations, each with an immediate component translation.

**Result:** P_{alpha beta gamma, mu}; Q_{alpha gamma} = S^mu_{alpha mu gamma}; divergence S^mu_{alpha beta gamma, mu}; T^{(mu nu)}, T^{[mu nu]}; b^{mu nu} = u^mu v^nu - v^mu u^nu.

**Concepts:** gradient of a tensor, tensor contraction, divergence of a tensor, transpose of tensor slots, symmetric and antisymmetric tensors, symmetrization, antisymmetrization, wedge product, bivector


## Analogies and intuitions

### Zooming in until the space looks flat → smooth manifold · strong · GA p.322 (pdf 339)

A space counts as a manifold if magnifying around any point eventually shows ordinary flat space; the non-manifolds in Fig. 31.1 keep showing a corner, junction or tip at every magnification.

**Where it breaks down:** Visual smoothness is not the formal definition (charts and differentiable transition maps); a kinked curve is still a topological manifold.

**App idea:** Magnifier lens over curves and surfaces with a flatness verdict.

### Removing the point from dP/dlambda leaves an empty machine → tangent vector as derivative operator · strong · GA §31.2 p.324 (pdf 341)

The vector is first the velocity of a moving point; deleting the point leaves d/dlambda with a blank slot that can accept points, functions or later 1-forms.

**Where it breaks down:** On a manifold points cannot be subtracted, so dP/dlambda is itself only heuristic; the rigorous route defines the operator on functions directly.

**App idea:** Animated equation where the symbol P fades out of dP/dlambda leaving a glowing empty bracket that the learner fills with f(x) = x^2 + y^2 to get a number.

### Tip minus base → directional derivative · useful · GA §31.2 p.325 (pdf 342)

The value of a function at the arrowhead of a vector minus its value at the tail approximates the directional derivative; reused for the gradient of a tensor (eqn 31.36).

**Where it breaks down:** Only a first-order approximation for small vectors; on a manifold the tip of a tangent vector is not a point of the manifold.

**App idea:** Heat-map function with a draggable short arrow; readouts of f(tip) - f(base) and v^alpha partial_alpha f converge as the arrow shrinks.

### Slot machines → tensor as multilinear map · strong · GA §31.4 p.329 (pdf 346)

Vectors, 1-forms and tensors are machines with labelled slots; feeding in the right objects makes a number, and operations are described as adding, pairing or swapping slots.

**Where it breaks down:** Hides multilinearity unless stressed; a slot machine metaphor may suggest randomness or single use.

**App idea:** Drag-and-drop tensor machine with typed slots (1-form or vector) that rejects wrong inputs and shows the component sum as it computes.

### 1-form as a stack of surfaces pierced by an arrow → duality pairing of vectors and 1-forms · strong · GA §31.3 p.327 (pdf 344)

The pairing counts how many sheets a vector crosses; spacing is the reciprocal of the component, and scaling the form packs the sheets.

**Where it breaks down:** Only literally works for constant 1-forms in flat space; non-integer counts and non-exact 1-forms (whose sheets cannot close up) need more care.

**App idea:** 3D sheet counter with adjustable spacing and orientation.

### Three floors: tangent space, number line, dual space → dual space · useful · GA §31.3 p.327 (pdf 344)

Fig. 31.8 places vectors and 1-forms on separate planes with the real line between, making each a map onto numbers for the other.

**Where it breaks down:** Suggests the dual space is a separate place with no link to the manifold; also invites confusing it with a metric-based identification of vectors and 1-forms.

**App idea:** Linked-planes visual in which choosing objects on two planes drops a marker onto the number line.

### Congruence as fluid streamlines → congruence of curves · useful · GA §31.2 p.326 (pdf 343)

A margin note previews that the space-filling non-crossing curves will represent the flow lines of a fluid in ch39.

**Where it breaks down:** Fluid streamlines carry a natural parameter (time) and density information that a bare congruence lacks.

**App idea:** Dye-in-water animation where particles trace the congruence and arrows show the vector field.


## Misconceptions addressed

### A vector is fundamentally an arrow stretching from one point of space to another. · GA §31.2 p.324 (pdf 341)

**Correction:** On a general manifold a vector lives at a single point, in that point's tangent space, and is best defined as a directional derivative d/dlambda along a curve.

**Why tempting:** School vectors are displacements in flat space where arrows between points make sense.

### The gradient of a function is naturally a vector normal to its level surfaces. · GA §31.1 p.323 (pdf 340)

**Correction:** The natural object is the 1-form df with lower-index components; turning it into a normal vector requires a metric, and in index notation h_{,alpha} e_alpha is visibly inconsistent.

**Why tempting:** Vector calculus in Euclidean space with an orthonormal basis hides the distinction.

### Vectors and 1-forms belong to the manifold itself. · GA p.322 (pdf 339)

**Correction:** They live in the tangent and dual spaces attached to each point, not in the manifold M.

**Why tempting:** In R^n the manifold, its tangent spaces and their duals can all be identified with R^n.

### Vectors at different points can be directly compared or subtracted. · GA §31.2 p.326 (pdf 343)

**Correction:** Different points have different tangent spaces; comparison needs a connection (parallel transport) or a flow (Lie derivative).

**Why tempting:** Sliding arrows around is harmless in flat space with Cartesian coordinates.

### Equal parameter values on different curves of a congruence mark equal distances. · GA §31.2 p.326 (pdf 343)

**Correction:** Each curve's parameter is independent; lambda = 2 may sit at different arc lengths on different curves.

**Why tempting:** Parameters are often thought of as distance or time.

### Pairing a 1-form with a vector requires a metric (it is the dot product). · GA §31.3 p.327 (pdf 344)

**Correction:** The pairing <sigma, v> is defined without any metric; the dot product of two vectors does need one. The book prefers angle brackets precisely to avoid the confusion.

**Why tempting:** Both are called inner products and both are written with a dot at times.

### The order of factors in a tensor product never matters. · GA §31.4 p.330 (pdf 347)

**Correction:** Order among the vector factors and among the 1-form factors matters (u tensor v is not v tensor u); only the relative listing of the vector group versus the 1-form group is a matter of bookkeeping.

**Why tempting:** Components u^alpha v^beta are ordinary numbers that commute.

### Every tensor is a single tensor product of vectors and 1-forms. · GA §31.4 p.329 (pdf 346)

**Correction:** A general tensor is a sum of such products (e.g. a generic (2,0) tensor in 4D needs up to four product terms); only the basis products span the space.

**Why tempting:** The chapter says any tensor can be built from n vectors and m 1-forms.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The component-wise partial derivative P_{alpha beta gamma, mu} is the gradient of a tensor on any manifold. · GA §31.5 p.330 (pdf 347)

**Correction:** Only in flat space with suitable coordinates; in general the gradient must use the covariant derivative with connection terms.

**Why tempting:** The coordinate-free notation nabla P looks the same everywhere.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Magnifying a point on a shape · GA p.322 (pdf 339)

**Setup:** Pick any point on a candidate space and keep enlarging a small neighbourhood of it.

**Lesson:** If the enlargement eventually looks like a flat line, plane or R^n at every point, the space is a manifold; persistent corners, crossings or apexes rule it out.

**App idea:** Magnifier over Fig. 31.1 style shapes.

### Many curves through one point · GA §31.2 p.325 (pdf 342)

**Setup:** Imagine all possible smooth curves passing through P and the tangent operator of each at P.

**Lesson:** Their tangent operators can be scaled and added and still correspond to some curve through P, so they fill out a vector space whose dimension equals the manifold's.

**App idea:** Spray of curves through P whose tangent arrows fill a plane.

### Trying to compare arrows at two points · GA §31.2 p.326 (pdf 343)

**Setup:** Given a vector at P and one at Q on a bare manifold, ask whether they are the same.

**Lesson:** Without a rule for carrying vectors between points the question is meaningless; this motivates connections and Lie derivatives.

**App idea:** On a sphere, carry a vector from P to Q along two different paths and show the arrivals disagree.

(GA §31.4 p.329 (pdf 346))

## Notation and conventions

- **Square versus round brackets on vectors:** v[f] denotes a vector acting on a function (directional derivative); v(sigma) denotes a vector eating a 1-form. v[ ] with an empty bracket denotes the operator awaiting input. — The book itself uses square brackets for 1-form slots in eqn 31.50. (GA §31.2 p.325 (pdf 342))
- **Pairing notation:** <sigma, v> = sigma(v) = v(sigma), sometimes sigma . v; angle brackets preferred to avoid confusion with the metric dot product. (GA §31.3 p.327 (pdf 344))
- **1-forms:** Written with a tilde (sigma-tilde, A-tilde); basis 1-forms omega^alpha with upper indices; vectors bold with basis e_alpha. — Some displayed equations print a hat instead of a tilde in the export; the PDF shows tildes. (GA §31.3 p.327 (pdf 344))
- **Bold d for differentials:** A bold d (dx^alpha, df) marks the differential or exterior derivative, distinct from the ordinary d in d/dlambda. — Explanation deferred to the exterior derivative chapter (ch33). (GA §31.3 p.328 (pdf 345))
- **Tensor valence:** (n, m) = n 1-form slots (upper indices) and m vector slots (lower indices); 'rank' is used interchangeably. — Contraction is said to lower the rank by two, meaning total index count. (GA §31.4 p.329 (pdf 346))
- **Symmetrization brackets:** Round brackets around indices, T^{(mu nu)}, denote symmetrization with 1/n!; square brackets, T^{[mu nu]}, antisymmetrization with 1/n!. — The wedge product u wedge v = u tensor v - v tensor u carries no 1/2, so u wedge v has components 2 u^{[mu} v^{nu]}. (GA §31.5 p.331 (pdf 348))
- **Comma derivative:** A comma before an index denotes a partial derivative with respect to that coordinate; semicolon (Exercise 31.5) a covariant derivative. (GA §31.5 p.330 (pdf 347))
- **Curly letters for points:** Points on the manifold are calligraphic P, Q, A, B; a curve is P(lambda). (GA §31.2 p.324 (pdf 341))

## Teaching gems

### Derive 'vector = derivative' in three strokes: the derivative of a parametrized segment is the arrow, the chain rule expresses a curve's tangent in coordinates, and deleting the point leaves the operator (dx^alpha/dlambda) partial_alpha. · GA §31.2 p.324 (pdf 341)

**Why it works:** Each step is familiar calculus, so the abstract identification arrives as an inevitable consequence rather than a definition imposed from above.

**App idea:** Three-panel stepper: segment with lambda slider, curved path on a coordinate grid, then the operator applied to a user-chosen test function, with the symbolic expression morphing between panels.

### Read components and basis vectors straight off the chain rule: dx^alpha/dlambda are the components and partial/partial x^alpha are the basis vectors. · GA §31.2 p.324 (pdf 341)

**Why it works:** Demystifies the odd-looking notation e_alpha = partial_alpha by showing it is forced by matching two expressions for the same object.

**App idea:** Coordinate switcher (Cartesian, polar) showing the basis operators as arrows along coordinate lines and components recomputed live for a fixed tangent vector.

### Reconcile the two ways a vector eats things: u[f] computed as a derivative equals <df, u> computed as a pairing. · GA §31.3 p.328 (pdf 345)

**Why it works:** Unifies analysis and linear algebra and quietly repairs the 'gradient as normal vector' problem raised at the start.

**App idea:** Contour map of f with df drawn as local level-line stacks; drag a vector and see the directional derivative and the sheet count agree.

### Dual basis check in one line: <dx^mu, partial_nu> = partial x^mu/partial x^nu = delta^mu_nu. · GA §31.3 p.328 (pdf 345)

**Why it works:** Turns the abstract duality condition into the obvious fact that a coordinate changes only along its own direction.

**App idea:** Grid with coordinate lines; clicking a basis arrow shows it crossing exactly one level set of its own coordinate and none of the others.

### Closure of tangent vectors shown by combining the tangent operators of two curves into the tangent of a third curve. · GA §31.2 p.326 (pdf 343)

**Why it works:** Gives the tangent space a concrete construction from curves rather than an axiom.

**App idea:** Sliders a, b generate a third curve through P whose tangent is av + bu.

### Congruence to vector field: non-crossing space-filling curves give one tangent per point. · GA §31.2 p.326 (pdf 343)

**Why it works:** Makes vector fields visual and prepares integral curves, flows and Lie dragging.

**App idea:** Streamline explorer toggling between curves and arrows, with uneven parameter ticks.

### Every tensor operation described as slot surgery: add a slot (gradient), pair two slots (contraction), swap slots (transpose), average over slot permutations ((anti)symmetrization). · GA §31.5 p.330 (pdf 347)

**Why it works:** Gives learners a small set of verbs to organize a long list of index manipulations.

**App idea:** Tensor workbench: a tensor shown as a box with typed slots; operations are buttons that visibly add, link or swap slots while the component expression updates.

### Penrose diagrams as an exercise for index bookkeeping. · GA p.333 (pdf 350)

**Why it works:** Converts error-prone index juggling into visual topology: lines are indices, joins are contractions, bars are (anti)symmetrizations.

**App idea:** Bidirectional Penrose-diagram editor linked to index notation.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **The derivation starts from dP/dlambda and B - A, which presuppose subtracting points, exactly what a bare manifold does not allow.** — Careful readers may feel the argument is circular or wonder what B - A means on a sphere. *Suggestion:* Present the point-based step as flat-space motivation, then define the tangent vector directly as the operator f -> d(f(P(lambda)))/dlambda, which needs no subtraction.
- **Printed eqn 31.27 labels the components T^{mu nu} on the left but uses alpha, beta on the right, and also places a tensor-product sign between u(omega^alpha) and v(omega^beta), which are plain numbers.** — Index-matching confusion in the very first component extraction. *Suggestion:* Use one consistent pair of indices throughout (T^{alpha beta} = u^alpha v^beta) and write the product of the two pairings as ordinary multiplication; the same product-of-numbers slip recurs in eqn 31.50.
- **Printed eqn 31.40 writes Q_{alpha beta} u^alpha v^gamma; the free indices do not match (should be Q_{alpha gamma}).** — Undermines the lesson that contraction leaves exactly the unpaired indices free. *Suggestion:* Correct to Q_{alpha gamma} = S^mu_{alpha mu gamma} and highlight which slots were paired.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'If you lived on a sphere and could not leave it, what could a vector at a point possibly be, since you cannot draw an arrow to another point?' Let the learner struggle before offering 'a way of moving through the point', i.e. a derivative along a curve.
- Check the prerequisites quickly: can the learner differentiate f(x(lambda), y(lambda)) with the chain rule, and do they know that a 1-form eats a vector to give a number (GA ch4)? If not, repair these first.
- Order of explanation that works: arrow from A to B, derivative of the segment, tangent to a curve, chain rule in coordinates, remove the point, apply to a function, read off components and basis, then closure and vector fields. Delay 1-forms until the learner can say 'v[f] is the directional derivative'.
- Use a concrete test function and curve, for instance f = x^2 + y^2 on the circle x = cos lambda, y = sin lambda: v[f] = 0, so the vector is tangent to level sets. This anchors the operator view numerically.
- Check for understanding: ask what partial/partial theta is as a vector in polar coordinates and how long it is at radius r (it grows with r, needing a metric to measure). This probes the difference between basis operators and unit vectors.
- Check for understanding: ask the learner to verify <dr, partial_theta> = 0 and <dtheta, partial_theta> = 1 in polar coordinates without using any metric.
- When 1-forms appear, stress that the pairing needs no metric, and that the dot product of two vectors (Exercise 31.1c) does. Many learners conflate them.
- Anticipated question: 'Why call it a vector if it is a derivative?' Answer: because these operators add and scale exactly like vectors and have the right number of independent directions; the arrow picture is one representation of the same vector space.
- Anticipated question: 'Isn't the gradient a vector?' Answer: df is a 1-form; its components are partial_alpha f with lower indices. A metric converts it to the normal vector of school calculus.
- For the tensor operations, have the learner say for each operation what happens to the slot count and valence before writing any components, then verify with indices. Fix the printed index typos in 31.27 and 31.40 as you go.
- Probe symmetry fluency: ask why A^{mu nu} T_{mu nu} = 0 when A is symmetric and T antisymmetric (relabel dummy indices), a trick used constantly later.
- If the learner is advanced, connect forward: the wedge product and antisymmetrization lead to p-forms (ch32), bold d to the exterior derivative (ch33), and the 'cannot compare at different points' remark to the Lie and covariant derivatives (ch33, ch34).
