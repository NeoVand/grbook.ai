# GA ch41 · Inflation

> Motivates cosmic inflation through the flatness and horizon problems, builds spontaneous symmetry breaking from Landau's theory of a magnet and carries it over to a phi^4 scalar field, derives the Hubble-damped field equation and the scalar-field energy density and pressure in a flat Robertson-Walker universe, uses the slow-roll approximation to obtain exponential growth of a(t), and argues that such expansion drives any initial curvature towards flatness before reheating ends it.

**Pages:** printed 445–452 · pdf 462–469 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

Mathematically light apart from Example 41.3, which needs comfort with covariant derivatives and Robertson-Walker connection coefficients; the rest is minimizing polynomials and substituting into Friedmann equations. Conceptually it juggles three worlds (cosmological puzzles, condensed-matter symmetry breaking, curved-space field theory) and relies heavily on Chapters 15-19 and 40. The analogies make symmetry breaking accessible, but the slow-roll and flattening arguments are verbal and quick, the quantitative flatness and horizon arguments are missing, and two printed equations (41.9, 41.22) contain slips. The reading copy also drops the double dot on phi in the slow-roll sentence.

## Role in the book

Placed in Part VI directly after the Lagrangian field theory of Chapter 40, this short chapter is the book's first application of that machinery to cosmology. It reaches back to Part III (Robertson-Walker geometry, the Friedmann equations, the de Sitter Universe 1 and Penrose diagrams of Chapters 15-19) and shows that a scalar field with a symmetry-breaking potential behaves like a temporary cosmological constant. It offers an early-Universe story that repairs two weaknesses of the hot Big-Bang model left open in Part III, and it prepares the reader for the status report on inflation and Lambda-CDM in Chapter 49 and the singularity discussion in Chapter 50.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can state the flatness problem using k/adot^2 = Omega - 1 and explain why an observed Omega near 1 looks like a fine-tuned initial condition in a decelerating universe.
- Reader can explain the horizon problem on a Penrose diagram: two sky directions seen by one observer trace back to events whose past light cones never overlap after the Big Bang.
- Reader can describe how an early phase of accelerating expansion 'pushes back' the Big Bang on a conformal diagram so that past light cones overlap, and state the caveat that the shared history can be small.
- Reader can distinguish the symmetry of a Hamiltonian from the symmetry of its ground state and explain spontaneous symmetry breaking with the up/down magnet and the buckling Euler strut.
- Reader can write Landau's expansion F = F_0 + a M^2 + b M^4, set a = a_0 (T - T_c), and find the broken-symmetry minima M_0^2 = -a/2b below T_c.
- Reader can map the Landau free energy onto the phi^4 potential, locate the vacua phi_0 = ±(6 mu^2/lambda)^{1/2}, and classify the stationary points with the second derivative.
- Reader can derive phi-double-dot + 3(adot/a) phi-dot + dU/dphi = 0 from the covariant scalar equation using the flat Robertson-Walker connection coefficients, and interpret 3H as Hubble friction.
- Reader can extract rho = phi-dot^2/2 + U and p = phi-dot^2/2 - U from the scalar energy-momentum tensor and show that a frozen field acts as an effective cosmological constant Lambda = 8 pi U.
- Reader can apply the slow-roll approximation to the coupled field and Friedmann equations to obtain a(t) proportional to exp[(8 pi U_0/3)^{1/2} t].
- Reader can derive the acceleration condition p < -rho/3 from the second Friedmann equation, translate it into phi-dot^2 < U, and use it to explain how inflation ends.
- Reader can argue from the cosh, exp and sinh Lambda-driven solutions that exponential expansion drives any spatial curvature towards the flat Omega = 1 case, and describe reheating as the hand-off to a standard hot Big Bang.

## Teaching approach

A compact, analogy-led chapter that borrows its central idea from condensed-matter physics rather than deriving it from cosmology. It opens with two puzzles of the standard hot Big Bang, then spends almost half its length on Landau's theory of a magnetic phase transition, using spin arrays, a buckling strut and double-well free-energy curves to make spontaneous symmetry breaking concrete before relabelling the magnetization as a scalar field. The general-relativistic content is then delivered as one boxed example (the scalar field in a flat expanding universe) and two short deductions: reading energy density and pressure off the field's energy-momentum tensor, and applying slow roll to get exponential expansion. The flatness claim is supported by pointing back to the Lambda-driven Friedmann solutions rather than by a fresh calculation, and the chapter ends with an explicit note that inflation is the dominant but not universally accepted paradigm.

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, analogy-driven, derivation-heavy, diagram-driven, conversational-informal, example-driven

**Narrative arc**

1. Epigraph from Alice growing after drinking from the bottle signals sudden enlargement
2. Motivating puzzle 1: the flatness problem, framed with k/adot^2 = Omega - 1 and the apparent fine-tuning of Omega near unity
3. Thermal-history interlude (Example 41.1): a cooling Universe passes through GUT, electroweak and confinement phase transitions, planting the idea that phase transitions shape cosmology
4. Motivating puzzle 2: the horizon problem shown on a Penrose diagram, with events q and r seen by observer p lacking any shared past
5. Proposed remedy: an early era of accelerating a(t) that pushes the Big Bang back on the diagram; caveat that s and t still share little history; claim that the result is also flat
6. Model borrowed from condensed matter: Landau's symmetry-breaking phase transition, built from a magnet whose high-T disorder and low-T order are compared under a global 180-degree spin flip
7. Puzzle of choice: the Hamiltonian treats up and down equally yet the ordered state picks one; the Euler strut shows the same thing mechanically
8. Formalism for the analogy: Landau free energy as a quartic power series in the order parameter, sign change of a at T_c, minima at ±M_0 (Example 41.2)
9. Translation to field theory: replace M by phi, F by the potential U of a phi^4 Lagrangian; flip the sign of the quadratic term to obtain two degenerate vacua; mention Higgs mechanism as physical precedent
10. Cosmological model: phi^4 field with temperature-dependent mu^2(T), uniform in space, evolving in a flat Robertson-Walker spacetime
11. Derivation (Example 41.3): second covariant derivative of a scalar, flat RW Christoffel symbols, trace with g^{mu nu} gives the equation with Hubble friction 3H
12. Back-reaction: energy density and pressure of the homogeneous field from T_{mu nu}; frozen field gives rho = -p and an effective Lambda = 8 pi U, recalling de Sitter
13. Close the system with the k = 0 Friedmann equation; highlight the coupled pair (field equation, expansion equation)
14. Approximation: slow roll on a shallow potential drops phi-double-dot and phi-dot^2, giving constant H and exponential a(t)
15. Why flat: compare with the cosh/exp/sinh Lambda solutions, which all approach exponential growth; hence Omega is driven to 1 whatever k was
16. Exit: acceleration condition p < -rho/3 becomes phi-dot^2 < U; inflation ends when the field speeds up or U falls; oscillation, damping and reheating hand over to ordinary matter and radiation
17. Status check: inflation is the reigning but not unanimous paradigm; pointer to Chapter 49

**Signature moves**

- Imports a thermodynamic idea (Landau theory) wholesale and then relabels symbols (M to phi, F to U, a to ±mu^2/2, b to lambda/4!) so the double-well picture carries straight into field theory.
- Uses paired before/after pictures (spin arrays flipped by 180 degrees) to define 'symmetry of the state' operationally instead of algebraically.
- Uses a purely mechanical system, the loaded strut, to show that symmetry breaking needs no quantum mechanics or thermodynamics.
- Shows the horizon problem and its cure as a pair of Penrose diagrams that differ only in how far down the Big-Bang line sits.
- Gets the cosmological behaviour of the field by reading rho and p off T_{mu nu} and matching to the perfect fluid, turning 'scalar field' into 'fluid with negative pressure' in two lines.
- Relies on the reader's stock of earlier exact solutions (de Sitter, cosh/sinh Lambda universes) as the proof of flattening rather than solving new equations.
- Keeps the derivation honest but minimal: one boxed covariant calculation, then approximation arguments stated in words.

## Section by section

### Chapter opening: the flatness problem and the thermal history (Example 41.1) — p.445 (pdf 462)

Recaps that the Universe is modelled as a Robertson-Walker spacetime beginning in a singularity, then rewrites the Friedmann equation as k/adot^2 = Omega - 1. If the Universe is flat, why was it set up that way; if Omega is merely close to one today, that requires a finely tuned past. This is named the flatness problem, with margin pointers to the fine-tuning debate. A second worry, the surprising uniformity of the early Universe, is raised, and Example 41.1 sketches the cooling sequence of early phase transitions (GUT separation near 10^-36 s, electroweak breaking near 10^-11 s, confinement near 10^-5 s).

**Concepts:** flatness problem, density parameter, fine-tuning of initial conditions, phase transitions in the early Universe, cosmic microwave background isotropy

**Key moves**
- Friedmann equation recast so that the sign of k is tied to whether Omega is above or below unity
- Two readings of Omega near 1: exactly flat forever, or a coincidence requiring tuned initial conditions
- Timeline of energy scales and temperatures linking cosmic time to particle-physics phase transitions

### The horizon problem and the idea of inflation — p.446 (pdf 463)

Regions that could never have exchanged light by the time of an early phase transition are nonetheless seen today at almost identical temperatures. A Penrose diagram makes this concrete: observer p sees events q and r in opposite directions whose past light cones reach the Big Bang without overlapping. The proposed remedy is an early period of accelerating expansion, inflation, which on the diagram lowers the Big-Bang line so that the pasts of q and r, and of events s and t on the transition surface, overlap. The authors caution that s and t still share only a sliver of history, so the horizon problem is eased rather than settled, and state that inflation also flattens space. The key tool announced is the symmetry-breaking phase transition, treated with the field theory of Chapter 40.

**Concepts:** horizon problem, causal connection, Penrose diagram, inflation, accelerating expansion

**Key moves**
- Define causal disconnection as light not yet having travelled between regions, let alone thermalized them
- Contrast inflation's accelerating a(t) with the decelerating solutions studied earlier
- Compare two conformal diagrams differing only in the depth of the Big-Bang boundary
- Flag Penrose's objection that overlap of pasts may be too slight to guarantee uniformity

### §41.1 Symmetry breaking — p.446 (pdf 463)

Develops Landau's picture of a continuous phase transition. Above T_c a magnet's up/down spins are disordered and the zero magnetization survives a global 180-degree flip; below T_c the spins align, the flip reverses M, and the ordered state has lower symmetry than the Hamiltonian, which does not favour either direction. The Euler strut buckling left or right illustrates the same spontaneous choice. Landau expands the free energy in powers of the order parameter; with a = a_0(T - T_c) and b > 0, the single minimum at M = 0 splits into two at ±M_0 below T_c (Example 41.2). The same shape appears in the phi^4 Lagrangian: with a positive quadratic term the vacuum is phi = 0, and with the sign reversed there are two vacua at ±(6 mu^2/lambda)^{1/2}, into which the system rolls. This is the mechanism of the Higgs sector, motivating scalar fields in cosmology.

**Concepts:** spontaneous symmetry breaking, global symmetry, Landau theory of phase transitions, order parameter, critical temperature, free energy minimization, phi^4 scalar field theory, vacuum of a field theory, Higgs mechanism

**Key moves**
- Operational test of symmetry: apply the global flip to the state and ask whether the macroscopic observable changes
- Separate symmetry of the Hamiltonian from symmetry of the ground state
- Expand F in even powers of M because the energy cannot depend on the sign of M
- Let the quadratic coefficient change sign at T_c to generate the transition
- Minimize: 2aM + 4bM^3 = 0 gives M_0^2 = -a/2b (Example 41.2)
- Identify the field value as the order parameter and U(phi) as the analogue of F
- Stationary points of the inverted-mass phi^4 potential and their second derivatives (margin note 11)
- Picture the field lowering its energy by rolling down into one vacuum

### §41.2 Effective potentials — p.449 (pdf 466)

Introduces the inflating field: a phi^4 field (explicitly not the Higgs) whose mass parameter mu^2(T) changes sign as the Universe cools through T_c, assumed spatially uniform but time dependent. Example 41.3 computes the covariant scalar equation in a flat Robertson-Walker spacetime, finding that expansion adds a friction term 3(adot/a)phi-dot. The energy-momentum tensor gives rho = phi-dot^2/2 + U and p = phi-dot^2/2 - U; a field that is not changing behaves as a fluid with p = -rho, i.e. an effective cosmological constant 8 pi U, recalling de Sitter growth. The k = 0 Friedmann equation with this rho closes the system. Slow roll on a shallow potential makes phi-double-dot negligible and the potential nearly constant at U_0, so the Hubble rate is constant and a(t) grows exponentially.

**Concepts:** inflaton field, temperature-dependent effective potential, second covariant derivative of a scalar, scalar field equation in an expanding universe, Hubble friction, energy-momentum tensor of a scalar field, energy density and pressure of a homogeneous scalar field, vacuum energy as an effective cosmological constant, Friedmann equation, slow-roll approximation, exponential (de Sitter-like) expansion

**Key moves**
- Temperature enters only through the sign-changing coefficient mu^2(T)
- Assume a uniform field so spatial gradients drop out
- Use phi_{;mu} = partial_mu phi for a scalar, then one connection term in the second derivative
- Only the diagonal of phi_{;mu nu} matters under the trace; Gamma^0_{ii} = a adot supplies the friction
- Read rho = T_00 and p = T_ii in the observer's local frame
- Match p = -rho to the vacuum fluid to identify Lambda = 8 pi U
- Couple field equation and Friedmann equation; drop phi-double-dot and phi-dot^2 under slow roll
- Constant H implies exponential a(t)

### §41.3 Why flat? — p.451 (pdf 468)

Justifies starting from k = 0 by arguing that inflation erases curvature anyway. The Lambda-dominated Friedmann solutions grow as cosh Ht, exp Ht and sinh Ht for k = +1, 0 and -1, and at late times the hyperbolic functions become exponentials, so every case approaches the flat Omega = 1 behaviour. The acceleration equation shows that positive acceleration needs p < -rho/3, which for the scalar field means phi-dot^2 < U. Inflation therefore stops when the field moves fast enough or the potential falls low enough; a field that reaches its minimum and oscillates is damped to rest, inflation ceasing if the minimum value is not positive, while couplings to other fields drain the field's energy into particles (reheating), leaving a flat universe of matter and radiation. The section closes by calling inflation the reigning but still debated paradigm.

**Concepts:** dilution of spatial curvature by inflation, condition for accelerated expansion, end of inflation, reheating, Robertson-Walker spatial curvature index

**Key moves**
- Use the already-known Lambda solutions as proxies for the inflating universe
- Asymptotics: cosh and sinh both tend to half an exponential, so curvature becomes irrelevant
- Insert rho and p of the field into the acceleration equation to get phi-dot^2 < U
- Read the same inequality backwards as the exit criterion
- Damped oscillation about the minimum and energy transfer to other fields

### Chapter summary and exercise — p.452 (pdf 469)

Three bullet points restate the logic: inflation is proposed to cure flatness and horizon problems, it rests on a symmetry-breaking transition as the Universe cools, and slow roll gives a finite stretch of exponential expansion ending in a flat Universe. A single two-part exercise asks for the scalar field equation in a curved (k not zero) Robertson-Walker universe and its reduction to the flat case.

**Concepts:** inflation, slow-roll approximation, spontaneous symmetry breaking

**Key moves**
- Summary ties the three conceptual strands (cosmological puzzles, phase transitions, slow roll) together
- Exercise generalizes Example 41.3 to k not zero using the Chapter 16 connection coefficients

## Concepts

### flatness problem

*phenomenon · core* · also: Omega fine-tuning problem

The puzzle that the observed Universe is very close to spatially flat (total density parameter near 1) even though in a decelerating Friedmann universe any departure of Omega from 1 grows with time, so a nearly flat Universe today requires an extraordinarily precise initial value of Omega.

**How introduced:** Opening paragraph: the Friedmann equation is written as k/adot^2 = Omega - 1 and the reader is asked why Omega should be near unity, either because k = 0 exactly or because of a coincidence requiring tuning in the past. The growth of |Omega - 1| is asserted rather than shown.

**Prerequisites:** density parameter, Friedmann equation, Robertson-Walker spatial curvature index

$$
\frac{k}{\dot a^2} = \Omega - 1
$$

**Notes:** Because adot decreases in matter- or radiation-dominated eras, k/adot^2 grows, which is the missing quantitative step (|Omega - 1| grows like a in the matter era and like a^2 in the radiation era). Inflation reverses this since adot grows.

**Where:** GA p.445 (pdf 462)

### density parameter

*physical-quantity · revisited* · also: Omega, density ratio rho/rho_c

The ratio Omega = rho/rho_c of the total energy density to the critical density rho_c = 3H^2/(8 pi) (G = c = 1); Omega = 1 corresponds to spatially flat k = 0, Omega > 1 to closed and Omega < 1 to open Robertson-Walker geometry.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Part III through eqn 41.1, with the observed value quoted as about 0.3.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann equation, critical density

$$
\Omega = \rho/\rho_c
$$

$$
\rho_c = \frac{3}{8\pi}\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2
$$

**Notes:** The quoted 0.3 is the matter share only; including dark energy the total is consistent with 1, which is what the flatness argument really concerns.

**Where:** GA p.445 (pdf 462); GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

### critical density

*physical-quantity · mention* · also: rho_c

The energy density that makes the Friedmann equation consistent with zero spatial curvature for a given expansion rate: rho_c = 3H^2/(8 pi) in geometrized units.

**How introduced:** Used implicitly in the definition of Omega in the opening; defined earlier in Chapter 17.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann equation, Hubble parameter

$$
\rho_c = \frac{3H^2}{8\pi}
$$

**Where:** GA p.445 (pdf 462)

### fine-tuning of initial conditions

*historical-idea · mention* · also: finely tuned Universe

The worry that a present-day property of the Universe (here Omega near 1) follows only if parameters at early times took values specified to absurd precision, suggesting a missing dynamical explanation.

**How introduced:** Invoked to make the flatness problem feel problematic, with a margin note pointing to the philosophical literature on fine tuning.

**Prerequisites:** flatness problem

**Notes:** Inflation replaces a tuned initial value with a dynamical attractor towards Omega = 1.

**Where:** GA p.445 (pdf 462)

### horizon problem

*phenomenon · core* · also: uniformity problem, homogeneity problem

The puzzle that regions of the early Universe which, in a decelerating hot Big-Bang model, had no causal contact before the time we observe them (for instance at CMB emission) nevertheless have the same temperature to about one part in 10^5.

**How introduced:** Stated in words after noting the surprising homogeneity of the early Universe, then shown on a Penrose diagram in which the past light cones of two events q and r, seen by one observer in different directions, reach the Big Bang without overlapping.

**Prerequisites:** causal connection, Penrose diagram, cosmic microwave background isotropy

**Notes:** Book caveat (citing Penrose 2004): inflation gives overlapping pasts but events on the transition surface may share only a small part of their history.

**Where:** GA p.445 (pdf 462); GA p.446 (pdf 463)

### causal connection

*definition · introduced* · also: causally connected regions, particle horizon (implicit)

Two regions are causally connected if light (hence any signal) has had time since the Big Bang to pass between them; only then could they interact or thermalize. In the book's Penrose-diagram usage for events q and r, the test is whether their past light cones overlap, i.e. whether they share any history.

**How introduced:** Margin note 4 defines the phrase in terms of photons having had time to travel between regions, and the Penrose diagram visualizes overlapping or disjoint past cones.

**Prerequisites:** Penrose diagram

**Where:** GA p.446 (pdf 463)

### Penrose diagram

*technique · revisited* · also: conformal diagram

A conformally compactified spacetime diagram in which radial light rays run at 45 degrees and infinities or singularities appear as finite boundaries, so causal relations (which events can influence which) can be read off geometrically.

**How introduced:** Reused from Chapter 19 without re-derivation; the Big Bang is a horizontal line and the effect of inflation is shown by lowering it.

**Notes:** Lowering the Big-Bang line corresponds to adding a large interval of conformal time before the phase transition.

**Where:** GA p.446 (pdf 463)

### cosmic microwave background isotropy

*experiment-or-observation · mention* · also: CMB uniformity, COBE blackbody spectrum

The observation that the CMB has a nearly perfect blackbody spectrum at T = 2.73 K with temperature variations across the sky of only about one part in 10^5.

**How introduced:** Margin note 2 on the COBE satellite, attached to the claim that the early Universe is surprisingly homogeneous.

**Where:** GA p.445 (pdf 462)

### phase transitions in the early Universe

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: GUT phase transition, electroweak symmetry-breaking transition, quark confinement transition, thermal history

The sequence of symmetry-changing transitions that a cooling, expanding Universe is thought to pass through: separation of the strong from the electroweak interaction at around 10^15 GeV, electroweak symmetry breaking by the Higgs field near 100 GeV, and quark confinement near 100 MeV.

**How introduced:** Example 41.1 lists times, temperatures and energies for each transition as a rough picture, explicitly as the phenomena on which the chapter is built.

**Prerequisites:** spontaneous symmetry breaking

$$
E \approx k_B T,\quad 1\ \mathrm{GeV} \leftrightarrow 1.16\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{K}
$$

**Notes:** The conversion formula is our addition; with it, 10^15 GeV corresponds to about 10^28 K, a factor of ten above the value printed in Example 41.1.

**Where:** GA p.445 (pdf 462); GA p.446 (pdf 463)

### inflation

*solution-or-model · core* · also: cosmic inflation, inflationary epoch

A proposed early phase in which the scale factor accelerates (addot > 0), typically growing nearly exponentially, driven by the potential energy of a slowly evolving scalar field; it drives spatial curvature to negligible values and gives distant regions a shared causal past before a reheating phase starts the standard hot Big Bang.

**How introduced:** Offered as the remedy for the flatness and horizon problems, illustrated by a modified Penrose diagram, then modelled as a symmetry-breaking scalar field in a flat Robertson-Walker spacetime.

**Prerequisites:** flatness problem, horizon problem, accelerating expansion, inflaton field, slow-roll approximation

$$
a(t) \propto \exp\left[\left(\frac{8\pi U_0}{3}\right)^{1/2} t\right]
$$

$$
\dot\phi^2 < U(\phi)
$$

**Notes:** Attributed in margin note 5 to Alan Guth. The chapter does not discuss the number of e-folds needed or the generation of density perturbations.

**Where:** GA p.446 (pdf 463); GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467); GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468); GA §41.3 p.452 (pdf 469)

### accelerating expansion

*phenomenon · developed* · also: positive addot, cosmic acceleration

Expansion in which the second time derivative of the scale factor is positive, so recession speeds of comoving objects grow; in a Friedmann universe without Lambda this needs p < -rho/3.

**How introduced:** Contrasted, in a bracketed remark, with the decelerating a(t) of most earlier model universes; quantified in §41.3 via the acceleration equation.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann equation

$$
\frac{\ddot a}{a} = -4\pi\left(p + \frac{\rho}{3}\right)
$$

**Where:** GA p.446 (pdf 463); GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

### spontaneous symmetry breaking

*phenomenon · core* · also: broken symmetry, symmetry lowering

The situation in which the laws (Hamiltonian, Lagrangian or free energy) are invariant under a symmetry but the lowest-energy state is not, so the system settles into one of several equivalent ground states related by that symmetry; which one is chosen is fixed by tiny perturbations, not by the laws.

**How introduced:** Built from a magnet: disordered spins keep zero magnetization under a global flip, aligned spins do not. The puzzle of how the system 'chooses' is posed and echoed by the buckling Euler strut, then formalized with Landau's free energy.

**Prerequisites:** global symmetry, order parameter

$$
F(M) = F(-M),\quad \text{but ground state } M = \pm M_0 \neq 0
$$

**Notes:** Margin note 9 stresses that in practice a very small fluctuation tips the system.

**Where:** GA §41.1 p.446 (pdf 463); GA §41.1 p.447 (pdf 464); GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465)

### global symmetry

*definition · introduced*

A symmetry transformation applied identically everywhere, for example rotating every spin through the same angle or sending phi(x) to -phi(x) at all points, as opposed to a local (gauge) transformation that may vary from point to point.

**How introduced:** Named after the 180-degree flip of all spins in the magnet, and later for the phi to -phi invariance of the phi^4 potential.

$$
\phi(x) \to -\phi(x)
$$

$$
M \to -M
$$

**Notes:** Local symmetries are the subject of Chapter 44 on gauge fields.

**Where:** GA §41.1 p.447 (pdf 464); GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465)

### Landau theory of phase transitions

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: Landau free energy, Landau expansion

A phenomenological description of a continuous phase transition in which the free energy near the transition is expanded in powers of an order parameter consistent with the system's symmetry, F = F_0 + a M^2 + b M^4, with a changing sign at the critical temperature; minimizing F gives the equilibrium state on both sides of T_c.

**How introduced:** Presented as Landau's way of ignoring microscopic detail: equilibrium balances low energy and high entropy, so minimize F = U - TS written as a power series in M, then take a = a_0(T - T_c).

**Prerequisites:** free energy minimization, order parameter, critical temperature

$$
F = F_0 + aM^2 + bM^4 + \dots
$$

$$
a = a_0(T - T_c)
$$

$$
M_0^2 = -\frac{a}{2b}
$$

**Where:** GA §41.1 p.446 (pdf 463); GA §41.1 p.447 (pdf 464); GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465)

### order parameter

*definition · developed*

A macroscopic variable that distinguishes the two phases: its equilibrium (thermal) average is zero on the symmetric, high-temperature side of T_c and takes a non-zero value once the symmetry is broken below T_c. For a ferromagnet it is the magnetization; for a scalar field theory it is the field value phi.

**How introduced:** Defined while setting up Landau's free energy for the magnet, then reassigned to the equilibrium value of phi(x) when moving to field theory.

**Prerequisites:** spontaneous symmetry breaking

**Where:** GA §41.1 p.447 (pdf 464); GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465)

### critical temperature

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: T_c, transition temperature

The temperature at which a continuous phase transition occurs; above it the symmetric state is stable, below it the order parameter becomes non-zero. In Landau theory it is where the quadratic coefficient a changes sign.

**How introduced:** Introduced with the experimental fact that cooling a magnet through T_c produces spontaneous magnetization; later mu^2(T) of the inflaton changes sign at T_c.

$$
a = a_0(T - T_c)
$$

**Where:** GA §41.1 p.447 (pdf 464); GA §41.2 p.449 (pdf 466)

### free energy minimization

*principle · introduced* · also: Helmholtz free energy F = U - TS

At fixed temperature a thermodynamic system in equilibrium minimizes the Helmholtz free energy F = U - TS, which balances the drive to low internal energy against the drive to high entropy.

**How introduced:** Given as Landau's motivation for studying F rather than energy alone.

$$
F = U - TS
$$

**Notes:** U here is internal energy, not the field potential U(phi) used later.

**Where:** GA §41.1 p.447 (pdf 464)

### phi^4 scalar field theory

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: phi-fourth theory, quartic scalar potential, double-well potential

A real scalar field with Lagrangian density -(1/2)(partial phi)^2 - U(phi) and potential U = (mu^2/2) phi^2 + (lambda/4!) phi^4 with lambda > 0; it has the global symmetry phi to -phi, a single vacuum at phi = 0 for a positive quadratic coefficient, and two degenerate vacua at phi_0 = ±(6 mu^2/lambda)^{1/2} when the quadratic term is negative.

**How introduced:** Obtained by transplanting the Landau free-energy shape into the Lagrangian of Chapter 40, splitting off all non-derivative terms as the potential energy density.

**Prerequisites:** Landau theory of phase transitions, vacuum of a field theory

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\frac12(\partial_\mu\phi)^2 - U(\phi)
$$

$$
U(\phi) = \frac{\mu^2}{2}\phi^2 + \frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4
$$

$$
\phi_0 = \pm\left(\frac{6\mu^2}{\lambda}\right)^{1/2}
$$

**Notes:** With the sign flipped the potential at its minima is U = -3 mu^4/(2 lambda) < 0; a constant must be added if the true vacuum is to have zero energy.

**Where:** GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465); GA §41.2 p.449 (pdf 466)

### vacuum of a field theory

*definition · introduced* · also: ground state, vacuum state, vacua

The lowest-energy, spatially uniform configuration of a field, located at a minimum of the potential U(phi); a potential with several degenerate minima has several possible vacua.

**How introduced:** Named when the positive-mu^2 potential has its minimum at phi = 0, and pluralized when the sign flip produces two minima.

**Prerequisites:** phi^4 scalar field theory

$$
U(\phi_0) = U_0
$$

**Where:** GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465)

### Higgs mechanism

*phenomenon · mention* · also: electroweak symmetry breaking

The process in the Standard Model by which a scalar (Higgs) field acquires a non-zero vacuum value as the Universe cools, breaking electroweak symmetry, separating electromagnetism from the weak force and giving mass to the W and Z bosons and to the quarks and charged leptons.

**How introduced:** Mentioned in Example 41.1 as the electroweak transition and after eqn 41.7 as the real-world instance of scalar symmetry breaking, which justifies studying scalar fields in cosmology.

**Prerequisites:** spontaneous symmetry breaking

**Notes:** Margin note 12 warns that the inflating field is not the Higgs even though it is sometimes given that name.

**Where:** GA p.446 (pdf 463); GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465)

### inflaton field

*physical-quantity · core* · also: scalar field phi driving inflation, (misleadingly) Higgs field

The hypothetical spatially uniform scalar field whose potential energy dominates the early Universe and drives inflation as it slowly evolves towards a potential minimum.

**How introduced:** Introduced in §41.2 as a phi^4 field present before the GUT transition, with temperature-dependent mass parameter; the book does not use the word inflaton.

**Prerequisites:** phi^4 scalar field theory, temperature-dependent effective potential

$$
\nabla\phi = 0,\quad \phi = \phi(t)
$$

**Where:** GA §41.2 p.449 (pdf 466); GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

### temperature-dependent effective potential

*definition · introduced* · also: mu^2(T), thermal effective potential

A potential for the field whose parameters depend on the ambient temperature, here through a quadratic coefficient mu^2(T) that is positive above T_c (symmetric minimum at phi = 0) and negative below it (broken-symmetry minima).

**How introduced:** Asserted in eqn 41.8 with the statement that the cosmic temperature T(t) makes mu^2 change sign in time; no derivation from finite-temperature field theory is given.

**Prerequisites:** phi^4 scalar field theory, critical temperature

$$
U = \frac{\mu^2(T)}{2}\phi^2 + \frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4
$$

**Where:** GA §41.2 p.449 (pdf 466)

### second covariant derivative of a scalar

*operation · developed* · also: phi_{;mu nu}, covariant d'Alembertian of a scalar

For a scalar the first covariant derivative is just the gradient; differentiating that gradient covariantly adds one connection term (the ordinary second derivative minus Gamma contracted with the gradient), and contracting the result with the inverse metric gives the wave operator of curved spacetime.

**How introduced:** Computed step by step in Example 41.3 for the flat Robertson-Walker metric, keeping only diagonal components because they are all the trace needs.

$$
\phi_{;\mu\nu} = \partial_\nu\partial_\mu\phi - \Gamma^\sigma_{\nu\mu}\partial_\sigma\phi
$$

**Where:** GA §41.2 p.449 (pdf 466)

### scalar field equation in an expanding universe

*law · core* · also: damped Klein-Gordon equation, inflaton equation of motion

For a spatially uniform scalar field in a Robertson-Walker spacetime the covariant field equation g^{mu nu} phi_{;mu nu} = dU/dphi becomes phi-double-dot + 3H phi-dot + dU/dphi = 0 with H = adot/a.

**How introduced:** Derived in Example 41.3 from the Chapter 40 covariant Euler-Lagrange equation, then boxed as one of the two key equations of inflation.

**Prerequisites:** second covariant derivative of a scalar, phi^4 scalar field theory, Robertson-Walker spatial curvature index

$$
\ddot\phi + 3\frac{\dot a}{a}\dot\phi + \frac{\partial U}{\partial\phi} = 0
$$

**Notes:** For a uniform field the result holds for any k, which is the point of Exercise 41.1.

**Where:** GA §41.2 p.449 (pdf 466); GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

### Hubble friction

*phenomenon · core* · also: Hubble drag, 3H damping term

The term 3H phi-dot in the scalar field equation, which acts like velocity-proportional friction on the field's motion in its potential; physically it reflects the dilution of the field's kinetic energy by cosmic expansion.

**How introduced:** Announced before Example 41.3 as a frictional term fixed by the expansion rate, and named an effective drag factor after eqn 41.14.

**Prerequisites:** scalar field equation in an expanding universe, Hubble parameter

$$
3H = 3\frac{\dot a}{a}
$$

**Notes:** Large H during inflation is what allows slow roll on a shallow slope; the same term damps the oscillations at the end.

**Where:** GA §41.2 p.449 (pdf 466); GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

### Hubble parameter

*physical-quantity · mention* · also: H, expansion rate

The fractional expansion rate H = adot/a of the scale factor.

**How introduced:** Appears as the coefficient of the friction term and as the constant rate H^2 = Lambda/3 in the Lambda-driven solutions.

$$
H = \dot a/a
$$

$$
H^2 = \Lambda/3
$$

**Where:** GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467); GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

### energy-momentum tensor of a scalar field

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: scalar stress-energy tensor

The symmetric tensor that a scalar field with kinetic term and potential U contributes as the source of Einstein's equation: the outer product of the field gradient with itself, minus the metric times the Lagrangian-like combination of half the squared gradient plus U (see formula).

**How introduced:** Quoted (from Chapter 40) as the theory's energy-momentum tensor, then evaluated for a uniform field.

**Prerequisites:** phi^4 scalar field theory

$$
T_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\phi\,\partial_\nu\phi - g_{\mu\nu}\left[\tfrac12 g^{\alpha\beta}\partial_\alpha\phi\,\partial_\beta\phi + U(\phi)\right]
$$

**Where:** GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

### energy density and pressure of a homogeneous scalar field

*physical-quantity · core* · also: scalar field as a perfect fluid, kinetic and potential contributions to rho and p

For a spatially uniform field seen by a comoving observer, rho = phi-dot^2/2 + U and p = phi-dot^2/2 - U: kinetic energy adds to both, potential energy adds to density but subtracts from pressure.

**How introduced:** Read off the time-time and space-space components of T_{mu nu} in the observer's local frame, and compared with the perfect fluid form in margin note 15.

**Prerequisites:** energy-momentum tensor of a scalar field

$$
\rho = \tfrac12\dot\phi^2 + U(\phi)
$$

$$
p = \tfrac12\dot\phi^2 - U(\phi)
$$

**Notes:** Equation-of-state parameter w = p/rho ranges from -1 (potential dominated) to +1 (kinetic dominated); this framing is ours, not the book's.

**Where:** GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467); GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

### vacuum energy as an effective cosmological constant

*principle · core* · also: false-vacuum energy, p = -rho equation of state, Lambda = 8 pi U

A source with p = -rho has an energy-momentum tensor proportional to the metric and is indistinguishable from a cosmological constant; a scalar field frozen at potential value U therefore acts as Lambda = 8 pi U (G = 1) and drives de Sitter-like expansion.

**How introduced:** Deduced by setting phi-dot = 0 in the field's rho and p and comparing with the fluid tensor, then linked to the exponential growth of Universe 1 in Chapter 15.

**Prerequisites:** energy density and pressure of a homogeneous scalar field

$$
\rho = -p
$$

$$
\Lambda = 8\pi U(\phi)
$$

$$
T_{\mu\nu} = (\rho + p)u_\mu u_\nu + p g_{\mu\nu} \to -\rho\, g_{\mu\nu}
$$

**Where:** GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

### Friedmann equation

*law · revisited* · also: first Friedmann equation, expansion equation

The time-time Einstein equation for a Robertson-Walker universe, (adot/a)^2 + k/a^2 = (8 pi/3) rho + Lambda/3 (G = c = 1); with the acceleration equation it governs a(t).

**How introduced:** Used in three guises: rearranged as k/adot^2 = Omega - 1 in the opening, with k = 0 and the scalar rho in §41.2, and as the acceleration equation in §41.3.

**Prerequisites:** Robertson-Walker spatial curvature index

$$
\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3}\left[\tfrac12\dot\phi^2 + U(\phi)\right]
$$

$$
\frac{\ddot a}{a} = -4\pi\left(p + \frac{\rho}{3}\right)
$$

**Where:** GA p.445 (pdf 462); GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467); GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

### slow-roll approximation

*technique · core* · also: slow rolling

The assumption that, on a sufficiently flat potential with strong Hubble friction, the field's acceleration is negligible (3H phi-dot ≈ -dU/dphi) and its kinetic energy is small compared with U, so that the expansion rate is set almost entirely by the nearly constant potential energy.

**How introduced:** Introduced to simplify the coupled equations: a shallow potential below T_c (Fig. 41.7) lets the field creep towards its minimum, making phi-double-dot and phi-dot small.

**Prerequisites:** scalar field equation in an expanding universe, Hubble friction, Friedmann equation

$$
3\frac{\dot a}{a}\dot\phi \approx -\frac{\partial U}{\partial\phi}
$$

$$
\frac{\dot a}{a} \approx \left(\frac{8\pi U_0}{3}\right)^{1/2}
$$

**Notes:** The standard slow-roll parameters epsilon and eta, which quantify 'shallow', are not introduced.

**Where:** GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467); GA p.452 (pdf 469)

### exponential (de Sitter-like) expansion

*solution-or-model · revisited* · also: de Sitter expansion, Universe 1 behaviour

Growth of the scale factor as a(t) proportional to e^{Ht} with constant H, the solution of the Friedmann equation for a constant vacuum energy; for the inflaton H = (8 pi U_0/3)^{1/2}.

**How introduced:** Obtained by integrating the slow-roll Friedmann equation, and compared with the cosh/exp/sinh Lambda solutions of earlier chapters.

**Prerequisites:** vacuum energy as an effective cosmological constant, slow-roll approximation

$$
a(t) \propto \exp\left[\left(\frac{8\pi U_0}{3}\right)^{1/2}t\right]
$$

**Where:** GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467); GA §41.2 p.451 (pdf 468)

### Robertson-Walker spatial curvature index

*convention · revisited* · also: k = +1, 0, -1

The constant k in the Robertson-Walker metric labelling closed (k = +1), flat (k = 0) and open (k = -1) spatial slices.

**How introduced:** Recalled in the opening and in §41.3, where the three cases are compared under Lambda-driven expansion.

**Where:** GA p.445 (pdf 462); GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

### dilution of spatial curvature by inflation

*phenomenon · developed* · also: inflation drives Omega to 1, flattening by exponential expansion

During near-exponential expansion adot grows, so the curvature term k/adot^2 = Omega - 1 shrinks rapidly; whatever k is, the universe becomes observationally indistinguishable from flat.

**How introduced:** Argued in §41.3 by noting that the cosh (k = +1) and sinh (k = -1) Lambda solutions become exponentials at late times, just like the k = 0 case, and in margin note 16 by noting that curvature effects are too small to matter at the start of expansion.

**Prerequisites:** exponential (de Sitter-like) expansion, density parameter, Robertson-Walker spatial curvature index

$$
a(t) \propto \cosh Ht,\ e^{Ht},\ \sinh Ht
$$

$$
|\Omega - 1| = \frac{|k|}{\dot a^2} \propto e^{-2Ht}
$$

**Notes:** The second formula is our explicit version of the book's verbal argument, following from eqn 41.1 with a proportional to e^{Ht}.

**Where:** GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

### condition for accelerated expansion

*law · developed* · also: p < -rho/3, violation of the strong energy condition

In a Friedmann universe without a separate Lambda, addot > 0 requires rho + 3p < 0, i.e. p < -rho/3; for a homogeneous scalar field this becomes phi-dot^2 < U(phi), kinetic energy smaller than potential energy.

**How introduced:** Derived by inserting the scalar rho and p into the acceleration equation, then read as the criterion that decides when inflation stops.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann equation, energy density and pressure of a homogeneous scalar field

$$
p < -\rho/3
$$

$$
\dot\phi^2 < U(\phi)
$$

**Where:** GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

### end of inflation

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: graceful exit

The termination of accelerated expansion when the field's kinetic energy becomes comparable with its potential energy or the potential falls to (or below) zero, typically as the field reaches its minimum and oscillates.

**How introduced:** Obtained by reading the acceleration condition in reverse; margin note 17 adds that a field resting at a positive minimum would inflate forever.

**Prerequisites:** condition for accelerated expansion, Hubble friction

$$
\dot\phi^2 \gtrsim U(\phi)
$$

**Where:** GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

### reheating

*phenomenon · introduced*

The conversion of the inflaton's energy into ordinary particles and radiation through couplings to other fields during or after its final roll and oscillation, which repopulates the empty, cold post-inflationary universe and starts the standard hot Big-Bang evolution.

**How introduced:** Named in bold at the close of §41.3 as the practical way inflation ends, leaving a flat Universe of matter and radiation.

**Prerequisites:** end of inflation

**Where:** GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468); GA §41.3 p.452 (pdf 469)

### (41.1) Friedmann equation in curvature form · central · GA p.445 (pdf 462)

$$
\frac{k}{\dot a^2} = \Omega - 1
$$

The spatial curvature index is tied to the departure of the total density parameter from one; since adot falls in ordinary expansion, any non-zero k makes Omega drift away from 1.

**Symbols:** k curvature index; a scale factor; Omega = rho/rho_c

### (41.2) Landau free energy · central · GA §41.1 p.447 (pdf 464)

$$
F = F_0 + aM^2 + bM^4 + \dots
$$

Free energy near the transition as an even power series in the order parameter, respecting the M to -M symmetry.

**Symbols:** F free energy; M magnetization (order parameter); a, b coefficients (b > 0), possibly temperature dependent

### Temperature dependence of the Landau coefficient · supporting · GA §41.1 p.447 (pdf 464)

$$
a = a_0(T - T_c)
$$

The quadratic coefficient is positive above and negative below the critical temperature, producing the transition.

**Symbols:** a_0 positive constant; T temperature; T_c critical temperature

### One-dimensional spin Hamiltonian (margin note 8) · supporting · GA §41.1 p.447 (pdf 464)

$$
\hat H = -J\sum_i \hat S_i^z \hat S_{i+1}^z

Nearest-neighbour coupling that rewards parallel alignment but has no preference for up over down, showing the symmetry the ordered state breaks.

**Symbols:** J coupling constant; S_i^z spin operator of site i along z

\frac{\partial F}{\partial M} = 2aM + 4bM^3 = 0

Condition for equilibrium states of the magnet.

**Symbols:** as in (41.2)

### (41.4) Broken-symmetry magnetization · supporting · GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465)

$$
M_0^2 = -\frac{a}{2b}
$$

Location of the two minima below T_c (real because a < 0); M = 0 becomes a maximum.

**Symbols:** M_0 spontaneous magnetization

### (41.5) Scalar field Lagrangian with general potential · central · GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465)

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\frac12(\partial_\mu\phi)^2 - U(\phi)

Kinetic term plus all non-derivative terms collected into a potential energy density.

**Symbols:** (partial_mu phi)^2 = g^{mu nu} partial_mu phi partial_nu phi; U potential energy density

U(\phi) = \frac{\mu^2}{2}\phi^2 + \frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4

Field-theory analogue of the Landau free energy, symmetric under phi to -phi; single minimum at phi = 0 for positive mu^2.

**Symbols:** mu mass-like parameter; lambda > 0 self-coupling

### Stationary points of the inverted phi^4 potential (margin note 11) · derivation-step · GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465)

$$
0 = -\mu^2\phi + \frac{\lambda}{3!}\phi^3,\qquad \frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial\phi^2} = -\mu^2 + \frac{\lambda\phi^2}{2}
$$

With U = -mu^2 phi^2/2 + lambda phi^4/4!, stationary points lie at 0 and ±(6mu^2/lambda)^{1/2}; curvature is -mu^2 at the origin (maximum) and +2mu^2 at the two minima.

**Symbols:** as in (41.6)

### (41.7) Broken-symmetry vacua · central · GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465)

$$
\phi_0 = \pm\left(\frac{6\mu^2}{\lambda}\right)^{1/2}
$$

The two degenerate vacua after the sign of the quadratic term is reversed.

**Symbols:** phi_0 vacuum field value

### (41.8) phi^4 Lagrangian with temperature-dependent mass · central · GA §41.2 p.449 (pdf 466)

$$
\mathcal{L} = -\frac12(\partial_\mu\phi)^2 - \frac{\mu^2(T)}{2}\phi^2 - \frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4
$$

Model Lagrangian for the inflating field; mu^2(T) changes sign as the Universe cools below T_c.

**Symbols:** T(t) cosmic temperature

### (41.9) Covariant field equation for the phi^4 field (corrected) · supporting · GA §41.2 p.449 (pdf 466)

\phi_{;\mu\nu}g^{\mu\nu} = \frac{\partial U}{\partial\phi} = \mu^2(T)\,\phi + \frac{\lambda}{3!}\phi^3

Euler-Lagrange equation of (41.8) in curved spacetime.

**Symbols:** semicolons denote covariant derivatives

### (41.10) Scalar Lagrangian (Example 41.3) · derivation-step · GA §41.2 p.449 (pdf 466)

\mathcal{L} = -\frac12(\partial_\mu\phi)^2 - U(\phi)

Starting point of the flat Robertson-Walker calculation; identical to (41.5).

### Flat Robertson-Walker inverse metric and connection (margin note 14) · supporting · GA §41.2 p.449 (pdf 466)

g^{tt} = -1,\quad g^{ii} = \frac{1}{a(t)^2},\qquad \Gamma^0_{ij} = a\dot a\,\delta_{ij},\quad \Gamma^i_{j0} = \frac{\dot a}{a}\delta^i_j

Ingredients for the covariant derivative in a spatially flat expanding universe.

General equation of motion for the scalar in curved spacetime.

Covector rule applied to the gradient of phi.

### (41.14) Scalar field equation with Hubble friction (partial-derivative form) · central · GA §41.2 p.449 (pdf 466)

\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial t^2} + 3\frac{1}{a}\frac{\partial a}{\partial t}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial U}{\partial\phi} = 0

Trace of (41.13) with spatial gradients dropped; expansion supplies a damping term.

T_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\phi\,\partial_\nu\phi - g_{\mu\nu}\left[\frac12 g^{\alpha\beta}\partial_\alpha\phi\,\partial_\beta\phi + U(\phi)\right]

Source term the field contributes to Einstein's equation.

\rho = \frac12\dot\phi^2 + U(\phi)

Kinetic plus potential energy of a uniform field.

p = \frac12\dot\phi^2 - U(\phi)

Kinetic minus potential energy; negative when the potential dominates.

### (41.18) Perfect-fluid energy-momentum tensor (margin note 15) · supporting · GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

T_{\mu\nu} = (\rho + p)u_\mu u_\nu + p\,g_{\mu\nu}

Comparison form: with p = -rho it reduces to -rho g_{mu nu}, a cosmological-constant term.

**Symbols:** u_mu fluid four-velocity

### Effective cosmological constant of a frozen field · central · GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

$$
\dot\phi = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \rho = -p,\qquad \Lambda = 8\pi U(\phi)
$$

A field not changing in time mimics vacuum energy and can drive de Sitter-type expansion.

**Symbols:** Lambda cosmological constant (G = 1)

### (41.19) Flat Friedmann equation · supporting · GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

$$
\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi\rho}{3}
$$

Expansion rate set by energy density when k = 0 and no separate Lambda.

### (41.20) Friedmann equation for a scalar-field universe · supporting · GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

$$
\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3}\left[\frac12\dot\phi^2 + U(\phi)\right]
$$

Closes the system by making the expansion rate depend on the field's energy.

### (41.21) Damped inflaton equation (key equation i) · central · GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

$$
\ddot\phi + 3\frac{\dot a}{a}\dot\phi + \frac{\partial U}{\partial\phi} = 0

The field rolls in its potential subject to Hubble friction.

### (41.22) Expansion equation (key equation ii, corrected) · central · GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

$$
\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3}\left(\frac12\dot\phi^2 + U\right)

Restatement of (41.20); together with (41.21) it forms the coupled inflation system.

### Slow-roll field equation · derivation-step · GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

$$
3\frac{\dot a}{a}\dot\phi = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial\phi}
$$

With acceleration neglected, friction balances the potential slope, so the field moves at terminal velocity.

### (41.23) Slow-roll expansion rate · central · GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

$$
\frac{\dot a}{a} \approx \left(\frac{8\pi U_0}{3}\right)^{1/2}
$$

With small phi-dot and nearly constant U, the Hubble rate is constant.

**Symbols:** U_0 near-constant value of the potential during the roll

### (41.24) Exponential inflation · central · GA §41.2 p.451 (pdf 468)

$$
a(t) \propto \exp\left[\left(\frac{8\pi U_0}{3}\right)^{1/2} t\right]
$$

The scale factor grows exponentially while slow roll holds.

### (41.25) Lambda-driven expansion for each curvature · central · GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

$$
a(t) \propto \begin{cases} \cosh Ht & (k = +1) \\ e^{Ht} & (k = 0) \\ \sinh Ht & (k = -1) \end{cases},\qquad H^2 = \Lambda/3
$$

All three approach exponential growth at late times, so curvature becomes irrelevant.

**Symbols:** H constant expansion rate

### (41.26) Acceleration equation · central · GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

$$
\frac{\ddot a}{a} = -4\pi\left(p + \frac{\rho}{3}\right)
$$

Pressure as well as density decelerates expansion; acceleration needs p < -rho/3.

### (41.27) Inflation condition for a scalar field · central · GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

$$
\dot\phi^2 < U(\phi)
$$

Acceleration continues only while the field's kinetic term is below its potential energy; violating it ends inflation.

### Fig. 41.1 · penrose-diagram · GA p.446 (pdf 463)

`book-sources/gifted-amateur/pages/page-463/img-287.jpeg`

**Caption (paraphrased):** Two conformal diagrams: (a) without inflation the pasts of q and r, and of s and t on the phase-transition line, never meet; (b) inflation moves the Big Bang far down so these pasts overlap.

**What it shows:** Two stacked line drawings. Panel (a): a short, wide triangle whose apex is event p; its two 45-degree sides are p's past light cone. Event q sits on the left side and r on the right side, part-way down; the sides end at s (left) and t (right) on a horizontal dotted line marked P.T. A solid horizontal line marked B.B. lies only a hair below the dotted line, so the triangle has almost no room beneath the transition. Panel (b): the same p, q, r, s, t and dotted P.T. line, but the solid B.B. line is moved far down. From s and t long 45-degree lines run down and outward to the new base, making a much larger triangle; from q and r further 45-degree lines descend and cross each other in an X below the P.T. line before reaching B.B., so the past cones of q and r (and of s and t) now share a region. ## Worked examples

### Example 41.1 · intro · GA p.445 (pdf 462)

**Problem:** Give a rough chronology of the very early Universe in terms of the phase transitions it undergoes as it cools.

**Method:** List successive epochs with cosmic time, temperature and energy scale: unified strong-electroweak interaction near 10^-43 s (10^19 GeV); GUT separation near 10^-36 s (10^15 GeV); Higgs-driven electroweak breaking near 10^-11 s (100 GeV); confinement near 10^-5 s (100 MeV). The box begins on printed p.445 and finishes on p.446.

**Key insight:** Cosmic history can be read as a cooling curve punctuated by symmetry-breaking transitions, which is why condensed-matter ideas are relevant to cosmology.

**Result:** t ~ 1e-43 s, T ~ 1e32 K, E ~ 1e19 GeV; t ~ 1e-36 s, E ~ 1e15 GeV, T printed as 1e27 K; t ~ 1e-11 s, T ~ 1e15 K, E ~ 100 GeV; t ~ 1e-5 s, T ~ 1e12 K, E ~ 100 MeV

**Concepts:** phase transitions in the early Universe, Higgs mechanism, spontaneous symmetry breaking

### Example 41.2 · intro · GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465)

**Problem:** Locate the equilibrium magnetization below T_c for the Landau free energy F = F_0 + aM^2 + bM^4.

**Method:** Differentiate with respect to M, set to zero, and use a < 0, b > 0 to find real non-zero roots; note the root at M = 0 is now a maximum.

**Key insight:** Two equal-depth minima appear symmetrically, so the model predicts that a transition must pick one arbitrarily.

**Result:** M_0^2 = -a/(2b); minima at ±M_0, maximum at M = 0

**Concepts:** Landau theory of phase transitions, order parameter, spontaneous symmetry breaking

### Example · intro · GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465)

**Problem:** Margin note 11: find and classify the stationary points of U = -mu^2 phi^2/2 + lambda phi^4/4!.

**Method:** Set dU/dphi = -mu^2 phi + lambda phi^3/3! = 0 and evaluate d^2U/dphi^2 = -mu^2 + lambda phi^2/2 at each root.

**Key insight:** The origin has negative curvature (unstable), the two outer roots have positive curvature 2mu^2 (stable vacua); the same structure as Example 41.2.

**Result:** phi = 0 (maximum, U'' = -mu^2); phi_0 = ±(6mu^2/lambda)^{1/2} (minima, U'' = +2mu^2)

**Concepts:** phi^4 scalar field theory, vacuum of a field theory

### Example 41.3 · standard · GA §41.2 p.449 (pdf 466)

**Problem:** Derive the equation of motion for a spatially uniform scalar field with potential U in a spatially flat Robertson-Walker spacetime.

**Method:** Start from the covariant field equation (traced second derivative equals the slope of U). The first covariant derivative of a scalar is its gradient; the second adds a single connection term acting on that gradient. Only diagonal components matter under the diagonal inverse metric. With Gamma^0_{ii} = a adot the space-space components acquire -a adot phi-dot; drop spatial derivatives and contract with g^{tt} = -1 and g^{ii} = 1/a^2.

**Key insight:** Expansion enters the field equation only through a connection coefficient, and it appears as friction proportional to 3H.

**Result:** phi-double-dot + 3(adot/a) phi-dot + dU/dphi = 0

**Concepts:** second covariant derivative of a scalar, scalar field equation in an expanding universe, Hubble friction

### Example · standard · GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

**Problem:** Find the energy density and pressure of a spatially uniform scalar field and determine when it behaves like a cosmological constant.

**Method:** Evaluate T_{mu nu} of eqn 41.15 in the comoving observer's local orthonormal frame with only phi-dot non-zero: T_00 gives rho, T_ii gives p. Set phi-dot = 0 and compare with the perfect-fluid tensor.

**Key insight:** Potential energy contributes positive density but negative pressure; a static field has p = -rho, which is exactly vacuum energy.

**Result:** rho = phi-dot^2/2 + U, p = phi-dot^2/2 - U; frozen field gives Lambda = 8 pi U

**Concepts:** energy-momentum tensor of a scalar field, energy density and pressure of a homogeneous scalar field, vacuum energy as an effective cosmological constant

### Example · standard · GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

**Problem:** Solve the coupled field and Friedmann equations in the slow-roll limit.

**Method:** Neglect phi-double-dot so friction balances the potential slope; on a shallow potential near the transition U is approximately a constant U_0 and phi-dot is small, so the Friedmann equation gives a constant Hubble rate, which integrates to an exponential.

**Key insight:** A nearly constant potential energy acts as a temporary cosmological constant, so the Universe inflates exponentially for as long as the plateau lasts.

**Result:** adot/a ≈ (8 pi U_0/3)^{1/2}; a(t) proportional to exp[(8 pi U_0/3)^{1/2} t]

**Concepts:** slow-roll approximation, exponential (de Sitter-like) expansion, Friedmann equation

### Example · intro · GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

**Problem:** Find the condition on the scalar field for the expansion to accelerate.

**Method:** Require addot > 0 in addot/a = -4 pi (p + rho/3), giving p < -rho/3; substitute rho and p from eqns 41.16-41.17 and simplify: (2/3)phi-dot^2 < (2/3)U.

**Key insight:** Acceleration is a contest between kinetic and potential energy of the field, and the same inequality signals the end of inflation.

**Result:** phi-dot^2 < U(phi)

**Concepts:** condition for accelerated expansion, end of inflation


## Analogies and intuitions

### Up/down spins in a magnet above and below the Curie point → spontaneous symmetry breaking · strong · GA §41.1 p.446 (pdf 463)

The disordered and ordered spin arrays are each subjected to a global flip; only the ordered array changes, which defines what it means for a state to have lower symmetry than its Hamiltonian.

**Where it breaks down:** The magnet's order parameter is a thermal average over many spins in a lab system with a heat bath; the cosmic field is a single homogeneous classical field whose 'temperature dependence' is put in by hand.

**App idea:** Ising lattice demo with a flip-all button and magnetization gauge, linked to the Landau curve.

### Euler strut buckling under a load → spontaneous symmetry breaking · strong · GA §41.1 p.447 (pdf 464)

A weighted elastic column is perfectly symmetric about its axis yet buckles left or right, with nothing in the physics predicting which; a margin note adds that a tiny imperfection decides.

**Where it breaks down:** Purely classical and mechanical, with load (not temperature) as the control parameter; no vacuum energy or cosmological consequence.

**App idea:** Strut with a load slider whose energy landscape morphs from single to double well at the Euler load.

### Landau free energy of a magnet as the potential of a scalar field → phi^4 scalar field theory · strong · GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465)

The identical quartic shape lets every statement about minima, maxima and symmetry of F(M) be transferred to U(phi), with phi playing the order parameter.

**Where it breaks down:** F is a thermodynamic free energy minimized in equilibrium; U is a term in a Lagrangian whose dynamics include kinetic energy and, in cosmology, Hubble friction, so the field need not sit at the minimum.

**App idea:** Dual-label plot toggling between magnet and field vocabularies.

### A ball rolling down a potential with friction → slow-roll approximation · strong · GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

The field value is pictured as a ball rolling towards a minimum; expansion adds a drag term, and on a shallow slope the ball creeps (Fig. 41.7b).

**Where it breaks down:** The 'friction' is not dissipative in the usual sense (energy is diluted by expansion, and its coefficient depends on the field's own energy through H); the ball moves in field space, not physical space.

**App idea:** Ball-in-treacle simulation where the treacle viscosity is 3H computed from the ball's own energy.

### A static scalar field as a fluid with negative pressure (vacuum energy) → vacuum energy as an effective cosmological constant · useful · GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

Matching the field's rho and p to the perfect-fluid tensor shows that potential energy behaves like Lambda, connecting inflation to the already familiar de Sitter model.

**Where it breaks down:** Only exact when phi-dot = 0; a slowly rolling field has a slowly varying effective Lambda, which is what allows inflation to end.

**App idea:** Equation-of-state dial showing w = p/rho as the kinetic-to-potential ratio is varied, with the region w < -1/3 shaded as 'accelerating'.

### Inflating a balloon flattens its surface (implicit in the cosh/sinh argument) → dilution of spatial curvature by inflation · useful · GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

The book argues from late-time asymptotics of cosh and sinh; the standard balloon picture (not in the text) makes the same point geometrically: enormous growth makes a patch of any curved surface look flat.

**Where it breaks down:** The balloon is extrinsically embedded and suggests a centre; spatial curvature of an open universe has no balloon picture.

**App idea:** 3D sphere and saddle patches inflated by a slider while a fixed-size observer's triangle angle sum converges to 180 degrees.

### Alice's sudden growth after drinking from the bottle (epigraph) → inflation · weak · GA p.445 (pdf 462)

Sets a playful tone for dramatic, rapid enlargement before the physics begins.

**Where it breaks down:** Alice grows inside a fixed room; inflation is expansion of space itself with no container.


## Misconceptions addressed

### Inflation completely solves the horizon problem because all regions now share a causal past. · GA p.446 (pdf 463)

**Correction:** Inflation makes past light cones overlap, but events on the phase-transition surface may share only a small portion of their history, so uniformity is not automatically guaranteed.

**Why tempting:** The modified Penrose diagram visibly shows overlapping cones, which looks like a complete fix.

### The field that drives inflation is the Higgs field. · GA §41.2 p.449 (pdf 466)

**Correction:** The inflating field is a different, hypothetical scalar field; the Higgs mechanism is only an example that scalar symmetry breaking occurs in nature.

**Why tempting:** Both are scalar fields with symmetry-breaking potentials, and some texts loosely call the inflating field a Higgs field.

### When symmetry breaks, the underlying laws must have become asymmetric or something must favour one direction. · GA §41.1 p.447 (pdf 464)

**Correction:** The Hamiltonian (or Lagrangian) keeps its symmetry; only the ground state lacks it, and the choice among equivalent states is fixed by tiny fluctuations.

**Why tempting:** In everyday reasoning an asymmetric outcome suggests an asymmetric cause.

### Expansion of the Universe always slows down. · GA p.446 (pdf 463)

**Correction:** The matter- and radiation-dominated solutions decelerate, but a component with p < -rho/3, such as potential-dominated scalar energy, makes addot positive.

**Why tempting:** Gravity is attractive, and most model universes studied before this chapter decelerate.

### Pressure only pushes on walls and plays no role in gravity, so a negative pressure cannot be what makes the expansion speed up. · GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

**Correction:** In general relativity pressure is a source of gravity: addot/a = -4 pi (p + rho/3), so sufficiently negative pressure produces acceleration.

**Why tempting:** In Newtonian intuition pressure is a mechanical force on walls, not a source of gravity, and a uniform pressure exerts no net force.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Exponential expansion only happens in a spatially flat universe, so starting inflation from k = 0 assumes the answer. · GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

**Correction:** The Lambda-driven solutions for k = +1 and k = -1 (cosh and sinh) approach exponential growth too, so the flat outcome is an attractor regardless of initial curvature.

**Why tempting:** The de Sitter solution usually appears in flat slicing and the chapter's calculation assumes k = 0.

### Once the field reaches the bottom of its potential, inflation must stop. · GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

**Correction:** What matters is whether phi-dot^2 < U still holds; a field at rest at a minimum with positive U would keep inflating forever, so a successful exit needs the minimum to have (near) zero or negative energy and the energy to be transferred to other fields.

**Why tempting:** Rolling to the bottom sounds like the end of the story.

### Below T_c the symmetric point M = 0 (or phi = 0) is a metastable state. · GA §41.1 p.447 (pdf 464)

**Correction:** It is a local maximum, hence unstable: any perturbation sends the system to a minimum. 'Metastable' properly describes a local minimum that is higher than the global one.

**Why tempting:** The book uses the word metastable for this point, and in first-order transitions a metastable false vacuum does exist.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### A measured Omega of about 0.3 means the Universe is far from flat. · GA p.445 (pdf 462)

**Correction:** About 0.3 is the matter density parameter; adding dark energy gives a total consistent with 1, and spatial curvature is observed to be very small.

**Why tempting:** The chapter quotes Omega ≈ 0.3 directly beside the flatness relation without saying which components are included.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Inflation means regions moved apart faster than light through space, violating relativity. · GA p.446 (pdf 463)

**Correction:** Nothing travels through space faster than light; the metric expands, and regions that were once in causal contact are carried beyond each other's Hubble distance.

**Why tempting:** The chapter describes causally connected regions separating 'rapidly enough', which invites a picture of superluminal motion.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Looking in two opposite directions at the early Universe · GA p.446 (pdf 463)

**Setup:** An observer today at event p receives light from events q and r in opposite parts of the sky; trace each event's past light cone back to the Big Bang on a conformal diagram.

**Lesson:** Without inflation the cones never overlap, so the observed equality of conditions at q and r is unexplained; adding inflation makes the cones intersect.

**App idea:** Draggable sky-point pair on a conformal diagram with an inflation e-fold slider.

### Cooling a magnet and asking which way it points · GA §41.1 p.447 (pdf 464)

**Setup:** A magnet with up/down spins is cooled through T_c with a perfectly symmetric Hamiltonian; the reader is asked what decides whether it ends up magnetized up or down.

**Lesson:** Nothing in the laws decides; a tiny fluctuation selects one of two equivalent ground states, which is spontaneous symmetry breaking.

**App idea:** Repeated cooling runs with random seeds producing a histogram of final magnetization signs.

### Perturbing a system poised at the symmetric point · GA §41.1 p.447 (pdf 464)

**Setup:** Prepare the magnet (or field) exactly at M = 0 below T_c and give it the slightest nudge.

**Lesson:** The state slides into one of the new minima at ±M_0, showing the symmetric point is no longer stable.

**App idea:** Ball balanced on the W-shaped curve with a nudge button.

### Inflating a curved universe · GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

**Setup:** Start a Lambda-dominated universe with positive, zero or negative spatial curvature and let it expand for many Hubble times.

**Lesson:** The cosh and sinh solutions become indistinguishable from the exponential one, so Omega is driven to 1 whatever the start.

**App idea:** Three synchronized a(t) curves on a log scale with |Omega - 1| plotted underneath.


## Applications and observations

- **Uniformity of the cosmic microwave background** (observation, GA p.445 (pdf 462)): COBE's blackbody CMB with tiny anisotropies is the evidence that causally disconnected regions share the same temperature. Key numbers: T = 2.73 K; anisotropy about 1 part in 10^5
- **Observed density parameter** (observation, GA p.445 (pdf 462)): The chapter cites an observational estimate of Omega about 0.3 as not unity but not far from it. This matches the matter component; current data (Planck 2018, external) give total Omega consistent with 1 and curvature parameter Omega_k = 0.001 ± 0.002. Key numbers: Omega ≈ 0.3 (book, matter); |Omega_k| ≲ 0.002 (external, Planck 2018)
- **Thermal history of the early Universe** (numerical-estimate, GA p.445 (pdf 462)): Example 41.1 ties cosmic times to temperatures and energy scales of the GUT, electroweak and confinement transitions. Key numbers: 1e-43 s / 1e32 K / 1e19 GeV; 1e-36 s / 1e15 GeV (printed 1e27 K; E = k_B T gives about 1e28 K); 1e-11 s / 1e15 K / 100 GeV; 1e-5 s / 1e12 K / 100 MeV
- **Inflation's Hubble rate for a GUT-scale potential (our estimate)** (numerical-estimate, GA §41.2 p.451 (pdf 468)): Our own estimate, not in the book: restoring units, H = (8 pi U_0/3)^{1/2}/M_Pl with M_Pl = 1.22e19 GeV. Taking U_0^{1/4} = 1e15 GeV gives H ≈ 2e11 GeV ≈ 4e35 s^-1, so one e-fold lasts about 3e-36 s and 60 e-folds take roughly 2e-34 s. Key numbers: U_0^{1/4} = 1e15 GeV -> H ≈ 4e35 s^-1; 60 e-folds in ~2e-34 s (own estimate)
- **How much flattening inflation provides (our estimate)** (numerical-estimate, GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)): Our own estimate from eqn 41.1: with a proportional to e^{Ht}, |Omega - 1| falls as e^{-2N} after N e-folds. Sixty e-folds suppress an order-one initial curvature by about e^{-120} ≈ 1e-52, enough to offset its later growth during radiation and matter domination. Key numbers: N = 60 -> suppression ~1e-52 (own estimate)

## Historical notes

- **Lev Landau:** Developed a general phenomenological theory of continuous phase transitions based on expanding the free energy in an order parameter. — Provides the conceptual template (symmetric laws, broken-symmetry ground state, double-well energy) that the chapter carries over to scalar fields and inflation. (GA §41.1 p.446 (pdf 463))
- **Lev Landau (1932):** Worked out, independently of Chandrasekhar and in 1932, the upper mass limit for a white dwarf supported by degeneracy pressure, as recorded in the biographical margin note. — Shows Landau's direct contributions to relativistic astrophysics beyond condensed matter. (GA §41.1 p.446 (pdf 463))
- **Alan Guth:** Proposed inflation in its original form, studying a scalar field in a universe with flat spatial sections, which is also the setting the chapter adopts. — Origin of the inflationary paradigm; related earlier and parallel work by Starobinsky, Linde, Albrecht and Steinhardt is not mentioned in the chapter. (GA p.446 (pdf 463))
- **History:** The COBE satellite measured a near-perfect blackbody CMB spectrum at 2.73 K with anisotropies only at the 10^-5 level. — The observational basis for the extreme uniformity that defines the horizon problem. (GA p.445 (pdf 462))
- **Roger Penrose (2004):** Criticized the claim that inflation fully resolves the horizon problem (cited as Penrose 2004). — The authors present inflation as dominant but contested, preparing the more cautious status discussion of Chapter 49. (GA p.446 (pdf 463))

## Notation and conventions

- **Metric signature:** (-,+,+,+): the flat Robertson-Walker inverse metric has g^{tt} = -1 and g^{ii} = 1/a^2, and the scalar Lagrangian carries -(1/2)(partial phi)^2. — In (+,-,-,-) texts the kinetic term appears with a plus sign. (GA §41.2 p.449 (pdf 466))
- **Units:** Geometrized units G = c = 1, so the Friedmann equation reads (adot/a)^2 = 8 pi rho/3 and a frozen field gives Lambda = 8 pi U. — Restore G/c^2 or a Planck mass when making numerical estimates. (GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467))
- **Potential symbol:** The scalar potential energy density is written U(phi), and U_0 is used for the (near-constant) potential value during and at the end of the roll. — Most cosmology literature writes V(phi). U is also used for internal energy in F = U - TS on p.447, a symbol clash. (GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465))
- **Landau coefficients versus scale factor:** a and b are the Landau expansion coefficients in §41.1; from §41.2 onward a(t) is the cosmological scale factor. — Clash of symbols; keep them visually distinct when teaching. (GA §41.1 p.447 (pdf 464))
- **Quartic coupling normalization:** The self-interaction is written lambda phi^4/4! so that dU/dphi contains lambda phi^3/3!. — Other texts use lambda phi^4/4, changing the numerical factor in phi_0. (GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465))
- **Sign of the mass parameter:** The text keeps mu^2 positive and flips the sign in front of the quadratic term to break symmetry, while the Fig. 41.6 caption says 'mu^2 < 0' yet still writes minima at ±(6mu^2/lambda)^{1/2}. — Pick one convention (e.g. U = -m^2 phi^2/2 + ... with m^2 > 0) when teaching. (GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465))
- **Covariant derivative notation:** Semicolons denote covariant derivatives (phi_{;mu nu}), commas or partial symbols denote ordinary derivatives; for a scalar phi_{;mu} = partial_mu phi. (GA §41.2 p.449 (pdf 466))
- **Shorthand for the kinetic term:** (partial_mu phi)^2 means g^{mu nu} partial_mu phi partial_nu phi. (GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465))
- **Density parameter:** Omega = rho/rho_c denotes the total density ratio; k/adot^2 = Omega - 1. — The book does not subscript Omega by component here. (GA p.445 (pdf 462))
- **Hubble rate:** H = adot/a; in the Lambda solutions H^2 = Lambda/3. (GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468))

## Teaching gems

### Define 'the state has less symmetry than the law' by physically applying the symmetry operation to two spin configurations and checking whether the observable changes. · GA §41.1 p.446 (pdf 463)

**Why it works:** It turns an abstract group-theoretic statement into a before/after comparison a novice can verify by eye.

**App idea:** Spin lattice with a 'flip all' button and a magnetization gauge; at high T the gauge sits at zero before and after, at low T it swings from +M to -M.

### Use the Euler strut to show that symmetry breaking needs no thermodynamics or quantum theory, just a symmetric system past an instability threshold. · GA §41.1 p.447 (pdf 464)

**Why it works:** A tangible mechanical object removes the mystery and prepares the double-well picture before any equation.

**App idea:** Load slider on a 3D elastic column with linked energy landscape that bifurcates at the Euler load.

### Relabel the Landau free energy F(M) as the field potential U(phi), so the magnet's double well becomes the vacuum structure of phi^4 theory. · GA §41.1 p.448 (pdf 465)

**Why it works:** One picture serves both systems; learners carry intuition about minima and stability straight into field theory.

**App idea:** Morphing curve whose axis labels and coefficient names toggle between magnet and field language while a temperature slider drives the sign change.

### Draw the horizon problem and its inflationary fix as two Penrose diagrams differing only in how far down the Big Bang lies. · GA p.446 (pdf 463)

**Why it works:** Causal overlap becomes a visible geometric fact, and the caveat about s and t is visible in the same picture.

**App idea:** Conformal diagram with an e-fold slider that extends the bottom edge and shades overlapping past light cones.

### Read the field's energy density and pressure straight off T_{mu nu}; with phi-dot = 0 you get p = -rho, so a stuck scalar field is a cosmological constant. · GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

**Why it works:** Connects an unfamiliar field model to the de Sitter universe the reader already knows, in two lines of algebra.

**App idea:** Bar chart of kinetic and potential energy with derived rho, p and w = p/rho, and a de Sitter badge that appears when w approaches -1.

### Show that expansion enters the field equation as friction 3H phi-dot, so inflation is a ball rolling in treacle whose thickness is set by the ball's own energy. · GA §41.2 p.450 (pdf 467)

**Why it works:** Damped motion is familiar from mechanics, and the self-consistency (H depends on rho) explains why slow roll is self-sustaining.

**App idea:** Side-by-side: ball on potential and phase-space (phi, phi-dot) trajectory flowing onto the slow-roll attractor, with H shown as a changing viscosity.

### Turn the acceleration equation into the single inequality phi-dot^2 < U, which both defines inflation and tells you when it ends. · GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

**Why it works:** A compact criterion learners can monitor in a simulation; it makes the exit mechanism a consequence rather than an add-on.

**App idea:** Traffic-light indicator on the inflation simulator that turns red when kinetic energy squared exceeds U, with an e-fold counter that freezes.

### Prove flattening by recalling that cosh Ht and sinh Ht both become e^{Ht}/2 at late times, so all curvature cases converge on the flat solution. · GA §41.3 p.451 (pdf 468)

**Why it works:** Reuses exact solutions the learner has already met, so the claim is checkable without new calculation.

**App idea:** Log-scale plot of three a(t) curves with a companion plot of |Omega - 1| = |k|/adot^2 plunging exponentially.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Printed eqn 41.9 writes the potential derivative as mu^2(T) phi^2 + (lambda/3!) phi^3; the first term should be mu^2(T) phi.** — Students differentiating U themselves get a mismatch and may doubt their calculus or the field equation. *Suggestion:* Use dU/dphi = mu^2(T) phi + lambda phi^3/3! and point out the slip.
- **Printed eqn 41.22 has (1/2) phi-dot instead of (1/2) phi-dot^2, contradicting eqn 41.20 which it restates.** — Dimensional inconsistency and confusion when coding the coupled equations. *Suggestion:* Use eqn 41.20; flag 41.22 as a typo.
- **In the reading copy the slow-roll sentence says phi is negligible; the printed page (rendered) says phi-double-dot is negligible.** — Anyone using the text export would think the field itself is set to zero, which contradicts the rolling picture. *Suggestion:* Always state slow roll as 'neglect the field acceleration and kinetic energy', never the field value.
- **The flatness problem is asserted rather than quantified: the text never shows that |Omega - 1| = |k|/adot^2 grows in decelerating eras and shrinks during inflation.** — Learners may not see why Omega near 1 is surprising, or why inflation is the natural cure. *Suggestion:* Use eqn 41.1 directly: adot decreases for a proportional to t^{1/2} or t^{2/3}, so |Omega - 1| grows as t or t^{2/3}; for exponential a it decays as e^{-2Ht}. Plot both.
- **Omega ≈ 0.3 is quoted beside the flatness relation without saying it is the matter density alone; with dark energy the total is consistent with 1.** — Readers may conclude the Universe is open and wonder why a flatness problem exists. *Suggestion:* Distinguish Omega_m ≈ 0.3, Omega_Lambda ≈ 0.7 and Omega_k ≈ 0, and cite modern curvature bounds (Planck 2018).
- **The symmetric point M = 0 below T_c is called metastable, though it is a maximum and therefore unstable.** — Conflates unstable equilibrium with metastable false vacua, which matter in first-order transitions and old inflation. *Suggestion:* Say 'unstable equilibrium' for the hump and reserve 'metastable' for a local minimum above the true minimum.
- **U_0 is ambiguous: defined as the potential at its minimum after eqn 41.7, then used as the near-constant plateau value that drives inflation, and finally as the minimum again when discussing U_0 < 0. For the pure phi^4 form the minimum value is -3mu^4/(2lambda), which is negative.** — It is unclear what energy actually powers inflation and why a positive vacuum energy is needed during the roll but not afterwards. *Suggestion:* Name the plateau value V_inf (powering inflation) separately from the true-vacuum value V_min (which must be close to zero today, hence a constant is added to U).
- **The Fig. 41.6 caption says mu^2 < 0 while giving minima at ±(6mu^2/lambda)^{1/2}, whereas the text keeps mu^2 > 0 and flips the sign of the term.** — Imaginary-looking square roots and sign confusion. *Suggestion:* Adopt one convention explicitly, e.g. write U = -m^2 phi^2/2 + lambda phi^4/4! with m^2 > 0.
- **The section titled 'Effective potentials' never derives or explains a finite-temperature effective potential; the sign change of mu^2(T) is simply assumed.** — Learners may think the title refers to something they missed. *Suggestion:* Add a qualitative note that thermal fluctuations add a term proportional to T^2 phi^2 to the potential, giving mu^2(T) = -m^2 + c T^2.
- **Key modern elements of inflation are absent: number of e-folds (about 60), slow-roll parameters epsilon and eta, quantum fluctuations seeding nearly scale-invariant density perturbations (n_s ≈ 0.965), the tensor-to-scalar ratio and the monopole problem.** — The observational case for inflation looks weaker than it is, and the chapter reads as dated relative to CMB-era cosmology. *Suggestion:* Supplement with a module on e-fold counting, slow-roll parameters and the primordial power spectrum, linked to Chapter 49.
- **The link between inflation and the horizon problem is described loosely ('regions separate rapidly enough'); the shrinking comoving Hubble radius, which is the real mechanism, is not introduced.** — Invites a picture of superluminal travel and does not explain why accelerating expansion (not just fast expansion) is required. *Suggestion:* Plot the comoving Hubble radius 1/(aH) versus ln a: it falls during inflation and rises afterwards, so modes exit and later re-enter the horizon.
- **Example 41.1's thermal history is loose: 10^15 GeV corresponds to about 10^28 K rather than 10^27 K; 10^19 GeV is the Planck scale, where gravity is expected to join in; and the Higgs gives mass to the W and Z bosons and to quarks as well as charged leptons, not only to some leptons.** — Numbers may be copied into estimates, and the particle-physics picture is slightly misleading. *Suggestion:* Use E = k_B T (1 GeV ≈ 1.16e13 K) and describe the electroweak transition more precisely.
- **Symbol clashes: U means internal energy in F = U - TS and potential energy density later; a is a Landau coefficient and then the scale factor; T is temperature and T_{mu nu} the energy-momentum tensor.** — Momentary confusion when the chapter moves from §41.1 to §41.2. *Suggestion:* Rename in teaching materials (e.g. E_int, V(phi), alpha for the Landau coefficient).

## Tutor notes

- Open with a puzzle rather than a definition: 'Two patches of the CMB on opposite sides of the sky never had time to exchange a photon, yet their temperatures agree to 1 part in 100,000. How could that be?' Then ask separately why the Universe should be so close to flat.
- Check prerequisites first: can the learner write the Friedmann equation, say what Omega and k mean, and read a light cone on a Penrose diagram? If not, detour to GA ch17 and ch19 before continuing.
- Order of explanation that works: (1) the two puzzles, (2) symmetry breaking with the magnet and strut, (3) double-well potential and field language, (4) field in an expanding universe with Hubble friction, (5) rho and p and the Lambda connection, (6) slow roll and exponential growth, (7) why curvature is erased, (8) how inflation ends and reheating.
- Understanding check for symmetry breaking: 'Does the Hamiltonian of the magnet change below T_c?' The correct answer is no; only the ground state loses the symmetry.
- Understanding check for vacuum energy: ask the learner to compute rho and p for phi-dot = 0 and to say what fluid behaves like that. Expected: p = -rho, a cosmological constant.
- Understanding check for acceleration: 'Why does negative pressure make the Universe speed up rather than slow down?' Guide them to addot/a = -4 pi (p + rho/3): pressure gravitates in GR.
- When teaching Example 41.3, have the learner compute only the diagonal of phi_{;mu nu} and the single connection coefficient that matters (Gamma^0_{ii} = a adot); insist they track the sign from g^{tt} = -1.
- Warn about the printed slips: eqn 41.9 should have mu^2 phi, not mu^2 phi^2, and eqn 41.22 should contain phi-dot squared.
- Common learner question: 'Is the inflaton the Higgs?' Answer: no, it is a separate hypothetical field; the Higgs only shows that scalar symmetry breaking happens in nature.
- Common learner question: 'Did inflation make things move faster than light?' Answer: no local motion exceeds c; space expands, and the comoving Hubble radius shrinks during acceleration.
- Common learner question: 'If the bottom of phi^4 is negative, doesn't that give a negative cosmological constant today?' Acknowledge the point and explain that a constant is added so the true vacuum has (nearly) zero energy.
- For advanced learners, extend with e-fold counting (N = integral of H dt ≈ 60), slow-roll parameters, and quantum fluctuations as the seeds of structure; for beginners, stop at the ball-on-a-plateau picture and the phi-dot^2 < U criterion.
- Be candid about status: inflation is the dominant paradigm and matches CMB data well, but it is not universally accepted, and the chapter itself signals this (see GA ch49 §49.8).
