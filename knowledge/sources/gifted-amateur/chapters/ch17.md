# GA ch17 · The Friedmann equations

> Fills the Robertson-Walker geometry with a perfect fluid plus vacuum energy, reads off the two Friedmann equations from the time-time and space-space parts of Einstein's equation, uses the first law of thermodynamics to show they carry one piece of information and to get the a^-3 (dust) and a^-4 (radiation) dilution laws, then recasts the first equation with the critical density and density parameters so that total density fixes spatial geometry and an integral over redshift gives cosmic ages.

**Pages:** printed 181–187 · pdf 198–204 · **Difficulty:** math 2/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

The algebra is light (product rule, logarithmic integration, substitution) once the Ricci components are accepted from Chapter 16. Conceptual load comes from juggling three links between geometry and matter (Einstein's equation, thermodynamics, particle counting) and from the Omega bookkeeping with t_0 superscripts. Friendliness is reduced by several printed slips in exactly the equations learners copy (2k/a in the Ricci margin, the missing factor 2 in 17.23, rho_c^(0) = 8 pi H_0/3, the missing H_0^2 in 17.31) and by the absence of any numbers, plots or solved models, all deferred to Chapter 18.

## Role in the book

Middle chapter of Part III (Cosmology). Chapter 15 set up homogeneity, the Hubble parameter and pure-vacuum de Sitter expansion; Chapter 16 built the Robertson-Walker metrics for k = 0, +1, -1 and supplied their Ricci tensor, which is the left-hand side of Einstein's equation. This short chapter supplies the right-hand side (matter content) and turns the field equation into ordinary differential equations for the expansion factor a(t). It is deliberately a toolkit chapter: the payoff, a catalogue of numbered model universes (Einstein-de Sitter, radiation-filled, dust plus Lambda, closed and open dust models), is deferred to Chapter 18, and horizons built on a(t) follow in Chapter 19. Thermodynamics used here in a light way is treated properly much later in Part VI (Chapter 39).

## Learning objectives

- Reader can derive a Friedmann-like equation from Newtonian energy conservation for a comoving shell in a uniform expanding ball and identify k with (minus twice) the specific energy of the shell.
- Reader can insert the perfect-fluid tensor (rho, p, p, p) and the Robertson-Walker Ricci components into the 0-hat 0-hat and i-hat i-hat components of Einstein's equation (with Lambda) and obtain both Friedmann equations.
- Reader can state which Friedmann equation is a first-order constraint (initial value equation) and which contains the second derivative (dynamic equation).
- Reader can derive the cosmological fluid equation d rho/(rho + p) = -3 da/a from the adiabatic first law applied to a comoving volume proportional to a^3.
- Reader can show that differentiating the first Friedmann equation and using the fluid equation reproduces the second, so only one of them is independent (given a-dot nonzero).
- Reader can derive rho proportional to a^-3 for pressureless dust and a^-4 for radiation, both from the fluid equation and from a counting argument (number dilution times per-quantum energy).
- Reader can write the Friedmann equations for a mixture of dust, radiation and vacuum energy referenced to a time t_0 and correct the radiation coefficient in the acceleration equation (rho + 3p).
- Reader can define the critical density, density parameters Omega_j and the curvature parameter Omega_k, and use k/a-dot^2 = Omega - 1 to decide whether space is spherical, flat or hyperbolic.
- Reader can rewrite the first Friedmann equation in terms of x = a/a(t_0) and today's density parameters, and turn it into an integral for the cosmic time at which light of redshift z was emitted, including the age of the universe at z = 0.
- Reader can evaluate simple cases by hand: a flat dust universe gives t(z) = (2/3H_0)(1+z)^(-3/2); a flat universe with rho proportional to a^-2 coasts with a proportional to t.

## Teaching approach

A compact, algebra-light bridge chapter built from five boxed examples. It opens with a Newtonian energy argument so the learner sees the shape of the first Friedmann equation before any tensors, then does the relativistic calculation in two short lines per component by reusing the Ricci tensor from Chapter 16 (reprinted in a margin note) and the fluid tensor from Chapter 10. Thermodynamics is brought in as an independent-looking second link between geometry and matter; it is immediately used to show the two Friedmann equations are redundant and to derive dilution laws for dust and radiation, each backed by a particle-counting rationalization in the margin. The chapter ends by repackaging the first equation into the observational language of critical density, Omega parameters and redshift, stopping just short of solving for any model universe.

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, example-driven, derivation-heavy, conversational-informal, computational-recipe

**Narrative arc**

1. Framing: the geometry (left side of Einstein's equation) is already known from Chapter 16; the job now is to specify the matter (right side).
2. Newtonian warm-up (Example 17.1): energy of a test mass on an expanding uniform sphere, written with comoving x and a(t), rearranges into the form of the first Friedmann equation with k set by the energy.
3. Model the matter: vacuum energy Lambda plus a dissipation-free perfect fluid with orthonormal components rho, p, p, p (margin recaps metric, vielbein and Ricci components).
4. Formalism (Example 17.2): take the time-time and space-space components of the Einstein equation to get the initial value equation (17.6) and the dynamic equation (17.7).
5. Recognize the missing link between rho and p: an equation of state, which depends on what the fluid is made of.
6. Second geometry-matter link (Example 17.3): adiabatic first law for a comoving volume V proportional to a^3 gives the fluid equation d rho/(rho + p) = -3 da/a.
7. Consistency check (Example 17.4): differentiate the first equation, use the fluid equation, recover the second; conclude only one Friedmann equation carries independent information.
8. Application to matter types (17.3): dust (p = 0) dilutes as a^-3, radiation (p = rho/3) as a^-4, each justified again by counting particles and photon redshift.
9. Assemble the multi-component Friedmann equations (17.22, 17.23) referenced to densities at t_0.
10. Observational repackaging (Example 17.5): critical density, Omega parameters, k/a-dot^2 = Omega - 1 classifies geometry; sum rule with Omega_k; dimensionless form in x = a/a_0; age-redshift integral 17.33.
11. Hand-off: matter content and geometry are now linked; Chapter 18 will solve for specific universes.

**Signature moves**

- Puts a Newtonian derivation first, inside a formal example box, so the relativistic result arrives as confirmation of a familiar energy balance rather than as a new law.
- Reprints the needed Robertson-Walker metric, vielbein and Ricci components in a margin note so the Einstein-equation substitution fits in a few displayed lines.
- Labels the two equations by their mathematical roles (initial value equation versus dynamic equation), anticipating the constraint-plus-evolution structure.
- Introduces thermodynamics as a second, independent-seeming bridge between geometry and mass-energy, then uses it to prove the two Friedmann equations are redundant.
- Every dilution law is derived twice: formally from the fluid equation and informally by a margin argument about particle number and per-particle energy (rest mass for dust, redshifted quantum for photons).
- Reduces the first Friedmann equation to an 'Omega minus one' diagnostic so a single measured number decides the sign of spatial curvature.
- Ends with an integral that converts density parameters and redshift into a time, setting z = 0 to get the age of the universe, without evaluating any model (left to exercises and Chapter 18).

## Section by section

### Chapter opening and Newtonian warm-up (Example 17.1) — p.181 (pdf 198)

Epigraphs from Marlowe's Doctor Faustus and the Fuller-Carlyle exchange set a playful 'fate of the universe' tone. The authors state the chapter's job: Chapter 16 provided the geometric side of Einstein's equation, and now idealized matter must be put on the other side, giving two equivalent evolution equations for a(t). Example 17.1 derives a Newtonian analogue: a test mass on the edge of a uniform expanding sphere has conserved energy kinetic plus gravitational; writing the radius as a(t) times a comoving coordinate turns energy conservation into (a-dot/a)^2 + k/a^2 = 8 pi G rho/3, with k fixed by the energy.

**Concepts:** Newtonian derivation of the Friedmann equation, comoving coordinates, scale factor, curvature constant k, first Friedmann equation

**Key moves**
- Total energy of a test mass at radius r in a uniform medium: kinetic (1/2) m r-dot^2 plus potential -GMm/r with M = (4/3) pi r^3 rho
- Substitute r = a(t) x with fixed comoving x, giving eqn 17.1
- Divide by (1/2) m a^2 x^2 to reach eqn 17.2 with k = -2U/(m x^2), constant because energy is conserved
- Observation that the first Friedmann equation is at heart an energy statement

### §17.1 Enter energy-momentum — p.182 (pdf 199)

Following Friedmann, the Robertson-Walker spaces are filled with vacuum energy (Lambda) and a perfect fluid whose orthonormal-frame tensor is diagonal with energy density rho and isotropic pressure p. Example 17.2 inserts these, together with the orthonormal Ricci components R_tt = -3 a-ddot/a and R_ii = a-ddot/a + 2(a-dot/a)^2 + 2k/a^2 and the scalar R (all recapped in the margin), into the time-time and space-space components of Einstein's equation. The results are boxed as Friedmann equation 1, the initial value equation, and Friedmann equation 2, the dynamic equation. The section closes (top of p.183) by noting that rho and p still need to be tied together by an equation of state fixed by the fluid's thermodynamics.

**Concepts:** perfect-fluid energy-momentum tensor, cosmological constant as vacuum energy, Robertson-Walker metric, Ricci tensor of the Robertson-Walker metric, Einstein field equation with cosmological constant, Friedmann equations, first Friedmann equation, second Friedmann equation, equation of state

**Key moves**
- Perfect fluid in orthonormal frame: T_tt = rho, T_rr = T_thth = T_phph = p (17.3); Exercise 17.1 checks this for k = +1, -1
- 0-hat 0-hat component: R_00 + R/2 = 8 pi rho + Lambda collapses to (a-dot/a)^2 + k/a^2 = 8 pi rho/3 + Lambda/3 (17.4)
- i-hat i-hat component: R_ii - R/2 = 8 pi p - Lambda gives 2 a-ddot/a + (a-dot/a)^2 + k/a^2 = -8 pi p + Lambda (17.5)
- Naming: first equation is a constraint on initial data (no a-ddot); second governs the dynamics
- An equation of state linking rho and p is still missing

### §17.2 Enter thermodynamics — p.183 (pdf 200)

Geometry and energy are also linked by thermodynamics. Example 17.3 applies the first law to a comoving volume V = b a^3 containing energy U = rho V, assuming adiabatic evolution (dS = 0: equilibrium, no phase transitions or shocks). Differentiating in time gives the fluid equation, boxed as d rho/(rho + p) = -3 da/a. Example 17.4 then differentiates the first Friedmann equation, substitutes the fluid equation, and after eliminating 8 pi rho/3 recovers the second Friedmann equation exactly, so the pair contains a single independent equation once energy conservation is imposed.

**Concepts:** first law of thermodynamics, adiabatic expansion of the cosmic fluid, fluid equation, redundancy of the Friedmann equations, acceleration equation

**Key moves**
- dU = T dS - p dV with dS = 0 and U = rho b a^3, V = b a^3 (17.8)
- Expand d(rho a^3)/dt = -p d(a^3)/dt (17.9) and rearrange to (17.10)-(17.11)
- Rewrite F1 as a-dot^2 + k = (Lambda/3 + 8 pi rho/3) a^2 (17.12) and differentiate (17.13)
- Insert rho-dot = -3(rho + p) a-dot/a (17.14), divide by a a-dot to get 2 a-ddot/a = 2 Lambda/3 - 8 pi rho/3 - 8 pi p (17.15)
- Eliminate 8 pi rho/3 with F1 to land on F2 (17.16)

### §17.3 Dust and radiation — p.184 (pdf 201)

On cosmological scales matter is treated as a continuous classical fluid split into dust (massive, pressureless, density rho_d) and radiation (a photon gas with density rho_r and pressure rho_r/3). The fluid equation gives rho_d proportional to a^-3 and rho_r proportional to a^-4, written relative to values at a reference time t_0 (often today, where H_0 is the Hubble constant). Margin notes justify both by counting: particle number dilutes with volume, dust particles keep their rest energy, photons lose energy in proportion to 1/a by redshift. Substituting gives the Friedmann equations for a dust-radiation-vacuum mixture (17.22-17.23), which determine a(t) for all times from densities and a at t_0.

**Concepts:** dust, radiation, radiation equation of state, scaling of energy density with the scale factor, cosmological redshift, Hubble parameter, Friedmann equations for a multi-component universe

**Key moves**
- Dust: p = 0 in 17.11 integrates to rho_d proportional to a^-3 (17.17), or rho_d/rho_d^(0) = [a(t_0)/a]^3 (17.18)
- Radiation: p = rho/3 gives d rho/rho = -4 da/a (17.20) and rho_r/rho_r^(0) = [a(t_0)/a]^4 (17.21)
- Counting rationalization: number density scales as a^-3, per-photon energy as a^-1
- Radiation dilutes fastest, so the universe empties with time and radiation fades first
- Assemble 17.22 (first equation) and 17.23 (acceleration form) for mixed content

### §17.3 Critical density, density parameters and the age integral (Example 17.5) — p.185 (pdf 202)

Example 17.5 defines the critical density rho_c = 3H^2/(8 pi) and vacuum density rho_Lambda = Lambda/(8 pi), turning the first Friedmann equation into 1 + k/(a^2 H^2) = sum of rho_j/rho_c, hence k/a-dot^2 = Omega - 1. Because a-dot^2 is positive, the sign of Omega - 1 fixes k: below critical is hyperbolic, exactly critical is flat, above critical is spherical. Referencing to t_0 with Omega_k^(0) = -k/(a_0^2 H_0^2) gives the sum rule Omega_d + Omega_r + Omega_Lambda + Omega_k = 1 and a dimensionless evolution equation in x = a/a_0. Using x = 1/(1+z) and setting t = 0 at infinite redshift, the emission time of light seen with redshift z becomes an integral (17.33); z = 0 gives the present age. The chapter then hands over to Chapter 18.

**Concepts:** critical density, density parameter, curvature density parameter, cosmic sum rule, density determines spatial geometry, dimensionless Friedmann equation in density parameters, cosmic time as a function of redshift, age of the universe

**Key moves**
- Define rho_c by replacing (a-dot/a)^2 in F1 (17.24-17.26)
- Divide by rho_c with rho_Lambda = Lambda/8 pi to get 17.27, then use a^2 H^2 = a-dot^2 (17.28-17.29)
- Read off k from the sign of Omega - 1 assuming a-dot > 0
- Present-day parameters and Omega_k^(0) give the sum rule 17.30
- Change variable to x = a/a_0 (17.32) and separate variables
- Use 1 + z = a_0/a to convert to an age-redshift integral with t = 0 at z = infinity (17.33)

### Chapter summary and exercises — p.187 (pdf 204)

The summary box restates the two Friedmann equations and the dust and radiation dilution laws. Four exercises follow: checking the fluid tensor components for curved k via the chi coordinate, verifying p = rho/3 from a photon partition function, solving a flat model whose density falls as a^-2, and finding the emission time of light from a galaxy at redshift z in a flat dust universe.

**Concepts:** Friedmann equations, dust, radiation, Einstein-de Sitter universe, coasting expansion

**Key moves**
- Summary equations 17.34-17.35 repeat 17.6-17.7
- Exercises move from frame bookkeeping to statistical mechanics to first solutions of the Friedmann equation

## Concepts

### Friedmann equations

*law · core* · also: Friedmann equations of motion, equations of motion for the expansion factor

The pair of ordinary differential equations for the scale factor a(t) of a homogeneous, isotropic (Robertson-Walker) universe obtained from Einstein's equation with a perfect fluid and cosmological constant: a first-order constraint relating the expansion rate to density and curvature, and a second-order equation for the acceleration involving pressure.

**How introduced:** Motivated as filling the right-hand side of Einstein's equation for the Robertson-Walker geometry; derived from two components in the orthonormal frame (Example 17.2) after a Newtonian preview (Example 17.1).

**Prerequisites:** Robertson-Walker metric, Einstein field equation with cosmological constant, perfect-fluid energy-momentum tensor, scale factor

$$
\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2+\frac{k}{a^2}=\frac{8\pi}{3}\rho+\frac{\Lambda}{3}
$$

$$
2\frac{\ddot a}{a}+\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2+\frac{k}{a^2}=-8\pi p+\Lambda
$$

**Notes:** Named after Alexander Friedmann (1922, 1924); the book keeps G = c = 1 and signature (-,+,+,+).

**Where:** GA p.181 (pdf 198); GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199); GA p.187 (pdf 204)

### first Friedmann equation

*law · core* · also: Friedmann equation 1, initial value equation, Friedmann constraint, Hubble equation

H^2 = (a-dot/a)^2 = 8 pi rho/3 + Lambda/3 - k/a^2: the squared expansion rate at any instant is fixed by the total energy density, the cosmological constant and the spatial curvature term. It contains no second derivative, so it constrains the initial data (a, a-dot) rather than evolving them.

**How introduced:** Obtained from the time-time orthonormal component of Einstein's equation, where the a-ddot terms cancel between R_00 and R/2; previewed as a Newtonian energy equation.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann equations, Hubble parameter, curvature constant k

$$
\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2+\frac{k}{a^2}=\frac{8\pi\rho}{3}+\frac{\Lambda}{3}
$$

$$
\dot a^2+k=\left(\frac{\Lambda}{3}+\frac{8\pi\rho}{3}\right)a^2
$$

**Where:** GA p.181 (pdf 198); GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199); GA §17.2 p.184 (pdf 201)

### second Friedmann equation

*law · core* · also: Friedmann equation 2, dynamic equation, space-space Friedmann equation

2 a-ddot/a + (a-dot/a)^2 + k/a^2 = -8 pi p + Lambda: the equation containing the acceleration of the scale factor, sourced by the pressure. Combined with the first equation it becomes the acceleration equation a-ddot/a = -(4 pi/3)(rho + 3p) + Lambda/3.

**How introduced:** From the space-space (i-hat i-hat) component of Einstein's equation in Example 17.2; later rederived from the first equation plus the fluid equation in Example 17.4.

**Prerequisites:** first Friedmann equation, pressure, Einstein field equation with cosmological constant

$$
2\frac{\ddot a}{a}+\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2+\frac{k}{a^2}=-8\pi p+\Lambda
$$

**Where:** GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199); GA §17.2 p.184 (pdf 201)

### acceleration equation

*law · developed* · also: Raychaudhuri equation for a homogeneous universe, a-ddot equation

a-ddot/a = -(4 pi/3)(rho + 3p) + Lambda/3, the difference of the two Friedmann equations: ordinary matter and pressure both decelerate the expansion (pressure counts three times as a gravitational source), while positive Lambda accelerates it.

**How introduced:** Appears as the intermediate step 17.15 in Example 17.4 (2 a-ddot/a = 2 Lambda/3 - 8 pi rho/3 - 8 pi p) and as 17.23 for dust plus radiation; the book never writes the (rho + 3p) form explicitly.

**Prerequisites:** first Friedmann equation, second Friedmann equation

$$
\frac{\ddot a}{a}=-\frac{4\pi}{3}(\rho+3p)+\frac{\Lambda}{3}
$$

**Notes:** Printed eqn 17.23 omits the factor 2 that radiation's pressure contributes (rho_r + 3p_r = 2 rho_r).

**Where:** GA §17.2 p.184 (pdf 201); GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202)

### Newtonian derivation of the Friedmann equation

*technique · introduced* · also: Newtonian cosmology, expanding dust ball argument

Heuristic reconstruction of the first Friedmann equation from Newtonian mechanics: a test mass on a comoving shell of a uniform expanding sphere conserves kinetic plus gravitational energy, and writing r = a(t) x gives (a-dot/a)^2 + k/a^2 = 8 pi G rho/3 with k = -2U/(m x^2) proportional to minus the shell's energy.

**How introduced:** Placed first as Example 17.1 to give physical insight before the relativistic derivation; framed as showing the equation is fundamentally about energy.

**Prerequisites:** comoving coordinates, scale factor

$$
U=\tfrac12 m\dot a^2x^2-\frac{4\pi G}{3}\rho a^2x^2 m
$$

$$
k=-\frac{2U}{mx^2}
$$

**Notes:** Implicitly requires the mass inside the shell, rho a^3, to be constant and relies on the shell theorem; k here is not normalized to 0, +1, -1 and has dimensions of velocity squared per comoving length squared.

**Where:** GA p.181 (pdf 198)

### scale factor

*physical-quantity · revisited* · also: expansion factor, effective radius of the universe, a(t), radius-like expansion factor

The time-dependent function a(t) multiplying the spatial part of the Robertson-Walker metric, so that proper distances between comoving observers all scale in proportion to a(t).

**How introduced:** Carried over from Chapters 15-16; here it becomes the unknown of the Friedmann equations and sets the comoving volume V proportional to a^3 in the thermodynamic argument.

**Prerequisites:** Robertson-Walker metric, comoving coordinates

$$
\vec r=a(t)\vec x
$$

$$
x=\frac{a(t)}{a(t_0)}
$$

**Notes:** The book calls a an 'effective radius', which is literal only for k = +1; it does not normalize a(t_0) = 1 in this chapter.

**Where:** GA p.181 (pdf 198); GA §17.1 p.183 (pdf 200); GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203)

### comoving coordinates

*definition · mention* · also: comoving coordinate x

Coordinates that stay fixed for observers moving with the cosmic expansion; physical separation equals the scale factor times the comoving separation.

**How introduced:** Used in Example 17.1 to split the radius of a Newtonian shell as a(t) x with x fixed.

**Prerequisites:** scale factor

$$
\vec r=a(t)\vec x
$$

**Where:** GA p.181 (pdf 198)

### curvature constant k

*definition · developed* · also: spatial curvature index, k = 0, +1, -1

The constant in the Robertson-Walker metric that fixes the geometry of constant-time slices: k = +1 spherical (closed), 0 flat, -1 hyperbolic (open). In the Friedmann equation it enters as the term k/a^2.

**How introduced:** Inherited from Chapter 16's metric; given physical meaning by the Newtonian argument (k proportional to minus the energy of a shell) and by the Omega classification in Example 17.5.

**Prerequisites:** Robertson-Walker metric

$$
\frac{k}{\dot a^2}=\Omega-1
$$

**Where:** GA p.181 (pdf 198); GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199); GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203)

### Robertson-Walker metric

*solution-or-model · revisited* · also: Robertson-Walker line element, Robertson-Walker spaces, FLRW metric

The most general spacetime metric with homogeneous, isotropic spatial slices: ds^2 = -dt^2 + a(t)^2 [dr^2/(1 - k r^2) + r^2 (d theta^2 + sin^2 theta d phi^2)].

**How introduced:** Recalled in a margin note with its metric components and vielbein, as the fixed geometric input to Einstein's equation.

**Prerequisites:** scale factor, curvature constant k

$$
ds^2=-dt^2+a(t)^2\left[\frac{dr^2}{1-kr^2}+r^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)\right]
$$

**Where:** GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199)

### Ricci tensor of the Robertson-Walker metric

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: orthonormal Ricci components for Robertson-Walker

In the orthonormal frame of comoving observers the Robertson-Walker Ricci tensor is diagonal with R_tt = -3 a-ddot/a and each spatial component R_ii = a-ddot/a + 2(a-dot/a)^2 + 2k/a^2; the Ricci scalar is R = 6(a-ddot/a + a-dot^2/a^2 + k/a^2).

**How introduced:** Quoted from Chapter 16 in a margin note together with the vielbein, so it can be substituted directly into the field equation.

**Prerequisites:** Robertson-Walker metric, vielbein

$$
R_{\hat t\hat t}=-3\frac{\ddot a}{a}
$$

$$
R_{\hat i\hat i}=\frac{\ddot a}{a}+2\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2+2\frac{k}{a^2}
$$

$$
R=6\left(\frac{\ddot a}{a}+\frac{\dot a^2}{a^2}+\frac{k}{a^2}\right)
$$

**Notes:** The printed margin note (and the first line of eqn 17.5) has 2k/a instead of 2k/a^2; the final equations use the correct power.

**Where:** GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199)

### vielbein

*mathematical-object · mention* · also: orthonormal frame components, tetrad

The set of components relating coordinate basis vectors to an orthonormal basis; for the diagonal Robertson-Walker metric its entries are the square roots of the magnitudes of the metric components (1, a/sqrt(1 - k r^2), a r, a r sin theta).

**How introduced:** Listed in the margin to justify working in the orthonormal frame; also used in Exercise 17.1.

**Prerequisites:** Robertson-Walker metric

$$
(e_r)^{\hat r}=\frac{a}{(1-kr^2)^{1/2}}
$$

**Where:** GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199); GA p.187 (pdf 204)

### Einstein field equation with cosmological constant

*law · revisited* · also: Einstein equation with Lambda

R_mu nu - (1/2) g_mu nu R = 8 pi T_mu nu - Lambda g_mu nu (G = c = 1): spacetime curvature is sourced by energy-momentum, with the cosmological constant acting like a uniform vacuum energy.

**How introduced:** Used component by component in the orthonormal frame in Example 17.2.

**Prerequisites:** Ricci tensor of the Robertson-Walker metric, perfect-fluid energy-momentum tensor

$$
R_{\hat\mu\hat\nu}-\tfrac12 g_{\hat\mu\hat\nu}R=8\pi T_{\hat\mu\hat\nu}-\Lambda g_{\hat\mu\hat\nu}
$$

**Where:** GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199)

### perfect-fluid energy-momentum tensor

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: perfect fluid, fluid without dissipation

Energy-momentum tensor of a fluid with no viscosity or heat conduction; in its rest frame's orthonormal basis it is diagonal with energy density rho in the time-time slot and the same pressure p in each spatial slot.

**How introduced:** Stated for spherical polars in the orthonormal frame (17.3), pointing back to Chapter 10's k = 0 version and forward to Exercise 17.1 for k = +1, -1.

**Prerequisites:** vielbein, pressure

$$
T_{\hat t\hat t}=\rho,\quad T_{\hat r\hat r}=T_{\hat\theta\hat\theta}=T_{\hat\phi\hat\phi}=p
$$

**Where:** GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199); GA p.187 (pdf 204)

### pressure

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: isotropic pressure p

The isotropic momentum flux (force per area) of the cosmic fluid measured by comoving observers; it appears in the space-space Einstein equation and in the pdV work of the first law.

**How introduced:** Enters through the fluid tensor and the first law; its effect is to change how fast density falls and to add to deceleration.

**Prerequisites:** perfect-fluid energy-momentum tensor

**Where:** GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199); GA §17.2 p.183 (pdf 200)

### cosmological constant as vacuum energy

*physical-quantity · revisited* · also: vacuum energy, Lambda, rho_Lambda

A constant Lambda in the field equation that behaves like a fluid with constant energy density rho_Lambda = Lambda/(8 pi) and pressure -rho_Lambda, so it neither dilutes nor decelerates the expansion.

**How introduced:** The universes are filled with vacuum energy of density Lambda alongside the fluid (the book loosely calls Lambda the energy density); Example 17.5 defines rho_Lambda = Lambda/8 pi.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein field equation with cosmological constant

$$
\rho_\Lambda=\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi}
$$

**Where:** GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199); GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202)

### equation of state

*definition · introduced* · also: rho-p relation

A relation p = p(rho) fixed by the microphysics and thermodynamics of the fluid; the Friedmann equations cannot be solved without it.

**How introduced:** Flagged at the end of section 17.1 as the missing link between the two unknowns rho and p; dust (p = 0) and radiation (p = rho/3) are the worked cases.

**Prerequisites:** pressure

$$
p_{\rm dust}=0
$$

$$
p_{\rm rad}=\rho/3
$$

**Where:** GA §17.1 p.183 (pdf 200); GA §17.3 p.184 (pdf 201)

### first law of thermodynamics

*law · developed* · also: energy conservation dU = T dS - p dV

The change in internal energy of a system equals heat added (T dS) minus work done by the system (p dV).

**How introduced:** Presented as a second, thermodynamic bridge between geometry (volume) and mass-energy; applied to a comoving volume in Example 17.3.

$$
dU=T\,dS-p\,dV
$$

**Where:** GA §17.2 p.183 (pdf 200)

### adiabatic expansion of the cosmic fluid

*principle · introduced* · also: isentropic expansion, dS = 0 assumption

The assumption that fluid elements stay in thermal equilibrium without entropy production (no phase transitions, shocks or dissipation), so comoving volumes evolve with dS = 0.

**How introduced:** Stated in Example 17.3 and justified in a margin note giving shockwaves (kinetic energy turned to heat) as a counterexample.

**Prerequisites:** first law of thermodynamics

$$
dS=0
$$

**Where:** GA §17.2 p.183 (pdf 200)

### fluid equation

*law · core* · also: continuity equation in cosmology, first law in Friedmann form, energy conservation equation for the cosmic fluid

rho-dot = -3 (a-dot/a)(rho + p), equivalently d rho/(rho + p) = -3 da/a: the energy density in a comoving volume drops both from dilution and from pdV work done by pressure as the volume grows like a^3.

**How introduced:** Derived from the adiabatic first law with V = b a^3 and U = rho V (Example 17.3); boxed as the thermodynamic statement tailored to Friedmann cosmologies.

**Prerequisites:** first law of thermodynamics, adiabatic expansion of the cosmic fluid, scale factor

$$
\frac{d\rho}{\rho+p}=-3\frac{da}{a}
$$

$$
\dot\rho=-3(\rho+p)\frac{\dot a}{a}
$$

**Notes:** In GR this is the time component of the covariant conservation law for T, itself guaranteed by the contracted Bianchi identity; the chapter does not make that link.

**Where:** GA §17.2 p.183 (pdf 200); GA §17.2 p.184 (pdf 201)

### redundancy of the Friedmann equations

*theorem · developed* · also: equivalence of the two Friedmann equations

Given the fluid equation and a-dot not equal to zero, the second Friedmann equation follows from the time derivative of the first, so the system has one independent equation plus the conservation law and an equation of state.

**How introduced:** Example 17.4: rearrange, differentiate, substitute rho-dot, divide by a a-dot, eliminate rho with the first equation.

**Prerequisites:** first Friedmann equation, second Friedmann equation, fluid equation

**Notes:** Fails for static configurations (a-dot = 0), where the second equation carries independent content.

**Where:** GA §17.2 p.184 (pdf 201)

### dust

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: matter with mass, pressureless matter, non-relativistic matter

A cosmic fluid of massive, slowly moving particles whose pressure is negligible (p = 0), with energy density rho_d dominated by rest mass.

**How introduced:** One of two idealized kinds of cosmic matter in section 17.3; its dilution law follows from setting p = 0 in the fluid equation.

**Prerequisites:** perfect-fluid energy-momentum tensor, equation of state

\rho_d\propto a^{-3}

**Where:** GA §17.3 p.184 (pdf 201); GA p.187 (pdf 204)

### radiation

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: massless matter, photon gas, relativistic matter

A cosmic fluid of massless particles (photons) with uniform energy density rho_r and pressure p = rho_r/3.

**How introduced:** The second idealized matter type of section 17.3; its equation of state is quoted from thermodynamics and checked by statistical mechanics in Exercise 17.2.

**Prerequisites:** perfect-fluid energy-momentum tensor, radiation equation of state

\rho_r\propto a^{-4}

**Where:** GA §17.3 p.184 (pdf 201); GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202); GA p.187 (pdf 204)

### radiation equation of state

*law · introduced* · also: p = rho/3

For a gas of massless particles the pressure is one third of the energy density, reflecting a traceless energy-momentum tensor.

**How introduced:** Stated as a fundamental thermodynamic result (17.19), with Exercise 17.2 deriving it from the photon partition function.

**Prerequisites:** equation of state

$$
p=\frac{\rho}{3}
$$

**Where:** GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202); GA p.187 (pdf 204)

### scaling of energy density with the scale factor

*law · core* · also: dilution laws, rho proportional to a^-3 and a^-4

As the universe expands, pressureless matter density falls as a^-3 (fixed number of particles in a growing volume, fixed energy per particle) and radiation density as a^-4 (same dilution plus a 1/a redshift of each photon's energy), so radiation fades fastest; vacuum energy stays constant.

**How introduced:** Derived formally by integrating the fluid equation for p = 0 and p = rho/3, and re-derived informally in margin notes by counting particles.

**Prerequisites:** fluid equation, dust, radiation, cosmological redshift

$$
\frac{\rho_d(t)}{\rho_d^{(0)}}=\left[\frac{a(t_0)}{a(t)}\right]^3
$$

$$
\frac{\rho_r(t)}{\rho_r^{(0)}}=\left[\frac{a(t_0)}{a(t)}\right]^4
$$

**Where:** GA §17.3 p.184 (pdf 201); GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202)

### cosmological redshift

*phenomenon · revisited* · also: redshift z, 1 + z = a_obs/a_em

Stretching of light wavelengths in proportion to the scale factor between emission and observation, so 1 + z = a(t_obs)/a(t_em) and each photon's energy scales as 1/a.

**How introduced:** Invoked in a margin note to explain the extra factor of a in radiation dilution, and via eqn 16.48 to convert x = a/a_0 into redshift for the age integral.

**Prerequisites:** scale factor

$$
x=\frac{a(t)}{a(t_0)}=\frac{1}{1+z}
$$

**Where:** GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202); GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203)

### Hubble parameter

*physical-quantity · revisited* · also: H(t), Hubble constant H_0

The fractional expansion rate H(t) = a-dot/a; its present value is the Hubble constant H_0 = H(t_0).

**How introduced:** Reintroduced in a margin note about the reference time t_0 and used to define the critical density.

**Prerequisites:** scale factor

$$
H=\frac{\dot a}{a}
$$

$$
H_0=H(t_0)
$$

**Where:** GA §17.3 p.184 (pdf 201); GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202)

### Friedmann equations for a multi-component universe

*law · developed* · also: Friedmann equations with dust, radiation and vacuum energy

The Friedmann equations with total density written as a sum of components, each scaling with its own power of a relative to reference values at t_0: rho_d^(0)(a_0/a)^3 + rho_r^(0)(a_0/a)^4 plus Lambda/8 pi.

**How introduced:** Assembled in section 17.3 immediately after the dilution laws (17.22-17.23), with the remark that knowing densities and a at one time determines a(t) at all times.

**Prerequisites:** first Friedmann equation, scaling of energy density with the scale factor

$$
\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2+\frac{k}{a^2}=\frac{8\pi}{3}\left\{\rho_d^{(0)}\left[\frac{a_0}{a}\right]^3+\rho_r^{(0)}\left[\frac{a_0}{a}\right]^4\right\}+\frac{\Lambda}{3}
$$

**Where:** GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202)

### critical density

*physical-quantity · core* · also: rho_c

rho_c = 3H^2/(8 pi) (3H^2/(8 pi G) in SI): the total energy density, including vacuum energy, for which the first Friedmann equation requires flat spatial sections (k = 0) at the given expansion rate.

**How introduced:** Defined in Example 17.5 by replacing (a-dot/a)^2 in the first Friedmann equation; the name is justified afterwards by the geometry classification.

**Prerequisites:** first Friedmann equation, Hubble parameter

$$
\rho_c=\frac{3}{8\pi}H^2=\frac{3}{8\pi}\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2
$$

$$
\rho_c^{(0)}=\frac{3H_0^2}{8\pi}
$$

**Notes:** Printed text on p.186 gives rho_c^(0) = 8 pi H_0/3, an inverted and unsquared version of the correct expression.

**Where:** GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202); GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203)

### density parameter

*physical-quantity · core* · also: Omega, Omega_j, density ratio

The ratio Omega_j = rho_j/rho_c of a component's energy density to the critical density; the total Omega is the sum over dust, radiation and vacuum energy. Each Omega_j generally changes with time; Omega_j^(0) denotes its value at t_0.

**How introduced:** Introduced in Example 17.5 to make the first Friedmann equation dimensionless and to read off the geometry.

**Prerequisites:** critical density

$$
\Omega_j=\frac{\rho_j}{\rho_c}
$$

$$
\Omega=\Omega_d+\Omega_r+\Omega_\Lambda
$$

**Where:** GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203)

### curvature density parameter

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: Omega_k, Omega_k^(0)

Omega_k^(0) = -k/(a_0^2 H_0^2): the curvature term of the first Friedmann equation expressed as if it were a density fraction; positive for open (k = -1) and negative for closed (k = +1) geometry.

**How introduced:** Defined in Example 17.5 so that all terms of the present-day Friedmann equation sum to one.

**Prerequisites:** density parameter, curvature constant k

$$
\Omega_k^{(0)}=-\frac{k}{a(t_0)^2H_0^2}
$$

**Where:** GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203)

### cosmic sum rule

*identity · developed* · also: closure relation, Omega sum rule

Evaluated at the reference time and divided by H_0 squared, the first Friedmann equation says the present density fractions of dust, radiation and vacuum energy, together with the curvature fraction, add up to exactly one.

**How introduced:** Derived from eqn 17.27 once Omega_k^(0) is defined.

**Prerequisites:** density parameter, curvature density parameter

$$
\Omega_d^{(0)}+\Omega_r^{(0)}+\Omega_\Lambda^{(0)}+\Omega_k^{(0)}=1
$$

**Where:** GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203)

### density determines spatial geometry

*principle · core* · also: Omega-geometry classification

Because k/a-dot^2 = Omega - 1 and a-dot^2 > 0, the total density relative to critical fixes the sign of k: Omega < 1 gives hyperbolic (k = -1), Omega = 1 flat (k = 0), Omega > 1 spherical (k = +1) space. Since k is constant, the sign of Omega - 1 cannot change while the universe expands.

**How introduced:** Read off from eqn 17.29 as a bullet list; used to justify the name 'critical'.

**Prerequisites:** density parameter, curvature constant k, first Friedmann equation

$$
\frac{k}{\dot a^2}=\Omega-1
$$

**Notes:** The classification is about spatial geometry, not about eventual recollapse; with Lambda present the two decouple (Chapter 18).

**Where:** GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203)

### dimensionless Friedmann equation in density parameters

*law · developed* · also: E(z) form, Friedmann equation in x = a/a_0

Form of the first Friedmann equation using the relative scale factor x = a/a_0: the squared fractional expansion rate equals H_0 squared times a sum of today's curvature, dust, radiation and vacuum fractions, weighted by inverse powers two, three, four and zero of x respectively, so each component dominates in a different epoch.

**How introduced:** Reached from 17.22 and the sum rule in Example 17.5 as a stepping stone to the age integral.

**Prerequisites:** cosmic sum rule, Friedmann equations for a multi-component universe

$$
\frac{1}{x^2}\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2=H_0^2\left(\frac{\Omega_k^{(0)}}{x^2}+\frac{\Omega_d^{(0)}}{x^3}+\frac{\Omega_r^{(0)}}{x^4}+\Omega_\Lambda^{(0)}\right)
$$

**Where:** GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203)

### cosmic time as a function of redshift

*technique · developed* · also: age-redshift relation, emission time t(z), lookback integral

Separating variables in the dimensionless Friedmann equation and using x = 1/(1+z) gives the cosmic time at which light now received with redshift z was emitted, measured from the moment of infinite redshift (a = 0).

**How introduced:** Final step of Example 17.5 (eqn 17.33), with Exercise 17.4 as the flat-dust special case.

**Prerequisites:** dimensionless Friedmann equation in density parameters, cosmological redshift

$$
t(z)=\frac{1}{H_0}\int_0^{(1+z)^{-1}}\frac{dx}{x\left(\Omega_k^{(0)}x^{-2}+\Omega_d^{(0)}x^{-3}+\Omega_r^{(0)}x^{-4}+\Omega_\Lambda^{(0)}\right)^{1/2}}
$$

**Notes:** Assumes a started from zero at a finite time (a big bang); lookback time would be t_0 - t(z).

**Where:** GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203); GA p.187 (pdf 204)

### age of the universe

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: present cosmic age t_0

Cosmic time elapsed since a = 0, obtained by evaluating the age-redshift integral at z = 0; of order 1/H_0 times a factor set by the density parameters.

**How introduced:** Mentioned as the z = 0 value of eqn 17.33.

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: flat dust universe, Universe 3

Spatially flat (k = 0) universe containing only pressureless matter and no Lambda; a grows as t^(2/3), the age is 2/(3H_0), and light of redshift z left at t = (2/3H_0)(1+z)^(-3/2).

Expansion at constant a-dot, obtained in a flat universe whose source density falls as a^-2 (a fluid with p = -rho/3), since then a-dot^2 is constant.

### (17.1) Newtonian energy of a comoving shell · supporting · GA p.181 (pdf 198)

$$
U=\frac12 m\dot a^2x^2-\frac{4\pi G}{3}\rho a^2x^2m

Conserved energy of a test mass on the surface of a uniform expanding sphere, written with r = a(t) x and M = (4/3) pi r^3 rho.

**Symbols:** U total energy; m test mass; x comoving radius; rho density; G Newton's constant

### (17.2) Newtonian Friedmann equation · central · GA p.181 (pdf 198)

$$
\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2+\frac{k}{a^2}=\frac{8\pi G\rho}{3},\qquad k=-\frac{2U}{mx^2}
$$

Same form as the first Friedmann equation, with the curvature-like constant set by minus the shell's energy: bound shells (U < 0) give k > 0.

**Symbols:** k constant (not normalized to 0, +1, -1 here)

### Robertson-Walker line element (margin) · supporting · GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199)

$$
ds^2=-dt^2+a(t)^2\left[\frac{dr^2}{1-kr^2}+r^2\left(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right]
$$

Geometry input for the chapter, reprinted with metric components g_tt = -1, g_rr = a^2/(1 - k r^2), g_thth = a^2 r^2, g_phph = a^2 r^2 sin^2 theta.

### Robertson-Walker vielbein (margin) · supporting · GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199)

$$
(e_t)^{\hat t}=1,\quad (e_r)^{\hat r}=\frac{a}{(1-kr^2)^{1/2}},\quad (e_\theta)^{\hat\theta}=ar,\quad (e_\phi)^{\hat\phi}=ar\sin\theta
$$

Diagonal vielbein converting coordinate components to the comoving orthonormal frame.

### Orthonormal Ricci tensor and scalar of Robertson-Walker (margin, corrected) · supporting · GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199)

$$
R_{\hat t\hat t}=-3\frac{\ddot a}{a},\qquad R_{\hat i\hat i}=\frac{\ddot a}{a}+2\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2+2\frac{k}{a^2},\qquad R=6\left(\frac{\ddot a}{a}+\frac{\dot a^2}{a^2}+\frac{k}{a^2}\right)
$$

Curvature side of Einstein's equation for comoving observers. The printed margin has 2k/a in R_ii (a typo; check: R = -R_tt + 3 R_ii requires k/a^2).

**Symbols:** hats orthonormal frame; i = r, theta, phi with no sum

### (17.3) Perfect fluid in the orthonormal frame · central · GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199)

$$
T_{\hat t\hat t}=\rho,\qquad T_{\hat r\hat r}=T_{\hat\theta\hat\theta}=T_{\hat\phi\hat\phi}=p
$$

Matter side: energy density on the diagonal time slot, equal pressure in each spatial slot, no off-diagonal stresses.

**Symbols:** rho mass-energy density; p pressure

### (17.4) Time-time component of Einstein's equation · derivation-step · GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199)

$$
R_{\hat0\hat0}-\frac{g_{\hat0\hat0}}{2}R=8\pi T_{\hat0\hat0}-\Lambda g_{\hat0\hat0}\;\Rightarrow\;-3\frac{\ddot a}{a}+3\left[\frac{\ddot a}{a}+\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2+\frac{k}{a^2}\right]=8\pi\rho+\Lambda\;\Rightarrow\;\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2+\frac{k}{a^2}=\frac{8\pi\rho}{3}+\frac{\Lambda}{3}
$$

With g_00 = -1 the a-ddot terms cancel, leaving a first-order equation.

**Symbols:** Lambda cosmological constant; G = c = 1

### (17.5) Space-space component of Einstein's equation · derivation-step · GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199)

$$
\left[\frac{\ddot a}{a}+2\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2+2\frac{k}{a^2}\right]-3\left[\frac{\ddot a}{a}+\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2+\frac{k}{a^2}\right]=8\pi p-\Lambda\;\Rightarrow\;2\frac{\ddot a}{a}+\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2+\frac{k}{a^2}=-8\pi p+\Lambda

R_ii - R/2 equated with 8 pi p - Lambda; the printed first line again shows 2k/a for 2k/a^2, but the result is correct.

Squared expansion rate equals density plus vacuum term minus curvature term; a constraint on initial data.

## Worked examples

### Example 17.1 · intro · GA p.181 (pdf 198)

**Problem:** Get an equation for the expansion factor of a uniformly expanding medium using only Newtonian energy conservation.

**Method:** Write kinetic plus gravitational energy for a test mass on a sphere of radius r enclosing mass (4/3) pi r^3 rho, set r = a(t) x with fixed comoving x, and divide through by (1/2) m a^2 x^2.

**Key insight:** The first Friedmann equation is an energy equation: expansion-rate squared minus gravitational pull equals a constant set by the shell's energy, which plays the role of curvature.

**Result:** (a-dot/a)^2 + k/a^2 = 8 pi G rho/3 with k = -2U/(m x^2) constant.

**Concepts:** Newtonian derivation of the Friedmann equation, comoving coordinates, curvature constant k

### Example 17.2 · standard · GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199)

**Problem:** Derive the equations governing a(t) for Robertson-Walker spacetimes filled with a perfect fluid and vacuum energy.

**Method:** Use the orthonormal-frame Ricci components and scalar from the margin, the fluid tensor (rho, p, p, p) and g_00 = -1, g_ii = +1 in R_mu nu - (1/2) g_mu nu R = 8 pi T_mu nu - Lambda g_mu nu; simplify the 0-0 and i-i components separately.

**Key insight:** In the comoving orthonormal frame everything is diagonal and isotropic, so Einstein's ten equations collapse to just two; the time-time one loses its a-ddot and becomes a constraint.

**Result:** Friedmann equations 17.6 and 17.7.

**Concepts:** Friedmann equations, Ricci tensor of the Robertson-Walker metric, perfect-fluid energy-momentum tensor, Einstein field equation with cosmological constant

### Example 17.3 · intro · GA §17.2 p.183 (pdf 200)

**Problem:** Find how energy density and pressure respond to a change in scale factor using thermodynamics.

**Method:** Apply dU = T dS - p dV with dS = 0 to a comoving volume V = b a^3 holding energy rho V; differentiate with respect to time using the product rule and cancel common factors.

**Key insight:** Expansion lowers density in two ways: pure volume dilution (the 3 rho term) and pdV work done by the fluid's pressure (the 3p term).

**Result:** d rho/(rho + p) = -3 da/a (17.11).

**Concepts:** first law of thermodynamics, adiabatic expansion of the cosmic fluid, fluid equation

### Example 17.4 · standard · GA §17.2 p.184 (pdf 201)

**Problem:** Show the second Friedmann equation contains no information beyond the first once the fluid equation holds.

**Method:** Multiply F1 by a^2, differentiate in time, substitute rho-dot from the fluid equation, divide by a a-dot, then replace 8 pi rho/3 using F1 again.

**Key insight:** Constraint plus conservation implies the evolution equation; a learner solving cosmological models only ever needs F1, the fluid equation and an equation of state (provided a-dot is not zero).

**Result:** Intermediate 2 a-ddot/a = 2 Lambda/3 - 8 pi rho/3 - 8 pi p (17.15), and then exactly F2 (17.16).

**Concepts:** redundancy of the Friedmann equations, acceleration equation, fluid equation

### Example · intro · GA §17.3 p.184 (pdf 201)

**Problem:** Determine how dust and radiation energy densities scale with a, and write the Friedmann equations for a mixture.

**Method:** Integrate the fluid equation with p = 0 (ln rho = -3 ln a) and with p = rho/3 (ln rho = -4 ln a), normalize to values at t_0, cross-check with particle counting, and substitute the sum into F1 and the acceleration equation.

**Key insight:** Different equations of state translate into different power laws in a, so the balance of components changes as the universe grows, with radiation losing out first.

**Result:** rho_d proportional to a^-3, rho_r proportional to a^-4; eqns 17.22 and 17.23 (the latter needs a factor 2 on the radiation term).

**Concepts:** scaling of energy density with the scale factor, dust, radiation, Friedmann equations for a multi-component universe

### Example 17.5 · standard · GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202)

**Problem:** Rewrite the first Friedmann equation in terms of density fractions and use it to classify spatial geometry.

**Method:** Define rho_c = 3H^2/8 pi and rho_Lambda = Lambda/8 pi, divide F1 by H^2, use a^2 H^2 = a-dot^2 to reach k/a-dot^2 = Omega - 1, and read off k from the sign.

**Key insight:** A single dimensionless number, total density relative to critical, decides whether space is spherical, flat or hyperbolic, and because k never changes that verdict holds for all times while a-dot is nonzero.

**Result:** Omega < 1: k = -1; Omega = 1: k = 0; Omega > 1: k = +1.

**Concepts:** critical density, density parameter, density determines spatial geometry

### Example 17.5 (continued): present-day parameters and the age integral · standard · GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203)

**Problem:** Express the expansion history in present-day observables and find the cosmic time at which light of redshift z was emitted.

**Method:** Define Omega_j^(0) and Omega_k^(0) = -k/(a_0^2 H_0^2) to get the sum rule, rewrite 17.22 in terms of x = a/a_0, take the square root to get dx/dt, separate variables, and change the upper limit using x = 1/(1+z) with t = 0 at x = 0.

**Key insight:** Measured H_0 and a handful of Omegas fix the whole expansion history; ages follow from a one-dimensional integral whose integrand shows which component dominates at each epoch.

**Result:** Eqn 17.33; setting z = 0 gives the present age.

**Concepts:** curvature density parameter, cosmic sum rule, dimensionless Friedmann equation in density parameters, cosmic time as a function of redshift, age of the universe


## Analogies and intuitions

### Projectile launched from a planet: whether it escapes, just escapes or falls back depends on the sign of its total energy → curvature constant k · strong · GA p.181 (pdf 198)

Example 17.1 shows the expanding universe obeys the same energy balance as a shell of test masses flying outward against the gravity of the enclosed mass; the constant in place of curvature is minus twice the energy per unit mass per comoving length squared.

**Where it breaks down:** Only the first equation is reproduced; pressure's gravitational effect (rho + 3p) and Lambda have no Newtonian origin, the infinite uniform medium has no well-defined centre in Newtonian gravity, and in GR k describes spatial geometry rather than an energy. With Lambda the energy sign no longer decides the fate.

**App idea:** Expanding ball of dust with a launch-speed slider: shells coloured by energy sign, a live plot of a(t), and a label switching between 'closed', 'flat' and 'open' geometry as the energy crosses zero.

### Gas in a cylinder pushing a piston outward and cooling → fluid equation · useful · GA §17.2 p.183 (pdf 200)

A comoving box of cosmic fluid is treated like a thermally isolated gas whose volume grows as a^3: its internal energy drops by p dV, which is exactly the extra 3p term in the fluid equation.

**Where it breaks down:** In a homogeneous universe there is no piston or surrounding region that receives the work (every box is expanding identically), and energy is not globally conserved in GR; the pdV bookkeeping is local.

**App idea:** Side-by-side: a piston cylinder and a comoving cube in an expanding lattice; the learner drags a to expand both and watches energy density bars for p = 0, p = rho/3 and p = -rho fall at different rates.

### Counting marbles in a stretching box: fewer per volume, and for photons each marble also shrinks in energy → scaling of energy density with the scale factor · strong · GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202)

Margin notes rationalize a^-3 for dust (fixed particle number in a volume growing as a^3, constant rest energy) and a^-4 for radiation (same number dilution times the 1/a redshift of each photon).

**Where it breaks down:** Relies on particle number being conserved (no creation or annihilation) and on sharply non-relativistic versus massless particles; vacuum energy does not dilute at all and has no particle picture.

**App idea:** Grid of dots and wavy photons in a comoving box: as a grows, dots stay the same size while photon wavelengths stretch; counters show number density, energy per particle and their product on log-log axes with slopes -3 and -4.

### Balancing a budget with a single dial: total density versus a threshold → critical density · useful · GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203)

Rewriting the first equation as k/a-dot^2 = Omega - 1 turns the geometry question into whether the measured density is below, at or above a threshold set by the expansion rate.

**Where it breaks down:** The threshold itself changes with time (H changes), and exceeding it fixes geometry, not destiny, when Lambda is present.

**App idea:** Omega slider with a three-panel geometry preview (sphere, plane, saddle) and a triangle drawn on each showing angle sums above, equal to or below 180 degrees.

### The expansion factor as the radius of the universe → scale factor · useful · GA §17.1 p.183 (pdf 200)

The book repeatedly calls a(t) the effective or radius-like expansion factor to make volumes proportional to a^3 feel natural.

**Where it breaks down:** Only the closed k = +1 universe has a literal radius; for flat and open cases a is just a ratio of distances, and its overall normalization is arbitrary.

### Expansion stretching a fluid reduces its force per unit area → pressure · weak · GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199)

A margin note suggests the vielbein factors make the spatial stress components look like effective pressures falling as 1/a^2, as expansion should lower pressure.

**Where it breaks down:** Misleading as stated: orthonormal-frame pressure is just p, and how p falls with a depends on the equation of state (zero for dust, a^-4 for radiation, constant for vacuum); the a^2 factors belong to coordinate components.


## Misconceptions addressed

### The two Friedmann equations are independent laws that must both be imposed separately. · GA §17.2 p.184 (pdf 201)

**Correction:** Given energy conservation (the fluid equation) and a-dot not equal to zero, the second follows from differentiating the first; in practice one solves F1 plus the fluid equation plus an equation of state.

**Why tempting:** They come from different components of Einstein's equation and look different, one first-order and one second-order.

### All kinds of energy thin out with volume, so every density scales as a^-3. · GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202)

**Correction:** Only pressureless matter does; radiation falls as a^-4 because each photon also redshifts, and vacuum energy stays constant. The pressure term in the fluid equation controls the exponent.

**Why tempting:** Everyday intuition about spreading a fixed amount of stuff over a bigger volume.

### The Friedmann equation is intrinsically relativistic and has no classical counterpart. · GA p.181 (pdf 198)

**Correction:** Its first form follows from Newtonian energy conservation for an expanding sphere; GR adds the geometric meaning of k, the role of pressure as a source, and Lambda.

**Why tempting:** It is presented as a consequence of Einstein's equation with curvature tensors.

### Pressure pushes outward, so a high-pressure fluid should help the universe expand. · GA §17.2 p.184 (pdf 201)

**Correction:** A uniform pressure exerts no net force (no gradients), but it adds to the gravitating source: the acceleration equation involves rho + 3p, so positive pressure increases deceleration. Only negative pressure, as for vacuum energy, can accelerate expansion.

**Why tempting:** Pressure in a balloon or a gas cylinder does push walls outward.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### A universe with density above critical must eventually recollapse, and one below critical must expand forever. · GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203)

**Correction:** In this chapter Omega only fixes spatial geometry (k). With Lambda included, a closed geometry can expand forever and the fate must be found by solving the equations (Chapter 18).

**Why tempting:** True for dust-only models without Lambda, which is how it is often quoted.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### A universe could start open (Omega < 1) and later become closed as its density changes. · GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203)

**Correction:** k is a fixed constant and a-dot^2 is positive, so the sign of Omega - 1 is locked; Omega can drift toward or away from 1 but cannot cross it while the universe expands.

**Why tempting:** Omega is built from time-dependent densities and H, so it seems it could take any value later.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The constant k from the Newtonian argument is the same +1, 0, -1 label used in the Robertson-Walker metric. · GA p.181 (pdf 198)

**Correction:** In Example 17.1, k = -2U/(m x^2) is any real number with dimensions; only its sign corresponds to the RW label after rescaling a and the comoving coordinates.

**Why tempting:** The same letter is used in a formula with identical shape.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Energy is conserved for the universe as a whole, so the energy lost by radiation must go somewhere. · GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202)

**Correction:** The fluid equation is a local statement; in an expanding spacetime without a global time-translation symmetry there is no conserved total energy, and radiation energy in a comoving volume genuinely decreases.

**Why tempting:** The derivation uses the first law of thermodynamics, which sounds like global energy conservation.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

## Applications and observations

- **Age of the universe from the density parameters** (numerical-estimate, GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203)): The book notes that z = 0 in eqn 17.33 yields the present age but gives no numbers. Own estimate: for a flat universe with Omega_m = 0.315, Omega_Lambda = 0.685 and H_0 = 67.4 km/s/Mpc (Planck 2018 values, radiation negligible), the integral gives about 13.8 Gyr; a flat dust-only model (Exercise 17.4) gives 2/(3H_0), only about 9.7 Gyr for the same H_0, younger than the oldest stars. Key numbers: 1/H_0 = 14.5 Gyr for H_0 = 67.4 km/s/Mpc; LambdaCDM age = 13.8 Gyr; Einstein-de Sitter age = 9.7 Gyr (own estimates).
- **Critical density today** (numerical-estimate, GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202)): Own estimate from rho_c = 3H_0^2/(8 pi G): the universe needs only a few hydrogen atoms per cubic metre of total energy density to be spatially flat. Key numbers: rho_c = 9.2e-27 kg/m^3 (about 5.5 proton masses per m^3) for H_0 = 70 km/s/Mpc; 8.5e-27 kg/m^3 for H_0 = 67.4 (own estimates).
- **Measured curvature and composition** (observation, GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203)): Own addition: combined CMB and baryon acoustic oscillation data find the curvature parameter consistent with zero, so the observed universe is flat to high precision, with vacuum energy and matter providing the rest of the sum rule. The chapter's Omega classification is exactly the language used to report this. Key numbers: |Omega_k| below about 0.002; Omega_Lambda about 0.69; Omega_m about 0.31; Omega_r about 9e-5 including neutrinos (Planck 2018, own addition).
- **Matter-radiation equality** (numerical-estimate, GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202)): Own estimate implied by 17.21 and 17.18: because rho_r/rho_d scales as 1/a, radiation dominated at early times. Equality occurs at a/a_0 = Omega_r/Omega_m. Key numbers: a_eq/a_0 about 2.9e-4, so z_eq about 3400 (own estimate with Planck parameters).
- **Cooling of the cosmic microwave background** (observation, GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202)): Own addition linking to the a^-4 law: for blackbody radiation rho is proportional to T^4, so T falls as 1/a and today's 2.725 K background was about 3000 K when it was released at z of about 1100. The chapter's photon-counting margin note is the underlying reason. Key numbers: T_0 = 2.725 K; T(z) = T_0 (1+z); about 3000 K at z = 1100 (own addition).

## Notation and conventions

- **Units:** Geometrized units G = c = 1 in all relativistic formulas (8 pi rho, 8 pi p); Newton's G is kept only in the Newtonian warm-up (eqns 17.1-17.2). — Restore 8 pi G/c^2 or 8 pi G/c^4 as needed; rho_c = 3H^2/(8 pi G) in SI. (GA p.181 (pdf 198))
- **Metric signature and field equation form:** Signature (-,+,+,+) with g_00 = -1; Einstein's equation written R_mu nu - (1/2) g_mu nu R = 8 pi T_mu nu - Lambda g_mu nu, so positive Lambda is positive vacuum energy. — Consistent with GA ch15 eqn 15.1. (GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199))
- **Hatted indices:** Hats denote components in the orthonormal frame of comoving observers (t-hat, r-hat, theta-hat, phi-hat; 0-hat and i-hat); R_ii with repeated i is a single component, not a sum. (GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199))
- **Scale factor names and normalization:** a(t) is called the expansion factor, effective radius or radius-like expansion factor; a(t_0) is kept explicit rather than set to 1, and x = a(t)/a(t_0) is introduced for the dimensionless form. — Chapter 15 normalized a(t_0) = 1; the later V = b a^3 with b dimensionless implies a carries length units, so normalizations are not consistent across chapters. (GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203))
- **Curvature constant:** k takes values 0, +1, -1 for the Robertson-Walker geometries; in Example 17.1 the same letter denotes an unnormalized constant -2U/(m x^2). (GA p.181 (pdf 198))
- **Reference time and superscript (0):** t_0 is an arbitrary reference time, often but not necessarily the present; quantities there carry a superscript (0), e.g. rho_d^(0), Omega_j^(0), and H_0 = H(t_0) is the Hubble constant. — Evaluation times may lie before or after t_0. (GA §17.3 p.184 (pdf 201))
- **Component subscripts:** Subscripts d (dust), r (radiation), Lambda (vacuum), c (critical), k (curvature) on rho and Omega; Omega_j = rho_j/rho_c and total Omega = Omega_d + Omega_r + Omega_Lambda. — Many modern texts write m (matter) instead of d. (GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203))
- **Vacuum energy density:** rho_Lambda = Lambda/(8 pi); the text also loosely says the vacuum energy has energy density Lambda. — Keep the 8 pi to avoid factor errors. (GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202))
- **Curvature parameter sign:** Omega_k^(0) = -k/(a_0^2 H_0^2), so open (k = -1) universes have positive Omega_k. (GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203))
- **Dots:** An overdot means derivative with respect to cosmic (comoving proper) time t. (GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199))
- **Equation numbering:** Eqn 17.24 sits in a margin note beside and after 17.25; 17.34-17.35 in the summary repeat 17.6-17.7; 17.36 belongs to Exercise 17.2. (GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202))

## Teaching gems

### Derive the first Friedmann equation from the energy of a test mass on an expanding uniform sphere before touching Einstein's equation, identifying the curvature constant with minus the shell's energy. · GA p.181 (pdf 198)

**Why it works:** Learners reach the key equation with tools they trust and gain an immediate interpretation (bound versus unbound) for the sign of k.

**App idea:** Newtonian cosmology sandbox: set density and initial expansion speed for a ball of dust, watch shells expand or recollapse, and see the computed k and a(t) overlaid on the GR solution for the same parameters.

### Reduce Einstein's equation for a homogeneous universe to two lines by working in the comoving orthonormal frame where both the Ricci tensor and the fluid tensor are diagonal. · GA §17.1 p.182 (pdf 199)

**Why it works:** Shows that symmetry, not heavy computation, is what makes cosmology tractable; the a-ddot cancellation in the time-time component reveals the constraint nature of F1.

**App idea:** Interactive substitution table: rows for 0-0 and i-i components, click to drop in R, g and T entries and watch terms cancel with colour highlighting until F1 and F2 remain.

### Get the fluid equation from the first law applied to a comoving box, separating dilution (3 rho) from pressure work (3p). · GA §17.2 p.183 (pdf 200)

**Why it works:** Makes the pressure term physically legible and gives one master equation from which every dilution law follows by choosing p(rho).

**App idea:** Equation-of-state slider w from -1 to 1 with a comoving cube; plot log rho against log a with slope -3(1+w), marking dust, radiation, curvature-like and vacuum cases.

### Prove the second Friedmann equation is redundant by differentiating the first and inserting the fluid equation. · GA §17.2 p.184 (pdf 201)

**Why it works:** Teaches the constraint-plus-conservation structure of GR in miniature and tells learners which equations they actually need to solve models.

**App idea:** Step-through derivation card where each click performs one algebraic move (multiply, differentiate, substitute, divide, eliminate) with the current expression shown and a symbolic check at the end.

### Justify rho proportional to a^-3 and a^-4 twice: formally from thermodynamics and informally by counting particles and redshifting photons. · GA §17.3 p.185 (pdf 202)

**Why it works:** Two independent routes to the same answer build confidence and give the tutor a quick intuitive explanation for any level of learner.

**App idea:** Log-log race of energy densities for radiation, matter and vacuum versus a, with a draggable present-day marker and highlighted equality epochs where the lines cross.

### Collapse the first Friedmann equation to k/a-dot^2 = Omega - 1 so that one measured ratio determines spatial geometry. · GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203)

**Why it works:** Connects the abstract k to a measurable quantity and to the observational vocabulary (Omega) learners meet in news and papers.

**App idea:** Omega_m and Omega_Lambda sliders on a triangle-plane chart with regions for closed, flat and open geometry and a live embedded surface (sphere, plane, saddle) with a geodesic triangle.

### Turn the dimensionless Friedmann equation into an integral that gives the emission time of light of redshift z and, at z = 0, the age of the universe. · GA §17.3 p.186 (pdf 203)

**Why it works:** Directly links theory to an observable (redshift) and a headline number (cosmic age) using a single calculation.

**App idea:** Cosmic age calculator: sliders for H_0 and the four Omegas constrained by the sum rule; outputs age, a(t) curve and t(z) for a draggable z, with presets for Einstein-de Sitter, radiation-only and LambdaCDM.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Printed eqn 17.23 gives the radiation term the same coefficient as dust; the correct acceleration equation has rho + 3p, which is 2 rho_r for radiation.** — Anyone deriving 17.23 from 17.15 gets a different answer and may doubt their algebra; using the printed version underestimates radiation's deceleration. *Suggestion:* Present the general form a-ddot/a = -(4 pi/3)(rho + 3p) + Lambda/3 first, then substitute each component's pressure explicitly.
- **Printed text defines rho_c^(0) = 8 pi H_0/3, which is inverted and unsquared relative to 17.24; eqn 17.31 also omits the overall factor H_0^2 on its right-hand side.** — Dimensional inconsistency that breaks the passage from 17.22 to 17.32. *Suggestion:* Use rho_c^(0) = 3H_0^2/(8 pi) and include H_0^2 in 17.31; have learners dimension-check each line.
- **The Ricci margin note and the first line of eqn 17.5 print 2k/a instead of 2k/a^2 (the same slip appears in Chapter 16's margin).** — Mixed powers of a make the space-space equation look dimensionally wrong. *Suggestion:* Check by computing R = -R_tt + 3R_ii and comparing with the printed scalar; show the corrected components.
- **Example 17.1 uses U both for total energy and (in the printed text) for the potential energy, and relies silently on the shell theorem, constant enclosed mass (rho a^3 fixed) and a Newtonian infinite medium.** — Confusion over symbols and over why an infinite universe can be treated like a sphere with a centre. *Suggestion:* Rename potential energy V, state the constant-mass assumption, and explain that any point can serve as centre because of homogeneity; flag this as a heuristic.
- **The margin claim that vielbein factors make effective pressures fall as 1/a^2 conflates coordinate and orthonormal components.** — Learners may think all pressures scale as a^-2 regardless of the fluid, contradicting the dust and radiation laws two pages later. *Suggestion:* Replace with: orthonormal pressure is p, and its time dependence is fixed by the fluid equation plus equation of state.
- **The fluid equation is introduced via thermodynamics as if it were extra physics, without noting it is the time component of the covariant conservation law for T (guaranteed by the Bianchi identity). The redundancy argument also assumes a-dot is not zero.** — Misses why the two Friedmann equations must be consistent, and why static solutions such as Einstein's static universe need both equations. *Suggestion:* After Example 17.4, show that the divergence of the perfect-fluid tensor in the Robertson-Walker metric gives the same equation, and discuss the a-dot = 0 exception.
- **Only dust and radiation are treated; vacuum energy is carried along as Lambda without showing p = -rho gives constant density, and the general law rho proportional to a^(-3(1+w)) is never stated even though Exercise 17.3 uses an a^-2 fluid.** — Learners do not see the unifying pattern or where curvature-like and vacuum-like behaviours fit. *Suggestion:* Add a w-parameter slide: integrate 17.11 with p = w rho, then tabulate w = 0, 1/3, -1/3, -1.
- **The Omega classification is stated purely as geometry; nothing warns that geometry and fate decouple once Lambda is present, and the reader may import the common but outdated 'closed means recollapse' rule.** — Misreads modern cosmology results (flat, accelerating) or older popular accounts. *Suggestion:* Pair the geometry table with a preview plot of a(t) for closed models with and without Lambda.
- **The age integral sets t = 0 at infinite redshift, implicitly assuming a big-bang start with a reaching zero; the chapter provides no numerical values for H_0, rho_c, Omegas or the resulting age.** — No sense of scale, and confusion if a model (such as pure de Sitter) has no a = 0 moment in the finite past. *Suggestion:* Evaluate the integral numerically for a few presets (Einstein-de Sitter, LambdaCDM) and note when it diverges.
- **Exercise 17.2 uses a single-particle (Boltzmann) partition function with fixed particle number, although real photons have zero chemical potential and variable number.** — The ratio p = rho/3 comes out right, but learners may think photon energy per particle is exactly 3 k_B T (the Bose-Einstein mean is about 2.7 k_B T). *Suggestion:* Mention that the ratio is robust (it follows from the massless dispersion relation) while absolute values need Bose-Einstein statistics.
- **Before eqn 17.28 the text says it substitutes for a^2 using eqn 17.25; what is actually used is a^2 H^2 = a-dot^2 (equivalently a^2 rho_c = 3 a-dot^2/(8 pi)), which turns k/(a^2 H^2) into k/a-dot^2.** — A learner who tries to replace a^2 alone will not reach 17.28 and may think a step is missing. *Suggestion:* State the identity a H = a-dot explicitly when moving from 17.27 to 17.28.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'We know the shape of space for a homogeneous universe; what else do we need to predict how it expands?' Aim for the answer 'what it is filled with'.
- For beginners, start with the Newtonian ball of dust (Example 17.1) and ask the learner to predict what happens for positive, zero and negative total energy before revealing the equation.
- Suggested order: Newtonian energy picture -> perfect fluid and Lambda as sources -> two components of Einstein's equation -> fluid equation from a comoving box -> redundancy -> dust and radiation scalings -> mixture equation -> critical density and Omega -> age integral.
- Check understanding: ask why the time-time component contains no a-ddot, and what that means for choosing initial conditions.
- Check understanding: ask the learner to explain a^-4 for radiation in one sentence without equations (number dilution plus redshift).
- When presenting 17.23, 17.31 or rho_c^(0), use the corrected forms and, for advanced learners, invite them to spot the printed errors by dimensional analysis or by rederiving from 17.15.
- Common learner question: 'Where does the energy lost by radiation go?' Answer that the fluid equation is local and GR has no global energy conservation for expanding spacetimes; avoid claiming it goes into gravity unless discussing pseudo-tensors.
- Common learner question: 'Why does pressure slow expansion if it pushes outward?' Emphasize uniform pressure has no gradient, and in GR pressure gravitates (rho + 3p).
- Common learner question: 'Is Omega = 1 a coincidence?' Explain that the sign of Omega - 1 is locked, but its size |Omega - 1| grows with time during matter- or radiation-dominated expansion, so near-flatness today requires extreme flatness early on (a preview of the flatness problem, not covered here).
- Link to observations early for motivated learners: quote H_0 about 70 km/s/Mpc, critical density of a few protons per cubic metre, and measured flatness, flagging these as not from the chapter.
- Before Chapter 18, have the learner solve F1 by hand for flat dust (a proportional to t^(2/3)) and flat radiation (a proportional to t^(1/2)) to cement the power-law link between dilution and expansion.
