# GA ch38 · Forms, chains, and Stokes' theorem

> Rebuilds integration on the Part V machinery: every multiple integral becomes the pairing of a p-form with the p-dimensional region (a chain) it is evaluated on, the boundary operator is introduced with the rule that boundaries have no boundary, and the generalized Stokes theorem, the integral of d(Omega) over C equals the integral of Omega over the boundary of C, is stated as the single statement behind the fundamental theorem of calculus, Green, Kelvin-Stokes and Gauss.

**Pages:** printed 397–410 · pdf 414–427 · **Difficulty:** math 4/5, conceptual 4/5, novice-friendliness 2/5

Short but dense and abstract: it assumes fluency with p-forms, p-vectors, ordered-index sums, Hodge duals and the exterior derivative from Chapters 32-37, and moves quickly between vector calculus, spacetime forms and topology. The telescoping opening and Fig. 38.1 are gentle, but Section 38.3 compresses the pullback definition of integration into a few lines and the orientation rule is stated tersely (with an index mismatch). Topological caveats are relegated to margin notes whose statements about simple connectivity are imprecise beyond degree one. Several misprints (Stokes' dates, a cross instead of a wedge, 'Example 12', f(x,y,x), 'ball S^2') add friction. Exercise 38.4 is substantially harder than anything in the main text.

## Role in the book

Final chapter of Part V (Geometry); a margin box calls it the logical end point of that part and says it can be skipped on a first pass. It gathers the p-forms, p-vectors and tube/cell pictures of Chapter 32, the exterior derivative and dd = 0 of Chapter 33, and the volume forms and Hodge duals of Chapter 37, and turns them into a theory of integration. It also explains after the fact where the Jacobian of Section 5.4 comes from. Its main customer is Chapter 43, where Stokes' theorem expresses charge conservation and the geometric meaning of the Bianchi identity, and Exercise 38.4 previews holonomy (rotation of a transported vector by enclosed curvature) in a two-dimensional setting.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can explain with a telescoping sum why integrating a derivative over an interval leaves only the end values, with a plus sign at the far end and a minus sign at the near end.
- Reader can rewrite a work integral as the integral of a 1-form and a flux integral as the integral of a 2-form, and say what each integral counts in the sheet, tube and cell pictures.
- Reader can derive the two-dimensional Jacobian determinant by expanding a wedge of two transformed basis 1-forms and using antisymmetry.
- Reader can evaluate the integral of an n-form over a parametrized n-dimensional region as the parameter integral of the pairing between the form and the wedge of the tangent vectors dP/dlambda^i.
- Reader can write the flat-spacetime volume 4-form and the 3-forms dSigma_mu, and interpret J^mu dSigma_mu as a flux 3-form ready to be integrated over a hypersurface.
- Reader can define chains, cycles and boundaries, apply the boundary operator to discs, cylinders, curves and spheres, and argue that the boundary of a boundary vanishes.
- Reader can state the generalized Stokes theorem with its boundary-orientation rule and describe the duality in which d raises form degree while the boundary operator lowers chain dimension.
- Reader can recover the fundamental theorem of calculus, Green's theorem, the Kelvin-Stokes theorem and the divergence theorem as special cases of the generalized theorem.
- Reader can distinguish closed from exact forms, quote the Poincaré lemma, and show that the vortex 1-form on the punctured plane is closed but not exact.
- Reader can use Green's theorem on the unit sphere to show that parallel transport round a closed loop rotates a vector by an angle equal to the enclosed area.

## Teaching approach

A consolidation chapter. It opens with the familiar family of vector-calculus integral theorems, isolates what they share (derivative integrated inside equals the function summed with signs on the edge), announces the one-line generalization at once, and then rebuilds integration out of Part V objects so that the one-liner can be read literally. The integrand is re-identified as a form that already contains its differentials; the domain is a parametrized region whose infinitesimal piece is a wedge of tangent vectors; the integral is their pairing, which makes the Jacobian automatic rather than a rule. Chains and the boundary operator are introduced by pictures and short lists rather than by algebraic topology, Stokes' theorem is stated without proof, and the theorem is exercised on volume and area elements and on recovering the divergence theorem. Topological subtleties (closed versus exact, cycles that are not boundaries) and the holonomy application live in margin notes and exercises.

**Style:** geometric-first, index-free-abstract, axiomatic-mathematical, spiral-revisit, example-driven, diagram-driven, conversational-informal

**Narrative arc**

1. Frame: epigraph on integrating infinitesimals and the long-s origin of the integral sign present integration as summation.
2. Motivating pattern: the fundamental theorem of calculus depends only on signed end values; slicing the interval into a telescoping sum shows why interior pieces cancel.
3. Extend by analogy to three dimensions (volume integral of a gradient equals a surface term, hence the divergence theorem) and to two dimensions (area integral of gradient-cross-area equals minus a loop integral, hence the curl theorem); note the family resemblance and the mismatched appearances.
4. Announce the destination immediately: the integral of dOmega over C equals the integral of Omega over the boundary of C (eqn 38.7).
5. Upgrade notation: a work integral is the integral of a 1-form and a flux integral is the integral of a 2-form; defend writing an integral with no explicit dx.
6. Promise the dual picture: integrands are forms, domains are chains, and their pairing is a number (eqn 38.12).
7. Geometric meaning: a line integral counts sheets crossed, a surface integral counts tubes pierced, a volume integral counts cells enclosed.
8. First payoff: the Jacobian of a change of variables drops out of wedge-product antisymmetry (Example 38.1).
9. Anatomy of the integrand: a function times a wedge of coordinate 1-forms; the spacetime volume 4-form, the 3-forms dSigma_mu and the flux 3-form J^mu dSigma_mu (Example 38.2).
10. Anatomy of the domain: an infinitesimal parallelepiped built from tangent vectors to a parametrized surface; the integral defined as the parameter integral of the pairing; the pairing of coordinate wedges with tangent wedges is the Jacobian (eqns 38.30-38.32).
11. Sanity check: the integral of df along a curve returns the end-value difference (Example 38.3).
12. Domains as dual objects: define boundary, list examples and draw them with the boundary operator (Example 38.4, Fig. 38.1).
13. Chains C_n, the dimension-lowering boundary operator, cycles Z_n, boundaries B_n, and the conclusion that applying the boundary operator twice gives zero.
14. Mirror operator: the exterior derivative raises degree; recompute curl-like and divergence-like components in 3D and verify dd = 0 (Example 38.5); present dd = 0 as the dual of the double-boundary rule; closed/exact forms and the Poincaré lemma in a margin note.
15. State Stokes' theorem with its boundary orientation rule; decline to prove it and point to Needham and Spivak.
16. Exercise the theorem: metric volume integrals with sqrt(g) (Example 38.6), surface integrals via an area 2-form (Example 38.7), recovery of the endpoint, surface-edge and volume-surface theorems and in detail the divergence theorem (Example 38.8, Fig. 38.2).
17. Forward look to charge conservation and curvature constraints in Part VI; exercises on Kelvin-Stokes, the sphere, the vortex form, and holonomy on the sphere.

**Signature moves**

- Stating the final theorem (eqn 38.7) near the start so every later definition visibly serves one goal.
- Openly defending the unconventional notation of an integral without a trailing differential, by folding the differentials into the form.
- Reusing the Chapter 32 pictures of forms (planes, tubes, cells) to turn integration into counting how many pieces of the form the domain meets.
- Presenting the Jacobian as a by-product that certifies the forms formalism, rather than as a correction factor to memorize.
- Defining the integral as a literal instance of the form-vector pairing, so that 'domains are dual to integrands' feels like the old statement that 1-forms eat vectors.
- Pictorial boundary algebra (Fig. 38.1): a column of shapes with the boundary operator applied, empty boundaries drawn as a dissolving puff borrowed from Douglas Adams.
- Placing the two operators side by side (d raises degree, boundary lowers dimension; dd = 0 mirrors the double-boundary rule) so that Stokes reads as an adjointness statement.
- Keeping rigorous topology (simplicial complexes, homology, the Poincaré lemma) in margin notes and exercises so the main line stays short.
- Closing the exercise set with a relativity-flavoured payoff: Stokes' theorem turns parallel transport round a loop on a sphere into a rotation by the enclosed area.

## Section by section

### §38.1 Integration — p.397 (pdf 414)

Starts from the fundamental theorem of calculus and asks why only the end values survive and why they carry opposite signs; slicing the interval gives a telescoping sum. The same pattern is pushed to three dimensions (a gradient integrated over a volume equals the function integrated over the oriented bounding surface, which yields the divergence theorem) and to two dimensions (a gradient-cross-area integral equals minus a loop integral, which yields the curl theorem). The three results clearly share one idea but look different, so the chapter announces the unifying statement and upgrades notation: work integrals become integrals of 1-forms, flux integrals become integrals of 2-forms written with wedges, and integrands (forms) are said to be dual to domains (chains). A margin box marks the chapter as optional on first reading and needed for Chapter 43; a margin note restricts attention to flat space.

**Concepts:** fundamental theorem of calculus, telescoping-sum picture of boundary terms, volume gradient theorem, divergence theorem, Kelvin-Stokes theorem, generalized Stokes theorem, integration of differential forms, duality between forms and chains

**Key moves**
- Discretize the integral of df/dx into differences f(x_{i+1}) - f(x_i); adjacent terms cancel leaving f(b) - f(a).
- Interpret the sign flip at the two ends as the orientation of the boundary; in 3D the outward surface element plays that role.
- Set f = a^j in the vector gradient theorem and sum over j to get the divergence theorem.
- Write the 2D analogue with a cross product, take its i-th component with epsilon_{ikm}, set f = a^j and contract i with j to get the curl theorem.
- Announce the target eqn 38.7 before building any machinery.
- Identify F_x dx + F_y dy + F_z dz as a 1-form and G_x dy^dz + ... as a 2-form, so both integrals become integral of a form over a domain.
- State the pairing slogan: (form, chain) gives a number, namely the multiple integral.

### §38.2 Integrating over forms — p.400 (pdf 417)

Recalls the pictures of forms of each degree (a function; stacked planes; a lattice of tubes; an array of cells) and reads each integral as a count: a curve integral of a 1-form counts the planes the curve crosses, a surface integral of a 2-form counts the tubes that pierce the surface, a volume integral of a 3-form counts the cells enclosed. Accepting this view explains the Jacobian: in new coordinates the integration cells must still tile the region, and the determinant that guarantees this is exactly what the wedge product generates. Example 38.1 expands the wedge of two transformed basis 1-forms and finds the 2x2 Jacobian; a margin note writes the general determinant as a Levi-Civita sum.

**Concepts:** counting interpretation of form integrals, Jacobian determinant, Jacobian from wedge-product antisymmetry, Levi-Civita symbol

**Key moves**
- Assign to each form degree a geometric picture and to each integral a counting rule (eqns 38.13-38.15).
- Recall that a coordinate change requires a Jacobian so that the new cells cover the region without gaps (eqns 38.16-38.17).
- Write each old basis 1-form as a linear combination of new ones via the chain rule (eqn 38.19).
- Wedge them together: squares vanish, the cross terms combine with a relative minus sign by antisymmetry, and the coefficient is the determinant (eqn 38.20).

### §38.3 Anatomy of an integral — p.401 (pdf 418)

Dissects a general multiple integral over a parametrized region into an integrand and a domain. The integrand, function times the product of differentials, is an n-form; Example 38.2 shows the flat-spacetime volume 4-form (restricted versus unrestricted sums with 1/4!), the 3-forms dSigma_mu dual to basis vectors, and the flux 3-form J^mu dSigma_mu. The domain is built locally as a wedge of the tangent vectors dP/dlambda^i scaled by parameter steps, an infinitesimal n-parallelepiped. The integral is then defined as the parameter integral of the pairing between the form and that wedge; pairing a wedge of coordinate 1-forms with the tangent wedge gives the Jacobian of coordinates with respect to parameters, so eqn 38.21 is recovered. Example 38.3 checks the definition on the integral of df along a curve, and margin notes preview the flux of a 2-form F through a small coordinate square, used in Chapter 43, and note that integrals of exact 1-forms are path independent. Conclusion: domains are dual to forms.

**Concepts:** integral as pairing of a form with tangent n-vectors, parametrized surface, p-vector, inner product of a p-form and a p-vector, volume form, hypersurface 3-form dSigma_mu, flux 3-form J^mu dSigma_mu, path independence of integrals of exact 1-forms

**Key moves**
- Write the general integral with an explicit Jacobian of x with respect to lambda (eqn 38.21) and split it into four claims: integrand is an n-form, domain is an n-vector, pairing reproduces the integrand with Jacobian, geometrically the integral counts cells inside the parallelepiped.
- Express the volume 4-form as (1/4!) epsilon_{mu nu alpha beta} dx^mu ^ dx^nu ^ dx^alpha ^ dx^beta and collapse it to dt^dx^dy^dz (eqn 38.23).
- Build dSigma_mu = (1/3!) epsilon_{mu alpha beta gamma} dx^alpha ^ dx^beta ^ dx^gamma and contract with J^mu (eqns 38.24-38.25).
- Displace a point along each parameter direction, wedge the tangent vectors, and pull the parameter steps outside (eqn 38.27).
- Define the integral as the iterated parameter integral of the pairing (eqn 38.30).
- Show the pairing of dx^1^...^dx^n with the tangent wedge equals the Jacobian determinant (eqn 38.32).
- Test on the integral of df: the pairing gives df/dlambda and the integral gives f at the end minus f at the start (eqn 38.33).

### §38.4 Boundaries and chains — p.404 (pdf 421)

Defines the boundary of a region as its non-interior points and treats the boundary operator as a machine: a disc gives a circle, a finite cylinder two circles, a curve its two end points, a sphere nothing (Example 38.4, Fig. 38.1). The integration domains are named chains, from 0-chains (points) to n-chains (n-volumes); a margin note mentions their origin as formal combinations of simplices in algebraic topology but says they can be thought of simply as spaces. The boundary operator lowers dimension by one. Chains with no boundary are cycles; chains that bound something are boundaries; every boundary is itself boundary-free, so applying the operator twice always gives zero. A margin note asks whether every cycle bounds and answers in terms of simple connectedness.

**Concepts:** boundary of a region, boundary operator, chain, cycle, boundary chain, boundary of a boundary is zero, simply connected space

**Key moves**
- Define the boundary as the set of non-interior points and give four concrete cases.
- List chains by dimension (eqn 38.34) and write the boundary operator as dimension-lowering (eqn 38.35).
- Name cycles (no boundary) and boundaries (B_n is the boundary of some C_{n+1}) (eqn 38.36).
- Observe that a boundary has no boundary (eqn 38.37) and, since C_{n+1} was arbitrary, conclude the operator squares to zero (eqn 38.38).

### §38.5 Stokes' theorem — p.405 (pdf 422)

Brings back the exterior derivative as the degree-raising counterpart of the boundary operator. Example 38.5 applies d to a general 1-form and 2-form in three dimensions, producing curl-like and divergence-like coefficients, and checks that a second d gives zero; dd = 0 is presented as the dual of the double-boundary rule, with closed and exact forms and the Poincaré lemma in a margin note. Stokes' theorem is then stated: for an n-form Omega and an (n+1)-chain C, integrating Omega over the boundary equals integrating dOmega over C, with the boundary oriented by dropping the first (outward) tangent direction. No proof is given. Examples 38.6 and 38.7 build the metric volume integral with sqrt(g) and a surface integral from a 2-form that yields the unit normal dotted into the area element; Example 38.8, with Fig. 38.2, recovers the endpoint theorem for a curve, the surface-to-edge theorem, the volume-to-surface theorem, and in detail the divergence theorem. The section closes by pointing to charge conservation and curvature constraints in Part VI.

**Concepts:** exterior derivative, nilpotency of the exterior derivative, duality between d and the boundary operator, closed form, exact form, Poincaré lemma, generalized Stokes theorem, induced boundary orientation, metric volume element, surface area 2-form, divergence theorem

**Key moves**
- Note the parallel: boundary maps (n+1)-chains to n-chains, d maps n-forms to (n+1)-forms.
- Compute dY for Y = A dx + B dy + C dz, reorder wedges and collect curl components (eqn 38.40); compute dX for a 2-form and get a divergence (eqn 38.41).
- State dd = 0 (eqn 38.42) and call it dual to the double-boundary rule.
- State Stokes' theorem (eqn 38.43) with the orientation convention (eqns 38.44-38.45).
- Insert box vectors dx e_x, dy e_y, dz e_z into sqrt(g) dx^dy^dz to get the invariant volume element (eqns 38.46-38.47).
- Fill the volume 3-form with two surface vectors and the unit normal to get a 2-form area element (eqns 38.48-38.50).
- Apply Stokes to f, f dx^i and f dx^i^dx^j for the three geometries of Fig. 38.2 (eqns 38.51-38.53).
- Take the flux 2-form A_x dy^dz + A_y dz^dx + A_z dx^dy, apply d, and read off the divergence theorem (eqns 38.54-38.57).

### Chapter summary — p.408 (pdf 425)

Four bullet points: integrals are pairings of n-forms with the n-vectors representing their domains; domains are chains whose edges come from the boundary operator; the double-boundary rule says boundaries of boundaries vanish; and Stokes' theorem is restated (eqn 38.58).

**Concepts:** integration of differential forms, chain, boundary of a boundary is zero, generalized Stokes theorem

**Key moves**
- Restate the chapter's four take-aways without new material.

### Exercises — p.408 (pdf 425)

Four problems that apply the theorem: derive the vector curl theorem from Stokes for a 1-form; show that exact 2-forms integrate to zero over the sphere and that x^1 dx^2^dx^3 is not exact there; show the planar vortex 1-form is closed yet not exact because the origin must be removed; and a multi-part holonomy problem in which the geodesic equation on the unit sphere in an orthonormal frame, Green's theorem and Stokes combine to show that a vector carried round a closed loop is rotated by the enclosed area, with a remark that the result matters for geometric phases in magnetism.

**Concepts:** Kelvin-Stokes theorem, exact form, closed form, Poincaré lemma, Green's theorem in the plane, vortex 1-form, holonomy of parallel transport on the sphere

**Key moves**
- Exercise 38.1: apply Stokes to A_i dx^i in flat 3D.
- Exercise 38.2: use the empty boundary of S^2 to kill integrals of exact forms, then exhibit a non-zero integral.
- Exercise 38.3: verify d(phi) = 0 for the vortex form while its loop integral round the origin is non-zero.
- Exercise 38.4: connection coefficients on the sphere, matrix-exponential solution, Green's theorem, rotation by enclosed area.

## Concepts

### fundamental theorem of calculus

*theorem · revisited* · also: FTC, Newton-Leibniz formula

For a differentiable function f on [a, b], the integral of its derivative over the interval equals f(b) - f(a): only boundary values survive, the upper end counted positively and the lower end negatively.

**How introduced:** Opening example of the chapter, used as the prototype whose structure (derivative inside, signed boundary values outside) every later theorem copies.

$$
\int_a^b \frac{df}{dx}\,dx = f(b)-f(a)
$$

**Notes:** Re-read at the end as the 0-form case of the generalized Stokes theorem (Examples 38.3 and 38.8).

**Where:** GA §38.1 p.397 (pdf 414)

### telescoping-sum picture of boundary terms

*technique · introduced* · also: slicing argument, cancellation of interior contributions

Approximate the integral of a derivative by a sum of differences between neighbouring sample values; every interior value appears once with each sign and cancels, leaving only the difference of the extreme values.

**How introduced:** Offered to explain the plus and minus signs in the fundamental theorem; later invoked verbally for the 2D theorem (interior gradients cancel, leaving circulation round the edge).

**Prerequisites:** fundamental theorem of calculus

$$
\int_a^b \frac{df}{dx}dx \approx \Delta x\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\frac{f(x_{i+1})-f(x_i)}{\Delta x}
$$

**Notes:** This is the discrete seed of both the boundary operator with signs and Stokes' theorem; the book does not return to it explicitly.

**Where:** GA §38.1 p.397 (pdf 414); GA §38.1 p.398 (pdf 415)

### volume gradient theorem

*identity · introduced* · also: gradient theorem for volumes, integral of a gradient over a volume

The volume integral of the gradient of a scalar equals the integral of the scalar times the outward vector surface element over the closed bounding surface; componentwise, the integral of the partial derivative of f equals the integral of f dS^i.

**How introduced:** Stated as the 3D generalization of the fundamental theorem; the sign flip of the 1D case is carried by the reversal of the outward normal on opposite faces.

**Prerequisites:** fundamental theorem of calculus

$$
\int \vec\nabla f\,dV = \oint f\,d\vec S
$$

$$
\int \partial_i f\,dV = \oint f\,dS^i
$$

**Where:** GA §38.1 p.397 (pdf 414); GA §38.1 p.398 (pdf 415)

### divergence theorem

*theorem · developed* · also: Gauss's theorem, Ostrogradsky theorem

For a vector field on a region V with closed boundary surface, the volume integral of its divergence equals the outward flux through the boundary.

**How introduced:** First obtained from the volume gradient theorem by setting f equal to each component and summing; later re-derived in Example 38.8 by applying d to the flux 2-form A_x dy^dz + A_y dz^dx + A_z dx^dy.

**Prerequisites:** volume gradient theorem, generalized Stokes theorem, exterior derivative

$$
\int_V \vec\nabla\cdot\vec a\,dV = \oint_{\partial V}\vec a\cdot d\vec S
$$

**Where:** GA §38.1 p.398 (pdf 415); GA §38.5 p.407 (pdf 424)

### Kelvin-Stokes theorem

*theorem · developed* · also: Stokes' theorem (vector calculus), curl theorem

For a vector field and an oriented surface S bounded by a closed curve, the flux of the curl through S equals the circulation of the field round the boundary, with the curve orientation fixed by the right-hand rule.

**How introduced:** Reached from an intermediate identity (area integral of gradient cross area element equals minus the loop integral of f) by taking components with the Levi-Civita symbol and contracting; set as Exercise 38.1 to rederive from the generalized theorem using a 1-form.

**Prerequisites:** telescoping-sum picture of boundary terms, Levi-Civita symbol

$$
\int_S (\vec\nabla\times\vec a)\cdot d\vec S = \oint_{\partial S}\vec a\cdot d\vec\ell
$$

$$
\int\vec\nabla f\times d\vec S = -\oint f\,d\vec\ell
$$

**Where:** GA §38.1 p.398 (pdf 415); GA p.408 (pdf 425)

### differential form as integrand

*mathematical-object · core* · also: p-form, integrand form

A p-form is a totally antisymmetric tensor that takes p vectors and returns a number; as an integrand, the function and the product of coordinate differentials together make up one form such as f dx^1 ^ ... ^ dx^p.

**How introduced:** Motivated by rewriting work and flux integrals: the combination F_x dx + F_y dy + F_z dz is recognised as a 1-form and the flux combination as a 2-form, and the absence of a separate differential in the integral is defended.

**Prerequisites:** wedge product

$$
\tilde\alpha = f(x^1,\dots,x^n)\,\mathbf d x^1\wedge\dots\wedge\mathbf d x^n
$$

**Notes:** Forms were introduced in GA ch32; the new point here is that they are the natural thing to integrate.

**Where:** GA §38.1 p.399 (pdf 416); GA §38.3 p.402 (pdf 419)

### wedge product

*operation · revisited* · also: exterior product

Antisymmetric product of forms (or of vectors): a ^ b = -b ^ a for 1-forms, so any repeated factor gives zero; wedges of basis 1-forms build basis p-forms, wedges of vectors build p-vectors.

**How introduced:** Relied on throughout: to write area elements as dy^dz etc., to generate the Jacobian, and to assemble tangent parallelepipeds.

$$
\mathbf d x\wedge\mathbf d y = -\mathbf d y\wedge\mathbf d x
$$

**Where:** GA §38.1 p.399 (pdf 416); GA §38.2 p.401 (pdf 418); GA §38.3 p.403 (pdf 420)

### line integral as integral of a 1-form

*technique · developed* · also: work integral of a 1-form

The integral of F . dr along a curve C is the integral over C of the 1-form F_x dx + F_y dy + F_z dz; the form contains the differentials.

**How introduced:** Introduced as the first notational upgrade, using the work done by a force in Cartesian coordinates.

**Prerequisites:** differential form as integrand

$$
\int_C \boldsymbol F\cdot d\boldsymbol r = \int_C \tilde\alpha,\quad \tilde\alpha = F_x\mathbf dx+F_y\mathbf dy+F_z\mathbf dz
$$

**Where:** GA §38.1 p.399 (pdf 416)

### flux integral as integral of a 2-form

*technique · developed* · also: surface integral of a 2-form

The flux integral of G . dS over a surface is the integral of the 2-form G_x dy^dz + G_y dz^dx + G_z dx^dy, whose components are the Hodge-dual assignment of the vector G to oriented coordinate planes.

**How introduced:** Second notational upgrade, recalling from Chapters 32 and 37 that oriented area elements are bivectors and flux is naturally a 2-form.

**Prerequisites:** differential form as integrand, wedge product

$$
\int_S \boldsymbol G\cdot d\boldsymbol S = \int_S (G_x\mathbf dy\wedge\mathbf dz + G_y\mathbf dz\wedge\mathbf dx + G_z\mathbf dx\wedge\mathbf dy)
$$

**Where:** GA §38.1 p.399 (pdf 416); GA §38.5 p.407 (pdf 424)

### counting interpretation of form integrals

*principle · developed* · also: sheets crossed, tubes pierced, cells enclosed

Geometrically, integrating a 1-form along a curve counts (with sign) the level sheets the curve crosses, integrating a 2-form over a surface counts the tubes that pass through it, and integrating a 3-form over a volume counts the cells it contains.

**How introduced:** Stated at the start of Section 38.2 by recalling the pictures of forms of each degree from Chapter 32.

**Prerequisites:** differential form as integrand

$$
\int_{\rm line}\tilde Y = \#\text{sheets cut}
$$

$$
\int_{\rm surface}\tilde X=\#\text{tubes cut}
$$

$$
\int_{\rm volume}\tilde W = \#\text{cells contained}
$$

**Notes:** The counts are signed and fractional in general; orientation decides the sign.

**Where:** GA §38.2 p.400 (pdf 417); GA §38.3 p.403 (pdf 420)

### Jacobian determinant

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: Jacobian, functional determinant

For a change of coordinates, the determinant of the matrix of partial derivatives of one set with respect to the other; it rescales the product of differentials so that new-coordinate cells tile the region exactly.

**How introduced:** Posed as a puzzle from ordinary multiple integration (why is there an extra factor?), then explained by the algebra of forms.

**Prerequisites:** Levi-Civita symbol

$$
J = \frac{\partial(x^0,\dots,x^n)}{\partial(y^0,\dots,y^n)} = \det\left[\frac{\partial x^\mu}{\partial y^\nu}\right]

$$
J = \varepsilon_{i_0 i_1\dots i_n}\frac{\partial x^{i_0}}{\partial y^0}\cdots\frac{\partial x^{i_n}}{\partial y^n}

**Notes:** First met in GA ch5 §5.4.

**Where:** GA §38.2 p.400 (pdf 417); GA §38.2 p.401 (pdf 418); GA §38.3 p.403 (pdf 420)

### Jacobian from wedge-product antisymmetry

*technique · developed* · also: Jacobian generated by forms

Substituting the chain-rule expansion of each old basis 1-form into their wedge product, and using that wedges of a 1-form with itself vanish and that swapping factors flips the sign, leaves the determinant of partial derivatives times the new basis wedge.

**How introduced:** Example 38.1 in two dimensions; generalized by the pairing formula of Section 38.3.

**Prerequisites:** wedge product, Jacobian determinant

$$
\mathbf d\alpha^1\wedge\mathbf d\alpha^2 = \frac{\partial(\alpha^1,\alpha^2)}{\partial(x^1,x^2)}\,\mathbf dx^1\wedge\mathbf dx^2
$$

**Where:** GA §38.2 p.401 (pdf 418); GA §38.3 p.403 (pdf 420)

### Levi-Civita symbol

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: alternating symbol, epsilon symbol

The totally antisymmetric symbol with epsilon_{0123} = +1 (or epsilon_{12...n} = 1) and value plus or minus one for even or odd permutations, zero if any index repeats; it builds determinants and volume forms.

**How introduced:** Used to express the Jacobian as a sum and the volume 4-form and 3-forms dSigma_mu.

$$
\varepsilon_{0123}=+1
$$

**Where:** GA §38.2 p.401 (pdf 418); GA §38.3 p.402 (pdf 419)

### volume form

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: volume 4-form, volume element form, omega

The top-degree form that assigns to an ordered set of n vectors the signed volume of their parallelepiped; in flat spacetime with Cartesian coordinates it is dt^dx^dy^dz, and in general coordinates it carries a factor sqrt of the absolute metric determinant. It is the Hodge dual of the constant function 1.

**How introduced:** Recalled in Example 38.2 from Chapter 37 as the integrand for 4-volume integrals; Example 38.6 builds the 3D version with sqrt(g) by feeding it coordinate box edges.

**Prerequisites:** Levi-Civita symbol, wedge product

$$
\tilde\omega = \tfrac{1}{4!}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}\,\mathbf dx^\mu\wedge\mathbf dx^\nu\wedge\mathbf dx^\alpha\wedge\mathbf dx^\beta = \mathbf dt\wedge\mathbf dx\wedge\mathbf dy\wedge\mathbf dz
$$

$$
\tilde\omega = \star 1
$$

**Where:** GA §38.3 p.402 (pdf 419); GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423)

### hypersurface 3-form dSigma_mu

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: 3-volume element form, dSigma_mu

In 4D, the 3-form obtained by contracting the volume form with a basis vector, (1/3!) epsilon_{mu alpha beta gamma} dx^alpha ^ dx^beta ^ dx^gamma; it is the Hodge dual of e_mu and measures oriented 3-volume of hypersurface elements.

**How introduced:** Example 38.2 alongside the volume 4-form; a margin note links it to the 3-volume 1-form of Chapter 37.

**Prerequisites:** volume form, Levi-Civita symbol

$$
d\tilde\Sigma_\mu = \tfrac{1}{3!}\varepsilon_{\mu\alpha\beta\gamma}\,\mathbf dx^\alpha\wedge\mathbf dx^\beta\wedge\mathbf dx^\gamma = \star\boldsymbol e_\mu
$$

**Where:** GA §38.3 p.402 (pdf 419)

### flux 3-form J^mu dSigma_mu

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: current 3-form, J dSigma

The 3-form obtained by contracting a 4-vector current with dSigma_mu; integrating it over a 3D hypersurface gives the flux of the current through that hypersurface (for example total charge on a spacelike slice).

**How introduced:** Flagged in Example 38.2 as the kind of object that will be integrated in many applications, preparing charge conservation in Chapter 43.

**Prerequisites:** hypersurface 3-form dSigma_mu

$$
J^\mu d\tilde\Sigma_\mu = J^\mu\varepsilon_{\mu|\alpha\beta\gamma|}\,\mathbf dx^\alpha\wedge\mathbf dx^\beta\wedge\mathbf dx^\gamma
$$

**Where:** GA §38.3 p.402 (pdf 419)

### parametrized surface

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: parametrized region, generalized surface P(lambda)

An n-dimensional region described by a map from parameters (lambda^1, ..., lambda^n) to points P; the partial derivatives dP/dlambda^i are tangent vectors, and small parameter steps span an infinitesimal parallelepiped.

**How introduced:** Used in Section 38.3 to build the domain side of the integral; also used to state the orientation rule for the boundary.

**Prerequisites:** p-vector

$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal P}{\partial\lambda^1}\Delta\lambda^1\wedge\dots\wedge\frac{\partial\mathcal P}{\partial\lambda^n}\Delta\lambda^n
$$

**Where:** GA §38.3 p.403 (pdf 420); GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423)

### p-vector

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: n-vector, multivector, bivector (p=2)

A totally antisymmetric contravariant tensor built from wedges of vectors; a simple p-vector u_1 ^ ... ^ u_p represents an oriented p-dimensional parallelepiped.

**How introduced:** Recalled to represent the infinitesimal piece of the integration domain.

**Prerequisites:** wedge product

$$
\boldsymbol u\wedge\boldsymbol v
$$

**Where:** GA §38.3 p.402 (pdf 419); GA §38.3 p.403 (pdf 420)

### inner product of a p-form and a p-vector

*operation · revisited* · also: pairing <alpha, v>, contraction of a form with a multivector

The number obtained by contracting a p-form with a p-vector using ordered (restricted) index sums; for simple p-vectors it equals the form evaluated on the constituent vectors, and geometrically it counts the form's cells inside the parallelepiped.

**How introduced:** Recalled as the 'machine' that combines integrand and domain; margin note gives the restricted-index rule and the 2-form-on-bivector case.

**Prerequisites:** p-vector, differential form as integrand

$$
\langle\tilde\alpha,\boldsymbol v\rangle = \alpha_{|i_1\dots i_n|}v^{i_1\dots i_n}
$$

$$
\langle\tilde F,\boldsymbol u\wedge\boldsymbol v\rangle = \tilde F(\boldsymbol u,\boldsymbol v)
$$

**Where:** GA §38.3 p.401 (pdf 418); GA §38.3 p.403 (pdf 420)

### integral as pairing of a form with tangent n-vectors

*definition · core* · also: anatomy of an integral, pullback definition of the integral

The integral of an n-form over an n-dimensional parametrized region is the ordinary n-fold integral over the parameters of the pairing between the form and the wedge of tangent vectors dP/dlambda^1 ^ ... ^ dP/dlambda^n; for a coordinate n-form the pairing is the Jacobian of coordinates with respect to parameters.

**How introduced:** Built step by step in Section 38.3: integrand as form, domain as wedge of tangent vectors, pairing as the multiplier; checked on the integral of df along a curve.

**Prerequisites:** inner product of a p-form and a p-vector, parametrized surface, Jacobian determinant

$$
\int_{\mathcal S}\tilde\alpha = \int\!\cdots\!\int\left\langle\tilde\alpha,\frac{\partial\mathcal P}{\partial\lambda^1}\wedge\dots\wedge\frac{\partial\mathcal P}{\partial\lambda^n}\right\rangle d\lambda^1\cdots d\lambda^n
$$

**Notes:** This is the pullback of the form to parameter space, though the book never uses the word.

**Where:** GA §38.3 p.403 (pdf 420); GA §38.3 p.404 (pdf 421)

### path independence of integrals of exact 1-forms

*principle · introduced* · also: conservative field, exactness implies path independence

If a 1-form is the exterior derivative of a function, its integral along any curve depends only on the end points and equals the change in the function; equivalently its integral round any closed loop vanishes.

**How introduced:** Margin note to Example 38.3, connecting the integral of df with conservative forces.

**Prerequisites:** exact form, integral as pairing of a form with tangent n-vectors

$$
\int_\gamma \mathbf df = f[\mathcal P(B)] - f[\mathcal P(A)]
$$

**Where:** GA §38.3 p.403 (pdf 420); GA §38.5 p.407 (pdf 424)

### duality between forms and chains

*principle · core* · also: forms are dual to chains, integration pairing <form, chain>

Integration pairs a p-form with a p-chain to give a number, linearly in each, in the same way a 1-form pairs with a vector; chains are the objects forms are evaluated on.

**How introduced:** Announced as a slogan in Section 38.1, justified in Section 38.3 by the pairing definition of the integral, and used to motivate the boundary operator as the chain-side partner of d.

**Prerequisites:** integral as pairing of a form with tangent n-vectors, chain

$$
\langle\text{form},\text{chain}\rangle = \int_{\text{chain}}\text{form}
$$

**Where:** GA §38.1 p.399 (pdf 416); GA §38.3 p.404 (pdf 421); GA §38.4 p.404 (pdf 421)

### boundary of a region

*definition · introduced* · also: edge, boundary set

The boundary of a region consists of its points that are not interior points; for manifolds with boundary it is the lower-dimensional edge, e.g. a disc's circle, a segment's end points.

**How introduced:** Given as the first definition of Section 38.4 with four quick examples (disc, cylinder, line, sphere).

**Notes:** The set-theoretic phrasing is loose: a circle regarded as a subset of the plane has itself as its topological boundary, while as a 1-manifold it has none. The examples use the manifold sense.

**Where:** GA §38.4 p.404 (pdf 421)

### boundary operator

*operation · core* · also: partial operator, del (boundary)

The operator that sends an n-chain to its (n-1)-dimensional oriented boundary; it is linear on chains and its square is zero.

**How introduced:** Treated as a machine that extracts edges, illustrated pictorially in Fig. 38.1 and then made dimension-lowering on chains.

**Prerequisites:** boundary of a region, chain

$$
\partial C_n = C_{n-1}
$$

**Notes:** The book's equation means 'is an (n-1)-chain', not equality of two specific chains; orientation signs of the boundary pieces are not discussed.

**Where:** GA §38.4 p.404 (pdf 421); GA §38.4 p.405 (pdf 422)

### chain

*mathematical-object · core* · also: n-chain, generalized surface, domain of integration

An n-chain is an n-dimensional region over which an n-form can be integrated: points (0), curves (1), surfaces (2), volumes (3) and so on; formally a combination with integer coefficients of oriented simplices, so that orientations and multiplicities are tracked.

**How introduced:** Named in Section 38.1 as the dual of forms and listed by dimension in Section 38.4; a margin note mentions algebraic topology and simplicial complexes but says to picture chains as spaces.

**Prerequisites:** parametrized surface

$$
C_0=\text{point},\ C_1=\text{line},\ C_2=\text{area},\ C_3=\text{3-volume},\ C_n=n\text{-volume}
$$

**Where:** GA §38.1 p.399 (pdf 416); GA §38.4 p.404 (pdf 421)

### cycle

*definition · introduced* · also: closed chain, Z_n

A chain whose boundary is zero, such as a circle or a sphere.

**How introduced:** Introduced in Section 38.4 as the boundary-free chains; a margin note asks whether all cycles are boundaries.

**Prerequisites:** chain, boundary operator

$$
\partial Z_n = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §38.4 p.404 (pdf 421); GA §38.4 p.405 (pdf 422)

### boundary chain

*definition · introduced* · also: B_n, boundary

An n-chain that is the boundary of some (n+1)-chain, such as a closed surface bounding a volume.

**How introduced:** Introduced with cycles; the observation that every boundary is a cycle leads to the double-boundary rule.

**Prerequisites:** chain, boundary operator

$$
B_n = \partial C_{n+1}
$$

$$
\partial B_n = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §38.4 p.404 (pdf 421); GA §38.4 p.405 (pdf 422)

### boundary of a boundary is zero

*identity · core* · also: partial squared equals zero, double-boundary rule

Applying the boundary operator twice to any chain gives the zero chain: an edge has no edge.

**How introduced:** Derived by combining 'boundaries are chains of the form dC' with 'boundaries have no boundary'; drawn as the last line of Fig. 38.1.

**Prerequisites:** boundary operator, cycle, boundary chain

$$
\partial\partial = \partial^2 = 0
$$

**Notes:** The book's derivation is essentially a restatement; the real reason is sign cancellation of shared faces (e.g. the boundary of the boundary of a triangle: each vertex appears once with plus and once with minus).

**Where:** GA §38.4 p.405 (pdf 422); GA §38.4 p.404 (pdf 421)

### simply connected space

*definition · mention* · also: no holes

A path-connected space in which every closed loop can be continuously shrunk to a point, so any two paths with the same end points can be deformed into each other.

**How introduced:** Margin notes use it to answer whether cycles are boundaries and whether closed forms are exact; Exercise 38.3 shows how removing a point breaks it.

**Notes:** Simple connectivity only settles the question for loops and 1-forms; for higher degrees one needs vanishing higher cohomology (e.g. a contractible or star-shaped region).

**Where:** GA §38.4 p.405 (pdf 422); GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423); GA p.408 (pdf 425)

### exterior derivative

*operation · revisited* · also: d operator, exterior differentiation

The linear operator that sends a p-form to a (p+1)-form, acting on functions as the gradient 1-form and satisfying a graded Leibniz rule and dd = 0; in 3D it reproduces gradient, curl and divergence on 0-, 1- and 2-forms.

**How introduced:** Brought back in Section 38.5 as the counterpart of the boundary operator; Example 38.5 recomputes it on general 1- and 2-forms in three dimensions.

**Prerequisites:** wedge product, differential form as integrand

$$
\mathbf d\tilde Y = \left(\partial_yC-\partial_zB\right)\mathbf dy\wedge\mathbf dz+\left(\partial_zA-\partial_xC\right)\mathbf dz\wedge\mathbf dx+\left(\partial_xB-\partial_yA\right)\mathbf dx\wedge\mathbf dy
$$

**Where:** GA §38.5 p.405 (pdf 422)

### nilpotency of the exterior derivative

*identity · revisited* · also: dd = 0, d squared equals zero

Applying the exterior derivative twice to any form gives zero, because mixed partial derivatives are symmetric while wedge products are antisymmetric; in 3D it contains curl grad = 0 and div curl = 0.

**How introduced:** Checked on the 1-form and 2-form of Example 38.5, recalled from Chapter 33, and called the dual of the double-boundary rule.

**Prerequisites:** exterior derivative

$$
\mathbf d\mathbf d = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §38.5 p.405 (pdf 422); GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423)

### duality between d and the boundary operator

*principle · core* · also: d is adjoint to boundary, Stokes duality

Under the integration pairing, the exterior derivative on forms and the boundary operator on chains are adjoint: pairing dOmega with C equals pairing Omega with the boundary of C. Degree up on one side matches dimension down on the other, and dd = 0 corresponds to the double-boundary rule.

**How introduced:** Stated in words when d is reintroduced and when dd = 0 is called the dual of the double-boundary rule; Stokes' theorem is the precise statement.

**Prerequisites:** exterior derivative, boundary operator, duality between forms and chains

$$
\langle\mathbf d\tilde\Omega, C\rangle = \langle\tilde\Omega,\partial C\rangle
$$

**Where:** GA §38.5 p.405 (pdf 422); GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423)

### closed form

*definition · introduced* · also: cocycle

A form whose exterior derivative vanishes.

**How introduced:** Defined in a margin note to Section 38.5; the vortex form of Exercise 38.3 is the worked illustration.

**Prerequisites:** exterior derivative

$$
\mathbf d\tilde A = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423); GA p.408 (pdf 425)

### exact form

*definition · introduced* · also: coboundary

A p-form that equals the exterior derivative of some (p-1)-form; every exact form is closed because dd = 0.

**How introduced:** Defined in the same margin note; Exercise 38.2 shows exact 2-forms integrate to zero over a sphere, Exercise 38.3 a closed form that fails to be exact.

**Prerequisites:** closed form, nilpotency of the exterior derivative

$$
\tilde A = \mathbf d\tilde B
$$

**Where:** GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423); GA p.408 (pdf 425)

### Poincaré lemma

*theorem · introduced*

On a contractible (for instance star-shaped) region, every closed p-form with p >= 1 is exact. For 1-forms, simple connectivity of the region is already enough.

**How introduced:** Quoted in a margin note (stated there for simply connected regions) and tested by the vortex form on the punctured plane in Exercise 38.3.

**Prerequisites:** closed form, exact form, simply connected space

**Notes:** The book's margin statement with 'simply connected' is too weak for p >= 2: the solid-angle 2-form on 3D space minus the origin is closed, the domain is simply connected, but the form is not exact.

**Where:** GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423); GA p.408 (pdf 425)

### generalized Stokes theorem

*theorem · core* · also: Stokes' theorem, fundamental theorem of exterior calculus, Newton-Leibniz-Gauss-Green-Ostrogradskii-Stokes-Poincaré formula

For an n-form Omega and an oriented (n+1)-chain C, the integral of dOmega over C equals the integral of Omega over the boundary of C with its induced orientation.

**How introduced:** Announced as the goal in Section 38.1, motivated by the family of vector-calculus theorems, stated in Section 38.5 once forms, chains, d and the boundary operator are in hand, and exercised by recovering the classical theorems; no proof.

**Prerequisites:** exterior derivative, boundary operator, integral as pairing of a form with tangent n-vectors, induced boundary orientation

$$
\int_{\partial C}\tilde\Omega = \int_C\mathbf d\tilde\Omega
$$

**Notes:** Metric-free: no sqrt(g) is needed in the theorem itself; metric factors enter only when converting forms into vector-calculus language.

**Where:** GA §38.1 p.398 (pdf 415); GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423); GA §38.5 p.407 (pdf 424); GA p.408 (pdf 425)

### induced boundary orientation

*convention · introduced* · also: outward-first convention, boundary orientation rule

If the region is oriented by the ordered tangent vectors (v_1, v_2, ..., v_n) with v_1 pointing outward at the boundary, the boundary is oriented by (v_2, ..., v_n); in 1D this gives the plus sign at the end point and minus at the start.

**How introduced:** Stated with Stokes' theorem via the wedge of parameter derivatives with the first one removed; a margin note glosses it as choosing a consistent positive sense pointing away from the region.

**Prerequisites:** parametrized surface, p-vector

$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal P}{\partial\lambda^1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\frac{\partial\mathcal P}{\partial\lambda^n}\ \to\ \frac{\partial\mathcal P}{\partial\lambda^2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\frac{\partial\mathcal P}{\partial\lambda^n}
$$

**Notes:** The book leaves implicit that lambda^1 must be the outward direction; it also labels an (n+1)-chain with only n parameters.

**Where:** GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423)

### metric volume element

*physical-quantity · revisited* · also: invariant volume element, sqrt(g) d^3x

In a coordinate basis the volume form is sqrt(|det g|) dx^1 ^ ... ^ dx^n (with a (-1)^s sign inside the root for s minus signs in the signature); feeding it the coordinate box edges gives the scalar volume element used in integrals of functions.

**How introduced:** Example 38.6 as a warm-up; margin notes recall the signature factor and that sqrt(g) returns in curved space or curvilinear coordinates.

**Prerequisites:** volume form

$$
dV = \tilde\omega(dx\,\boldsymbol e_x, dy\,\boldsymbol e_y, dz\,\boldsymbol e_z) = \sqrt g\,dx\,dy\,dz
$$

**Where:** GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423)

### surface area 2-form

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: area element from the volume form and unit normal, Sigma 2-form

In flat 3D, inserting a unit normal into the volume 3-form leaves the 2-form n^x dy^dz + n^y dz^dx + n^z dx^dy, which on two tangent vectors du, dv gives n . (du x dv), the scalar area of their parallelogram.

**How introduced:** Example 38.7, used to integrate a scalar function over a surface.

**Prerequisites:** volume form, wedge product

$$
\tilde\Sigma = \hat n^x\mathbf dy\wedge\mathbf dz + \hat n^y\mathbf dz\wedge\mathbf dx+\hat n^z\mathbf dx\wedge\mathbf dy
$$

**Notes:** This is the interior product of the unit normal with the volume form; it differs from the flux 2-form, where the vector field replaces the unit normal.

**Where:** GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423); GA §38.5 p.407 (pdf 424)

### Green's theorem in the plane

*theorem · introduced* · also: Green's theorem

For functions L(x, y) and M(x, y) and a planar region S bounded by a counterclockwise curve, the circulation of L dx + M dy round the curve equals the area integral of dM/dx - dL/dy.

**How introduced:** Exercise 38.4(b), to be verified from the generalized theorem and then applied on the sphere.

**Prerequisites:** generalized Stokes theorem, exterior derivative

$$
\oint_\gamma (L\,dx+M\,dy) = \int_S \left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial y}\right)dx\,dy
$$

**Where:** GA p.408 (pdf 425)

### vortex 1-form

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: angular form, d theta form

The planar 1-form A(-y dx + x dy)/r^2, which is closed away from the origin but not exact there; its integral once round the origin is 2 pi A, which no single-valued function could produce.

**How introduced:** Exercise 38.3, with commentary that the singular origin must be removed so the plane is no longer simply connected and the Poincaré lemma does not apply.

**Prerequisites:** closed form, exact form, Poincaré lemma, simply connected space

$$
\tilde\phi = \frac{A}{r^2}(-y\,\mathbf dx + x\,\mathbf dy)
$$

$$
\mathbf d\tilde\phi = 0
$$

**Notes:** Locally it is A d(theta) with theta the polar angle, which is multivalued; the loop value 2 pi A is our own computation.

**Where:** GA p.408 (pdf 425)

### holonomy of parallel transport on the sphere

*phenomenon · mention* · also: rotation by enclosed area, geometric phase on the sphere

On the unit sphere, a tangent vector parallel transported round a closed loop returns rotated by an angle equal to the enclosed area (modulo 2 pi); on a sphere of radius R the angle is area divided by R^2.

**How introduced:** Exercise 38.4, built from the orthonormal-frame geodesic equation, a matrix-exponential solution, Green's theorem and Stokes; the authors credit Blennow and Ohlsson and mention geometric phases in magnetism.

**Prerequisites:** Green's theorem in the plane, generalized Stokes theorem

$$
\mathcal I = \int_0^T d\tau\,\cos\theta\,\dot\phi = -\mathcal A
$$

**Notes:** A 2D instance of curvature as holonomy (GA ch11 and ch35 treat the general case).

**Where:** GA p.408 (pdf 425); GA p.409 (pdf 426)

## Key equations

### (38.1) Fundamental theorem of calculus · central · GA §38.1 p.397 (pdf 414)

$$
\int_a^b\frac{df}{dx}\,dx = f(b)-f(a)
$$

Integrating a derivative over an interval gives the signed end values only.

**Symbols:** f: function; a, b: end points

### (38.2) Telescoping sum · supporting · GA §38.1 p.397 (pdf 414)

$$
\int_a^b\frac{df}{dx}\,dx\approx\Delta x\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}\frac{f(x_{i+1})-f(x_i)}{\Delta x}
$$

Discretized version in which interior values cancel pairwise, leaving f(x_N) - f(x_1).

**Symbols:** Delta x: slice width; x_i: sample points, x_1 = a, x_N = b

### (38.3) Volume gradient theorem · supporting · GA §38.1 p.397 (pdf 414)

$$
\int\vec\nabla f\,dV = \int f\,d\vec S\qquad\left(\int\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^i}dV = \int f\,dS^i\right)
$$

3D analogue of the fundamental theorem; the outward surface vector supplies the sign flip. Component form from margin note 1.

**Symbols:** dV: volume element; dS: outward vector surface element over the closed boundary

### (38.4) Divergence theorem · central · GA §38.1 p.398 (pdf 415)

$$
\int\vec\nabla\cdot\vec a\,dV = \int\vec a\cdot d\vec S
$$

Obtained from (38.3) by setting f = a^j and summing over j.

**Symbols:** a: vector field

### (38.5) Surface gradient identity · derivation-step · GA §38.1 p.398 (pdf 415)

$$
\int\vec\nabla f\times d\vec S = -\oint f\,d\vec\ell\qquad\left(\varepsilon_{ikm}\int\partial^k f\,dS^m = -\oint f\,d\ell_i\right)
$$

2D analogue: gradients inside a surface cancel, leaving a loop term around the edge.

**Symbols:** dS: vector area element; dl: line element of the bounding contour

### (38.6) Kelvin-Stokes (curl) theorem · central · GA §38.1 p.398 (pdf 415)

$$
\int\vec\nabla\times\vec a\cdot d\vec S = \oint\vec a\cdot d\vec\ell
$$

Obtained from (38.5) by setting f = a^j and contracting the free index i with j.

**Symbols:** a: vector field

### (38.7) Generalized Stokes theorem (announced) · central · GA §38.1 p.398 (pdf 415)

$$
\int_C\mathbf d\tilde\Omega = \int_{\partial C}\tilde\Omega
$$

Target of the chapter, stated before the machinery is built.

**Symbols:** Omega: n-form; C: (n+1)-chain; partial C: its boundary; d: exterior derivative

### (38.8) Work integral · supporting · GA §38.1 p.399 (pdf 416)

$$
I_1 = \int_C\boldsymbol F\cdot d\boldsymbol r = \int_C F_x\,dx+F_y\,dy+F_z\,dz
$$

A typical line integral in Cartesian coordinates.

**Symbols:** F: force; C: path

### (38.9) Line integral of a 1-form · supporting · GA §38.1 p.399 (pdf 416)

$$
I_1 = \int_C\tilde\alpha,\qquad\tilde\alpha = F_x\mathbf dx+F_y\mathbf dy+F_z\mathbf dz
$$

The same integral written as the integral of a 1-form with no separate differential.

**Symbols:** alpha: 1-form built from F

### (38.10) Flux integral with wedges · supporting · GA §38.1 p.399 (pdf 416)

$$
I_2 = \int_S\boldsymbol G\cdot d\boldsymbol S = \int_S(G_x\mathbf dy\wedge\mathbf dz+G_y\mathbf dz\wedge\mathbf dx+G_z\mathbf dx\wedge\mathbf dy)
$$

Surface integral with oriented area elements recast as wedges of basis 1-forms.

**Symbols:** G: vector field; S: surface

### (38.11) Surface integral of a 2-form · supporting · GA §38.1 p.399 (pdf 416)

$$
I_2 = \int_S\tilde\beta
$$

Flux integral as the integral of the 2-form beta = G_x dy^dz + G_y dz^dx + G_z dx^dy.

**Symbols:** beta: 2-form

### (38.12) Form-chain pairing slogan · supporting · GA §38.1 p.399 (pdf 416)

$$
\langle\text{form},\text{chain}\rangle\ \text{``=''}\ (\text{multiple integral}) = (\text{number})
$$

Integrands and domains are dual; putting them together gives a number, like a 1-form eating a vector.

### (38.13)-(38.15) Counting rules for integrals of forms · supporting · GA §38.2 p.400 (pdf 417)

$$
\int_{\rm line}\tilde Y = \#\,\text{surfaces cut},\quad\int_{\rm surface}\tilde X = \#\,\text{tubes cut},\quad\int_{\rm volume}\tilde W = \#\,\text{cells contained}
$$

Geometric meaning of integrating 1-, 2- and 3-forms.

**Symbols:** Y: 1-form; X: 2-form; W: 3-form

### (38.16) Cartesian volume integral · derivation-step · GA §38.2 p.400 (pdf 417)

$$
\int_V f(x,y,z)\,dx\,dy\,dz
$$

Standard multiple integral as a sum over a Cartesian cell mesh.

**Symbols:** V: region

### (38.17) Jacobian determinant · supporting · GA §38.2 p.400 (pdf 417)

$$
J = \frac{\partial(x^0,\dots,x^n)}{\partial(y^0,\dots,y^n)} = \begin{vmatrix}\partial x^0/\partial y^0&\cdots&\partial x^0/\partial y^n\\ \vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ \partial x^n/\partial y^0&\cdots&\partial x^n/\partial y^n\end{vmatrix}
$$

Factor needed so that cells of new coordinates tile the region without gaps.

**Symbols:** x, y: two coordinate systems

### (38.18) Jacobian as epsilon sum · derivation-step · GA §38.2 p.401 (pdf 418)

$$

Margin note: determinant written with the antisymmetric symbol, anticipating its appearance from wedges.

**Symbols:** epsilon: Levi-Civita symbol

### (38.19) Chain rule for basis 1-forms · derivation-step · GA §38.2 p.401 (pdf 418)

\mathbf d\alpha^1 = \frac{\partial\alpha^1}{\partial x^1}\mathbf dx^1+\frac{\partial\alpha^1}{\partial x^2}\mathbf dx^2,\qquad \mathbf d\alpha^2 = \frac{\partial\alpha^2}{\partial x^1}\mathbf dx^1+\frac{\partial\alpha^2}{\partial x^2}\mathbf dx^2

**Symbols:** alpha^i: old coordinates; x^i: new coordinates

### (38.20) Jacobian from the wedge product · central · GA §38.2 p.401 (pdf 418)

$$
\mathbf d\alpha^1\wedge\mathbf d\alpha^2 = \left(\frac{\partial\alpha^1}{\partial x^1}\frac{\partial\alpha^2}{\partial x^2}-\frac{\partial\alpha^1}{\partial x^2}\frac{\partial\alpha^2}{\partial x^1}\right)\mathbf dx^1\wedge\mathbf dx^2 = \varepsilon_{\mu\nu}\frac{\partial\alpha^\mu}{\partial x^1}\frac{\partial\alpha^\nu}{\partial x^2}\mathbf dx^1\wedge\mathbf dx^2 = \frac{\partial(\alpha^1,\alpha^2)}{\partial(x^1,x^2)}\mathbf dx^1\wedge\mathbf dx^2

## Worked examples

### Example 38.1 · intro · GA §38.2 p.401 (pdf 418)

**Problem:** Change a 2D integration mesh from coordinates alpha^1, alpha^2 to coordinates x^1, x^2 and find how the area 2-form d alpha^1 ^ d alpha^2 transforms.

**Method:** Expand each old basis 1-form with the chain rule in the new basis, wedge the two expansions, drop terms with a repeated factor, and flip dx^2 ^ dx^1 to -dx^1 ^ dx^2 so the cross terms combine.

**Key insight:** The antisymmetry of the wedge product is exactly the antisymmetry of the determinant, so the Jacobian is built in rather than added by hand.

**Result:** d alpha^1 ^ d alpha^2 = [d(alpha^1, alpha^2)/d(x^1, x^2)] dx^1 ^ dx^2.

**Concepts:** Jacobian from wedge-product antisymmetry, wedge product, Jacobian determinant

### Example 38.2 · standard · GA §38.3 p.402 (pdf 419)

**Problem:** Express the 4-volume element of flat spacetime and the 3-volume elements as forms, and interpret J^mu dSigma_mu.

**Method:** Write the volume 4-form with the Levi-Civita symbol, either with an unrestricted sum and 1/4! or an ordered sum, and collapse it using epsilon_0123 = 1; contract one index to get dSigma_mu with 1/3!; contract with a current.

**Key insight:** Restricted sums and factorial prefactors are two bookkeeping choices for the same form; dSigma_mu is the Hodge dual of a basis vector, so J^mu dSigma_mu is the flux 3-form of the current.

**Result:** omega = dt ^ dx ^ dy ^ dz; dSigma_mu = epsilon_{mu|alpha beta gamma|} dx^alpha ^ dx^beta ^ dx^gamma; J^mu dSigma_mu = J^mu epsilon_{mu|alpha beta gamma|} dx^alpha ^ dx^beta ^ dx^gamma.

**Concepts:** volume form, hypersurface 3-form dSigma_mu, flux 3-form J^mu dSigma_mu, Levi-Civita symbol

### Example 38.3 · intro · GA §38.3 p.403 (pdf 420)

**Problem:** Check the pairing definition of the integral on the line integral of df along a curve parametrized by lambda from 0 to 1.

**Method:** Pair df with the tangent vector dP/dlambda to get df/dlambda, then integrate over lambda.

**Key insight:** The definition reproduces the fundamental theorem of calculus; the answer depends only on the end points, so exact 1-forms describe conservative fields.

**Result:** Integral of df along the curve = f[P(1)] - f[P(0)].

**Concepts:** integral as pairing of a form with tangent n-vectors, path independence of integrals of exact 1-forms

### Example 38.4 · intro · GA §38.4 p.404 (pdf 421)

**Problem:** Find the boundaries of a closed unit disc, a finite cylinder, a line segment and the unit sphere.

**Method:** Identify the non-interior points of each (as manifolds): the rim, the two end circles, the two end points, and nothing.

**Key insight:** Boundaries lower dimension by one, and closed objects like the sphere have no boundary at all; the pictures in Fig. 38.1 make this an algebra of shapes.

**Result:** Unit circle; two circles; two end points; empty set.

**Concepts:** boundary of a region, boundary operator, cycle

### Example 38.5 · standard · GA §38.5 p.405 (pdf 422)

**Problem:** In 3D compute the exterior derivatives of a general 1-form A dx + B dy + C dz and a general 2-form a dx^dy + b dy^dz + c dz^dx, and apply d a second time.

**Method:** Differentiate each coefficient in every variable, wedge the differential in front, delete terms with a repeated 1-form, reorder wedges to the cyclic basis dy^dz, dz^dx, dx^dy with sign changes, and collect.

**Key insight:** d on a 1-form produces curl components and d on a 2-form produces a divergence, so d unifies grad, curl and div; a second d vanishes (curl grad = 0, div curl = 0) because mixed partials commute while wedges anticommute.

**Result:** dY has coefficients (dC/dy - dB/dz, dA/dz - dC/dx, dB/dx - dA/dy) on (dy^dz, dz^dx, dx^dy); dX = (da/dz + db/dx + dc/dy) dx^dy^dz; ddY = ddX = 0.

**Concepts:** exterior derivative, nilpotency of the exterior derivative

### Example 38.6 · intro · GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423)

**Problem:** Integrate a function over a 3-volume in a coordinate frame with metric g.

**Method:** Take the volume 3-form sqrt(g) dx^dy^dz, evaluate it on the coordinate box edges dx e_x, dy e_y, dz e_z to get dV, multiply by f and integrate.

**Key insight:** Integrating a function really means integrating the 3-form f omega; the metric enters only through the volume form.

**Result:** Integral of f omega over C_3 = integral over V of f sqrt(g) dx dy dz; in flat Cartesian space g = 1.

**Concepts:** metric volume element, volume form, chain

### Example 38.7 · standard · GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423)

**Problem:** Integrate a function over a surface in flat 3D using forms.

**Method:** Build the thin box on a surface patch du, dv and the unit normal; the volume form evaluated on it gives n . (du x dv); recognise this as the 2-form n^x dy^dz + n^y dz^dx + n^z dx^dy fed with du and dv; integrate f times that 2-form.

**Key insight:** Inserting the unit normal into the volume form produces an area 2-form, so scalar surface integrals are also integrals of forms.

**Result:** Integral of f Sigma over C_2 = integral over S of f n . dS.

**Concepts:** surface area 2-form, volume form

### Example 38.8 · standard · GA §38.5 p.407 (pdf 424)

**Problem:** Recover the classical integral theorems for a curve, a capped surface and a volume (Fig. 38.2) from Stokes' theorem, and derive the divergence theorem in detail.

**Method:** Apply Stokes to the forms f, f dx^i and f dx^i ^ dx^j on the three geometries; then take the flux 2-form A_x dy^dz + A_y dz^dx + A_z dx^dy, compute its d (divergence times dx^dy^dz), and translate wedges into ordinary area and volume elements.

**Key insight:** Each classical theorem is one choice of form degree and chain dimension in the same identity; which vector operator appears is decided by what d does to that degree.

**Result:** Endpoint theorem, surface/edge identity, volume/surface identity, and the divergence theorem (eqn 38.57).

**Concepts:** generalized Stokes theorem, divergence theorem, exterior derivative, flux integral as integral of a 2-form

### Embedded: divergence theorem from the volume gradient theorem · intro · GA §38.1 p.398 (pdf 415)

**Problem:** Obtain the divergence theorem from the statement that the volume integral of a gradient equals the surface integral of f dS.

**Method:** Use the component form (integral of the i-th partial of f equals integral of f dS^i), substitute f = a^j, set i = j and sum.

**Key insight:** The divergence theorem is a contraction of a more primitive vector identity with the same boundary-term structure as the fundamental theorem.

**Result:** Integral of div a over V = flux of a through the boundary.

**Concepts:** volume gradient theorem, divergence theorem

### Embedded: curl theorem from the surface gradient identity · standard · GA §38.1 p.398 (pdf 415)

**Problem:** Derive the curl theorem from the identity that the area integral of grad f cross dS is minus the loop integral of f dl.

**Method:** Write the i-th component with epsilon_{ikm}, substitute f = a^j, contract i with j, and use the cyclic property of epsilon to recognise (curl a) . dS, absorbing the minus sign.

**Key insight:** Even in vector language the curl theorem is a 'derivative inside equals function on edge' result; the cross product hides the structure that forms later make explicit.

**Result:** Flux of curl a through S = circulation of a round its edge.

**Concepts:** Kelvin-Stokes theorem, Levi-Civita symbol

### Embedded: pairing of coordinate wedge with tangent wedge gives the Jacobian · standard · GA §38.3 p.403 (pdf 420)

**Problem:** Show that the pairing definition of the integral reproduces the Jacobian of the traditional multiple-integral formula.

**Method:** Insert the coordinate n-form dx^1 ^ ... ^ dx^n and the tangent n-vector built from dP/dlambda^i; the pairing is the determinant of the components dx^i/dlambda^j.

**Key insight:** The Jacobian is the pairing of a coordinate cell with the parameter parallelepiped, i.e. how many coordinate cells fit in one parameter cell.

**Result:** Pairing = d(x^1..x^n)/d(lambda^1..lambda^n) (eqn 38.32).

**Concepts:** integral as pairing of a form with tangent n-vectors, Jacobian determinant, inner product of a p-form and a p-vector

### Embedded: flux of a 2-form through a small coordinate square (margin note 10) · intro · GA §38.3 p.403 (pdf 420)

**Problem:** Integrate a 2-form F over the infinitesimal square spanned by Delta x e_x and Delta y e_y.

**Method:** Pair F with e_x ^ e_y, which is F(e_x, e_y) = F_xy, and multiply by Delta x Delta y.

**Key insight:** Components of a 2-form are fluxes through unit coordinate squares; this is the step Chapter 43 uses for the electromagnetic field tensor.

**Result:** Integral = F_xy Delta x Delta y.

**Concepts:** inner product of a p-form and a p-vector, flux integral as integral of a 2-form


## Analogies and intuitions

### The integral sign is an elongated letter S standing for the Latin word for sum. → integral as a limit of sums · useful · GA §38.1 p.397 (pdf 414)

Sets up the slicing argument that explains boundary terms.

**Where it breaks down:** A Riemann-sum picture alone does not explain orientation or why forms rather than functions are integrated.

### Telescoping sum: slice the interval and every interior value is added once and subtracted once. → telescoping-sum picture of boundary terms · strong · GA §38.1 p.397 (pdf 414)

Explains why only f(b) and f(a) survive and why they carry opposite signs; implicitly the prototype for cancellation over shared faces in higher dimensions.

**Where it breaks down:** The book does not carry the picture into 2D and 3D explicitly (shared edges and faces cancelling), where it would also explain the double-boundary rule.

**App idea:** Bar-chart animation: sampled f values with difference bars between neighbours; bars collapse in cancelling pairs leaving a red end bar and a blue start bar; then extend to a grid of squares whose shared edge circulations cancel.

### Integrals as counting: a curve counts how many sheets of a 1-form it crosses, a surface how many tubes of a 2-form pierce it, a volume how many cells of a 3-form it holds. → counting interpretation of form integrals · strong · GA §38.2 p.400 (pdf 417)

Gives each integral a geometric meaning independent of coordinates, and grounds the claim that integrands are forms.

**Where it breaks down:** Counts are signed and fractional; the density of sheets varies from point to point; the picture needs orientation to fix signs; it does not show metric notions such as length.

**App idea:** Draggable curve through a field of stacked sheets with a live crossing counter, a draggable patch in a tube lattice, and a draggable box in a cell lattice.

### A 1-form eating a vector to give a number is the model for a p-form eating a region to give an integral. → duality between forms and chains · strong · GA §38.3 p.401 (pdf 418)

Motivates calling domains dual to integrands and writing the integral as a pairing.

**Where it breaks down:** The pairing with a finite region is a sum of infinitely many local pairings, not one algebraic contraction; linearity in the chain needs chains as formal sums.

### The Jacobian ensures the cells of a new coordinate grid mesh together to cover the region with no gaps or overlaps. → Jacobian determinant · useful · GA §38.2 p.400 (pdf 417)

Motivates why a factor is needed before showing that wedge algebra supplies it.

**Where it breaks down:** Says nothing about orientation (sign of the determinant) and treats cells as rigid parallelograms only in the infinitesimal limit.

**App idea:** Warpable grid over a region: learner drags a coordinate map; each small cell shows its area ratio (the Jacobian) as a colour, and the weighted cell count stays equal to the region's area.

### A space with no boundary vanishes 'in a puff of logic' (Douglas Adams) when the boundary operator acts on it. → boundary of a boundary is zero · useful · GA §38.4 p.404 (pdf 421)

Visual shorthand in Fig. 38.1 for the empty boundary of a circle, a sphere, and of any boundary.

**Where it breaks down:** Suggests the chain disappears physically; in the algebra the result is the zero chain, which pairs with any form to give zero, and non-bounding cycles do not vanish in any other sense.

### Gradients inside a surface add up to nothing except a circulating term round its edge. → Kelvin-Stokes theorem · weak · GA §38.1 p.398 (pdf 415)

One-sentence intuition for the 2D intermediate identity (eqn 38.5).

**Where it breaks down:** Only a verbal gesture; without drawing small cells with shared edges the learner may not see what cancels.

### The exterior derivative and the boundary operator are mirror images: one raises form degree, the other lowers chain dimension, and both square to zero. → duality between d and the boundary operator · strong · GA §38.5 p.405 (pdf 422)

Frames Stokes' theorem as moving an operator from one side of the pairing to the other.

**Where it breaks down:** The mirror is exact only through integration; d is a local differential operator while the boundary is a global geometric one, and the book does not spell out adjointness.

**App idea:** Two-column ladder: forms Omega_0..Omega_3 with d arrows up on the left, chains C_3..C_0 with boundary arrows down on the right, horizontal pairing rungs; clicking a rung shows the equal integrals.


## Misconceptions addressed

### An integral written without a trailing dx is incomplete or sloppy. · GA §38.1 p.399 (pdf 416)

**Correction:** When the integrand is a differential form the differentials are part of the form; the integral of a 1-form over a curve is complete as written.

**Why tempting:** School calculus drills the habit of always writing d(something) at the end.

### The Jacobian is an ad hoc correction that must be memorised for each coordinate change. · GA §38.2 p.401 (pdf 418)

**Correction:** It is produced automatically when basis 1-forms are rewritten and wedged together; it is the pairing of coordinate cells with parameter cells.

**Why tempting:** Multivariable calculus courses usually introduce it as a rule with a picture, not as a consequence of antisymmetry.

### The fundamental theorem of calculus, Green, the curl theorem and the divergence theorem are separate facts. · GA §38.1 p.398 (pdf 415)

**Correction:** They are the same statement, Stokes' theorem, for forms of degree 0, 1 and 2 on chains of dimension 1, 2 and 3.

**Why tempting:** They look different in vector notation (dot products, cross products, different operators) and are taught in different courses.

### If dA = 0 then A = dB for some B everywhere. · GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423)

**Correction:** Closed forms are exact only on topologically trivial regions; the vortex form on the punctured plane is closed but its loop integral round the origin is non-zero, so it cannot be exact.

**Why tempting:** Every exact form is closed, and on ordinary Euclidean space the converse holds, so the reverse implication feels automatic.

### Every chain without a boundary is the boundary of something. · GA §38.4 p.405 (pdf 422)

**Correction:** A cycle need not bound: a loop around a hole in the punctured plane, or a sphere surrounding a removed point in 3D, is closed but bounds nothing in the space.

**Why tempting:** In the unpunctured plane or ordinary space every closed curve or surface does enclose a region.

### Simple connectedness is enough to make every closed form exact (and every cycle a boundary) in all degrees. · GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423)

**Correction:** Simple connectedness controls loops and 1-forms only. Space minus a point is simply connected, yet the solid-angle 2-form there is closed and not exact, and a sphere round the point is a 2-cycle that bounds nothing.

**Why tempting:** The book's margin notes state both claims in terms of simple connectedness, and the only counterexample offered (the vortex form) is a 1-form.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### A sphere and a ball are the same object, so S^2 has a boundary. · GA p.408 (pdf 425)

**Correction:** The sphere S^2 is the 2D surface and has no boundary; the solid ball is 3D and its boundary is the sphere. Exercise 38.2 uses the sphere (its wording calls it a ball).

**Why tempting:** Everyday language uses 'ball' for the surface, the exercise text says 'ball', and Fig. 38.1 draws a shaded sphere that looks solid.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Boundaries are just sets of points, so signs in f(b) - f(a) come from somewhere else. · GA §38.4 p.404 (pdf 421)

**Correction:** Boundaries are oriented chains: a segment from A to B has boundary B minus A, and this orientation is exactly what produces the minus sign; Stokes' theorem requires the induced orientation.

**Why tempting:** The book defines the boundary set-theoretically and writes the boundary of an n-chain as 'an (n-1)-chain' without signs.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### d and the boundary operator are the same kind of derivative. · GA §38.5 p.405 (pdf 422)

**Correction:** d acts on forms (integrands) and raises degree by one; the boundary operator acts on chains (domains) and lowers dimension by one. They are partners under integration, not the same operation.

**Why tempting:** Both are written with similar symbols, both square to zero, and the boundary symbol looks like a partial derivative.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Stokes' theorem needs a metric (sqrt(g) factors) to hold in curved space. · GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423)

**Correction:** The theorem involves only forms, d and oriented chains, none of which need a metric; metric factors appear only when translating forms into scalar or vector integrands.

**Why tempting:** The chapter restricts itself to flat space and a margin note says sqrt(g) must be reinstated in curved space, which is true for volume elements but not for the theorem itself.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Theorems named after Stokes were discovered by Stokes. · GA §38.5 p.405 (pdf 422)

**Correction:** The result grew from work by Newton, Leibniz, Gauss, Green, Ostrogradsky and others; the modern forms version is due to Cartan (1930s) with Poincaré's input, which is why some prefer the name 'fundamental theorem of exterior calculus'.

**Why tempting:** Eponymous names suggest a single inventor.


## Thought experiments

### Slicing the interval · GA §38.1 p.397 (pdf 414)

**Setup:** Chop [a, b] into many thin slices and write the integral of df/dx as a sum of differences of f across each slice.

**Lesson:** Interior contributions cancel in pairs, so only the boundary values survive, with signs set by orientation.

**App idea:** Slider for the number of slices; interior bars fade out as they cancel; generalize to a tiled surface where interior edge circulations cancel.

### Applying the boundary operator twice · GA §38.4 p.404 (pdf 421)

**Setup:** Take a disc, find its edge (a circle), then try to find the edge of that circle; repeat with a cylinder, a ball, a curve.

**Lesson:** Whatever you start with, the edge of an edge is empty, which is the chain-side version of dd = 0.

**App idea:** Part of the Fig. 38.1 redesign: a 'boundary' button pressed twice on any shape.

### Removing the origin from the plane · GA p.408 (pdf 425)

**Setup:** Consider the vortex 1-form, which blows up at the origin, so the origin must be cut out; integrate the form round loops that do or do not encircle the hole.

**Lesson:** A form can be closed everywhere it is defined yet not exact, because the domain has a hole; topology of the domain controls whether closed implies exact.

**App idea:** Draggable loop in the punctured plane with a live circulation readout showing 0 or 2 pi A depending on winding number.


## Applications and observations

- **Conservation of electric charge** (observation, GA §38.5 p.407 (pdf 424)): The flux 3-form J^mu dSigma_mu (Example 38.2) and Stokes' theorem are flagged as the ingredients for a geometric statement of charge conservation; the margin preview of integrating a 2-form over a small coordinate square leads into the field-tensor treatment of Chapter 43.
- **Geometric phases in magnetism from holonomy on a sphere** (observation, GA p.409 (pdf 426)): Exercise 38.4 closes by noting that rotation of a transported vector by the enclosed area is useful for computing geometric phases in magnetism (e.g. a spin direction carried round a loop on the sphere of orientations). Key numbers: Rotation angle = enclosed solid angle on the unit sphere (modulo 2 pi).
- **Holonomy estimate for simple loops (our own estimate)** (numerical-estimate, GA p.408 (pdf 425)): Using the result of Exercise 38.4: a vector carried round the boundary of one octant of a unit sphere (pole to equator, along a quarter of the equator, back to the pole) returns rotated by the octant area; on a sphere of radius R the angle is area divided by R^2. For a latitude circle at latitude L the enclosed polar cap has area 2 pi (1 - sin L), so the rotation is 2 pi (1 - sin L), which modulo 2 pi is a turn of magnitude 2 pi sin L in the opposite sense: the same angle through which a Foucault pendulum's swing plane turns relative to the ground in one sidereal day. Key numbers: Octant area 4 pi/8 = pi/2, so a 90 degree rotation; at L = 51.75 degrees, sin L = 0.785 and 2 pi sin L = 4.93 rad, about 283 degrees per sidereal day.
- **Circulation of the vortex form (our own estimate)** (numerical-estimate, GA p.408 (pdf 425)): Integrating the vortex 1-form of Exercise 38.3 once counterclockwise round any loop encircling the origin gives the same value, while loops not enclosing the origin give zero; this is the obstruction to exactness. Key numbers: Circulation = 2 pi A for winding number one, 2 pi A n for winding number n.

## Notation and conventions

- **Forms and exterior derivative:** Forms carry a tilde (alpha-tilde, Omega-tilde); basis 1-forms and the exterior derivative use bold upright d (dx, dOmega), while roman d is kept for ordinary integration measures (dx dy dz, d lambda). — Easy to miss in print; eqn 38.31 writes the n-form as a bold alpha without its tilde, and the chapter summary's d in eqn 38.58 is the bold exterior derivative, not an ordinary differential. (GA §38.1 p.399 (pdf 416))
- **Integral of a form:** Written as an integral subscripted by the domain with the form as integrand and no trailing differential. (GA §38.1 p.399 (pdf 416))
- **Boundary operator:** The partial symbol applied to a region or chain: boundary of C written with that symbol before C; its square is zero. — Not to be confused with a partial derivative. (GA §38.4 p.404 (pdf 421))
- **Chains, cycles, boundaries:** C_n for an n-chain, Z_n for a cycle (closed chain), B_n for an n-chain that is a boundary. (GA §38.4 p.404 (pdf 421))
- **Pairing:** Angle brackets <alpha, v> for the contraction of a p-form with a p-vector, computed with ordered indices: alpha_{|i1...in|} v^{i1...in}. (GA §38.3 p.403 (pdf 420))
- **Ordered-index sums:** Vertical bars around indices restrict a sum to increasing index order; equivalently use an unrestricted sum with a 1/p! factor. — The margin note writes the bars around alpha beta gamma delta while the display uses mu nu alpha beta. (GA §38.3 p.402 (pdf 419))
- **Coordinate labels:** Coordinates are numbered from 1 in this chapter's general n-dimensional formulas, instead of from 0 as for spacetime elsewhere in the book; eqn 38.17 still uses 0 to n. (GA §38.3 p.401 (pdf 418))
- **Levi-Civita normalization and volume form:** epsilon_{0123} = +1 so the volume 4-form is dt ^ dx ^ dy ^ dz; the volume form is the Hodge dual of 1 and dSigma_mu the Hodge dual of e_mu. (GA §38.3 p.402 (pdf 419))
- **Metric determinant factor:** In a coordinate frame the volume form carries [(-1)^s det g_{mu nu}]^{1/2}, with s the number of minus signs in the signature; for 3D space s = 0 and det g is written g. — The book's spacetime signature is (-,+,+,+) (Chapters 2 and 37), so s = 1 for spacetime and the factor is sqrt(-g); s = 0 only for the Euclidean 3D case used here. (GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423))
- **Boundary orientation:** If the region is oriented by dP/dlambda^1 ^ ... ^ dP/dlambda^n, its boundary is oriented by dP/dlambda^2 ^ ... ^ dP/dlambda^n (first direction taken outward). (GA §38.5 p.406 (pdf 423))
- **Flat-space restriction:** The chapter works in flat space and spacetime with Cartesian coordinates unless a metric factor is written explicitly. (GA §38.1 p.399 (pdf 416))
- **Vector notation:** Both arrows (vector a, del) and bold symbols (F, dS) are used for 3-vectors; dS and dSigma denote vector surface elements. (GA §38.1 p.398 (pdf 415))

## Teaching gems

### Telescoping sum as the origin of boundary terms and their signs. · GA §38.1 p.397 (pdf 414)

**Why it works:** Uses nothing beyond a Riemann sum, yet contains the whole mechanism of Stokes: interior contributions cancel pairwise, and orientation decides which end is plus.

**App idea:** Animated slice-cancellation on an interval that the learner can then 'extrude' to a grid of squares and cubes, watching shared edges and faces cancel to leave only the outer boundary.

### Integration as counting pieces of a form met by the domain (sheets crossed, tubes pierced, cells enclosed). · GA §38.2 p.400 (pdf 417)

## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Chains are never presented as formal sums with signed (oriented) pieces, and the boundary is defined as a point set; the minus sign in f(b) - f(a) is not connected to the boundary of a segment being B minus A.** — The orientation rule for Stokes feels arbitrary and the double-boundary rule looks like a tautology rather than a cancellation of signed pieces. *Suggestion:* Introduce oriented simplices: boundary of [A,B] is B - A, of a triangle is the signed sum of its edges; show that the boundary of that sum cancels vertex by vertex.
- **The margin notes claim that in a simply connected space every closed chain is a boundary and every closed form is exact; this is only true for loops and 1-forms.** — Learners may wrongly apply the Poincaré lemma to 2-forms on domains like space minus a point (e.g. the Coulomb field's flux form, or magnetic monopole potentials). *Suggestion:* State the Poincaré lemma for contractible (star-shaped) regions and give the solid-angle 2-form on space minus the origin as the higher-degree counterexample.
- **The orientation rule (eqns 38.44-38.45) labels an (n+1)-chain with n parameters and leaves implicit that the dropped first direction must point outward.** — Sign errors in applications, particularly in odd dimensions or when parameters are ordered differently. *Suggestion:* Write the rule as: orientation of C = (outward normal) ^ (orientation of boundary), and test it on a segment, a disc (counterclockwise edge) and a ball (outward normal).
- **Stokes' theorem is stated without proof or even a sketch.** — The theorem can feel like magic; the connection between the telescoping picture and the general theorem is left unmade. *Suggestion:* Sketch the proof for a small cube: d of a form integrated over the cube reduces, by the fundamental theorem in one variable, to opposite-face differences; then glue cubes and cancel shared faces.
- **The pairing definition of the integral is the pullback of the form to parameter space, but the concept and its independence from parametrization are not named or checked.** — Learners may worry that different parametrizations give different answers, and later texts using 'pullback' will seem unrelated. *Suggestion:* Name the pullback, show that reparametrizing multiplies the pairing by the parameter Jacobian and the measure by its inverse, and note the sign flip for orientation-reversing maps.
- **The set-theoretic definition of boundary (non-interior points) conflicts with the manifold notion used in the examples.** — A careful reader may object that a circle in the plane is its own topological boundary, yet Fig. 38.1 says its boundary is empty. *Suggestion:* Say explicitly that the boundary is taken intrinsically, for the region regarded as a manifold with boundary, not as a subset of a larger space.
- **Example 38.7's area 2-form (unit normal inserted into the volume form) is easily confused with the flux 2-form of eqns 38.10 and 38.54, and eqn 38.50 writes a scalar area integral with the notation n . dS.** — Learners may think the area of a surface and the flux of a field are computed by the same 2-form. *Suggestion:* Contrast explicitly: the flux form uses the field components, the area form uses the unit normal of a particular surface and is therefore surface-dependent.
- **Misprints: Stokes' birth year given as 1919 (should be 1819); a cross product sign where a wedge is meant in d alpha^1 x d alpha^2 just before Example 38.1; f(x, y, x) for f(x, y, z) in Example 38.6; 'as we saw in Example 12' (meaning Example 38.3) in Example 38.8; 'The ball S^2' in Exercise 38.2 (the sphere is meant); 'Ostragradskii' for Ostrogradskii in note 15; the boundary-orientation displays use n parameters for an (n+1)-chain.** — Small but distracting, and the ball/sphere slip undermines the point of the exercise. *Suggestion:* Silently correct in derived materials and flag the ball/sphere distinction as a teaching point.
- **Exercise 38.4(c) asserts that the accumulated angle equals minus the enclosed area, which in (theta, phi) coordinates holds only modulo 2 pi (a loop round the pole picks up an extra 2 pi from the frame singularity; e.g. a latitude circle gives 2 pi cos theta while the cap area is 2 pi (1 - cos theta)).** — Learners checking the formula on a latitude circle find an apparent contradiction. *Suggestion:* Note that rotation angles are defined modulo 2 pi and that Green's theorem must be applied on a chart that excludes the poles or to the complementary region.
- **The chapter is restricted to flat space, and a margin note says sqrt(g) must return in curved space, without stressing that Stokes' theorem itself is metric-independent and holds on any manifold.** — Learners may think the theorem needs modification for GR, when only volume-element translations do. *Suggestion:* State clearly that forms, d, chains and Stokes need no metric; the metric only enters when converting forms to scalar or vector densities.
- **The derivation of the curl theorem in Section 38.1 says 'set i = j' when it means contract (sum over) i and j, and hides a sign from the Levi-Civita cyclic reordering.** — Index-novices may not reproduce the step. *Suggestion:* Write the contraction explicitly: epsilon_{jkm} d_k a_j dS_m = -(curl a) . dS, which cancels the minus sign in eqn 38.5.
- **Homology and de Rham cohomology are hinted at (cycles versus boundaries, closed versus exact) but never named.** — The parallel between the two margin notes is easy to miss, and the learner lacks vocabulary for later reading on topology in physics. *Suggestion:* Add a short sidebar: cycles modulo boundaries (homology) pair with closed forms modulo exact forms (cohomology) via integration, which is why a non-zero integral of a closed form over a cycle detects a hole.

## Tutor notes

- Open by asking the learner to state the fundamental theorem of calculus and then to explain why the answer depends only on the end points and why one end gets a minus sign; guide them to the telescoping sum before introducing any forms.
- Before Section 38.3, check that the learner can evaluate a 2-form on two vectors and knows the wedge is antisymmetric; if not, revisit GA ch32 briefly, because the Jacobian and pairing arguments depend on it.
- Suggested order: vector theorems and their shared shape, integrands as forms (with the missing dx defended), counting pictures, Jacobian from wedges, pairing definition of the integral, boundaries with pictures, chains and the double-boundary rule, d as the partner operator, Stokes, then Example 38.8 as payoff.
- Emphasise orientation early: have the learner compute the boundary of a segment as B minus A and the boundary of a square as four signed edges, then show the boundary of that sum cancels; this makes the double-boundary rule a computation instead of a slogan.
- Check for understanding: ask which classical theorem results from applying Stokes to a 1-form on a 2-chain in 3D (curl theorem) and to a 1-form on a planar region (Green); ask what d does to each degree in 3D.
- Common question: 'Why do we need chains when we already have surfaces?' Answer: chains track orientation and allow sums of pieces (e.g. the boundary of an annulus is two oppositely oriented circles), which is exactly what the theorem needs.
- Common question: 'Is the boundary operator a derivative?' Clarify that it acts on domains and lowers dimension, while d acts on integrands and raises degree; they are paired by integration.
- Common question: 'If dA = 0, can I always find a potential?' Use the vortex form: compute its loop integral round the origin (2 pi A) to show no potential exists; stress that the answer depends on holes in the domain, and warn that simple connectivity is enough only for 1-forms.
- Flag the misprints when the learner reads the book: sphere versus ball in Exercise 38.2, 'Example 12' meaning Example 38.3, and the cross that should be a wedge in d alpha^1 x d alpha^2 (p. 401).
- For learners heading to GR, connect to physics: the flux 3-form J^mu dSigma_mu plus Stokes gives global charge conservation from the local continuity equation, and Exercise 38.4 shows curvature as rotation of transported vectors, previewing the Riemann tensor and Bianchi identity in Chapter 43.
- For advanced learners, name the pullback, homology and cohomology, and state that Stokes' theorem needs no metric; for novices, keep to 2D and 3D pictures and the telescoping idea.
- A quick diagnostic calculation: ask the learner to compute d(x dy) in the plane and integrate over the unit square both ways (region integral of dx^dy and boundary integral of x dy counterclockwise); both give 1.
