# GA ch10 · Making measurements in relativity

> Explains how an observer turns coordinate-free objects into laboratory numbers: the observer carries an orthonormal frame whose time axis is their own 4-velocity, components are read off by projection (giving E = -p.u), vielbein matrices translate between the coordinate bases used for calculation and the non-coordinate orthonormal bases where measurements happen, and two workhorse frames are built: the stationary frame of an observer at rest in the coordinates and the parallel-transported freely falling frame.

**Pages:** printed 108–119 · pdf 125–136 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

The physical idea (measurements are projections onto an observer's frame, E = -p.u) is simple and well illustrated, and most examples use diagonal 2D metrics. Difficulty comes from dense index bookkeeping with hatted and unhatted indices, the proliferation of vielbein rules stated rather than motivated in the main text (proofs sit in margin notes), several printing slips (10.28, the summary's missing square, g_phiphi in note 14, sin^2 phi in Exercise 10.1), and a freely falling frame section that asserts rather than shows vanishing connection. Nothing beyond linear algebra and ch7 covariant derivatives is required.

## Role in the book

Placed between the connection and geodesic chapters (GA ch7-ch9) and the curvature chapter (GA ch11). Once geodesics and covariant derivatives can be computed in any coordinates, the book must say what a laboratory would actually record, taking Einstein's warning that coordinates have no built-in metric meaning as its theme. The tools introduced here are used straight away in §11.7 (tidal deviation in a falling frame) and then repeatedly: orthonormal fluid components (ch12), Robertson-Walker observers (ch16), static and infalling observers and photon energies in the Schwarzschild geometry (ch21-ch25), and the frame-based curvature calculations of Cartan's method in Part V (ch36). The authors mark everything from §10.2 onward as optional on a first pass, so the chapter doubles as a reference toolkit.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can explain why a coordinate labelled t or r need not be a time or a distance, and how numbers acquire meaning only through an observer's local orthonormal frame.
- Reader can obtain a measured component of any vector by dotting it with the observer's hatted basis vector, and raise or lower hatted indices with eta.
- Reader can construct an observer's orthonormal frame by setting e_0hat equal to their 4-velocity and completing it with orthogonal unit spacelike vectors, as for the uniformly accelerated observer.
- Reader can use E = -p.u_obs to compute the energy or frequency an observer measures, including gamma m for a moving observer and the exponential redshift omega exp(-g tau) seen by a uniformly accelerated observer.
- Reader can distinguish a coordinate basis from a non-coordinate (orthonormal) basis and state the commutator test.
- Reader can derive the radial and transverse acceleration components in a rotating orthonormal polar basis from the time derivatives of the unit vectors.
- Reader can define vielbein components (e_alphahat)^mu and (e_mu)^alphahat, treat them as mutually inverse matrices, and use them to convert vector, 1-form and tensor components between frames.
- Reader can state and check the defining relations g_{mu nu} = eta_{alphahat betahat} (e_mu)^alphahat (e_nu)^betahat and its inverse.
- Reader can write down the stationary orthonormal frame for any diagonal metric by normalizing with 1/sqrt|g_mumu|, including the static observer in the Schwarzschild geometry.
- Reader can transform a perfect-fluid energy-momentum tensor into orthonormal components and see it reduce to diag(rho, p, p, p).
- Reader can define a freely falling frame by parallel transport of an orthonormal tetrad along a geodesic and build it for radial infall from rest at infinity in Schwarzschild.
- Reader can show that in a freely falling frame the covariant proper-time derivative of a vector has frame components equal to ordinary derivatives of its frame components.

## Teaching approach

A toolkit chapter framed by a philosophical warning. It opens with Einstein's remark that coordinates lack direct metric meaning, then makes that concrete: a measurement is a projection onto an observer's own orthonormal axes, whose time axis is the observer's 4-velocity. A 2D momentum triangle and a world line decorated with little frames carry the picture; the first payoff, E = -p.u, is checked on a moving observer and on an accelerated observer seeing an exponential redshift. The authors then detour through a familiar Newtonian case (rotating polar unit vectors in the Kepler problem) to show why orthonormal bases are non-coordinate bases, and introduce vielbein components as the translation matrices. Almost everything is carried by short numbered examples with two-dimensional metrics (polar plane, unit sphere) before the payoff metrics (Schwarzschild static observer, perfect fluid, radial infall). Heavier 1-form justifications are pushed into margin notes, and there is an explicit skip-ahead sign for impatient readers.

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, components-first, example-driven, computational-recipe, conversational-informal, diagram-driven

**Narrative arc**

1. Motivating tension: GR lives in curved spacetime with arbitrary coordinates, but experiments are done locally in labs where spacetime looks Minkowskian; Einstein quote and slogan that coordinates carry no immediate metric meaning
2. Model of a measurement: a vector exists independently of coordinates; the observer projects it onto their hatted orthonormal axes (Fig. 10.1, Example 10.1)
3. Orienting the frame: the time basis vector equals the observer's 4-velocity (Fig. 10.2); remaining axes chosen orthogonal; worked for the uniformly accelerated observer (Example 10.2)
4. First payoff: measured energy E = -p.u_obs; checked for a particle seen by a moving observer (gamma m) and applied to starlight seen by the accelerated observer (exponential redshift, Example 10.3)
5. Complication announced: the natural orthonormal frame is a non-coordinate basis; optional-reading sign posted
6. Familiar illustration: normalized polar basis in the Kepler problem, where rotating unit vectors generate the r-theta-dot-squared and 2 r-dot theta-dot terms (Example 10.4, Fig. 10.3)
7. Formalism: coordinate basis dot products give g, orthonormal ones give eta; vielbein components translate components both ways (10.17-10.21), demonstrated on polar coordinates (Example 10.5)
8. Matrix view: vielbein as square matrix and its inverse on the unit sphere (Example 10.6); rules for swapping indices on vectors, 1-forms and tensors (10.38-10.40); defining relations boxed (10.41-10.42); 1-form justification in margin
9. Check: E = -p.u rederived with vielbeins (Example 10.7)
10. First standard frame: stationary orthonormal frame of an observer at rest in the coordinates; orthonormality as a matrix equation (Example 10.8) and the square-root normalization recipe for diagonal metrics (Example 10.9, eqn 10.51)
11. Applications: static observer in Schwarzschild (Example 10.10); perfect-fluid stress tensor becomes diag(rho,p,p,p) in the orthonormal frame (Example 10.11)
12. Second standard frame: freely falling frame defined by parallel transport of the tetrad along a geodesic, motivated by the equivalence principle (eqn 10.60)
13. Application: tetrad for radial infall from rest at infinity in Schwarzschild (Example 10.12)
14. Consistency check: in the falling frame covariant derivatives of vectors become ordinary derivatives of frame components (Example 10.13), setting up geodesic deviation in ch11

**Signature moves**

- Uses Einstein's own diagnosis of why GR took seven more years (coordinates are not measurements) as the organizing theme for a technical chapter.
- Defines a measurement operationally as a dot product with the observer's basis vector, so that the sign in E = -p.u falls straight out of eta_00 = -1.
- Identifies the observer's time axis with their 4-velocity and shows frames riding along a world line, making 'observer' a geometric object rather than a coordinate system.
- Explains non-coordinate bases through a Newtonian problem the reader already knows (polar unit vectors in orbital mechanics) before any GR use.
- Builds every vielbein first for a 2D metric (plane polar, unit sphere) where the matrices are diagonal and checkable by eye, then scales up to Schwarzschild.
- Summarizes the vielbein as 'square roots of the metric components' for diagonal metrics, a compact working rule.
- Records vielbein components for each metric in margin notes, a habit the book promises to continue in later chapters.
- Places the 1-form derivation of the index-swapping rules in a long margin note, keeping the main line recipe-like while giving careful readers a proof.
- Explicit reader-routing box telling curvature-hungry readers they can skip from §10.2 to ch11.

## Section by section

### Chapter opening: coordinates are not measurements — p.108 (pdf 125)

Opens with Einstein's reflection that the slow progress toward GR came from assuming coordinates must directly measure lengths and times. States that experiments are local, so an observer's small laboratory sees Minkowski spacetime, and that the link between curved-spacetime objects and laboratory numbers is the local orthonormal frame, whose components the book marks with hats and whose metric is diag(-1,1,1,1). Warns that labels such as t and r must not be read as time and radius until related to an observer's measurements; a margin note adds an H. G. Wells line about time as a fourth dimension.

**Concepts:** coordinates have no immediate metrical significance, local orthonormal frame

**Key moves**
- Global curved stage versus local flat laboratory
- Hatted indices introduced for orthonormal-frame components; eta_{muhat nuhat} = e_muhat . e_nuhat = diag(-1,1,1,1)
- Slogan: coordinates acquire meaning only via local measurements

### §10.1 Observers and their observations — p.108 (pdf 125)

A particle's momentum is a coordinate-free vector; an observer measures one of its components by dotting it with one of their orthonormal axes, which yields the lower-index component that eta can raise. The observer's axes are oriented by making the time basis vector equal to the observer's 4-velocity, with the spatial vectors chosen orthogonal and normalized; Example 10.2 carries this out for the uniformly accelerated observer, obtaining a boosted pair of cosh-sinh vectors. The energy an observer measures is then E = -p.u_obs, checked for a particle at rest viewed by a moving observer (E = gamma m) and applied to a photon from a star seen by the accelerated observer, whose measured frequency decays exponentially with proper time.

**Concepts:** local orthonormal frame, measurement as projection onto the observer's basis, observer 4-velocity as the time basis vector, uniformly accelerated observer's orthonormal frame, energy measured by an observer, exponential redshift seen by a uniformly accelerated observer, wave 4-vector of light

**Key moves**
- p . e_alphahat = p^muhat eta_{muhat alphahat} = p_alphahat (10.1)
- e_0hat = u_obs, so the time axis has the components of the 4-velocity (10.2-10.3)
- Accelerated observer: impose orthogonality (10.5) and normalization (10.6) to find e_1hat = (sinh g tau, cosh g tau, 0, 0) (10.7)
- E = -p.u_obs = -p_0hat = p^0hat (10.8)
- Moving observer: E = gamma m (10.9); photon: omega(tau) = omega exp(-g tau) (10.10)

### §10.2 Coordinate and non-coordinate bases — p.110 (pdf 127)

Flagged as skippable on a first read. Recalls that polar coordinate basis vectors come from derivatives of Cartesian ones and have |e_theta| = r, whereas normalizing e_theta gives an orthonormal set that cannot be generated that way and whose members fail to commute: a non-coordinate basis. Example 10.4 shows such bases are natural in mechanics by differentiating rotating polar unit vectors to get the radial and transverse acceleration components. Calculations are usually done in coordinate bases (e_mu . e_nu = g) and measurements in orthonormal ones (e_alphahat . e_betahat = eta), so vielbein components are introduced as the matrices converting components between the two, with plane polar coordinates (Example 10.5) and the unit sphere (Example 10.6) as worked cases. General rules follow for converting upper and lower components and tensor components, the defining relations linking g and eta through the vielbein are boxed, a margin note justifies the rules using basis 1-forms, and Example 10.7 rederives E = -p.u in vielbein language.

**Concepts:** coordinate basis, non-coordinate basis, rotating orthonormal polar basis vectors, acceleration components in plane polar coordinates, metric as dot products of basis vectors, vielbein, vielbein as an invertible matrix, vielbein transformation of tensor components, vielbein defining relations, orthonormal basis 1-forms, energy measured by an observer

**Key moves**
- Normalization e_thetahat = e_theta / r breaks the coordinate-basis property (footnote: non-commuting basis vectors)
- d e_rhat/dt = omega e_thetahat and d e_thetahat/dt = -omega e_rhat (10.13-10.14) give a^rhat = r-ddot - r theta-dot^2 and a^thetahat = r theta-ddot + 2 r-dot theta-dot (10.15-10.16)
- Same vector, two expansions: a = a^alpha e_alpha = a^betahat e_betahat (10.19)
- a^alpha = a^betahat (e_betahat)^alpha and a^betahat = a^alpha (e_alpha)^betahat (10.20-10.21)
- Check orthonormality with g_{alpha beta}(e_muhat)^alpha (e_nuhat)^beta = eta (10.28)
- Vielbein matrices are inverses of each other (10.31)
- Index-swap rules for vectors, 1-forms, tensors (10.38-10.40); boxed defining relations (10.41-10.42)
- Margin derivation via e_muhat = (e_muhat)^alpha e_alpha and <omega^mu, e_nuhat> = (e_nuhat)^mu (10.33-10.37)

### §10.3 The orthonormal frame — p.114 (pdf 131)

Introduces the stationary orthonormal frame, belonging to an observer at rest with respect to the coordinates, whose 4-velocity has only a time component fixed by g_00 (u^0)^2 = -1. Margin notes establish it as the default frame whenever the book does not name an observer, and caution that its metric is Minkowskian but its connection coefficients need not vanish (that stronger property belongs to a local inertial frame). Finding the frame amounts to diagonalizing and normalizing the metric; Example 10.8 writes the condition as a matrix product for 1+1 dimensions, and Example 10.9 gives the recipe for diagonal metrics, summarized as vielbein components equal to reciprocal square roots (or square roots) of |g_mumu|. Example 10.10 applies it to the static observer in the Schwarzschild geometry and Example 10.11 converts a perfect-fluid energy-momentum tensor in a spherical-coordinate metric to orthonormal components, where it becomes diag(rho,p,p,p).

**Concepts:** stationary orthonormal frame, normalization of a static observer's 4-velocity, orthonormal frame for a diagonal metric, matrix form of the orthonormality condition, Schwarzschild metric, static observer frame in the Schwarzschild geometry, perfect-fluid energy-momentum tensor in an orthonormal frame, connection coefficients, local inertial frame

**Key moves**
- u^mu = (u^0,0,0,0) with u^0 = (-g_00)^{-1/2} (10.47)
- Solve g(e_alphahat, e_betahat) = eta_{alphahat betahat} (10.44-10.45); matrix form E g E^T = eta (10.46)
- Diagonal metric: e_0hat = e_0/sqrt(-g_00), e_ihat = e_i/sqrt(g_ii) (10.48-10.49); (e_muhat)^mu = 1/sqrt|g_mumu| (10.51)
- Schwarzschild static tetrad (10.53-10.55)
- T_{thetahat thetahat} = (e_thetahat)^theta (e_thetahat)^theta T_{theta theta} = p (10.58)

### §10.4 Freely falling frames — p.116 (pdf 133)

Recalls from ch6 that a local inertial frame has Minkowski metric and vanishing first metric derivatives (hence vanishing connection) at a point, and singles out the freely falling frame as the most useful way to realize one: by the equivalence principle a small falling laboratory should see no gravity. The frame's time vector is the geodesic's 4-velocity, spatial vectors are chosen orthonormal to it at one event and parallel transported along the geodesic, so the frame is characterized by the covariant derivative of each basis vector along u vanishing (automatic for the time vector because the world line is a geodesic). Example 10.12 builds the tetrad for radial infall from rest at infinity in Schwarzschild, and Example 10.13, with a margin argument using parallel-transported basis 1-forms, shows that in this frame the components of D chi/d tau are simply the proper-time derivatives of the frame components of chi.

**Concepts:** local inertial frame, freely falling orthonormal frame, equivalence principle, geodesic, parallel transport, infalling observer frame in the Schwarzschild geometry, covariant derivative along a world line, covariant derivative reduces to ordinary derivative in a freely falling frame, orthonormal basis 1-forms

**Key moves**
- Equivalence principle motivates vanishing connection along the fall
- Definition nabla_u e_alphahat = 0 for all alpha (10.60), with e_0hat = u satisfied by the geodesic equation
- Infall 4-velocity (10.61); diagonal choices for angular vectors; e_rhat fixed by normalization (10.62) with (e_rhat)^r = 1 (10.63)
- Pull the vielbein inside the covariant derivative because omega^muhat is parallel transported (10.65-10.68)
- Covariant derivative of a scalar is a directional derivative, giving (D chi/d tau)^muhat = d chi^muhat / d tau (10.69-10.71)

### Chapter summary and exercises — p.118 (pdf 135)

The summary lists five points: observers measure in orthonormal frames with e_0hat = u; the vielbein converts between frames; the stationary and freely falling frames are the two convenient choices; for a static observer u^0 = (-g_00)^{-1/2}; and diagonal-metric vielbein components are square roots of metric components. Seven exercises follow on orthonormal components in an open Robertson-Walker-type metric, orthonormal connection coefficients and commutators, radial motion seen by a local observer, gradient and divergence in orthogonal coordinates, the absence of frequency shift between points on a centrifuge rim, the Newman-Penrose null tetrad, and a vielbein for a light-cone metric.

**Concepts:** vielbein, stationary orthonormal frame, freely falling orthonormal frame, Newman-Penrose null tetrad, non-coordinate basis

**Key moves**
- Summary restates u^0 = (-g_00)^{-1/2} and (e_i)^ihat = sqrt(g_ii)
- Exercises extend to connection coefficients in a non-coordinate basis and to non-orthonormal (null) tetrads

## Concepts

### coordinates have no immediate metrical significance

*principle · developed* · also: coordinates are meaningless labels

Coordinate values in general relativity are arbitrary labels on events; a coordinate called t or r becomes a time or a distance only after it is related, through the metric, to intervals measured by some observer in their local frame.

**How introduced:** Motivated by Einstein's remark that the delay in completing GR came from assuming coordinates directly measure lengths and times; stated as a slogan in the chapter opening and made operational by the orthonormal-frame machinery.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, local orthonormal frame

**Notes:** Returns implicitly whenever the book later distinguishes coordinate speed dr/dt from locally measured speed (Exercise 10.3).

**Where:** GA p.108 (pdf 125)

### local orthonormal frame

*definition · core* · also: orthonormal tetrad, local Lorentz frame, hatted frame, observer's laboratory frame

A set of four basis vectors e_alphahat at an event, one timelike and three spacelike, that are mutually orthogonal and unit-normalized, so that e_alphahat . e_betahat = eta_{alphahat betahat} = diag(-1,1,1,1). Components referred to it are what an observer carrying the frame measures, and hatted indices are raised and lowered with eta.

**How introduced:** Introduced as the bridge between curved-spacetime objects and laboratory numbers, pictured first as ordinary 2D axes on which a momentum vector is resolved (Fig. 10.1), then as little frames carried along a world line (Fig. 10.2).

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, basis vectors, Minkowski metric

$$
\boldsymbol e_{\hat\alpha}\cdot\boldsymbol e_{\hat\beta} = \eta_{\hat\alpha\hat\beta} = \mathrm{diag}(-1,1,1,1)
$$

**Notes:** The book notes the axes are 'usually' orthogonal; Exercise 10.6 shows a non-orthonormal (null) local basis as an alternative.

**Where:** GA p.108 (pdf 125); GA §10.1 p.108 (pdf 125); GA §10.1 p.109 (pdf 126); GA §10.2 p.111 (pdf 128)

### measurement as projection onto the observer's basis

*operation · core* · also: projecting out a component

An observer measures the component of a vector along one of their axes by taking its dot product with that basis vector; because the frame metric is eta, this returns the lower-index frame component, and the upper-index component follows by raising with eta.

**How introduced:** A particle's momentum passing through a lab is resolved on the lab's axes (Fig. 10.1) and Example 10.1 carries out the dot product.

**Prerequisites:** local orthonormal frame, dot product

$$
\boldsymbol p\cdot\boldsymbol e_{\hat\alpha} = p^{\hat\mu}\eta_{\hat\mu\hat\alpha} = p_{\hat\alpha}
$$

$$
p^{\hat\beta} = \eta^{\hat\alpha\hat\beta}p_{\hat\alpha}
$$

**Where:** GA §10.1 p.108 (pdf 125); GA §10.1 p.109 (pdf 126)

### observer 4-velocity as the time basis vector

*convention · core* · also: e_0hat = u_obs

An observer's orthonormal frame is oriented so that its timelike basis vector coincides with the observer's 4-velocity, the unit tangent to their world line; the three spatial basis vectors are then any orthonormal triad perpendicular to it.

**How introduced:** Argued from the fact that a clock at rest in the observer's lab moves through spacetime along the world line; drawn as frames along a curved world line (Fig. 10.2) and used in every later example.

**Prerequisites:** 4-velocity, local orthonormal frame

$$
\boldsymbol e_{\hat 0} = \boldsymbol u_{\rm obs}
$$

$$
(\boldsymbol e_{\hat 0})^\mu = u^\mu_{\rm obs}
$$

**Notes:** The spatial triad is fixed only up to a rotation; the book resolves this by convenient choices (aligning with coordinate directions) or, for falling frames, by parallel transport.

**Where:** GA §10.1 p.109 (pdf 126); GA §10.3 p.114 (pdf 131); GA §10.4 p.116 (pdf 133); GA p.118 (pdf 135)

### uniformly accelerated observer's orthonormal frame

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: accelerated observer tetrad, Rindler observer frame

For an observer with constant proper acceleration g along x in Minkowski space, the frame is e_0hat = (cosh g tau, sinh g tau, 0, 0), e_1hat = (sinh g tau, cosh g tau, 0, 0), with e_2hat and e_3hat along y and z; it is a Lorentz boost with rapidity g tau that changes along the world line.

**How introduced:** Example 10.2 revisits the hyperbolic world line from ch2, writes a trial e_1hat with two unknown functions and fixes them by orthogonality to u and unit normalization.

**Prerequisites:** observer 4-velocity as the time basis vector, hyperbolic motion

$$
(\boldsymbol e_{\hat 0})^\mu = (\cosh g\tau, \sinh g\tau, 0,0)
$$

$$
(\boldsymbol e_{\hat 1})^\mu = (\sinh g\tau, \cosh g\tau, 0,0)
$$

**Notes:** The name Rindler is not used here; the example re-uses the letter g both for the acceleration and for an unknown component function.

**Where:** GA §10.1 p.109 (pdf 126)

### energy measured by an observer

*identity · core* · also: E = -p.u, observed energy formula

The energy that an observer with 4-velocity u_obs assigns to a particle of 4-momentum p is the scalar E = -p.u_obs; since e_0hat = u_obs this is minus the lower time component in the observer's frame, which equals the upper time component because eta_00 = -1. The same rule with the wave vector gives the measured frequency.

**How introduced:** Presented as a particularly handy tool right after the frame is oriented, proved in one line (10.8), tested on a particle at rest viewed by a moving observer (gamma m), and reproved in vielbein language in Example 10.7.

**Prerequisites:** measurement as projection onto the observer's basis, observer 4-velocity as the time basis vector, 4-momentum

$$
E = -\boldsymbol p\cdot\boldsymbol u_{\rm obs} = -p_{\hat 0} = p^{\hat 0}
$$

$$
E = -g_{\mu\nu}p^\mu(\boldsymbol e_{\hat 0})^\nu = -p_\nu(\boldsymbol e_{\hat 0})^\nu
$$

**Notes:** Because the right-hand side is a scalar it may be evaluated in any convenient frame (margin note 2). This is the single most reused result of the chapter, e.g. for gravitational redshift in Part IV.

**Where:** GA §10.1 p.110 (pdf 127); GA §10.2 p.113 (pdf 130)

### wave 4-vector of light

*mathematical-object · mention* · also: k vector, photon 4-momentum

The 4-vector k with components (omega, k^x, k^y, k^z) describing a light wave, with omega = |k| for light in units c = 1; with hbar = 1 it doubles as the photon's 4-momentum, so an observer measures frequency omega = -k.u_obs.

**How introduced:** Mentioned in a margin note to justify treating frequency exactly like energy in Example 10.3.

**Prerequisites:** energy measured by an observer

$$
k^\mu = (\omega, k^x, k^y, k^z),\ \omega = |\vec k|
$$

$$
\omega_{\rm obs} = -\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol u_{\rm obs}
$$

**Where:** GA §10.1 p.110 (pdf 127)

### exponential redshift seen by a uniformly accelerated observer

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: accelerated-observer Doppler shift

Light of frequency omega travelling along the direction of acceleration from a source at rest in an inertial frame is received by a uniformly accelerated observer with frequency omega exp(-g tau), decaying exponentially with the observer's proper time.

**How introduced:** Second half of Example 10.3: plug the photon wave vector (omega, omega, 0, 0) and the accelerated 4-velocity into omega = -k.u, and combine cosh minus sinh into an exponential; a margin note links it to Hartle and to black-hole redshift in ch26-ch27.

**Prerequisites:** energy measured by an observer, uniformly accelerated observer's orthonormal frame, wave 4-vector of light

$$
\omega(\tau) = \omega[\cosh g\tau - \sinh g\tau] = \omega e^{-g\tau}
$$

**Notes:** Equivalently a Doppler factor exp(-rapidity) with rapidity g tau. The chapter does not connect it to the accelerated observer's horizon, beyond which no signals ever reach them.

**Where:** GA §10.1 p.110 (pdf 127)

### coordinate basis

*definition · revisited* · also: holonomic basis

A basis whose vectors are the partial-derivative directions of a coordinate system, e_mu = d/dx^mu; its members commute and their dot products are the metric components g_{mu nu}, which in general are not those of eta.

**How introduced:** Recalled from ch3 via plane polar coordinates, where |e_r| = 1 but |e_theta| = r because e_theta is built from derivatives of Cartesian components.

**Prerequisites:** basis vectors, metric tensor

$$
\boldsymbol e_\mu\cdot\boldsymbol e_\nu = g_{\mu\nu}
$$

$$
[\boldsymbol e_\mu, \boldsymbol e_\nu] = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §10.2 p.110 (pdf 127); GA §10.2 p.111 (pdf 128)

### non-coordinate basis

*definition · developed* · also: anholonomic basis, non-holonomic basis

A basis of vector fields that is not the set of partial-derivative directions of any coordinate system; its members generally have non-zero commutators. Orthonormal frames in a curved or curvilinear setting are usually of this type.

**How introduced:** Obtained by normalizing the polar e_theta to e_theta/r, which gives a frame convenient for measurements but not derivable from Cartesian coordinates; the commutator criterion from ch3 is quoted in a margin note. Framed as an 'unpleasant' property of observers' natural frames.

**Prerequisites:** coordinate basis, commutator of vector fields

$$
\boldsymbol e_{\hat\theta} = \boldsymbol e_\theta/r
$$

$$
[\boldsymbol e_{\hat r},\boldsymbol e_{\hat\theta}] = -\boldsymbol e_{\hat\theta}/r \neq 0

**Notes:** The commutator value is from the book's Appendix E answer to Exercise 10.2, not the chapter text. In a non-coordinate basis the connection coefficients are no longer symmetric in their lower indices.

### rotating orthonormal polar basis vectors

*technique · developed* · also: time derivatives of unit vectors

For a point moving in a plane described with polar unit vectors, d e_rhat/dt = theta-dot e_thetahat and d e_thetahat/dt = -theta-dot e_rhat: each unit vector turns at the angular rate, the change being perpendicular to itself.

**How introduced:** Example 10.4 differentiates r = r e_rhat twice for the Kepler problem, with Fig. 10.3 showing the two unit vectors at neighbouring angles and their difference vectors.

**Prerequisites:** non-coordinate basis

$$
\frac{d\boldsymbol e_{\hat r}}{dt} = \omega\,\boldsymbol e_{\hat\theta}
$$

$$
\frac{d\boldsymbol e_{\hat\theta}}{dt} = -\omega\,\boldsymbol e_{\hat r}

**Where:** GA §10.2 p.111 (pdf 128)

### acceleration components in plane polar coordinates

*identity · developed* · also: radial and transverse acceleration

In the orthonormal polar frame the acceleration has radial component r-ddot minus r theta-dot squared (the second term being centripetal) and transverse component r theta-ddot plus 2 r-dot theta-dot (the second term being the Coriolis-like cross term).

**How introduced:** Result of Example 10.4, used to show that non-coordinate bases are standard and useful in classical mechanics.

**Prerequisites:** rotating orthonormal polar basis vectors

$$
a^{\hat r} = \ddot r - r\dot\theta^2
$$

$$
a^{\hat\theta} = r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta

**Notes:** The Kepler problem itself is treated in ch20.

**Where:** GA §10.2 p.111 (pdf 128)

### metric as dot products of basis vectors

The metric components in any basis are the dot products of its basis vectors; for a coordinate basis this gives g_{mu nu}, for an orthonormal frame it gives eta.

**How introduced:** Recalled at the start of the vielbein discussion to set the two bases side by side (10.17-10.18).

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, basis vectors

### vielbein

*mathematical-object · core* · also: vierbein, tetrad components, frame field components, (e_betahat)^alpha

The set of components (e_betahat)^alpha of the orthonormal basis vectors expressed in a coordinate basis, together with the inverse set (e_alpha)^betahat of coordinate basis vectors in the orthonormal basis. Each is a square matrix that converts vector components between the coordinate description and the orthonormal description.

**How introduced:** Introduced as the translator needed because calculations are done in coordinate bases but measurements in orthonormal ones; the German name (many-legged, four-legged in spacetime) and a reading as 'making global coordinates local' are given in margin notes; worked for polar coordinates and the sphere.

**Prerequisites:** local orthonormal frame, coordinate basis, non-coordinate basis

$$
a^\alpha = a^{\hat\beta}(\boldsymbol e_{\hat\beta})^\alpha
$$

$$
a^{\hat\beta} = a^\alpha(\boldsymbol e_\alpha)^{\hat\beta}
$$

**Notes:** The bracket around e is decorative; e_mu^alphahat is an equivalent notation. The notation follows Hartle.

**Where:** GA §10.2 p.112 (pdf 129); GA §10.2 p.113 (pdf 130); GA §10.3 p.115 (pdf 132); GA p.118 (pdf 135)

### vielbein as an invertible matrix

*identity · developed* · also: inverse vielbein

The matrix of coordinate-basis components in the orthonormal frame and the matrix of orthonormal components in the coordinate frame are inverses of each other, so contracting one with the other on either index gives a Kronecker delta.

**How introduced:** Example 10.6 on the unit sphere writes the vielbein as a 2x2 matrix diag(1, sin theta) and inverts it to diag(1, 1/sin theta).

**Prerequisites:** vielbein

$$
(\boldsymbol e_\mu)^{\hat\alpha}(\boldsymbol e_{\hat\alpha})^\nu = \delta_\mu^\nu
$$

$$
(\boldsymbol e_\mu)^{\hat\alpha}(\boldsymbol e_{\hat\beta})^\mu = \delta^{\hat\alpha}_{\hat\beta}
$$

**Notes:** For a diagonal metric the inverse is simply the reciprocal of each diagonal entry, with no summation implied (margin note 13).

**Where:** GA §10.2 p.113 (pdf 130); GA §10.3 p.114 (pdf 131)

### vielbein transformation of tensor components

*operation · core* · also: index-swapping rule, converting between hatted and unhatted indices

Contracting with a vielbein trades a coordinate index for a frame index or vice versa, one index at a time: (e_muhat)^beta turns a lower coordinate index into a lower hatted one and an upper hatted index into an upper coordinate one, while (e_mu)^betahat does the reverse; a tensor needs one vielbein factor per index.

**How introduced:** Stated as a general rule after Example 10.6, with a margin-note proof from the expansions of basis vectors and basis 1-forms, and applied to the fluid stress tensor in Example 10.11 and to the covariant derivative in Example 10.13.

**Prerequisites:** vielbein, 1-form components

$$
(\boldsymbol e_{\hat\mu})^\beta p_\beta = p_{\hat\mu},\ (\boldsymbol e_{\hat\mu})^\beta p^{\hat\mu} = p^\beta
$$

$$
(\boldsymbol e_\mu)^{\hat\beta}p_{\hat\beta} = p_\mu,\ (\boldsymbol e_\mu)^{\hat\beta}p^\mu = p^{\hat\beta}
$$

$$
T_{\alpha\beta} = (\boldsymbol e_\alpha)^{\hat\mu}(\boldsymbol e_\beta)^{\hat\nu}T_{\hat\mu\hat\nu}
$$

**Notes:** Does not apply to connection coefficients, which are not tensors (hint to Exercise 10.2).

**Where:** GA §10.2 p.113 (pdf 130); GA §10.3 p.115 (pdf 132); GA §10.4 p.117 (pdf 134)

### vielbein defining relations

*identity · core* · also: metric from vielbein, g = e^T eta e

The metric in coordinates equals eta contracted with two copies of the vielbein, and conversely eta equals g contracted with two copies of the inverse vielbein; these relations are the conditions any proposed orthonormal frame must satisfy.

**How introduced:** Boxed as the summary of §10.2; used as the check in Example 10.5 and as the equation to solve in §10.3 and Example 10.12.

**Prerequisites:** vielbein, metric tensor

$$
g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\hat\alpha\hat\beta}(\boldsymbol e_\mu)^{\hat\alpha}(\boldsymbol e_\nu)^{\hat\beta}
$$

$$
\eta_{\hat\alpha\hat\beta} = g_{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol e_{\hat\alpha})^\mu(\boldsymbol e_{\hat\beta})^\nu
$$

**Notes:** Given g, the vielbein is not unique: any local Lorentz transformation of the hatted index gives another valid frame, which is why the observer's 4-velocity must be specified.

**Where:** GA §10.2 p.112 (pdf 129); GA §10.2 p.113 (pdf 130); GA §10.3 p.114 (pdf 131)

### orthonormal basis 1-forms

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: dual frame 1-forms, omega^muhat

The basis 1-forms omega^muhat dual to an orthonormal frame, satisfying <omega^muhat, e_nuhat> = delta; they are related to coordinate 1-forms by the vielbein and allow vielbein components to be written as pairings such as (e_nuhat)^mu = <omega^mu, e_nuhat>.

**How introduced:** Used in margin note 10 to derive the index-swapping rules, in margin note 17 for the falling frame, and in the Example 10.13 margin argument; Exercise 10.6(b) asks for them for a null tetrad.

**Prerequisites:** 1-forms, vielbein

$$
\boldsymbol\omega^{\hat\mu} = (\boldsymbol e_\alpha)^{\hat\mu}\boldsymbol\omega^\alpha
$$

$$
\boldsymbol\omega^\alpha = (\boldsymbol e_{\hat\mu})^\alpha\boldsymbol\omega^{\hat\mu}
$$

$$
\langle\boldsymbol\omega^\mu,\boldsymbol e_{\hat\nu}\rangle = (\boldsymbol e_{\hat\nu})^\mu
$$

**Notes:** Margin note stresses that <omega^mu, e_nuhat> is not a Kronecker delta because the two objects belong to different bases.

**Where:** GA §10.2 p.113 (pdf 130); GA §10.4 p.116 (pdf 133); GA §10.4 p.117 (pdf 134)

### stationary orthonormal frame

*definition · core* · also: static observer frame, orthonormal frame of an observer at rest in the coordinates

The orthonormal frame carried by an observer whose spatial coordinates stay fixed, so their 4-velocity points along e_0 with u^0 = (-g_00)^{-1/2}; its time vector is e_0 rescaled to unit length and its spatial vectors are orthonormalized coordinate directions. Its metric is eta, but connection coefficients in it need not vanish.

**How introduced:** Chosen as the natural frame for many later computations, particularly of curvature; margin notes make it the default whenever the book speaks of 'the orthonormal frame' without naming an observer, and contrast it with a local inertial frame.

**Prerequisites:** observer 4-velocity as the time basis vector, vielbein defining relations

$$
u^\mu = (u^0,0,0,0),\quad g_{00}(u^0)^2 = -1
$$

**Notes:** Exists only where g_00 < 0 (e.g. outside a Schwarzschild horizon, or within the light cylinder of a rotating frame); the chapter does not mention this restriction.

**Where:** GA §10.3 p.114 (pdf 131); GA p.118 (pdf 135)

### normalization of a static observer's 4-velocity

*identity · developed* · also: u^0 = (-g_00)^{-1/2}

For an observer at rest in the coordinates the only non-zero 4-velocity component is fixed by u.u = -1 to be the inverse square root of -g_00.

**How introduced:** Example 10.9 and the chapter summary.

**Prerequisites:** stationary orthonormal frame, 4-velocity

$$
u^0 = (-g_{00})^{-1/2}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol e_{\hat 0} = \boldsymbol e_0/\sqrt{-g_{00}}
$$

**Notes:** The printed summary writes g_00 (u^0) = -1 without the square.

**Where:** GA §10.3 p.114 (pdf 131); GA p.118 (pdf 135)

### orthonormal frame for a diagonal metric

*technique · core* · also: square-root rule for vielbeins, normalizing a diagonal metric

When the metric is diagonal, the stationary orthonormal frame is obtained by dividing each coordinate basis vector by the square root of the absolute value of its metric component, so the non-zero vielbein components are 1/sqrt|g_mumu| and their inverses sqrt|g_mumu|.

**How introduced:** Example 10.9 derives it from the normalization of u and checks it against the defining relation; the authors then phrase it as vielbein components being square roots of metric components, and apply it to Schwarzschild and to a spherical-coordinate metric.

**Prerequisites:** stationary orthonormal frame, vielbein defining relations

$$
(\boldsymbol e_{\hat\mu})^\mu = 1/\sqrt{|g_{\mu\mu}|}
$$

$$
(\boldsymbol e_\mu)^{\hat\mu} = \sqrt{|g_{\mu\mu}|}
$$

**Notes:** No sum over the repeated mu. For non-diagonal metrics (e.g. Exercise 10.5) one must orthogonalize, typically by Gram-Schmidt starting from e_0hat = u.

**Where:** GA §10.3 p.114 (pdf 131); GA §10.3 p.115 (pdf 132); GA p.118 (pdf 135)

### matrix form of the orthonormality condition

*identity · introduced* · also: E g E^T = eta

Collecting the orthonormal basis-vector components into a matrix whose rows are the frame vectors, the conditions g(e_alphahat, e_betahat) = eta become the single matrix equation E g E^T = eta; finding a frame means finding such an E, i.e. diagonalizing and normalizing g.

**How introduced:** Example 10.8 displays it for 1+1 dimensions without solving it, as a lead-in to the diagonal case.

**Prerequisites:** vielbein defining relations

$$
E\,g\,E^{T} = \eta
$$

**Where:** GA §10.3 p.114 (pdf 131)

### Schwarzschild metric

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: Schwarzschild geometry

The vacuum geometry outside a spherically symmetric mass M, with line element -(1-2M/r) dt^2 + (1-2M/r)^{-1} dr^2 + r^2 dOmega^2 in units G = c = 1.

**How introduced:** Quoted ahead of its derivation in ch21 as the prime example of a diagonal metric for which to build a static frame, and reused for radial infall in Example 10.12.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor

ds^2 = -\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)dt^2 + \left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}dr^2 + r^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)

**Notes:** Margin note 14 lists g_phiphi = r^2 sin theta, which should read r^2 sin^2 theta.

**Where:** GA §10.3 p.115 (pdf 132); GA §10.4 p.116 (pdf 133)

### static observer frame in the Schwarzschild geometry

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: Schwarzschild stationary tetrad

For an observer at fixed r, theta, phi outside a Schwarzschild mass, the orthonormal frame is e_that = (1-2M/r)^{-1/2} e_t, e_rhat = (1-2M/r)^{1/2} e_r, e_thetahat = e_theta/r, e_phihat = e_phi/(r sin theta).

(\boldsymbol e_{\hat t})^t = (1-2M/r)^{-1/2},\ (\boldsymbol e_{\hat r})^r = (1-2M/r)^{1/2},\ (\boldsymbol e_{\hat\theta})^\theta = 1/r,\ (\boldsymbol e_{\hat\phi})^\phi = 1/(r\sin\theta)

**Notes:** Used later to turn conserved quantities into locally measured energies (gravitational redshift) and to express Schwarzschild curvature in frame components (ch11 Exercise 11.2).

### perfect-fluid energy-momentum tensor in an orthonormal frame

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: fluid stress tensor diag(rho,p,p,p)

A static perfect fluid whose coordinate components in a spherical-coordinate metric carry factors chi^2 and chi^2 sin^2 theta has, in the orthonormal frame, the simple rest-frame form T_{alphahat betahat} = diag(rho, p, p, p).

### connection coefficients

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: Christoffel symbols, Gamma

The coefficients describing how basis vectors change from point to point, which enter covariant derivatives; they are not tensor components, so they cannot be transformed with vielbeins, and they need not vanish in an orthonormal frame.

**How introduced:** Relied on rather than developed: a margin note stresses the stationary frame can have non-zero connection coefficients, §10.4 seeks frames where they vanish, and Exercise 10.2 asks for them in an orthonormal polar frame.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative along a world line

$$
\Gamma^{\hat\mu}{}_{\hat\alpha\hat\beta} - \Gamma^{\hat\mu}{}_{\hat\beta\hat\alpha} = \langle\boldsymbol\omega^{\hat\mu},[\boldsymbol e_{\hat\alpha},\boldsymbol e_{\hat\beta}]\rangle
$$

**Notes:** The commutator relation (from Exercise 10.2b) holds when the first lower index is the direction of differentiation, which is the ordering used in the Appendix E answer; ch7 writes the derivative index last. In a coordinate basis the distinction is invisible because Gamma is symmetric.

**Where:** GA §10.3 p.114 (pdf 131); GA §10.4 p.116 (pdf 133); GA p.118 (pdf 135)

### local inertial frame

*definition · revisited* · also: LIF, locally inertial frame, local flatness theorem

Coordinates about an event in which the metric equals eta and all first derivatives of the metric vanish there, so that the connection coefficients vanish at that event; physics there looks like special relativity to first order.

**How introduced:** Recalled from ch6 in margin note 12 (as the stronger requirement the stationary frame lacks) and at the start of §10.4 as the property freely falling frames are designed to have.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, connection coefficients, equivalence principle

$$
g_{\mu\nu}(\mathcal P) = \eta_{\mu\nu},\ \partial_\alpha g_{\mu\nu}(\mathcal P) = 0 \Rightarrow \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}(\mathcal P) = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §10.3 p.114 (pdf 131); GA §10.4 p.116 (pdf 133)

### equivalence principle

*principle · revisited*

A sufficiently small freely falling laboratory cannot detect gravity by local experiments, so its natural coordinates behave as if the connection vanished.

**How introduced:** Invoked in one sentence as the physical reason to expect a falling lab's frame to have vanishing connection coefficients.

**Where:** GA §10.4 p.116 (pdf 133)

### geodesic

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: free-fall world line

A curve whose tangent vector is parallel transported along itself; freely falling bodies follow timelike geodesics, so u of a falling observer satisfies nabla_u u = 0.

**How introduced:** Recalled as the world line of the falling laboratory; the fact that it is a geodesic is what makes nabla_u e_0hat = 0 automatic.

**Prerequisites:** parallel transport

$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol u}\boldsymbol u = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §10.4 p.116 (pdf 133)

### parallel transport

*operation · revisited*

Carrying a vector along a curve so that its covariant derivative along the tangent vanishes.

**How introduced:** Used to extend an orthonormal triad chosen at one event along the whole free-fall geodesic.

**Prerequisites:** covariant derivative along a world line

$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol u}\boldsymbol V = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §10.4 p.116 (pdf 133); GA §10.4 p.117 (pdf 134)

### freely falling orthonormal frame

*definition · core* · also: freely falling frame, parallel-propagated tetrad, freely falling local inertial frame

An orthonormal frame carried along a timelike geodesic whose time vector is the geodesic's 4-velocity and whose basis vectors (and dual 1-forms) are all parallel transported along it, nabla_u e_alphahat = 0. It realizes, along the whole world line, the laboratory of a freely falling observer.

**How introduced:** Motivated by the equivalence principle; defined by choosing a triad orthogonal to u at one event and parallel transporting; built explicitly for radial Schwarzschild infall and shown to turn covariant derivatives into ordinary ones.

**Prerequisites:** local inertial frame, geodesic, parallel transport, observer 4-velocity as the time basis vector, equivalence principle

$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol u}\boldsymbol e_{\hat\alpha} = 0
$$

$$
\nabla_{\boldsymbol u}\boldsymbol\omega^{\hat\alpha} = 0
$$

**Notes:** The text says the connection coefficients vanish along the geodesic; strictly, parallel transport guarantees only the coefficients whose differentiation direction is u; making all of them vanish on the world line requires Fermi normal coordinates (Fermi transport appears in GA Exercise 32.2). A ch6 summary point describes the same frame as flat only in a small spatial region but for long proper times.

**Where:** GA §10.4 p.116 (pdf 133); GA §10.4 p.117 (pdf 134); GA p.118 (pdf 135)

### infalling observer frame in the Schwarzschild geometry

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: radial infall tetrad, rain-drop observer frame

For radial fall from rest at infinity, u^alpha = [(1-2M/r)^{-1}, -(2M/r)^{1/2}, 0, 0]; the frame has e_that = u, e_rhat = (-(2M/r)^{1/2}(1-2M/r)^{-1}, 1, 0, 0), e_thetahat = e_theta/r and e_phihat = e_phi/(r sin theta).

### covariant derivative reduces to ordinary derivative in a freely falling frame

In a parallel-transported orthonormal frame the hatted components of the covariant derivative of any vector along the world line equal the ordinary proper-time derivatives of its hatted components, because the frame vectors themselves do not change along the curve.

**How introduced:** Example 10.13: pull the vielbein into the covariant derivative using the parallel-transported basis 1-forms (margin note 18), recognize a scalar, and use the chain rule.

## Key equations

### Orthonormal frame metric · central · GA p.108 (pdf 125)

$$
\eta_{\hat\mu\hat\nu} = \boldsymbol e_{\hat\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol e_{\hat\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(-1,1,1,1)
$$

Defines an orthonormal frame: its basis vectors have Minkowski dot products.

**Symbols:** Hats mark frame indices.

### (10.1) Measurement as projection · central · GA §10.1 p.109 (pdf 126)

$$
\boldsymbol p\cdot\boldsymbol e_{\hat\alpha} = p^{\hat\mu}\boldsymbol e_{\hat\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol e_{\hat\alpha} = p^{\hat\mu}\eta_{\hat\mu\hat\alpha} = p_{\hat\alpha}
$$

Measuring a component is a dot product with the observer's axis, and it returns the lower-index frame component.

**Symbols:** p: 4-momentum; e_alphahat: observer basis vector.

### (10.2) Orientation of the observer's frame · central · GA §10.1 p.109 (pdf 126)

$$
\boldsymbol e_{\hat 0} = \boldsymbol u_{\rm obs}
$$

The observer's time axis is their 4-velocity.

**Symbols:** u_obs: observer 4-velocity.

### (10.3) · supporting · GA §10.1 p.109 (pdf 126)

$$
(\boldsymbol e_{\hat 0})^\mu = u^\mu_{\rm obs}
$$

In any coordinate system the time basis vector has the 4-velocity's components.

### (10.4) · derivation-step · GA §10.1 p.109 (pdf 126)

$$
(\boldsymbol e_{\hat 0})^\mu = u^\mu_{\rm obs}(\tau) = (\cosh g\tau, \sinh g\tau, 0, 0)
$$

Time axis of a uniformly accelerated observer.

**Symbols:** g: proper acceleration; tau: proper time.

### (10.5) · derivation-step · GA §10.1 p.109 (pdf 126)

$$
\eta_{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol e_{\hat 0})^\mu(\boldsymbol e_{\hat 1})^\nu = -\cosh(g\tau)f(\tau) + \sinh(g\tau)g(\tau) = 0
$$

Orthogonality of the trial x-axis to the time axis.

**Symbols:** f, g(tau): unknown components of e_1hat (the g here is not the acceleration).

### (10.6) · derivation-step · GA §10.1 p.109 (pdf 126)

$$
-f^2(\tau) + g^2(\tau) = 1
$$

Unit spacelike normalization of the trial x-axis.

### (10.7) Accelerated observer's tetrad · supporting · GA §10.1 p.109 (pdf 126)

$$
(\boldsymbol e_{\hat 1})^\mu = (\sinh g\tau, \cosh g\tau, 0,0),\ (\boldsymbol e_{\hat 2})^\mu = (0,0,1,0),\ (\boldsymbol e_{\hat 3})^\mu = (0,0,0,1)
$$

Complete orthonormal frame of the accelerated observer (with e_0hat from 10.4).

### (10.8) Observed energy · central · GA §10.1 p.110 (pdf 127)

$$
-\boldsymbol p\cdot\boldsymbol u_{\rm obs} = -p^{\hat\mu}\eta_{\hat\mu\hat 0} = -p_{\hat 0} = p^{\hat 0} = E
$$

Energy measured by an observer is minus the dot product of momentum with their 4-velocity.

### (10.9) · supporting · GA §10.1 p.110 (pdf 127)

$$
E = -\boldsymbol p\cdot\boldsymbol u_{\rm obs} = m\gamma
$$

A particle at rest has energy gamma m according to an observer moving at speed v.

**Symbols:** gamma = (1-v^2)^{-1/2}.

### (10.10) Accelerated-observer redshift · supporting · GA §10.1 p.110 (pdf 127)

$$
\omega(\tau) = -\boldsymbol k\cdot\boldsymbol u_{\rm obs} = k^0u^0 - k^1u^1 = \omega[\cosh g\tau - \sinh g\tau] = \omega e^{-g\tau}
$$

Frequency of starlight measured by a uniformly accelerated observer decays exponentially in proper time.

**Symbols:** k^mu = (omega, omega, 0, 0) in the star frame.

### (10.11) · derivation-step · GA §10.2 p.111 (pdf 128)

$$
\boldsymbol v = \frac{dr}{dt}\boldsymbol e_{\hat r} + r\frac{d\boldsymbol e_{\hat r}}{dt}
$$

Velocity from differentiating r = r e_rhat, including the turning of the unit vector.

## Worked examples

### Example 10.1 · intro · GA §10.1 p.109 (pdf 126)

**Problem:** Show what an observer obtains by dotting a momentum vector with one of their frame vectors.

**Method:** Expand p in the hatted basis, use e_muhat . e_alphahat = eta, and raise the index with the inverse Minkowski metric if the upper component is wanted.

**Key insight:** Projection gives the lower-index component directly; in an orthonormal frame raising or lowering only flips the sign of the time component.

**Result:** p . e_alphahat = p_alphahat; p^betahat = eta^{alphahat betahat} p_alphahat.

**Concepts:** measurement as projection onto the observer's basis, local orthonormal frame

### Example 10.2 · intro · GA §10.1 p.109 (pdf 126)

**Problem:** Construct the full orthonormal frame of the uniformly accelerated observer of ch2.

**Method:** Take e_0hat as the known 4-velocity (cosh g tau, sinh g tau, 0, 0); put e_2hat and e_3hat along y and z; write e_1hat with two unknown t and x components and impose orthogonality to e_0hat and unit normalization.

**Key insight:** The spatial axis is the Minkowski 'mirror image' of the time axis: swapping cosh and sinh; the frame is a boost whose rapidity grows linearly with proper time.

**Result:** e_1hat = (sinh g tau, cosh g tau, 0, 0), e_2hat = (0,0,1,0), e_3hat = (0,0,0,1).

**Concepts:** uniformly accelerated observer's orthonormal frame, observer 4-velocity as the time basis vector

### Example 10.3 · intro · GA §10.1 p.110 (pdf 127)

**Problem:** (i) Find the energy of a particle at rest as measured by an observer moving with speed v. (ii) Find the frequency of starlight measured by the uniformly accelerated observer.

**Method:** Evaluate the scalar -p.u_obs in the particle's rest frame with u_obs = (gamma, gamma v, 0, 0); then evaluate -k.u_obs with k = (omega, omega, 0, 0) and the accelerated 4-velocity, using cosh x - sinh x = exp(-x).

**Key insight:** Because -p.u is a scalar, pick whichever frame makes the arithmetic trivial; the same one-line formula yields relativistic energy and Doppler shifts.

**Result:** E = gamma m (gamma m c^2 with c restored); omega(tau) = omega exp(-g tau), an exponentially growing redshift.

**Concepts:** energy measured by an observer, exponential redshift seen by a uniformly accelerated observer, wave 4-vector of light

### Example 10.4 · intro · GA §10.2 p.111 (pdf 128)

**Problem:** Derive the velocity and acceleration components of a particle in an orthonormal polar basis (Kepler problem setting).

**Method:** Differentiate r = r e_rhat, remembering that the unit vectors rotate: d e_rhat/dt = theta-dot e_thetahat and d e_thetahat/dt = -theta-dot e_rhat (Fig. 10.3); collect terms along each unit vector.

**Key insight:** Extra terms in the acceleration are not forces but consequences of a basis that changes from place to place; this is a classical preview of connection coefficients in a non-coordinate basis.

**Result:** a^rhat = r-ddot - r theta-dot^2; a^thetahat = r theta-ddot + 2 r-dot theta-dot.

**Concepts:** rotating orthonormal polar basis vectors, acceleration components in plane polar coordinates, non-coordinate basis

### Example 10.5 · intro · GA §10.2 p.112 (pdf 129)

**Problem:** Set up vielbein components between the coordinate basis and the orthonormal basis for the flat plane in polar coordinates, and verify orthonormality.

**Method:** Read g_rr = 1 and g_thetatheta = r^2 from the line element; choose e_rhat = e_r and e_thetahat = e_theta/r; write the trivial self-components, then the cross components (e_thetahat)^theta = 1/r and (e_theta)^thetahat = r; check with g_{alpha beta}(e_muhat)^alpha (e_nuhat)^beta = eta.

**Key insight:** The only non-trivial vielbein entries are the scale factors; checking a frame means contracting with g, not with eta.

**Result:** (e_rhat)^mu = (1,0), (e_thetahat)^mu = (0,1/r); (e_r)^muhat = (1,0), (e_theta)^muhat = (0,r).

**Concepts:** vielbein, vielbein defining relations, non-coordinate basis

### Example 10.6 · intro · GA §10.2 p.113 (pdf 130)

**Problem:** Write the vielbein for the unit-sphere metric as a matrix and find its inverse.

**Method:** Normalize the diagonal metric d theta^2 + sin^2 theta d phi^2 to get (e_mu)^alphahat = diag(1, sin theta); use the matrix inverse relations to get (e_alphahat)^mu = diag(1, 1/sin theta).

**Key insight:** Vielbeins are ordinary square matrices, so inverse relations are just matrix inversion; for diagonal metrics it is entry-by-entry reciprocals.

**Result:** (e_mu)^alphahat = diag(1, sin theta); (e_alphahat)^mu = diag(1, 1/sin theta).

**Concepts:** vielbein as an invertible matrix, vielbein

### Example 10.7 · intro · GA §10.2 p.113 (pdf 130)

**Problem:** Show that the vielbein formalism reproduces E = -p.u_obs.

**Method:** Write the dot product with g, lower the index on p, recognize (e_0hat)^nu p_nu as the hatted component p_0hat via the index-swap rule, then raise with eta_00 = -1.

**Key insight:** The observer-energy rule is just the vielbein rule applied to the time index, guaranteed by choosing e_0hat = u_obs.

**Result:** E = -p_nu (e_0hat)^nu = -p_0hat = p^0hat.

**Concepts:** energy measured by an observer, vielbein transformation of tensor components

### Example 10.8 · intro · GA §10.3 p.114 (pdf 131)

**Problem:** Express the orthonormality condition as a matrix equation in 1+1 dimensions.

**Method:** Arrange frame components as a matrix E (rows = frame vectors) and write E g E^T = diag(-1, 1).

**Key insight:** Finding a frame is a matrix congruence problem: diagonalize the symmetric metric and rescale to plus or minus one.

**Concepts:** matrix form of the orthonormality condition, vielbein defining relations

### Example 10.9 · intro · GA §10.3 p.114 (pdf 131)

**Problem:** Find the stationary orthonormal frame for a general diagonal metric.

**Method:** Normalize the static 4-velocity, g_00 (u^0)^2 = -1, set e_0hat = u, then divide each spatial coordinate vector by the square root of its metric component; verify with the defining relation.

**Key insight:** For diagonal metrics no diagonalization is needed; the vielbein is the square root of the metric entry by entry.

**Result:** e_0hat = e_0/sqrt(-g_00), e_ihat = e_i/sqrt(g_ii); (e_mu)^muhat = 1/(e_muhat)^mu.

**Concepts:** stationary orthonormal frame, orthonormal frame for a diagonal metric, normalization of a static observer's 4-velocity

### Example 10.10 · standard · GA §10.3 p.115 (pdf 132)

**Problem:** Find the orthonormal frame of an observer at rest in the Schwarzschild geometry.

**Method:** Apply the diagonal-metric recipe to the four Schwarzschild metric components and confirm u.u = -1.

**Key insight:** Static observers' clocks and rulers are the coordinate ones rescaled by the gravitational factors (1-2M/r)^{-/+1/2}, which will later give gravitational time dilation and redshift.

**Result:** e_that = (1-2M/r)^{-1/2} e_t, e_rhat = (1-2M/r)^{1/2} e_r, e_thetahat = e_theta/r, e_phihat = e_phi/(r sin theta).

**Concepts:** static observer frame in the Schwarzschild geometry, orthonormal frame for a diagonal metric, Schwarzschild metric

### Example 10.11 · intro · GA §10.3 p.115 (pdf 132)

**Problem:** Convert a static perfect fluid's energy-momentum tensor from coordinate to orthonormal components in flat spherical coordinates.

**Method:** Use the diagonal vielbein, one factor per index, e.g. multiply T_thetatheta = p chi^2 by (1/chi)^2.

**Key insight:** Orthonormal components strip out the coordinate scale factors, exposing the physical density and isotropic pressure.

**Result:** T_{thatthat} = rho and all three spatial diagonal components equal p.

**Concepts:** perfect-fluid energy-momentum tensor in an orthonormal frame, vielbein transformation of tensor components

### Example 10.12 · standard · GA §10.4 p.116 (pdf 133)

**Problem:** Build the freely falling frame of an observer falling radially from rest at infinity in Schwarzschild.

**Method:** Set e_that = u from the known infall 4-velocity; keep the angular frame vectors diagonal as for the static observer; solve the normalization of e_rhat within the t-r plane with its r component taken as 1.

**Key insight:** A falling frame mixes t and r components: it is the static frame boosted inward, so its radial vector acquires a time component.

**Result:** e_rhat = (-(2M/r)^{1/2}(1-2M/r)^{-1}, 1, 0, 0), angular vectors (0,0,1/r,0) and (0,0,0,1/(r sin theta)).

**Concepts:** infalling observer frame in the Schwarzschild geometry, freely falling orthonormal frame, vielbein defining relations

### Example 10.13 · standard · GA §10.4 p.117 (pdf 134)

**Problem:** Show that in a freely falling frame the frame components of D chi/d tau are ordinary derivatives d chi^muhat/d tau.

**Method:** Write the frame component of nabla_u chi as a pairing with omega^muhat; use the Leibniz rule and nabla_u omega^muhat = 0 to move the derivative outside; treat chi^muhat as a scalar whose covariant derivative is u^beta partial_beta; apply the chain rule.

**Key insight:** Parallel-transported frames make the frame vectors behave like constant Cartesian axes along the world line, generalizing Example 7.10 to curved spacetime.

**Result:** (D chi/d tau)^muhat = d chi^muhat/d tau.

**Concepts:** covariant derivative reduces to ordinary derivative in a freely falling frame, orthonormal basis 1-forms, freely falling orthonormal frame

### Example · standard · GA p.118 (pdf 135)

**Problem:** Exercise 10.3 (answer in Appendix E): coordinate versus locally measured radial speed for a particle with conserved u_t = a in a static spherically symmetric field.

**Method:** Use u.u = -1 with the diagonal metric to get u^r, form dr/dt = u^r/u^t, then convert time and radial intervals to the static observer's frame with the diagonal vielbein.

**Key insight:** Coordinate speed is not what anyone measures; the local observer's speed involves sqrt(g_rr/(-g_tt)) and simplifies to a formula in g_tt and a alone.

**Result:** d rhat/d that = sqrt(1 + g_tt/a^2), with dr/dt = g_tt u^r / a on the way (Appendix E, E.93-E.94; re-derived independently).

**Concepts:** stationary orthonormal frame, orthonormal frame for a diagonal metric, coordinates have no immediate metrical significance


## Analogies and intuitions

### Resolving a 2D arrow on a set of drawn axes → measurement as projection onto the observer's basis · useful · GA §10.1 p.108 (pdf 125)

The familiar picture of splitting a planar vector into components along x-hat and y-hat stands in for an observer extracting energy and momentum along four spacetime axes.

**Where it breaks down:** Euclidean projection hides the minus sign of eta_00: in spacetime the time component obtained by dotting with e_0hat is minus the energy, and boosted axes do not look perpendicular on a diagram.

**App idea:** Morph the Euclidean picture into a Minkowski diagram as a 'signature' slider moves from + to -, so learners see where the sign flip enters.

### An observer as a clock-carrying laboratory whose clock ticks along the world line → observer 4-velocity as the time basis vector · strong · GA §10.1 p.109 (pdf 126)

A clock at rest in the lab moves through spacetime along the world line, so the lab's time axis must point along the tangent.

**Where it breaks down:** Says nothing about how to orient the spatial axes; a rotating lab and a non-rotating lab share the same e_0hat.

**App idea:** Clock-and-rulers glyph attached to a world line in a spacetime diagram, with a gyroscope option that distinguishes parallel-transported from rotating spatial axes.

### Vielbein components as square roots of the metric → orthonormal frame for a diagonal metric · useful · GA §10.3 p.115 (pdf 132)

Since g = e^T eta e, for a diagonal metric each vielbein entry is the square root of the corresponding |g_mumu|, like taking a matrix square root.

**Where it breaks down:** Only literally true for diagonal metrics; in general a vielbein is a non-unique matrix 'square root' defined up to Lorentz transformations, and off-diagonal metrics need orthogonalization.

**App idea:** Metric-to-vielbein calculator: enter a 2x2 or 4x4 metric; diagonal cases show square roots; non-diagonal cases animate Gram-Schmidt from e_0hat = u.

### Vielbein makes global coordinates local → vielbein · useful · GA §10.2 p.112 (pdf 129)

Margin note suggests picturing the coordinate frame as global labels such as (t, r, theta, phi), with the vielbein converting them into the local rulers and clocks of an observer.

**Where it breaks down:** The vielbein acts on tangent vectors at a point, not on the coordinates themselves; there are generally no coordinates whose basis is the orthonormal frame.

### Rotating unit vectors in orbital mechanics → non-coordinate basis · strong · GA §10.2 p.111 (pdf 128)

The Kepler-problem habit of using e_rhat and e_thetahat shows that non-coordinate bases are everyday tools, and that their turning produces extra acceleration terms.

**Where it breaks down:** In the plane the non-commutation is a coordinate artefact of flat space; it does not by itself signal curvature or a gravitational field.

**App idea:** Orbit sandbox where the learner sees a^rhat and a^thetahat split into 'real force' and 'basis turning' parts for an elliptical Kepler orbit.

### A small falling laboratory sees no gravity, like carrying fixed Cartesian axes → covariant derivative reduces to ordinary derivative in a freely falling frame · strong · GA §10.4 p.116 (pdf 133)

Equivalence-principle intuition motivates choosing parallel-transported axes, along which the frame vectors behave as if constant, so derivatives of components are the whole story.

**Where it breaks down:** Holds only along the world line and to first order away from it; tidal effects (curvature) appear at second order, which is the subject of ch11.

**App idea:** Elevator-in-free-fall view next to a coordinate view of the same world line near a mass, with frame arrows that never twist in the elevator view.

### Time Traveller's remark that time differs from space only because consciousness moves along it → coordinates have no immediate metrical significance · weak · GA p.108 (pdf 125)

A literary margin note (H. G. Wells) that dramatizes the geometric view of time as a coordinate direction.

**Where it breaks down:** Decorative; it can even encourage the misreading that a coordinate labelled t is automatically time.


## Misconceptions addressed

### A coordinate named t is a time and a coordinate named r is a radius or distance. · GA p.108 (pdf 125)

**Correction:** Coordinates are labels; physical times and lengths come from the metric evaluated in some observer's local orthonormal frame (e.g. static-observer proper time is sqrt(-g_tt) dt).

**Why tempting:** Coordinate names are chosen by analogy with flat space, and in weak fields the numbers nearly agree.

### Energy is simply the time component p^0 of the momentum in whatever coordinates are being used. · GA §10.1 p.110 (pdf 127)

**Correction:** Energy is observer-dependent: it is -p.u_obs, i.e. the time component in that observer's orthonormal frame; coordinate components p^t are not energies unless the coordinate frame happens to be that observer's orthonormal frame.

**Why tempting:** In special relativity with inertial Cartesian coordinates p^0 is the energy, so the habit transfers.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Any orthonormal frame is a local inertial frame, so its connection coefficients vanish. · GA §10.3 p.114 (pdf 131)

**Correction:** Orthonormality only makes the metric Minkowskian at the point; vanishing connection (first metric derivatives) is an extra condition met, along a geodesic, by the freely falling frame.

**Why tempting:** Both kinds of frame have eta as their metric, and 'local frame' is used loosely for both.

### Connection coefficients can be transformed to an orthonormal frame with vielbeins like any other indexed object. · GA p.118 (pdf 135)

**Correction:** They are not tensor components; they must be computed from how the frame vectors change, or transformed with an inhomogeneous law.

**Why tempting:** Every other object in the chapter converts index by index with vielbein factors.

### An orthonormal basis must be a coordinate basis, since normalized polar unit vectors look like they come from polar coordinates. · GA §10.2 p.111 (pdf 128)

**Correction:** Normalized basis vectors such as e_theta/r are generally not derivative directions of any coordinates; their commutator is non-zero.

**Why tempting:** Undergraduate vector calculus uses r-hat and theta-hat alongside (r, theta) without distinguishing the two notions.

### The vielbein relation <omega^mu, e_nuhat> is a Kronecker delta, like the duality relation for a single basis. · GA §10.2 p.113 (pdf 130)

**Correction:** Pairing a coordinate 1-form with a frame vector gives the vielbein component (e_nuhat)^mu, which differs from delta because the two belong to different bases.

**Why tempting:** Index patterns look identical to <omega^mu, e_nu> = delta once hats are overlooked (hats are easily lost in handwriting and OCR).

### Extra terms like -r theta-dot^2 in polar acceleration are real forces. · GA §10.2 p.111 (pdf 128)

**Correction:** They arise from the rotation of the unit vectors used to express the acceleration; the geometric acceleration vector is unchanged.

**Why tempting:** In mechanics courses these terms are often called centrifugal and Coriolis forces.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### There is a single 'correct' orthonormal frame at each event. · GA §10.3 p.114 (pdf 131)

**Correction:** Frames are fixed only once an observer's 4-velocity is chosen and a spatial orientation picked; static and infalling observers at the same event have different frames related by a boost.

**Why tempting:** The chapter speaks of 'the orthonormal frame' and a margin note makes the static frame the default.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Static observers can be placed anywhere in a geometry, e.g. at any r in Schwarzschild. · GA §10.3 p.115 (pdf 132)

**Correction:** A static frame requires g_00 < 0; at and inside r = 2M (or beyond the light cylinder in a rotating frame) no observer can stay at fixed spatial coordinates and u^0 = (-g_00)^{-1/2} is undefined.

**Why tempting:** The recipe u^0 = (-g_00)^{-1/2} is presented without its domain of validity.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Particle crossing a laboratory · GA §10.1 p.108 (pdf 125)

**Setup:** A particle with momentum p passes through a lab whose observer carries orthonormal axes; the observer wants its energy and momentum components.

**Lesson:** Measurement is the projection of a frame-independent vector onto the observer's own axes; different observers get different components of the same vector.

**App idea:** Several labs with different velocities at the same event, each displaying its own readout of the same particle's E and p.

### Accelerating rocket watching a star · GA §10.1 p.110 (pdf 127)

**Setup:** A rocket with constant proper acceleration heads directly away from a star that is at rest in an inertial frame, so the starlight catches up with it from behind along the line of motion. The crew records the frequency they receive as their proper time goes on.

**Lesson:** The received frequency falls exponentially, omega exp(-g tau); this is the flat-space prototype of the exponential redshift of light from near a black-hole horizon.

**App idea:** Rindler diagram with incoming null rays from the star and a colour-shifting star image in the rocket window versus proper time; slider for g.

### Small laboratory in free fall around a mass · GA §10.4 p.116 (pdf 133)

**Setup:** A sealed lab falls freely along a geodesic; its occupants set up axes at one moment and keep them non-rotating.

**Lesson:** By the equivalence principle their frame is parallel transported and locally inertial along the fall, so derivatives of frame components behave as in special relativity.

**App idea:** Side-by-side: static hovering observer and infalling observer at the same r in Schwarzschild, with frame arrows on a t-r diagram showing the relative boost of speed sqrt(2M/r).

### Signal around a centrifuge rim (Exercise 10.5) · GA p.119 (pdf 136)

**Setup:** A source and a detector sit on the rim of a spinning centrifuge, separated by an angle; light is sent from one to the other.

**Lesson:** Both are static in the rotating (stationary) metric at the same radius, so they measure the same frequency: rotation-induced shifts depend on radius, not on angular separation.

**App idea:** Spinning disk with draggable source and detector; readout of emitted and received frequency showing a shift only when their radii differ.


## Applications and observations

- **Relativistic energy of a moving particle** (numerical-estimate, GA §10.1 p.110 (pdf 127)): Measured energy of a particle at rest seen by an observer moving at speed v follows from E = -p.u. Key numbers: E = gamma m c^2.
- **Exponential redshift for a uniformly accelerated observer** (numerical-estimate, GA §10.1 p.110 (pdf 127)): Frequency received from a star by an observer with constant proper acceleration g decays as exp(-g tau). Own estimate: for g = 9.8 m/s^2 the e-folding proper time is c/g, roughly 3.06e7 s or about 0.97 yr. Key numbers: omega(tau) = omega exp(-g tau/c) with c restored; c/g ~ 0.97 yr at 1 g (own estimate).
- **Kepler problem in orthonormal polar components** (data-analysis, GA §10.2 p.111 (pdf 128)): Radial and transverse acceleration components used for planetary orbits come from differentiating rotating unit vectors; full treatment in ch20. Key numbers: a^rhat = r-ddot - r theta-dot^2, a^thetahat = r theta-ddot + 2 r-dot theta-dot.
- **Static observers outside a spherical mass (Schwarzschild)** (astrophysical-system, GA §10.3 p.115 (pdf 132)): Clocks of static observers run at sqrt(1-2M/r) of coordinate time, and radial rulers are stretched by (1-2M/r)^{-1/2}. Own estimate using E = -p.u: a photon from infinity is blueshifted by (1-2GM/rc^2)^{-1/2}; at the Earth's surface 2GM/rc^2 is about 1.4e-9, giving a fractional shift near 7e-10; at a neutron-star surface (M ~ 1.4 solar masses, r ~ 12 km) 2GM/rc^2 is about 0.34, giving a factor near 1.23. Key numbers: (e_that)^t = (1-2M/r)^{-1/2}; Earth surface shift ~7e-10; neutron star factor ~1.23 (own estimates).
- **Perfect fluid seen in an orthonormal frame** (numerical-estimate, GA §10.3 p.115 (pdf 132)): Coordinate components of a fluid's stress tensor carry metric factors; in the orthonormal frame they reduce to density and isotropic pressure, the form used for stars and cosmology. Key numbers: T_{alphahat betahat} = diag(rho, p, p, p).
- **Radial free fall into a Schwarzschild black hole** (astrophysical-system, GA §10.4 p.116 (pdf 133)): Frame of an observer dropped from rest at infinity; with ch11 it gives the tidal stretching felt on the way in. Own derivation: relative to a static observer at the same r the infaller moves inward at the Newtonian escape speed sqrt(2M/r), reaching the speed of light only at r = 2M, where static observers cease to exist. Key numbers: u^r = -(2M/r)^{1/2}; local relative speed sqrt(2M/r) (own derivation).
- **Centrifuge frequency-shift experiment** (experiment, GA p.119 (pdf 136)): Exercise 10.5 shows no frequency shift between emitter and detector on the same rim of a rotating centrifuge, since both are static in the stationary rotating metric at the same radius (Appendix E reasoning). Rotor experiments of this kind with Mossbauer sources (e.g. Hay, Schiffer, Cranshaw and Egelstaff, 1960) test the transverse Doppler effect when source and absorber are at different radii; this historical link is not made in the book. Key numbers: Shift factor depends on (1 - Omega^2 r^2)^{1/2} at each radius; zero for equal radii.

## Notation and conventions

- **Hatted indices:** Components in a local orthonormal frame carry hats (e_alphahat, p^muhat); hatted indices are raised and lowered with eta = diag(-1,1,1,1). Coordinate-frame indices are unhatted. — Hats are frequently lost or garbled in the reading copy; the rendered pages are reliable. (GA p.108 (pdf 125))
- **Signature and units:** Signature (-,+,+,+); c = 1 (restored as gamma m c^2 in Example 10.3); hbar = 1 for photons; G = 1 in Schwarzschild expressions. — Exercise 10.6 uses Newman-Penrose normalizations natural in (+,-,-,-). (GA §10.1 p.110 (pdf 127))
- **Vielbein component notation:** (e_betahat)^alpha is the alpha coordinate component of the betahat frame vector; (e_alpha)^betahat is the betahat frame component of the alpha coordinate vector. The bracket is decorative and e_alpha^betahat means the same. Follows Hartle. — Vielbein components for each metric are recorded in margin notes throughout the book. (GA §10.2 p.112 (pdf 129))
- **No summation on mixed hatted/unhatted repeats:** In expressions such as (e_mu)^muhat = 1/(e_muhat)^mu or g_mumu (e_0hat)^mu (e_0hat)^mu, an index appearing with and without a hat is not summed. — Margin note 13. (GA §10.3 p.114 (pdf 131))
- **Stationary frame as default:** When the book refers to 'the orthonormal frame' without naming an observer, it means the frame of an observer at rest in the coordinate system. — Margin note 11. (GA §10.3 p.114 (pdf 131))
- **D/dtau:** D V/dtau denotes nabla_u V, the covariant derivative along the world line; its components are written (D V/dtau)^mu. — Appendix B explains the choice. (GA §10.4 p.117 (pdf 134))
- **Numbered versus named indices:** Indices are written as numbers (0,1,2,3 or 1,2 in 2D examples) or as coordinate names (t, r, theta, phi, chi) interchangeably, e.g. e_that = e_0hat. (GA §10.4 p.117 (pdf 134))
- **Local metric symbols for general tetrads:** In Exercise 10.6 the local metric for a null tetrad is still written eta_{muhat nuhat} (overbars in the print), although it is no longer diag(-1,1,1,1). (GA p.119 (pdf 136))

## Teaching gems

### Energy measured by any observer is -p.u_obs, derived in one line from e_0hat = u and eta_00 = -1. · GA §10.1 p.110 (pdf 127)

**Why it works:** A single scalar formula replaces Lorentz transformations, Doppler formulas and gravitational redshift recipes, and it can be evaluated in whatever frame is easiest.

**App idea:** Universal 'what does this observer measure?' widget: pick a spacetime (Minkowski, Rindler, Schwarzschild), an emitter and a receiver world line; it computes -k.u at both ends and animates the photon with a colour shift.

### An observer is a world line with a frame attached, time axis along the tangent (Fig. 10.2). · GA §10.1 p.109 (pdf 126)

**Why it works:** Turns the vague word 'observer' into a concrete geometric object that can be drawn, computed and compared.

**App idea:** World-line editor that attaches a live tetrad and shows how it tilts under acceleration and boosts.

### Accelerated observer's frame by swapping cosh and sinh, and the resulting exponential redshift. · GA §10.1 p.110 (pdf 127)

**Why it works:** Short, fully explicit calculation that previews horizon redshift physics in flat spacetime with no curvature.

**App idea:** Rindler rocket simulator: learner sets g and watches received starlight redshift, plotting ln(omega) against tau as a straight line of slope -g.

### Non-coordinate bases introduced through rotating polar unit vectors in the Kepler problem. · GA §10.2 p.111 (pdf 128)

**Why it works:** Anchors an abstract distinction in mechanics every physics learner has seen, and shows 'basis turning' terms before connection coefficients in GR.

**App idea:** Orbit tracer that splits the acceleration arrow into d(components)/dt and 'basis rotation' contributions.

### For diagonal metrics the vielbein is just square roots of |g_mumu|, checked by contracting with g. · GA §10.3 p.115 (pdf 132)

**Why it works:** Gives a two-second recipe that covers most metrics met in the book (Schwarzschild, Robertson-Walker, spheres) and a check that catches mistakes.

**App idea:** Drag-in-a-metric card: diagonal entries produce vielbein and inverse instantly, with a green tick when g = e^T eta e checks.

### Stationary versus freely falling frames as the two canonical observers, built for the same Schwarzschild geometry. · GA §10.4 p.116 (pdf 133)

**Why it works:** Juxtaposes a hovering and a falling observer at the same event, the key comparison for black-hole physics later.

**App idea:** t-r spacetime diagram near a black hole with static and infalling tetrads at a chosen r; slider in r shows the relative boost speed sqrt(2M/r) approaching 1 at the horizon and the static tetrad degenerating.

### In a parallel-transported frame, covariant derivatives become ordinary derivatives of frame components (Example 10.13). · GA §10.4 p.117 (pdf 134)

**Why it works:** Justifies doing special-relativistic calculus inside a falling lab and sets up geodesic deviation as an ordinary ODE.

**App idea:** Toggle between coordinate-component and frame-component plots of a transported vector along a Schwarzschild geodesic: coordinate components wiggle, frame components stay flat.

### Perfect-fluid tensor collapses to diag(rho, p, p, p) in the orthonormal frame. · GA §10.3 p.115 (pdf 132)

**Why it works:** Shows immediately why physicists quote densities and pressures in orthonormal frames: the coordinate scale factors are artefacts.

**App idea:** Side-by-side matrices of T in coordinate and orthonormal components as the learner moves a point in chi and theta.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Printed slips: eqn 10.28 has (e_rhat)^theta where (e_thetahat)^theta is meant; Example 10.5 calls the plane 'cylindrical-polar' and writes d phi^2 with coordinates (r, theta); Fig. 10.3 caption has 'which' for 'while'; the chapter summary writes g_00 (u^0) = -1 without the square; margin note 14 gives g_phiphi = r^2 sin theta instead of r^2 sin^2 theta; Exercise 10.1 has sin^2 phi for sin^2 theta; on p.111 the text says e_r and e_theta cannot be expanded in Cartesian derivatives where the normalized e_rhat and e_thetahat are meant; and Example 10.4 speaks of 'cylindrical coordinates' for what is a plane-polar problem.** — Careful readers checking the algebra may lose confidence or copy wrong components. *Suggestion:* Present corrected forms in the app and flag each slip briefly in tutor responses when a learner quotes the book.
- **Example 10.12 says '(e_rhat)^r = 1' is a choice, but once e_that = u and e_rhat lies in the t-r plane, orthogonality to u plus normalization forces (e_rhat)^r = +/-1; the orthogonality condition that fixes the sign of the t component is never written.** — Learners may think vielbeins carry arbitrary free choices here, or get the sign of (e_rhat)^t wrong. *Suggestion:* Write both conditions: g(u, e_rhat) = 0 gives (e_rhat)^t = -(2M/r)^{1/2}(1-2M/r)^{-1}(e_rhat)^r, then normalization gives (e_rhat)^r = 1.
- **The claim that connection coefficients vanish along the geodesic in the freely falling frame is asserted; parallel transport of the tetrad only removes connection terms with the derivative along u, and the chapter never checks that Example 10.12's tetrad is actually parallel transported.** — Learners may believe all frame connection coefficients vanish on the world line for any extension of the tetrad, and conflate the falling frame with Riemann normal or Fermi normal coordinates. *Suggestion:* State precisely: nabla_u e_alphahat = 0 means Gamma^muhat_{alphahat 0hat} = 0 along the curve; show quickly that nabla_u e_thetahat = 0 for radial infall (u^r d(1/r)/dr + (1/r)u^r Gamma^theta_{r theta} = 0); name Fermi normal coordinates as the full construction.
- **The stationary-frame recipe is given without its domain: it needs g_00 < 0, and for non-diagonal metrics (Exercises 10.5, 10.7) the square-root rule fails.** — Applying u^0 = (-g_00)^{-1/2} inside r = 2M or beyond a rotating frame's light cylinder yields imaginary results; learners stall on Exercise 10.7. *Suggestion:* Add the condition g_00 < 0 and demonstrate Gram-Schmidt starting from e_0hat = u for a non-diagonal metric (the rotating frame is a good example).
- **Index ordering of connection coefficients: ch7 writes the derivative index last (d e_mu/dx^alpha = Gamma^lambda_{mu alpha} e_lambda) while the commutator identity in Exercise 10.2(b) and its Appendix E answer treat the first lower index as the derivative direction.** — In a non-coordinate basis Gamma is not symmetric, so mixing conventions produces sign errors in the commutator check. *Suggestion:* Fix one convention explicitly (e.g. nabla_{e_beta} e_alpha = Gamma^mu_{alpha beta} e_mu) and restate the identity in that convention before the exercise.
- **Exercise 10.5 relies on conservation of the covariant time component of photon momentum in a time-independent metric, which the chapter has not introduced.** — The one-line Appendix E answer ('same r, so same E') looks like it ignores the photon's travel through the gravitational-like potential. *Suggestion:* Explain that a t-independent metric conserves p_t along geodesics, and static observers measure E = -p_t u^t, so equal u^t implies equal frequencies.
- **No explicit example compares what two different observers measure at the same event, although this is the chapter's conceptual core.** — Learners may treat frames as bookkeeping rather than as the physical distinction between, e.g., hovering and falling observers. *Suggestion:* Add a worked comparison: -u_static.u_infall = (1-2M/r)^{-1/2} = gamma, giving relative speed sqrt(2M/r), and the different energies each assigns to the same photon.
- **Example 10.2 uses g both for the proper acceleration and for an unknown component function g(tau); Example 10.4 uses omega for angular velocity while Example 10.3 uses it for light frequency.** — Symbol collisions invite algebra mistakes when solving (10.5)-(10.6). *Suggestion:* Rename the unknown functions (e.g. A(tau), B(tau)) and use theta-dot for angular speed.
- **The margin argument in Example 10.13 invokes the covariant derivative of a scalar via 'the earlier sidenote' without pointing to where it was shown, and relies on the Leibniz rule for the pairing, which ch7 postpones to Part V.** — The key step (10.68) can feel like sleight of hand. *Suggestion:* State plainly: components chi^muhat = <omega^muhat, chi> are scalars; the covariant derivative of a scalar is its directional derivative; Leibniz plus nabla_u omega^muhat = 0 finishes the proof.
- **Newman-Penrose normalizations in Exercise 10.6 (l.n = 1, m.m-bar = -1) are those of a (+,-,-,-) signature, contrary to the book's (-,+,+,+); the Appendix E answer also contains index slips.** — Learners in the book's signature may find the conditions inconsistent with real null vectors built from their eta. *Suggestion:* Note the signature mismatch and give the (-,+,+,+) version (l.n = -1, m.m-bar = 1) as an alternative.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'If a metric has a coordinate called t, is t what your wristwatch reads?' Use the static Schwarzschild observer to show sqrt(-g_tt) dt is the proper time, which makes the Einstein quotation concrete.
- Recommended order: vector versus components -> measurement as projection -> e_0hat = u -> E = -p.u (moving observer, accelerated observer) -> coordinate vs non-coordinate basis (polar unit vectors) -> vielbein as translation matrix -> diagonal square-root recipe -> Schwarzschild static frame -> freely falling frame and Example 10.13.
- For novices, skip §10.2's formal rules at first: teach E = -p.u and the diagonal square-root recipe, then return to index-swap rules when the learner needs them (ch11 §11.7, Part IV).
- Check for understanding: 'Two observers pass through the same event with different velocities. Do they agree on p? On p^0hat?' Expected: same vector, different components.
- Check: ask the learner to derive E = gamma m from -p.u by evaluating in the particle's rest frame, and to explain why that shortcut is legitimate (the dot product is a scalar).
- Check: give ds^2 = -(1+2 Phi) dt^2 + (1-2 Phi)(dx^2+dy^2+dz^2) and ask for the static vielbein; then ask what frequency a static observer at Phi measures for light that has frequency omega_inf far away (using conservation of p_t).
- Common question: 'Why do we need non-coordinate bases at all?' Answer: measurements need orthonormal axes, and orthonormal axes in curved or curvilinear settings usually cannot come from any coordinate grid; the polar e_theta/r example and its non-zero commutator show this.
- Common question: 'Is the vielbein unique?' Answer: no; any Lorentz transformation of the hatted index gives another valid frame; choosing an observer (u) fixes the boost and a spatial orientation fixes the rest.
- Common question: 'Is the freely falling frame the same as the static frame?' Use Examples 10.10 and 10.12 at the same r and compute the relative speed sqrt(2M/r); emphasize static observers do not exist at r <= 2M.
- Watch for sign errors with eta_00 = -1: learners often write E = p.u or forget that p_0hat = -p^0hat.
- Watch for learners trying to transform connection coefficients with vielbeins (Exercise 10.2 hint); redirect to computing nabla_{e_betahat} e_alphahat directly.
- Watch for missing hats: when a learner's result is off by metric factors, first ask which basis each index belongs to.
- Bridge forward: Example 10.13's result is exactly what lets ch11 write geodesic deviation as d^2 xi^ihat/dtau^2 = -R^ihat_{0hat jhat 0hat} xi^jhat.
