# GA ch15 · An introduction to cosmology

> Opens Part III by arguing that gravity controls the largest scales, adopting the cosmological principle at scales above about 100 Mpc, turning Hubble's law into a scale factor a(t) with comoving coordinates, building cosmic time from a foliation by spacelike slices threaded by Weyl's fundamental observers, writing the general comoving metric, and then solving two toy universes: empty Minkowski space (Universe 0) and the spatially flat, exponentially expanding de Sitter model driven purely by a cosmological constant (Universe 1).

**Pages:** printed 157–168 · pdf 174–185 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

Sections 15.1-15.2 are gentle and largely verbal with simple algebra. Section 15.3 is conceptually dense (foliation, isometries, Weyl's postulate, orthogonality argument) but light on computation and somewhat hand-waving. Example 15.3 is the hardest part: curvature components are quoted rather than derived, and the orthonormal-frame sign bookkeeping with eta and the vielbein demands comfort with ch10-11; index notation in 15.25 is loose. Example 15.4 is easy once R is in hand. The negative-pressure interpretation is intuitive but physically imprecise. A few printed slips (missing square on dt in 15.32 and on a(t0) in margin note 22) can confuse careful readers.

## Role in the book

First chapter of Part III. Part II ended with the field equation (ch13) and its weak-field and Schwarzschild consequences (ch14); this chapter is the first place the full equation, including Lambda, is applied to a whole spacetime rather than to a star. It sets up the kinematic language (homogeneity, isotropy, scale factor, comoving observers, cosmic time) and gives a first taste of the recipe used through Part III: guess a symmetric metric, compute curvature (here via the orthonormal frame and vielbein from ch10-11), and match against an energy-momentum content. The general spatial geometries (k = -1, 0, +1) are deferred to ch16, realistic matter to ch17, a zoo of models to ch18, and horizons and conformal infinity to ch19; the de Sitter horizon and light-cone narrowing are only previewed in exercises and margin notes.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can explain why gravity, although the weakest interaction, dominates the dynamics of the Universe on large scales (long range, only attractive, additive).
- Reader can state the cosmological principle, separate homogeneity from isotropy, and explain why it fails locally but holds after coarse-graining on scales of roughly 100 Mpc.
- Reader can convert between AU, light-years and parsecs and place stars, galaxies and the homogeneity scale on a ladder of distances.
- Reader can derive v = H r from uniform scaling r(t) = a(t) r(t0), show it holds for every observer, and identify H(t) = a-dot/a.
- Reader can distinguish comoving from physical coordinates and Hubble flow from peculiar (proper) motion, and give the CMB dipole as an example of the latter.
- Reader can describe a foliation of spacetime by spacelike hypersurfaces, define cosmic time and comoving observers, and state Weyl's postulate.
- Reader can argue from isotropy that fluid world lines must be orthogonal to the slices (g_{0i} = 0) and use the vanishing of Gamma^i_{00} to test that coordinates are comoving.
- Reader can write the general comoving homogeneous-isotropic line element ds^2 = -dt^2 + a^2 gamma_ij dx^i dx^j and say what a(t) and gamma_ij encode.
- Reader can show that Minkowski spacetime solves the empty-universe Einstein equation and explain why Ricci-flat does not in general mean Riemann-flat.
- Reader can compute the orthonormal-frame Riemann and Ricci components, and the Ricci scalar R = 6(a-ddot/a + a-dot^2/a^2), for the spatially flat expanding metric.
- Reader can solve the 00 component of the Einstein equation with only Lambda to obtain a(t) = exp(Ht) with H^2 = Lambda/3, and check the spatial components.
- Reader can interpret a positive cosmological constant as a fluid with rho = Lambda/8pi and p = -rho, and explain (with the correct GR reason) why it drives accelerated expansion.

## Teaching approach

A short, bridging chapter that moves from physical motivation and order-of-magnitude astronomy to kinematic geometry and then to a single computed solution. The authors first make the simplifying assumption plausible by zooming out through distance scales, then derive Hubble's law as a consequence of uniform rescaling so that it no longer seems to put us at the centre. Geometry is introduced pictorially (stacked sheets pierced by world lines) before being turned into metric conditions. The two model universes are deliberately minimal: an empty one that is just Minkowski space in disguise, and one with only vacuum energy, where the curvature computation doubles as practice with the orthonormal frame machinery of ch10. The chapter ends with an intuitive (and somewhat loose) negative-pressure story and defers generality to later chapters.

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, conversational-informal, diagram-driven, derivation-heavy, example-driven, observational-astrophysics

**Narrative arc**

1. Why gravity: compare the four interactions and argue that long range plus no cancellation makes gravity the ruler of cosmic structure; flag cosmic evolution (good news) and singularities (bad news).
2. Goal: solve Einstein's equation with Lambda for the whole matter content, which means finding a metric everywhere.
3. Simplify: Copernican principle leads to the cosmological principle (homogeneity plus isotropy).
4. Reality check: the principle is obviously false in daily life and in the Solar System and Galaxy; a distance ladder (AU, ly, pc, kpc, Mpc) shows lumpiness smoothing out above about 100 Mpc, so matter is coarse-grained into a perfect cosmic fluid.
5. Observation: Hubble's law; resolve the apparent 'we are the centre' paradox by uniform scaling, introducing a(t), H = a-dot/a, comoving versus physical coordinates, Hubble flow versus proper motions, and the CMB dipole.
6. Scope limit: bound systems like the Solar System ignore the expansion; Part III looks only at the largest scales.
7. Geometry: cosmic time via a foliation by spacelike hypersurfaces on which the cosmological principle holds; comoving observers pierce the slices.
8. Constraints: isotropy forces slices orthogonal to fluid world lines, giving g_{0i}=0, e_0=u, g_00=-1; Gamma^i_{00}=0 identifies comoving coordinates; Weyl's postulate enshrines the geodesic bundle.
9. General comoving metric with a(t) and gamma_ij (Robertson-Walker form previewed).
10. Universe 0: T=0, show Minkowski solves it, geodesics are straight lines; caveat that Ricci-flat need not be flat.
11. Universe 1: spatially flat expanding metric; compute curvature in the orthonormal frame, transform back with the vielbein, get R.
12. Match with pure vacuum energy: 00 equation gives exponential expansion with H^2 = Lambda/3; interpret Lambda as negative pressure.
13. Summary and exercises that preview constant curvature, cosmological horizons, redshifting of momenta and the vacuum-energy problem.

**Signature moves**

- Admits the central assumption is 'clearly wrong' and then rescues it by a zoom-out through distance scales, making coarse-graining the explicit justification.
- Defuses the anthropocentric reading of Hubble's law by using separation vectors r_i - r_j, so the origin drops out.
- Pictures cosmic time as a stack of paper-like sheets pierced by a bundle of arrows before writing any metric component.
- Uses isotropy to exclude a tilt between world lines and slices (a tilt would single out a direction), turning a symmetry statement into g_{0i}=0.
- Gives a practical diagnostic for comoving coordinates: check that g_{00,i} and g_{0i,0} vanish so Gamma^i_{00} = 0 and particles at rest stay at rest.
- Numbers its toy models (Universe 0, Universe 1) to build a ladder of increasingly realistic universes continued in ch18.
- Recycles the cosmological example as practice with the orthonormal frame and vielbein, spelling out every sign from eta when raising and lowering.
- Reads the cosmological constant back as a perfect fluid with an effective negative pressure to give a physical story for acceleration.
- Pushes the full connection and Riemann calculation into exercises and uses margin notes to preview later chapters (light-cone narrowing, horizons, Ricci as volume change).

## Section by section

### Part III opener and chapter opening: why gravity rules the cosmos — p.157 (pdf 174)

A one-page Part III preface maps the arc: two toy universes here, possible constant-curvature geometries (ch16), filling them with matter (ch17), many models (ch18), and infinity and causal structure (ch19). The chapter opening then justifies cosmology as a gravitational problem: of the four interactions gravity is the feeblest, yet it is long range and never screened by opposite charges, so its effects add up over huge distances. It previews two themes: solvable models typically have spatial geometry that changes with time (cosmic evolution, unsettling to early relativists who expected a static cosmos), and many models begin or end in a singularity where classical field theory fails.

**Concepts:** cosmology, gravity as the dominant long-range interaction, cosmic evolution, spacetime singularity

**Key moves**
- Compare gravity with the strong, weak and electromagnetic forces: range and sign, not strength, decide which force governs the largest scales.
- Frame the solvable models as having time-dependent spatial metric components.
- Flag singularities as a known difficulty to be handled later.

### §15.1 The cosmological principle — p.159 (pdf 176)

States the programme: solve the field equation with Lambda for the entire matter content, i.e. find the metric at every event. A margin note fixes G = c = 1 for Part III and recommends the strategy of matching Einstein-tensor components to those of T and Lambda g. Another stresses the block-universe view: spacetime as a whole is fixed even though spatial metric components depend on t. The Copernican principle (our viewpoint is typical) motivates the cosmological principle of spatial homogeneity and isotropy. The authors immediately concede it fails on everyday, Solar-System and galactic scales, and Example 15.1 climbs a distance ladder (AU, light-year, parsec, kpc, Mpc) to show that lumpiness washes out only beyond roughly 100 Mpc. Cosmological models therefore apply only above that scale, with matter smeared (coarse-grained) into a uniform perfect fluid, dust if non-interacting.

**Concepts:** Einstein field equation with cosmological constant, Copernican principle, cosmological principle, spatial homogeneity, spatial isotropy, parsec, homogeneity scale and coarse-graining, cosmological perfect fluid

**Key moves**
- Frame the goal of cosmology as determining the metric at every event from the total (smoothed) matter and Lambda content.
- Distinguish homogeneity (same at every place) from isotropy (same in every direction).
- Use deliberate counterexamples (sky versus ground, mountains versus valleys, Solar System versus intergalactic space) to show the principle is a large-scale statement.
- Example 15.1: build the distance ladder and identify about 100 Mpc (3e24 m) as the scale where the Universe looks smooth.
- Replace discrete galaxies by a continuous perfect fluid (coarse-graining), with a pixelated-screen analogy in a margin note.

### §15.2 The Hubble flow — p.160 (pdf 177)

Introduces Hubble's law v = H0 r from redshift measurements, with H0 quoted as 70 km/s/Mpc, and reads it as expansion. It resolves the apparent special-position paradox by writing all positions as a(t) times their values at t0, so every pairwise separation scales the same way and every observer sees the same linear law; differentiating gives H(t) = a-dot/a, a parameter that in general varies with time. Physical coordinates r(t) are distinguished from comoving coordinates r(t0), in which galaxies sit still. The Hubble flow is the expansion-driven motion; proper (peculiar) motions such as orbital motion in clusters, or our motion that produces the CMB dipole, sit on top of it. Gravitationally bound systems such as the Solar System are governed by local interactions and do not expand.

**Concepts:** Hubble's law, Hubble parameter, scale factor, comoving coordinates, physical coordinates, Hubble flow, peculiar motion, CMB rest frame and dipole anisotropy, bound systems do not expand

**Key moves**
- Assume uniform rescaling r(t) = a(t) r(t0) with a(t0) = 1.
- Subtract two position vectors so the origin choice cancels: r_ij(t) = a(t) r_ij(t0).
- Differentiate to get v_ij = (a-dot/a) r_ij, so the Hubble law is the same for every galaxy and H = a-dot/a.
- Define comoving coordinates (fixed labels) versus physical coordinates (stretched by a).
- Separate Hubble flow from superposed proper motions; use the CMB dipole as evidence of our own motion.
- Limit the theory to scales where local binding is irrelevant.

### §15.3 Cosmic time — p.162 (pdf 179)

Builds the coordinate system for cosmology. A cosmic time t, which a margin note ties to any monotonically evolving scalar such as the radiation temperature, labels a stack of non-intersecting spacelike hypersurfaces that foliate spacetime; on each slice density, pressure and curvature are uniform and no direction is special. Observers moving relative to this system would see anisotropy, so it is singled out. Comoving observers ride with the cosmic fluid and their world lines pierce the slices. Example 15.2 argues that isotropy forces those world lines to be orthogonal to the slices, giving g_{0i}=0, e_0 = u and g_00 = -1, and shows that when g_{00,i} and g_{0i,0} vanish, Gamma^i_{00}=0 so particles at rest stay at rest. Weyl's postulate asserts a smooth, non-crossing bundle of timelike geodesics of fundamental observers whose proper time equals cosmic time. The section ends with the general comoving metric ds^2 = -dt^2 + a(t)^2 gamma_ij dx^i dx^j, credited to Robertson and Walker, with details deferred to ch16.

**Concepts:** cosmic time, spacelike hypersurface, foliation of spacetime, isometry, comoving observer, orthogonality of world lines to homogeneous slices, test for comoving coordinates, Weyl's postulate, Robertson-Walker form of the metric, spatial metric gamma_ij

**Key moves**
- Define time by a physical clock shared by all: a monotonic scalar field (e.g. CMB temperature), which forces all equivalent coordinate systems to agree on t.
- Picture spacetime as a stack of sheets (homogeneity relates points on one sheet by an isometry at fixed t; isotropy is a rotation isometry fixing a point).
- Argue that a tilt between world lines and slices would define a preferred spatial direction, contradicting isotropy.
- Translate the geometry into metric conditions g_{0i}=0 and g_00=-1 and identify e_0 with the fluid four-velocity.
- Use the geodesic equation: Gamma^i_{00}=0 means a particle at rest in these coordinates stays at rest.
- State Weyl's postulate and identify cosmic time with the fundamental observers' proper time.
- Write the general comoving metric with a(t) multiplying a time-independent spatial metric.

### §15.4 Universe 0: an empty universe — p.163 (pdf 180)

The simplest model has no matter and no energy, so the Einstein tensor must vanish; since R is the trace of the Ricci tensor, Ricci-flatness suffices. Minkowski spacetime in Cartesian coordinates is comoving, fits the general form with a = 1 and flat gamma_ij, and has vanishing Riemann tensor, so it solves the problem; its slices are Euclidean and its geodesics are straight lines in proper time. Margin notes warn that Ricci-flat does not generally mean Riemann-flat, since positive and negative curvature contributions can cancel in the contraction (the Schwarzschild exterior is our example, not the book's), mention the iconic photo of Einstein writing R_ik = 0, and give the metric in spherical polars and in light-cone coordinates u = t - r, v = t + r for later use. The model is dismissed as unrealistic.

**Concepts:** empty universe (Universe 0), vacuum Einstein equation, Ricci-flat versus Riemann-flat, Minkowski spacetime, light-cone coordinates, straight-line geodesics

**Key moves**
- Set T = 0 and note that R_mu nu = 0 implies G_mu nu = 0.
- Check that Minkowski satisfies the comoving conditions and has zero Riemann tensor.
- Write geodesics as x^mu = b^mu tau + c^mu.
- Caution (margin): Ricci can vanish while tidal Riemann components do not; Ricci measures volume change.
- Rewrite flat space in (u, v) null coordinates as a tool for later causal-structure chapters.

### §15.5 Universe 1: flat and expanding — p.164 (pdf 181)

Takes flat spatial slices scaled by a(t) and calls the model de Sitter. Margin notes point out that a slice at fixed t is Euclidean space scaled by a(t0) and that radial light rays obey dt/dr = plus or minus a(t), so light cones drawn in comoving coordinates narrow as a grows. Although slices are flat, spacetime is curved. Example 15.3 reads off orthonormal-frame Riemann components ä/a and (ȧ/a)^2, builds the vielbein, and contracts carefully with eta to get R_00 = -3ä/a, R_ii = aä + 2ȧ^2 and R = 6(ä/a + ȧ^2/a^2). It then asks what source fits and tries pure vacuum energy, G = -Lambda g. Example 15.4 solves the 00 equation: 3ȧ^2/a^2 = Lambda, so a = exp(Ht) with H^2 = Lambda/3, an accelerating expansion that also satisfies the spatial equations. The line element is rewritten with this a(t), H is identified as a Hubble parameter, and Lambda is reinterpreted as a fluid with energy density Lambda/8pi and pressure -Lambda/8pi. A chapter summary closes the unit.

**Concepts:** de Sitter model, spatially flat expanding metric, orthonormal frame and vielbein, Riemann components of the flat expanding metric, Ricci scalar of the flat expanding metric, cosmological constant as vacuum energy, exponential expansion, negative pressure of vacuum energy, narrowing of light cones in comoving coordinates, constant-curvature spacetime

**Key moves**
- Write the flat expanding metric in Cartesian and spherical coordinates.
- Note flat slices do not imply flat spacetime.
- Choose e_0 = u and e_i-hat = e_i / a; vielbein components 1 and a.
- Raise and lower in the local frame with eta, tracking two sign flips from pair swaps, to relate R^0_{i0i} to R^i_{0i0}.
- Contract to R_00 = -3ä/a and R_ii = a ä + 2ȧ^2; take the trace to get R.
- Pose the inverse problem: which T produces this geometry? Try pure Lambda.
- Solve the first-order 00 equation for exponential a(t); check spatial components.
- Match T_vac = -Lambda g / 8pi with perfect-fluid form to read off p = -rho.

### Exercises — p.167 (pdf 184)

Eight problems: a Milky Way star-count estimate; connection coefficients and Riemann tensor of de Sitter; the constant-curvature condition giving Lambda = R/4 and its check against the computed R; a 2D Chebyshev-net metric whose constant-curvature condition becomes the sine-Gordon equation; the vacuum energy density of the strong interaction and the absurdly small curvature radius it would imply; radial geodesics in de Sitter showing momenta decay as 1/a; a light signal whose comoving reach saturates, revealing an event horizon; and straight-line geodesics of massive particles in exponential de Sitter.

**Concepts:** constant-curvature spacetime, cosmological event horizon, decay of peculiar momentum with expansion, cosmological constant problem, sine-Gordon equation

**Key moves**
- Push the full Christoffel and Riemann computation onto the reader.
- Connect the Lambda-only model to maximal symmetry via R_mu nu = R g_mu nu / 4.
- Preview horizons (ch19) and redshifting via simple integrals.

## Concepts

### cosmology

*definition · introduced* · also: relativistic cosmology

The study of the large-scale structure, origin and evolution of the Universe as a whole; in general relativity it means finding spacetime metrics that solve Einstein's equation for the smoothed-out total matter and energy content.

**How introduced:** Motivated as the most ambitious application of the field equation just built; framed as specifying the metric at every point of space and time.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein field equation with cosmological constant

**Where:** GA p.158 (pdf 175); GA §15.1 p.159 (pdf 176)

### gravity as the dominant long-range interaction

*principle · mention* · also: why gravity rules the cosmos

Although gravity is by far the weakest fundamental interaction, it has infinite range and (for ordinary matter) is always attractive, so contributions from all masses add without cancellation; electromagnetism, though also long range, is neutralized by balanced charges. Hence gravity controls cosmic dynamics.

**How introduced:** Opening comparison of the four fundamental interactions, with a margin note listing them.

**Where:** GA p.158 (pdf 175)

### cosmic evolution

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: evolving universe, non-static universe

The property of cosmological solutions that the spatial part of the metric depends on the time coordinate, so distances between comoving objects change in time.

**How introduced:** Announced as the 'good news' of solvable models; noted that it troubled early relativists expecting a static cosmos. A margin note reconciles this with the block-universe view that spacetime as a whole is fixed.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor

**Where:** GA p.158 (pdf 175); GA §15.1 p.159 (pdf 176)

### spacetime singularity

*definition · mention* · also: singularity

An event or boundary of spacetime where curvature or other quantities diverge and the smooth classical field description, and known physics, break down; many cosmological models begin (and some end) in one.

**How introduced:** Named as the 'bad news' of cosmological models, with a promise that later chapters will show how to cope with them; reappears in Weyl's postulate as the only place fundamental world lines may meet.

**Where:** GA p.158 (pdf 175); GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180)

### Einstein field equation with cosmological constant

*law · revisited* · also: Einstein equation, G = 8 pi T - Lambda g

The field equation of general relativity in geometrized units, in which the Einstein tensor plus Lambda times the metric equals eight pi times the energy-momentum tensor; it ties spacetime curvature to matter and energy, with Lambda a constant uniform contribution.

**How introduced:** Restated at the start of the chapter as the equation cosmology must solve for all the matter in the Universe; a margin note describes the working strategy of matching components of G with those of kappa T and Lambda g.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein tensor, energy-momentum tensor, cosmological constant

$$
R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12 g_{\mu\nu} R = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu} - \Lambda g_{\mu\nu}
$$

**Notes:** Sign convention: Lambda appears with a minus sign on the right, so positive Lambda acts like positive vacuum energy density.

**Where:** GA §15.1 p.159 (pdf 176); GA §15.5 p.165 (pdf 182)

### Copernican principle

*principle · introduced*

The assumption that our location in the Universe is not privileged: observers elsewhere at the same cosmic epoch would see essentially the same Universe.

**How introduced:** Introduced as the first simplifying idea, with a biographical margin note on Copernicus, Gingerich's census of De Revolutionibus, and Bondi naming the modern principle; used later to answer whether Hubble's law puts us at the centre.

**Where:** GA §15.1 p.159 (pdf 176); GA §15.2 p.160 (pdf 177)

### cosmological principle

*principle · core*

The assumption that, on sufficiently large scales, the Universe is spatially homogeneous and isotropic at each cosmic time.

**How introduced:** Stated in a highlighted box after the Copernican principle, then immediately challenged by everyday counterexamples and rescued by moving to scales above about 100 Mpc.

**Prerequisites:** Copernican principle, spatial homogeneity, spatial isotropy

**Notes:** Isotropy about every point implies homogeneity; the book states both separately.

**Where:** GA §15.1 p.159 (pdf 176); GA §15.3 p.162 (pdf 179); GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183)

### spatial homogeneity

*definition · core* · also: homogeneity

Invariance of physical properties under translations within a spatial slice: every point of a constant-time hypersurface is equivalent to every other, related by an isometry that keeps t fixed.

**How introduced:** Given as a bullet under the cosmological principle and later made geometric in Fig. 15.1 as points p and q on one hypersurface related by an isometry.

**Prerequisites:** spacelike hypersurface, isometry

**Where:** GA §15.1 p.159 (pdf 176); GA §15.3 p.162 (pdf 179)

### spatial isotropy

*definition · core* · also: isotropy

Invariance under rotations about a point: at any event, a comoving observer finds no preferred spatial direction; geometrically, there is an isometry fixing the point and rotating any spatial direction into any other.

**How introduced:** Bullet definition under the cosmological principle, then Fig. 15.2 shows two spacelike vectors at a point related by a rotation isometry; used to force world lines perpendicular to slices.

**Prerequisites:** spacelike hypersurface, isometry, comoving observer

**Where:** GA §15.1 p.159 (pdf 176); GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180)

### homogeneity scale and coarse-graining

*technique · developed* · also: coarse-graining, smoothing scale, 100 Mpc scale

Averaging the discrete distribution of stars and galaxies over regions large enough (about 100 Mpc or more) that the averaged density is uniform, so matter can be modelled as a continuous fluid; the resulting models are not meant to describe smaller scales.

**How introduced:** Arrived at via the distance ladder of Example 15.1 and a margin analogy with a grey screen made of pixels.

**Prerequisites:** cosmological principle, parsec

$$
100 Mpc ~ 3e24 m
$$

**Where:** GA §15.1 p.160 (pdf 177); GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178)

### parsec

*convention · introduced* · also: pc, kpc, Mpc

The distance at which one astronomical unit subtends one arcsecond; 1 pc = 3.086e16 m, about 3.26 light-years. Kiloparsecs and megaparsecs measure galactic and cosmological distances.

**How introduced:** Example 15.1 compares AU, light-year and parsec, with a margin note deriving the name from parallax of one arcsecond.

$$
1\,\mathrm{pc} = 3.086\times10^{16}\,\mathrm{m}
$$

**Where:** GA §15.1 p.159 (pdf 176)

### cosmological perfect fluid

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: cosmic fluid, cosmological fluid, dust

The coarse-grained matter content modelled as a perfect fluid of uniform density (and pressure) whose elements move with the cosmic expansion; with no internal interactions it is pressureless dust.

**How introduced:** Recalled from ch12 as the natural stand-in for smoothed galaxies; allowed to move, expand and contract.

**Prerequisites:** perfect fluid energy-momentum tensor, homogeneity scale and coarse-graining

**Where:** GA §15.1 p.160 (pdf 177); GA §15.3 p.162 (pdf 179)

### Hubble's law

*law · core* · also: Hubble-Lemaitre law

The observed linear relation between recession velocity and distance for galaxies, v = H0 r, valid at low redshift; interpreted as the uniform expansion of the Universe.

**How introduced:** Presented as an observational fact from redshifts, then derived kinematically from uniform scaling to show every galaxy sees the same law.

**Prerequisites:** scale factor, Hubble parameter

$$
\vec v = H_0 \vec r
$$

**Notes:** The book writes it as an exact vector law; in relativity the linear velocity-distance relation is only a low-redshift statement.

**Where:** GA §15.2 p.160 (pdf 177); GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178)

### Hubble parameter

*physical-quantity · core* · also: Hubble constant H0, H(t), expansion rate

The fractional expansion rate H(t) = a-dot/a; its present value H0 is the Hubble constant, about 70 km/s per Mpc as quoted in the book. In the de Sitter model H is truly constant, equal to sqrt(Lambda/3).

**How introduced:** Introduced with Hubble's law, then derived from the scale factor; a margin note warns that it is generally time-dependent. Reappears as the rate constant in the exponential solution.

**Prerequisites:** scale factor

$$
H(t) = \dot a / a
$$

$$
H^2 = \Lambda/3 \text{ (de Sitter)}
$$

**Notes:** The book says Hubble's original value was nearly an order of magnitude too large (about 500 versus about 70 km/s/Mpc, i.e. roughly sevenfold).

**Where:** GA §15.2 p.160 (pdf 177); GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178); GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183)

### scale factor

*physical-quantity · core* · also: a(t), expansion factor, scaling factor

A dimensionless function of cosmic time that multiplies all comoving separations to give physical separations; normalized so that a(t0) = 1 today.

**How introduced:** Introduced as the uniform stretch r(t) = a(t) r(t0) that makes Hubble's law origin-independent, then inserted into the general metric and solved for in Universe 1.

**Prerequisites:** comoving coordinates

$$
\vec r(t) = a(t)\vec r(t_0)
$$

$$
a(t_0)=1
$$

**Where:** GA §15.2 p.160 (pdf 177); GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178); GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180); GA §15.5 p.164 (pdf 181)

### comoving coordinates

*convention · core* · also: comoving system

Spatial coordinates attached to the cosmic fluid, so that galaxies without peculiar motion keep fixed coordinate labels while their physical separations grow with a(t); a particle at rest in them stays at rest.

**How introduced:** Defined as r(t0) versus physical r(t) in the Hubble-flow discussion; a margin note gives the operational definition; Example 15.2 supplies the metric test Gamma^i_00 = 0.

**Prerequisites:** scale factor

**Where:** GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178); GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180)

### physical coordinates

*definition · introduced* · also: proper distance, real coordinates

Positions or separations measured at fixed cosmic time as actual distances, equal to the scale factor times the comoving values.

**How introduced:** Paired with comoving coordinates when defining the Hubble flow.

**Prerequisites:** comoving coordinates, scale factor

$$
\vec r_{ij}(t) = a(t)\,\vec r_{ij}(t_0)
$$

**Where:** GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178)

### Hubble flow

*phenomenon · core*

The recession of objects caused purely by the growth of the scale factor, with all comoving labels fixed.

**How introduced:** Named in the second bullet of section 15.2 and contrasted with superposed proper motions.

**Prerequisites:** scale factor, comoving coordinates

**Where:** GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178); GA §15.3 p.162 (pdf 179)

### peculiar motion

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: proper motion (book's term)

Motion of an object relative to the local comoving frame, produced by local gravitational interactions, superposed on the Hubble flow.

**How introduced:** Illustrated by galaxies bound in clusters and by the Earth's motion relative to the CMB.

**Prerequisites:** Hubble flow

**Notes:** The book calls these 'proper motions'; in astronomy that term usually means angular motion on the sky, so 'peculiar velocity' is the standard cosmology name.

**Where:** GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178)

### CMB rest frame and dipole anisotropy

*experiment-or-observation · introduced* · also: CMB dipole, cosmic microwave background frame

The cosmic microwave background is almost uniform over the sky, but our motion relative to the frame in which it is isotropic Doppler-shifts it into a small dipole pattern (slightly hotter ahead, cooler behind).

**How introduced:** Given as an example of proper motion superposed on the Hubble flow; a margin note explains the CMB as relic radiation from recombination and says the frame is physically distinguished though not privileged by the laws of physics.

**Prerequisites:** peculiar motion, comoving observer

**Where:** GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178)

### bound systems do not expand

*principle · mention* · also: local systems decouple from expansion

Gravitationally (or otherwise) bound systems such as planetary orbits, stars and galaxies are held together by local interactions; the cosmic scale factor does not stretch them, and the expansion description applies only on scales above the homogeneity scale.

**How introduced:** A brief remark (with a joke about waistlines) that the Solar System ignores a(t), since local forces dominate and the cosmic curvature there is negligible.

**Prerequisites:** Hubble flow, homogeneity scale and coarse-graining

**Where:** GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178)

### cosmic time

*definition · core* · also: cosmic standard time

A global time coordinate t equal to the proper time of comoving fundamental observers, whose constant-t slices are the hypersurfaces on which the Universe is homogeneous and isotropic; operationally it can be read from any monotonically evolving scalar such as the background radiation temperature.

**How introduced:** Built as the time a sensible astronomer would use; a margin note argues that defining t through a scalar S forces all equivalent systems to share the same t.

**Prerequisites:** spacelike hypersurface, comoving observer

**Where:** GA §15.3 p.162 (pdf 179); GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180)

### spacelike hypersurface

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: spatial slice, constant-time slice

A three-dimensional submanifold of spacetime obtained here by fixing t, whose tangent vectors are all spacelike; it represents space at one instant.

**How introduced:** Defined by holding one coordinate fixed; drawn as curled sheets in Figs. 15.1 and 15.3.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor

**Where:** GA §15.3 p.162 (pdf 179)

### foliation of spacetime

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: slicing

A decomposition of spacetime into a one-parameter family of non-intersecting hypersurfaces that together cover every event exactly once.

**How introduced:** Introduced with Fig. 15.1 as a stack of sheets labelled by cosmic time.

**Prerequisites:** spacelike hypersurface

**Where:** GA §15.3 p.162 (pdf 179)

### isometry

*definition · introduced*

A map of a (pseudo-)Riemannian space to itself that preserves all distances, i.e. leaves the metric unchanged; symmetries such as homogeneity (translations in a slice) and isotropy (rotations about a point) are isometries.

**How introduced:** Defined in the captions of Figs. 15.1 and 15.2 to make homogeneity and isotropy precise.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor

**Where:** GA §15.3 p.162 (pdf 179); GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180)

### comoving observer

*definition · core* · also: fundamental observer, privileged observer

An observer at rest in comoving coordinates, moving with the cosmic fluid; their world line is a timelike geodesic orthogonal to the homogeneous slices, their proper time is cosmic time, and they see the Universe as isotropic.

**How introduced:** Described as going with the Hubble flow; drawn as arrows piercing sheets in Fig. 15.3; enshrined in Weyl's postulate.

**Prerequisites:** comoving coordinates, cosmic time

$$
\boldsymbol e_0 = \boldsymbol u
$$

**Where:** GA §15.3 p.162 (pdf 179); GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180)

### orthogonality of world lines to homogeneous slices

*principle · core* · also: hypersurface orthogonality, g_0i = 0

In a homogeneous, isotropic cosmology the four-velocity of the cosmic fluid is normal to the constant-time slices, so in comoving coordinates g_{0i} = 0 and, with t as proper time, g_00 = -1.

**How introduced:** Example 15.2: a tilt between world lines and slices would pick out a spatial direction and contradict isotropy; the conclusion is written as three metric/basis conditions.

**Prerequisites:** spatial isotropy, comoving observer, spacelike hypersurface

$$
\boldsymbol e_0\cdot\boldsymbol e_i = g_{0i} = 0
$$

$$
\boldsymbol e_0\cdot\boldsymbol e_0 = g_{00} = -1
$$

**Where:** GA §15.3 p.162 (pdf 179); GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180)

### test for comoving coordinates

*technique · developed* · also: Gamma^i_00 = 0 criterion

If Gamma^i_{00} = 0, the geodesic equation shows that a particle initially at rest in the coordinates remains at rest, so the spatial coordinates are comoving; with g_{0i}=0 this holds when g_{00,i} = 0 and g_{0i,0} = 0.

**How introduced:** Given in Example 15.2 as a quick diagnostic and reused in margin notes to certify eqns 15.11 and 15.15.

**Prerequisites:** connection coefficients, geodesic equation, comoving coordinates

$$
\Gamma^i_{00} = \tfrac12 g^{ij}\left(2g_{j0,0} - g_{00,j}\right)
$$

**Where:** GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180); GA §15.4 p.164 (pdf 181)

### Weyl's postulate

*principle · developed*

The assumption that cosmic spacetime contains a family of fundamental observers whose world lines form a smooth congruence of timelike geodesics that never intersect, except possibly at an initial or final singularity.

**How introduced:** Stated in a highlighted box after Example 15.2 with a margin note on Weyl and on the informal meaning of 'bundle'; illustrated by Fig. 15.3.

**Prerequisites:** comoving observer, geodesic

**Where:** GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180)

### Robertson-Walker form of the metric

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: general comoving cosmological metric, FLRW metric (preview)

The line element ds^2 = -dt^2 + a(t)^2 gamma_ij dx^i dx^j, in which homogeneity and isotropy restrict time dependence to an overall scale factor multiplying a fixed spatial metric of constant curvature.

**How introduced:** Assembled at the end of section 15.3 from g_00 = -1, g_0i = 0 and a homogeneous isotropic 3-metric; credited to Robertson and Walker and deferred to ch16 for the allowed gamma_ij.

**Prerequisites:** scale factor, spatial metric gamma_ij, orthogonality of world lines to homogeneous slices

$$
\mathrm ds^2 = -\mathrm dt^2 + a(t)^2\gamma_{ij}\mathrm dx^i\mathrm dx^j
$$

**Where:** GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180)

### spatial metric gamma_ij

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: 3-metric of the slices

The time-independent metric on a constant-t slice (before scaling), a function of the spatial coordinates only, encoding the spatial curvature; for flat space it is the identity in Cartesian coordinates.

**How introduced:** Introduced when writing the metric of each slice as dsigma^2 = gamma_ij dx^i dx^j.

**Prerequisites:** spacelike hypersurface

$$
\mathrm d\sigma^2 = \gamma_{ij}\mathrm dx^i\mathrm dx^j
$$

**Where:** GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180)

### empty universe (Universe 0)

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: Universe 0, Minkowski cosmology

The cosmological model with T_mu nu = 0 and Lambda = 0, satisfied by flat Minkowski spacetime written in comoving coordinates with a = 1 and Euclidean slices.

**How introduced:** The first rung of the book's numbered ladder of model universes; checked against the general comoving form and dismissed as unrealistic.

**Prerequisites:** vacuum Einstein equation, Minkowski spacetime

$$
\mathrm ds^2 = -\mathrm dt^2+\mathrm dx^2+\mathrm dy^2+\mathrm dz^2
$$

**Notes:** An empty universe can also be written with a(t) = t on hyperbolic slices (the Milne model); the book does not mention this here.

**Where:** GA §15.4 p.163 (pdf 180); GA §15.4 p.164 (pdf 181)

### vacuum Einstein equation

*law · revisited* · also: R_mu nu = 0

With no matter and no cosmological constant the field equation reduces to G_mu nu = 0, which is equivalent to R_mu nu = 0 because taking the trace forces R = 0.

**How introduced:** Derived for Universe 0 in one line from R = g^mu nu R_mu nu.

**Prerequisites:** Ricci tensor, Einstein tensor

$$
G_{\mu\nu} = R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12 g_{\mu\nu}R = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §15.4 p.163 (pdf 180); GA §15.4 p.164 (pdf 181)

### Ricci-flat versus Riemann-flat

*principle · mention*

A vanishing Ricci tensor does not imply a vanishing Riemann tensor: tidal (Weyl) curvature can remain, as outside a star, while volume-changing curvature cancels.

**How introduced:** Margin caution accompanying Universe 0: Ricci can vanish because positive and negative curvature parts cancel when it is formed, not because every Riemann component is zero; it adds that Ricci encodes the tendency of gravity to shrink volumes of matter and points ahead to ch35.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann curvature tensor, Ricci tensor

**Notes:** The 'outside a star' illustration in the definition is the reviewer's, not the book's.

**Where:** GA §15.4 p.164 (pdf 181)

### light-cone coordinates

*technique · mention* · also: null coordinates, u = t - r, v = t + r

Coordinates u = t - r and v = t + r adapted to radial light rays, in which flat spacetime reads ds^2 = -du dv + (v - u)^2/4 dOmega^2.

**How introduced:** Margin note to Universe 0, flagged for use in later chapters on causal structure.

**Prerequisites:** Minkowski spacetime

$$
\mathrm ds^2 = -\mathrm du\,\mathrm dv + \tfrac14(v-u)^2\mathrm d\Omega^2
$$

**Where:** GA §15.4 p.164 (pdf 181)

### de Sitter model

*solution-or-model · core* · also: Universe 1, flat de Sitter slicing, de Sitter spacetime

The spatially flat cosmology with metric ds^2 = -dt^2 + e^{2Ht}(dx^2+dy^2+dz^2), sourced only by a positive cosmological constant with H = sqrt(Lambda/3); it is a spacetime of constant curvature expanding exponentially.

**How introduced:** Introduced as a flat expanding metric with unspecified a(t), its curvature computed in Example 15.3, and its a(t) fixed in Example 15.4 by demanding a pure-Lambda source.

**Prerequisites:** Robertson-Walker form of the metric, cosmological constant as vacuum energy, Ricci scalar of the flat expanding metric

$$
a(t) = e^{Ht}
$$

$$
H = (\Lambda/3)^{1/2}
$$

**Notes:** The flat coordinates cover only half of the full de Sitter hyperboloid; the book's name is applied before a(t) is determined, when strictly only the exponential case is de Sitter.

**Where:** GA §15.5 p.164 (pdf 181); GA §15.5 p.165 (pdf 182); GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183); GA p.168 (pdf 185)

### Riemann components of the flat expanding metric

*identity · developed* · also: curvature of a spatially flat FRW metric

For ds^2 = -dt^2 + a^2 (flat 3-metric), the orthonormal-frame curvature has R^0_{i0i} = ä/a (time-space) and R^i_{jij} = (ȧ/a)^2 for i not equal to j (space-space), with no sums.

**How introduced:** Quoted in Example 15.3 with the derivation from Christoffel symbols deferred to Exercise 15.2.

**Prerequisites:** orthonormal frame and vielbein, Riemann curvature tensor

$$
R^{\hat0}{}_{\hat i\hat0\hat i} = \ddot a/a
$$

$$
R^{\hat i}{}_{\hat j\hat i\hat j} = (\dot a/a)^2
$$

**Where:** GA §15.5 p.164 (pdf 181)

### Ricci scalar of the flat expanding metric

*identity · developed* · also: R = 6(ä/a + ȧ^2/a^2)

Contracting gives R_00 = -3ä/a, R_ii = aä + 2ȧ^2 (coordinate basis) and R = 6(ä/a + ȧ^2/a^2), the same in any frame.

**How introduced:** Worked through in Example 15.3 by contracting in the orthonormal frame and transforming to coordinates with the vielbein.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann components of the flat expanding metric, Ricci scalar

$$
R_{00} = -3\ddot a/a
$$

$$
R_{ii} = a\ddot a + 2\dot a^2
$$

$$
R = 6\left(\ddot a/a + \dot a^2/a^2\right)
$$

**Where:** GA §15.5 p.165 (pdf 182)

### orthonormal frame and vielbein

*technique · revisited* · also: local Lorentz frame, tetrad

A basis e_mu-hat with e_mu-hat . e_nu-hat = eta_mu nu, related to the coordinate basis by vielbein components; here e_0-hat = u and e_i-hat = e_i / a, so (e_0)^0-hat = 1 and (e_i)^i-hat = a.

**How introduced:** Revisited from ch10 as practice: components are raised and lowered with eta in the hatted frame, then transformed back to coordinates.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, comoving observer

$$
(\boldsymbol e_0)^{\hat0}=1,\ (\boldsymbol e_i)^{\hat i}=a
$$

**Where:** GA §15.5 p.165 (pdf 182)

### cosmological constant as vacuum energy

*physical-quantity · core* · also: vacuum energy, Lambda

A term Lambda g_mu nu in the field equation that can be moved into the source as T_vac = -Lambda g / 8pi, i.e. a perfect fluid with uniform energy density rho = Lambda/8pi and pressure p = -rho in every frame.

**How introduced:** After the curvature of the flat expanding metric is computed, the book asks what source could produce it and tries pure vacuum energy (no matter), which turns out to be consistent; at the end of 15.5 it is recast as a fluid by comparison with the vacuum tensor of ch13.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein field equation with cosmological constant, perfect fluid energy-momentum tensor

$$
T^{\mathrm{vac}}_{\mu\nu} = -\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}/8\pi
$$

$$
\rho=\Lambda/8\pi,\ p=-\Lambda/8\pi
$$

**Where:** GA §15.5 p.165 (pdf 182); GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183); GA p.167 (pdf 184)

### exponential expansion

*phenomenon · developed* · also: accelerating expansion, inflationary expansion

Growth of the scale factor as e^{Ht} with constant H, so that ä > 0 and every comoving separation grows by the same factor in equal times.

**How introduced:** Result of solving 3ȧ^2/a^2 = Lambda in Example 15.4; plotted in Fig. 15.4 and pictured as a flaring grid in Fig. 15.5.

**Prerequisites:** de Sitter model, Hubble parameter

$$
a = e^{Ht}
$$

**Where:** GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183)

### negative pressure of vacuum energy

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: p = -rho

Vacuum energy has pressure equal to minus its energy density; in general relativity pressure contributes to gravity through rho + 3p, so a component with p = -rho has net repulsive effect and accelerates expansion.

**How introduced:** Offered at the end of section 15.5 by matching the vacuum tensor to the perfect-fluid form; the book's intuition is that such a medium, squeezed, tends to expand.

**Prerequisites:** cosmological constant as vacuum energy

$$
\ddot a/a = -\tfrac{4\pi}{3}(\rho+3p) \text{ (standard result, not in this chapter)}
$$

**Notes:** The rho + 3p explanation is the standard one and is not given in this chapter; see gaps_and_pitfalls.

**Where:** GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183)

### narrowing of light cones in comoving coordinates

*phenomenon · mention*

Radial null rays in a flat expanding metric satisfy dt/dr = plus or minus a(t), so when drawn in comoving coordinates the light cones close up as a grows: light covers less comoving distance per unit time.

**How introduced:** Margin note to section 15.5, pointing ahead to ch25.

**Prerequisites:** light-cone coordinates, comoving coordinates, scale factor

$$
\mathrm dt/\mathrm dr = \pm a(t)
$$

**Where:** GA §15.5 p.164 (pdf 181)

### constant-curvature spacetime

*definition · introduced* · also: maximally symmetric spacetime, Einstein space

A spacetime whose curvature is fixed entirely by the Ricci scalar, with R_mu nu = (R/4) g_mu nu in four dimensions; de Sitter is an example, and for it Lambda = R/4.

**How introduced:** Asserted in the chapter summary for de Sitter and developed in Exercise 15.3; a 2D analogue in Exercise 15.4 leads to the sine-Gordon equation.

**Prerequisites:** Ricci tensor, de Sitter model

$$
R_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac14 R\,g_{\mu\nu}
$$

$$
\Lambda = R/4
$$

**Notes:** R_mu nu proportional to g is strictly the Einstein-space condition; constant curvature additionally fixes the full Riemann tensor, which holds for de Sitter.

**Where:** GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183); GA p.167 (pdf 184)

### cosmological event horizon

*phenomenon · mention* · also: de Sitter horizon

In an eternally accelerating universe the comoving distance that light emitted at a given time can ever reach is finite, so events beyond a certain distance can never be influenced by, or send signals to, a given observer.

**How introduced:** Exercise 15.7 from the finite limit of the light-travel integral, pointing to ch19.

**Prerequisites:** de Sitter model, narrowing of light cones in comoving coordinates

$$
\ell = H^{-1}\left(e^{-Ht_s}-e^{-Ht_r}\right) \to H^{-1}e^{-Ht_s}
$$

**Where:** GA p.168 (pdf 185)

### decay of peculiar momentum with expansion

*phenomenon · mention* · also: momentum redshift

A freely moving particle in an expanding universe loses momentum relative to comoving observers, with the physical speed-squared (spatial four-velocity) scaling as 1/a^2.

**How introduced:** Exercise 15.6 via radial geodesics in de Sitter.

**Prerequisites:** de Sitter model, geodesic equation

|\vec u|^2 \propto a^{-2}

**Where:** GA p.167 (pdf 184); GA p.168 (pdf 185)

### cosmological constant problem

*phenomenon · mention* · also: vacuum energy catastrophe

Taking zero-point energies of quantum fields at face value gives a vacuum energy density so large that the corresponding curvature radius of the universe would be absurdly tiny, in flagrant conflict with observation.

**How introduced:** Exercise 15.5 asks for an order-of-magnitude estimate using the strong interaction's energy and length scales.

**Prerequisites:** cosmological constant as vacuum energy

**Where:** GA p.167 (pdf 184)

The nonlinear wave equation theta_{,uv} = alpha sin theta; in geometry it expresses constant Gaussian curvature for a surface in Chebyshev-net coordinates.

**How introduced:** Exercise 15.4 derives it from a constant-curvature condition on a 2D metric with angle function theta(u, v).

## Key equations

### (15.1) Einstein field equation with Lambda · central · GA §15.1 p.159 (pdf 176)

$$
R_{\mu\nu} - \frac12 g_{\mu\nu}R = 8\pi T_{\mu\nu} - \Lambda g_{\mu\nu}
$$

The equation to be solved for the entire smoothed matter content of the Universe; geometry on the left, matter and cosmological constant on the right.

**Symbols:** R_mu nu Ricci tensor; R Ricci scalar; g metric; T energy-momentum tensor; Lambda cosmological constant; G = c = 1

### (15.2) Parsec · supporting · GA §15.1 p.159 (pdf 176)

$$
1\,\mathrm{pc} = 3.086\times10^{16}\,\mathrm{m}
$$

Conversion of the astronomer's distance unit; about 3.26 light-years.

**Symbols:** pc parsec

### (15.3) Hubble's law · central · GA §15.2 p.160 (pdf 177)

$$
\vec v = H_0\,\vec r
$$

Recession velocity proportional to distance, with H0 quoted as 70 km/s/Mpc.

**Symbols:** v velocity; r position relative to us; H0 Hubble constant

### (15.4) Uniform scaling · central · GA §15.2 p.160 (pdf 177)

$$
\vec r(t) = a(t)\,\vec r(t_0)
$$

All positions scale with a single time-dependent factor.

**Symbols:** a(t) scale factor with a(t0) = 1

### (15.5) Scaling of separations · derivation-step · GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178)

$$
\vec r_{ij}(t) = a(t)\,\vec r_{ij}(t_0)
$$

Separations between any two objects scale the same way, independent of the choice of origin.

**Symbols:** r_ij = r_i - r_j

### (15.6) Relative velocity in uniform expansion · derivation-step · GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178)

$$
\vec v_{ij}(t) = \dot{\vec r}_{ij}(t) = \dot a(t)\,\vec r_{ij}(t_0) = \frac{\dot a(t)}{a(t)}\,\vec r_{ij}(t)
$$

Every pair recedes at a rate proportional to its current separation, so every observer sees a Hubble law.

**Symbols:** dot = d/dt

### (15.7) Hubble parameter · central · GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178)

$$
H(t) = \frac{\dot a(t)}{a(t)}
$$

Expansion rate as the logarithmic derivative of the scale factor; generally time dependent.

**Symbols:** H Hubble parameter

### (15.8) Slices orthogonal to world lines · central · GA §15.3 p.162 (pdf 179)

$$
\boldsymbol e_0\cdot\boldsymbol e_i = g_{0i} = 0
$$

No time-space cross terms in comoving coordinates, required by isotropy.

**Symbols:** e_0 timelike basis vector; e_i spatial basis vectors

### (15.9) Time basis vector is the fluid velocity · supporting · GA §15.3 p.162 (pdf 179)

$$
\boldsymbol e_0 = \boldsymbol u
$$

The comoving observer's four-velocity coincides with the coordinate time direction.

**Symbols:** u four-velocity of cosmic fluid

### (15.10) Normalization of cosmic time · supporting · GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180)

$$
\boldsymbol e_0\cdot\boldsymbol e_0 = g_{00} = -1
$$

Cosmic time equals the proper time of comoving observers.

### Comoving test via geodesic equation · supporting · GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180)

$$
\ddot x^\mu + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}\dot x^\alpha\dot x^\beta = 0,\quad \Gamma^i_{00}=0 \Rightarrow \ddot x^i = 0 \text{ for a particle at rest}
$$

If Gamma^i_00 vanishes (guaranteed when g_00,i = g_0i,0 = 0) a particle at rest stays at rest, so the coordinates are comoving.

**Symbols:** Gamma connection coefficients; dots derivatives along the world line

### (15.11) General comoving homogeneous-isotropic metric · central · GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180)

$$
\mathrm ds^2 = -\mathrm dt^2 + a(t)^2\,\gamma_{ij}\,\mathrm dx^i\mathrm dx^j
$$

Robertson-Walker form: proper-time cosmic clock plus a fixed spatial geometry scaled by a(t).

**Symbols:** gamma_ij spatial metric depending only on x^i

### (15.12) Empty-universe Einstein equation · supporting · GA §15.4 p.163 (pdf 180)

G_{\mu\nu} = R_{\mu\nu} - \frac12 g_{\mu\nu}R = 0

Field equation for Universe 0 with no matter, energy or Lambda; satisfied whenever R_mu nu = 0.

**Symbols:** G Einstein tensor

\frac{\ddot a}{a} = -R_{\hat0\hat i\hat0\hat i} = -R_{\hat i\hat0\hat i\hat0} = -R^{\hat i}{}_{\hat0\hat i\hat0}
$$

Lowering 0 with eta_00 = -1 gives a sign; swapping within both index pairs gives two cancelling signs; raising a spatial index costs nothing.

**Symbols:** eta Minkowski metric in the local frame

### (15.23) Orthonormal R_00 · derivation-step · GA §15.5 p.165 (pdf 182)

$$
R_{\hat0\hat0} = -3\frac{\ddot a}{a}
$$

Sum of three equal time-space contributions.

### (15.24) Coordinate R_00 · supporting · GA §15.5 p.165 (pdf 182)

$$
R_{00} = (\boldsymbol e_0)^{\hat0}(\boldsymbol e_0)^{\hat0}R_{\hat0\hat0} = -3\frac{\ddot a}{a}
$$

Unchanged by the vielbein because (e_0)^0-hat = 1.

### (15.25) Orthonormal R_ii · derivation-step · GA §15.5 p.165 (pdf 182)

$$
R_{\hat i\hat i} = R^{\hat0}{}_{\hat i\hat0\hat i} + \sum_{j\neq i}R^{\hat j}{}_{\hat i\hat j\hat i} = \frac{\ddot a}{a} + 2\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2
$$

One time-space term plus two space-space terms (j = i contributes zero by antisymmetry).

### (15.26) Coordinate R_ii · supporting · GA §15.5 p.165 (pdf 182)

$$
R_{ii} = (\boldsymbol e_i)^{\hat i}(\boldsymbol e_i)^{\hat i}R_{\hat i\hat i} = a^2R_{\hat i\hat i} = a\ddot a + 2\dot a^2
$$

Transforming back to coordinates multiplies by a^2.

### (15.27) Ricci scalar of flat expanding metric · central · GA §15.5 p.165 (pdf 182)

$$
R = g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu} = 3\frac{\ddot a}{a} + \frac{3}{a^2}\left(2\dot a^2 + a\ddot a\right) = 6\left(\frac{\ddot a}{a} + \frac{\dot a^2}{a^2}\right)
$$

Frame-independent curvature scalar for any spatially flat FRW metric.

### (15.28) Einstein equation with vacuum energy only · central · GA §15.5 p.165 (pdf 182)

$$
R_{\mu\nu} - \frac12 g_{\mu\nu}R = -\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}
$$

Source for Universe 1: no matter, only a cosmological constant.

### (15.29) 00 component substituted · derivation-step · GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183)

$$
-\frac{3\ddot a}{a} - \frac{g_{00}}{2}\times 6\left(\frac{\ddot a}{a} + \frac{\dot a^2}{a^2}\right) = -\Lambda g_{00}
$$

Insert R_00 and R with g_00 = -1; the second-derivative terms cancel.

### (15.30) Friedmann-type equation for pure Lambda · central · GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183)

$$
3\frac{\dot a^2}{a^2} = \Lambda
$$

First-order constraint: the expansion rate squared is fixed by the vacuum energy (special case of the Friedmann equation H^2 = 8 pi rho/3 with rho = Lambda/8pi).

### (15.31) de Sitter scale factor · central · GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183)

$$
a(t) = e^{Ht},\qquad H^2 = \Lambda/3
$$

Exponential, accelerating expansion with constant Hubble parameter; also satisfies the spatial equations.

### (15.32) de Sitter line element (flat slicing) · central · GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183)

$$
\mathrm ds^2 = -\mathrm dt^2 + e^{2Ht}\left[\mathrm dr^2 + r^2\left(\mathrm d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm d\phi^2\right)\right],\quad H = (\Lambda/3)^{1/2}
$$

Universe 1 geometry. The book prints -dt instead of -dt^2 here (typo corrected).

### Vacuum energy as a perfect fluid · central · GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183)

$$
T^{\mathrm{vac}}_{\mu\nu} = -\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi}g_{\mu\nu}\ \Rightarrow\ \rho = \frac{\Lambda}{8\pi},\quad p = -\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi}
$$

Comparing with the diagonal perfect-fluid components (rho, p, p, p) in flat spacetime gives negative pressure equal in size to the energy density.

**Symbols:** rho energy density; p pressure

### (15.33) de Sitter connection coefficients (Exercise 15.2) · supporting · GA p.167 (pdf 184)

$$
\Gamma^0{}_{ij} = a\dot a\,\delta_{ij},\qquad \Gamma^i{}_{0j} = \Gamma^i{}_{j0} = \frac{\dot a}{a}\delta^i{}_j
$$

Only nonzero Christoffel symbols of the flat expanding metric.

### (15.34) Constant-curvature (Einstein space) condition · supporting · GA p.167 (pdf 184)

$$
R_{\mu\nu} = \frac14 R\,g_{\mu\nu}
$$

Ricci proportional to metric in 3+1 dimensions; leads to Lambda = R/4 for de Sitter (Exercise 15.3).

### (15.35) 2D Chebyshev-net metric (Exercise 15.4) · supporting · GA p.167 (pdf 184)

$$
\mathrm ds^2 = \mathrm du^2 + \mathrm dv^2 + 2\,\mathrm du\,\mathrm dv\cos\theta(u,v)
$$

General 2D line element with coordinate lines meeting at angle theta.

**Symbols:** theta(u,v) angle between coordinate lines

### (15.38) Ricci scalar of the 2D metric · derivation-step · GA p.167 (pdf 184)

$$
R = -2\frac{\theta_{,uv}}{\sin\theta}
$$

Curvature from mixed second derivative of the net angle; follows from Riemann (15.36) and Ricci (15.37) components.

### (15.39) Sine-Gordon equation · supporting · GA p.167 (pdf 184)

$$
\theta_{,uv} = \alpha\sin\theta
$$

Constant curvature K turns the curvature formula into sine-Gordon (alpha = -K in the book's sign convention, by our own calculation).

### (15.40) Radial Lagrangian in de Sitter (Exercise 15.6) · supporting · GA p.167 (pdf 184)

$$
L = \left[\left(\frac{\mathrm dt}{\mathrm d\tau}\right)^2 - a(t)^2\left(\frac{\mathrm dr}{\mathrm d\tau}\right)^2\right]^{1/2}
$$

Geodesic Lagrangian for radial motion; the reading copy garbles the second term as dt/dtau, the page shows dr/dtau.

### (15.41) Time equation of radial geodesics · derivation-step · GA p.167 (pdf 184)

$$
\frac{\mathrm d^2t}{\mathrm d\tau^2} + \frac{\dot a}{a}\left[\left(\frac{\mathrm dt}{\mathrm d\tau}\right)^2 - 1\right] = 0
$$

Evolution of the time component of the four-velocity after eliminating dr/dtau.

### (15.42) First integral · supporting · GA p.167 (pdf 184)

$$
a(t)^2\left[\left(\frac{\mathrm dt}{\mathrm d\tau}\right)^2 - 1\right] = \text{const}
$$

Conserved quantity implying the physical momentum per unit mass falls as 1/a.

### (15.43) Comoving reach of a light signal · supporting · GA p.168 (pdf 185)

$$
\ell = \frac1H\left(e^{-Ht_s} - e^{-Ht_r}\right)
$$

Comoving radius reached by light emitted at t_s by time t_r; bounded by e^{-Ht_s}/H as t_r grows, signalling an event horizon.

**Symbols:** t_s send time; t_r receive time

### (15.44) de Sitter metric in Cartesian flat slicing · supporting · GA p.168 (pdf 185)

$$
\mathrm ds^2 = -\mathrm dt^2 + e^{2Ht}\left(\mathrm dx^2+\mathrm dy^2+\mathrm dz^2\right)
$$

Starting point for showing massive-particle geodesics are straight lines (Exercise 15.8).

## Worked examples

### Example 15.1 · intro · GA §15.1 p.159 (pdf 176)

**Problem:** Place astronomical distances on a common ladder to find the scale at which the Universe starts to look homogeneous and isotropic.

**Method:** Compare the AU, light-year and parsec (defined via one-arcsecond parallax); quote nearest-star distances, Milky Way size and star count, and the distance to Andromeda; then jump to 100 Mpc and express it in metres and in AU.

**Key insight:** Up to Mpc scales matter is lumpy; only above roughly 100 Mpc does the cosmological principle hold, which fixes the domain of validity of every model in Part III.

**Result:** 1 pc = 3.086e16 m ~ 3.26 ly; Proxima 1.3 pc, Sirius 2.7 pc; ~1e11 stars in a disc of radius ~25 kpc, thickness ~0.3 kpc; Andromeda ~770 kpc; 100 Mpc ~ 3e24 m, over 13 orders of magnitude beyond 1 AU.

**Concepts:** parsec, homogeneity scale and coarse-graining, cosmological principle

### Example · intro · GA §15.2 p.160 (pdf 177)

**Problem:** Show that Hubble's law does not single out our location.

**Method:** Assume all positions scale as a(t) r(t0); form separation vectors so the origin cancels; differentiate in time.

**Key insight:** Uniform scaling automatically gives a linear velocity-distance law about every point, so the law is compatible with the Copernican principle and defines H = a-dot/a.

**Result:** v_ij = (a-dot/a) r_ij and H(t) = a-dot/a (eqns 15.5-15.7).

**Concepts:** Hubble's law, scale factor, Hubble parameter, Copernican principle

### Example · standard · GA §15.3 p.162 (pdf 179)

**Problem:** Show that a cosmic time defined by a monotonic physical scalar must be shared by all equivalent coordinate systems (margin note 14).

**Method:** Let S (for example the background temperature) decrease monotonically and define t through S; since S is a scalar its value at an event is coordinate independent, so S(t) = S(t') forces t = t'.

**Key insight:** A universal clock can be tied to a physical field that evolves in the same way everywhere on a homogeneous slice.

**Result:** t = t' for all cosmic-standard systems.

**Concepts:** cosmic time

### Example 15.2 · standard · GA §15.3 p.162 (pdf 179)

**Problem:** Turn homogeneity and isotropy into conditions on the metric of comoving coordinates, and give a test that coordinates are comoving.

**Method:** Argue that fluid world lines must be orthogonal to the homogeneous slices, else a preferred direction appears; express this as dot products of basis vectors; then use the geodesic equation to show Gamma^i_00 = 0 keeps resting particles at rest and relate this to vanishing g_00,i and g_0i,0.

**Key insight:** Symmetry arguments fix g_0i = 0 and g_00 = -1 before any field equation is solved, leaving only the spatial part to be determined.

**Result:** g_0i = 0, e_0 = u, g_00 = -1; comoving if g_00,i = g_0i,0 = 0.

**Concepts:** orthogonality of world lines to homogeneous slices, test for comoving coordinates, comoving observer

### Example · intro · GA §15.4 p.163 (pdf 180)

**Problem:** Find a cosmological solution for a universe with no matter, energy or cosmological constant (Universe 0).

**Method:** Reduce G = 0 to R_mu nu = 0 using the trace; test Minkowski in comoving Cartesian coordinates against eqn 15.11 and note its Riemann tensor vanishes; write its geodesics.

**Key insight:** The trivial solution already fits the cosmological framework (a = 1, flat gamma), which calibrates the formalism before adding content.

**Result:** ds^2 = -dt^2 + dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2; geodesics x^mu = b^mu tau + c^mu.

**Concepts:** empty universe (Universe 0), vacuum Einstein equation, Ricci-flat versus Riemann-flat

### Example 15.3 · challenging · GA §15.5 p.164 (pdf 181)

**Problem:** Compute the Ricci tensor and Ricci scalar of the spatially flat expanding metric using the orthonormal frame.

**Method:** Write the metric and inverse; quote orthonormal Riemann components; set up the vielbein e_0-hat = u, e_i-hat = e_i/a; contract to R_00-hat with careful sign tracking via eta (one sign for lowering 0, two cancelling signs from pair swaps); contract R_ii-hat from one time-space and two space-space terms; transform back with vielbein factors 1 and a^2; take the trace with g^mu nu.

**Key insight:** Flat spatial slices do not mean flat spacetime: ä controls time-space curvature and ȧ^2 controls space-space curvature, and the Ricci scalar is frame independent.

**Result:** R_00 = -3ä/a, R_ii = aä + 2ȧ^2, R = 6(ä/a + ȧ^2/a^2).

**Concepts:** Riemann components of the flat expanding metric, Ricci scalar of the flat expanding metric, orthonormal frame and vielbein

### Example 15.4 · standard · GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183)

**Problem:** Determine a(t) when the only source is a cosmological constant.

**Method:** Substitute R_00 and R into the 00 component of G = -Lambda g; the ä terms cancel leaving a first-order ODE for a; integrate to an exponential; state that spatial components also check.

**Key insight:** With pure vacuum energy the expansion rate is fixed, H^2 = Lambda/3, so growth is exponential and accelerating. Our own check of the ii equation: aä + 2ȧ^2 - 3a^2(ä/a + ȧ^2/a^2) = -2aä - ȧ^2 = -3H^2 a^2 = -Lambda a^2, consistent.

**Result:** 3ȧ^2/a^2 = Lambda, a(t) = exp(Ht), H = sqrt(Lambda/3).

**Concepts:** de Sitter model, exponential expansion, cosmological constant as vacuum energy

### Example · intro · GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183)

**Problem:** Interpret the cosmological constant as a material source.

**Method:** Move Lambda g to the right-hand side as T_vac = -Lambda g/8pi (from ch13) and compare with the perfect-fluid components (rho, p, p, p) in flat spacetime.

**Key insight:** Vacuum energy behaves as a fluid with positive density and equal and opposite pressure, the property responsible for accelerated expansion.

**Result:** rho = Lambda/8pi, p = -Lambda/8pi.

**Concepts:** cosmological constant as vacuum energy, negative pressure of vacuum energy


## Analogies and intuitions

### A uniform grey patch on a screen that turns out, under a magnifier, to be a mosaic of tiny dots → homogeneity scale and coarse-graining · strong · GA §15.1 p.160 (pdf 177)

Margin note: the Universe looks smooth only when you step back far enough; close up (below ~100 Mpc) the pixels are galaxies and stars.

**Where it breaks down:** Screen pixels are regularly arranged and identical; galaxies cluster into filaments and voids with structure on many scales, so smoothness emerges statistically rather than exactly.

**App idea:** Zoom slider over a simulated galaxy distribution with a density-variance meter that drops toward zero as the averaging window approaches 100 Mpc.

### Looking up (sky) versus down (ground), or walking from mountains to valleys → cosmological principle · useful · GA §15.1 p.159 (pdf 176)

Deliberate counterexamples showing the principle is obviously false locally, motivating the need to specify a scale.

**Where it breaks down:** Only shows failure; does not quantify at what scale isotropy is restored.

### Stack of curled paper sheets pierced by arrows → foliation of spacetime · strong · GA §15.3 p.162 (pdf 179)

Figs. 15.1 and 15.3 depict slices of constant cosmic time as sheets and fundamental observers as arrows crossing them.

**Where it breaks down:** Paper sheets are two-dimensional and embedded in 3D space; real slices are 3D, and their curvature in the picture has no direct meaning. Spacing between sheets does not show proper time.

**App idea:** Rotatable 3D stack with adjustable spacing tied to a(t), and threads that can be tilted to show what breaks isotropy.

### Grid lines drawn on an expanding block (grid cells same in number, larger in size) → comoving coordinates · useful · GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183)

Fig. 15.5 shows flat slices whose four-by-four grid enlarges with time while labels stay put.

**Where it breaks down:** The picture has an edge and an outside; the model universe has neither, and nothing expands 'into' anything.

**App idea:** Toggle between comoving and physical views of the same grid, with galaxies pinned at nodes.

### A medium with negative pressure which, when squeezed, wants to expand → negative pressure of vacuum energy · weak · GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183)

Closing intuition for why positive Lambda leads to extra expansion.

**Where it breaks down:** Misleading if taken literally: uniform pressure exerts no net force, and a medium with negative pressure behaves like tension (it pulls inward). The real GR reason is that pressure itself gravitates via rho + 3p, which is negative for p = -rho.

**App idea:** Acceleration equation widget: sliders for rho and p (or w = p/rho) showing the sign of ä flip at w = -1/3.

### Einstein's second most famous equation after E = mc^2: the blackboard 'R_ik = 0?' → vacuum Einstein equation · weak · GA §15.4 p.164 (pdf 181)

Light cultural aside anchoring the vacuum equation in memory.

**Where it breaks down:** Purely mnemonic.


## Misconceptions addressed

### Since every galaxy recedes from us, we must be at the centre of the expansion. · GA §15.2 p.160 (pdf 177)

**Correction:** Uniform scaling gives the same linear velocity-distance law about every point; separations r_i - r_j scale with a(t) regardless of origin.

**Why tempting:** Explosion pictures have a centre, and we naturally measure from ourselves.

### The cosmological principle claims the Universe is uniform everywhere, which is plainly false. · GA §15.1 p.159 (pdf 176)

**Correction:** It is a statement about averages over scales above roughly 100 Mpc; below that the Universe is lumpy and the models are not intended to apply.

**Why tempting:** Everyday experience and astronomy up to galaxy clusters are strongly inhomogeneous.

### Cosmic expansion stretches the Solar System, atoms, rulers and people. · GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178)

**Correction:** Bound systems are held by local forces and do not follow the Hubble flow; the expansion description applies only on very large scales.

**Why tempting:** 'Space itself expands' suggests every length grows.

### The CMB rest frame is a privileged frame that violates relativity. · GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178)

**Correction:** It is physically distinguished by the matter content (the radiation is isotropic there), but the laws of physics are identical in all inertial frames.

**Why tempting:** Having a measurable absolute velocity (from the CMB dipole) sounds like an ether frame.

### Hubble's 'constant' is the same at all times. · GA §15.2 p.160 (pdf 177)

**Correction:** H(t) = a-dot/a generally changes in time; it is constant only in special models such as de Sitter.

**Why tempting:** It is called a constant and quoted as a single number.

### If the Ricci tensor vanishes, spacetime is flat. · GA §15.4 p.164 (pdf 181)

**Correction:** Ricci-flat only means volume-changing curvature cancels; tidal curvature (Riemann/Weyl) can remain, as in Schwarzschild. In Universe 0 the Riemann tensor happens to vanish too.

**Why tempting:** R_mu nu = 0 is called the vacuum equation and Minkowski is the first solution met.

### If space is flat, spacetime is flat. · GA §15.5 p.164 (pdf 181)

**Correction:** The de Sitter slices are Euclidean yet spacetime has nonzero curvature (R = 12H^2); time-space curvature comes from ä and space-space curvature from ȧ^2.

**Why tempting:** 'Flat universe' in popular cosmology refers to spatial flatness and is easily confused with no curvature at all.

### An empty universe must be static, and any expanding universe must contain matter. · GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183)

**Correction:** Universe 1 contains no matter at all, only vacuum energy, and yet expands exponentially; even an empty universe can be described with expanding coordinates (Milne).

**Why tempting:** Newtonian intuition ties dynamics to masses.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Negative pressure pushes things apart like a compressed gas. · GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183)

**Correction:** Negative pressure is tension; its effect on expansion is gravitational, through the rho + 3p source term, which is negative when p = -rho.

**Why tempting:** The book's own 'if squeezed, must expand' phrasing and everyday association of pressure with pushing.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Galaxies recede because they move through space away from each other. · GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178)

**Correction:** In comoving coordinates galaxies are at rest; separation grows because the metric's scale factor grows, with peculiar motions as small additions.

**Why tempting:** Velocity language (v = H r) and Doppler intuition.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### An exponentially expanding universe must start in a Big Bang singularity. · GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183)

**Correction:** a = e^{Ht} is positive for all t and never reaches zero; de Sitter has no initial singularity, though the flat coordinates cover only part of the full spacetime.

**Why tempting:** The chapter opening stresses that many models begin in a singularity.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Zooming out from a grain of sand to 100 Mpc · GA §15.1 p.160 (pdf 177)

**Setup:** Start on Earth and repeatedly enlarge the field of view: Solar System, nearest stars, Galaxy, Local Group, then boxes of hundreds of megaparsecs, asking at each step whether the view is homogeneous and isotropic.

**Lesson:** Homogeneity and isotropy are scale-dependent statistical properties that emerge only on the largest scales.

**App idea:** Powers-of-ten zoom with a live histogram of density fluctuations in the current window.

### Every galaxy as its own centre · GA §15.2 p.160 (pdf 177)

**Setup:** Lay out galaxies on a uniformly stretching lattice and compare the Hubble diagram measured from two different galaxies.

**Lesson:** Uniform expansion produces identical linear laws for all observers; no centre is required.

**App idea:** Click any galaxy to recentre; overlay both Hubble diagrams to show they coincide.

### Speeding spacecraft in the cosmic frame · GA §15.3 p.162 (pdf 179)

**Setup:** An observer travels at high speed relative to the local comoving observers and looks at the sky.

**Lesson:** Isotropy holds only for comoving observers; the moving observer sees a dipole, which singles out the cosmic standard frame.

**App idea:** Velocity slider that colours an all-sky map with the relativistic Doppler temperature pattern T/(gamma(1 - beta cos theta)).

### Tilted slices · GA §15.3 p.162 (pdf 179)

**Setup:** Imagine the constant-time slices were not orthogonal to the fluid world lines.

**Lesson:** The fluid would appear to flow in a particular direction within each slice, violating isotropy; hence g_0i = 0.

**App idea:** Tilt control on a 1+1 spacetime diagram showing the resulting in-slice drift velocity.

### Racing a light pulse across de Sitter · GA p.168 (pdf 185)

**Setup:** Emit a light signal at time t_s and follow its comoving distance as the universe expands exponentially (Exercise 15.7).

**Lesson:** The comoving reach saturates at e^{-Ht_s}/H: some regions can never be reached, an event horizon.

**App idea:** Comoving spacetime diagram with narrowing light cones and an asymptote line for the horizon.

- **Cosmic distance ladder** (numerical-estimate, GA §15.1 p.159 (pdf 176)): Distances from the AU up to the homogeneity scale, used to justify coarse-graining. Key numbers: 1 AU = 1.496e11 m; 1 ly ~ 9.46e15 m (>60,000 AU); 1 pc = 3.086e16 m = 3.26 ly; Proxima 1.3 pc; Sirius 2.7 pc; Andromeda ~770 kpc; 100 Mpc ~ 3e24 m.
- **Milky Way structure** (astrophysical-system, GA §15.1 p.160 (pdf 177)): Disc galaxy with typical star spacing of order a parsec. Key numbers: ~1e11 stars; disc radius ~25 kpc; thickness ~0.3 kpc. Our own check for Exercise 15.1: volume pi(25e3 pc)^2(300 pc) ~ 6e11 pc^3, so ~1e11-1e12 stars at one per pc^3.
- **Hubble expansion** (observation, GA §15.2 p.160 (pdf 177)): Redshifts of emission lines show recession velocity proportional to distance. Key numbers: H0 = 70 km/s/Mpc as quoted (book claims ~10% accuracy). Our estimate: Hubble time 1/H0 ~ 14 Gyr; Hubble length c/H0 ~ 4.3 Gpc. Current measurements disagree at the ~8% level (about 67 vs 73 km/s/Mpc, the 'Hubble tension'), post-dating the book.
- **CMB dipole anisotropy** (observation, GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178)): The microwave background is nearly isotropic but shows a dipole due to our motion relative to its rest frame, with an annual modulation from Earth's orbit and a net motion of the Local Group toward Hydra. Key numbers: Not given in the book. Standard values: dipole amplitude ~3.4 mK on T ~ 2.725 K, corresponding to ~370 km/s for the Solar System.
- **Vacuum energy from the strong interaction** (numerical-estimate, GA p.167 (pdf 184)): Exercise 15.5: treat zero-point energy at nuclear scales as real vacuum energy and estimate the resulting curvature radius. Key numbers: Inputs 1 GeV over 1e-15 m. Our estimate: energy density ~1.6e35 J/m^3 (mass density ~2e18 kg/m^3); c/H with H^2 = 8 pi G rho/3 gives a curvature radius of order 10 km, versus an observable universe of order 1e26 m.
- **Dark energy and accelerating expansion (context)** (observation, GA §15.5 p.166 (pdf 183)): The de Sitter model describes the late-time fate of a universe dominated by a positive cosmological constant, consistent with the observed acceleration of cosmic expansion; the book does not discuss the observations here. Key numbers: Our note: observed Lambda-energy fraction ~0.7 of the critical density; not in this chapter.

## Historical notes

- **Nicolaus Copernicus, Arthur Koestler, Owen Gingerich, Hermann Bondi:** Copernicus's heliocentric De Revolutionibus displaced Earth from the centre; Koestler called it an unread flop, Gingerich refuted this by surveying surviving copies; Bondi later named the cosmological Copernican principle. — Roots the modern no-special-place assumption in the history of astronomy. (GA §15.1 p.159 (pdf 176))
- **Edwin Hubble:** Found the linear velocity-distance relation from galaxy redshifts; the book notes his first value of H0 was nearly an order of magnitude above the modern one (historically about 500 km/s/Mpc, some seven times larger; that figure is our addition). — Observational basis for an expanding universe and for introducing a(t). (GA §15.2 p.160 (pdf 177))
- **Hermann Weyl:** Formulated the postulate that fundamental observers form a smooth non-crossing family of timelike geodesics. — Supplies the preferred observers and cosmic time on which cosmological metrics are built. (GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180))
- **Howard P. Robertson, Arthur G. Walker (1930s):** Derived the general form of homogeneous isotropic metrics by symmetry (kinematic) arguments. — The metric form 15.11 used throughout Part III. (GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180))
- **Albert Einstein:** Iconic photograph of Einstein at a blackboard with 'R_ik = 0?' written on it. — Cultural anchor for the vacuum field equation. (GA §15.4 p.164 (pdf 181))
- **Willem de Sitter:** Gave his name to the vacuum-energy-dominated expanding model (Universe 1). — First non-trivial cosmological solution in the book; revisited in ch18-19. (GA §15.5 p.164 (pdf 181))
- **History:** Early relativists expected an unchanging, static universe and were uneasy with time-dependent solutions. — Explains the historical resistance to cosmic evolution and the original motivation for Lambda. (GA p.158 (pdf 175))

## Notation and conventions

- **Units:** G = c = 1 throughout Part III unless stated. (GA §15.1 p.159 (pdf 176))
- **Metric signature:** (-,+,+,+): g_00 = -1 for comoving time, eta_00 = -1 in orthonormal frames. (GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180))
- **Cosmological constant sign:** Field equation written G_mu nu = 8 pi T_mu nu - Lambda g_mu nu, so T_vac = -Lambda g / 8 pi and positive Lambda means positive vacuum energy density. — Equivalent to the common G + Lambda g = 8 pi T. (GA §15.1 p.159 (pdf 176))
- **Scale factor normalization:** a(t0) = 1 at the present time t0; a is dimensionless. (GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178))
- **Physical versus comoving position:** r(t) denotes physical coordinates and r(t0) comoving coordinates; r_ij = r_i - r_j is a separation. (GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178))
- **'Proper motion':** Used for motion relative to the Hubble flow. — Standard cosmology term is peculiar velocity; astronomers reserve proper motion for angular motion on the sky. (GA §15.2 p.161 (pdf 178))
- **Spatial metric:** gamma_ij for the unscaled 3-metric of slices, dsigma^2 for its line element. (GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180))
- **Hatted indices:** Hats mark orthonormal-frame components; vielbein components written (e_mu)^nu-hat and (e_mu-hat)^nu. — Summation temporarily suspended in eqns 15.19-15.26; sums written explicitly. (GA §15.5 p.165 (pdf 182))
- **Riemann index convention:** Ricci formed by contracting first and third indices: R_mu nu = R^alpha_{mu alpha nu}; orthonormal component R^0_{i0i} = ä/a is positive in this convention. — Consistent with ch11. (GA §15.5 p.165 (pdf 182))
- **Dots and commas:** Dot = d/dt (cosmic time) for a(t); comma = partial derivative, e.g. g_00,i and theta_,uv. — In the geodesic equation the dot means d/dtau. (GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180))
- **Numbered model universes:** Universe 0 (empty, Minkowski) and Universe 1 (flat, de Sitter); numbering continues in ch18. (GA §15.4 p.163 (pdf 180))

## Teaching gems

### Derive Hubble's law from uniform scaling of separation vectors so that the origin cancels, instead of quoting it as an observation only. · GA §15.2 p.160 (pdf 177)

**Why it works:** Turns an apparently anthropocentric fact into a one-line consequence of a single function a(t), and naturally defines H = a-dot/a.

**App idea:** Stretchable 2D galaxy lattice: learner picks any galaxy as origin and watches the velocity-distance scatter plot remain a straight line of the same slope.

### Admit that the cosmological principle is false in daily life, then climb a distance ladder until it becomes true. · GA §15.1 p.159 (pdf 176)

**Why it works:** Builds trust by confronting the obvious objection and gives the model a clear domain of validity (~100 Mpc).

**App idea:** Powers-of-ten zoom with a fluctuation meter (rms density contrast in the viewing box) falling toward zero near 100 Mpc.

### Show slices and fundamental observers as a stack of sheets threaded by arrows before writing any metric component. · GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180)

**Why it works:** Gives a concrete geometric picture of cosmic time, homogeneity (within a sheet) and Weyl's postulate (threads never cross).

**App idea:** Rotatable 3D foliation with threads; tilting threads or letting them cross triggers warnings about broken isotropy or Weyl's postulate.

### Use isotropy to kill metric cross terms: a tilt between world lines and slices would reveal a direction. · GA §15.3 p.162 (pdf 179)

**Why it works:** Converts a symmetry principle directly into g_0i = 0, teaching how symmetry constrains the metric before dynamics.

**App idea:** 1+1 diagram with a tilt slider; readouts show the induced in-slice velocity and the nonzero g_0t term.

### Give a quick practical test for comoving coordinates: Gamma^i_00 = 0, guaranteed by g_00,i = g_0i,0 = 0. · GA §15.3 p.163 (pdf 180)

**Why it works:** Links an abstract coordinate property to the behaviour of a dropped test particle via the geodesic equation.

**App idea:** Metric playground: learner edits g_00 and g_0i functions and releases a particle at rest to see whether it drifts.

### Ladder of numbered toy universes starting with an empty one that is secretly Minkowski space. · GA §15.4 p.163 (pdf 180)

**Why it works:** Calibrates the formalism on a known answer, then adds exactly one ingredient (Lambda) to get genuine dynamics.

**App idea:** Universe selector (0, 1, later matter and radiation) that swaps metric, a(t) plot, curvature readouts and source content side by side.

### Flat slices but curved spacetime: ä sets time-space curvature and ȧ^2 sets space-space curvature. · GA §15.5 p.164 (pdf 181)

**Why it works:** Breaks the common identification of spatial flatness with no curvature and gives each Riemann component a physical handle.

**App idea:** Choose any a(t) curve; live panels show R^0_{i0i} = ä/a, R^i_{jij} = (ȧ/a)^2 and R, highlighting that exponential a gives constant values.

### Invert the problem: compute curvature first, then ask which source fits, discovering that pure vacuum energy works. · GA §15.5 p.165 (pdf 182)

**Why it works:** Shows the 'guess metric, compute G, read off T' recipe used throughout Part III and makes Lambda feel discovered rather than imposed.

**App idea:** Guess-the-source game: learner adjusts a(t) family parameters until the implied rho and p satisfy p = -rho, landing on exponential growth.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Eqn 15.32 is printed with -dt instead of -dt^2.** — A careful reader may think the metric is inconsistent or dimensionally wrong. *Suggestion:* Write ds^2 = -dt^2 + e^{2Ht}[...] and note the typo.
- **Margin note 22 writes the fixed-time slice metric as a(t0)(dx^2+dy^2+dz^2); it should be a(t0)^2.** — Confusion about whether lengths scale with a or a^2. *Suggestion:* Emphasize that distances scale with a and the metric with a^2.
- **The negative-pressure explanation ('if squeezed, must expand') is physically loose; uniform pressure exerts no force and negative pressure is tension. The actual reason is that pressure gravitates via rho + 3p in the acceleration equation.** — Learners form a wrong mechanical picture of dark energy and struggle later with the Friedmann acceleration equation. *Suggestion:* Preview ä/a = -(4 pi/3)(rho + 3p) and show that p = -rho gives ä/a = (8 pi/3) rho > 0, i.e. H^2 = Lambda/3 consistent with Example 15.4.
- **The model is named de Sitter before a(t) is determined; only the exponential case is de Sitter, and the flat slicing covers only half of the full de Sitter hyperboloid.** — Learners may think any flat expanding universe is de Sitter, or that flat-slicing de Sitter is the whole spacetime. *Suggestion:* Call the ansatz a spatially flat FRW metric and reserve 'de Sitter' for a = e^{Ht}; mention the hyperboloid picture (ch18).
- **Orthonormal Riemann components (15.19) are quoted without derivation, and index notation in 15.25 (sum j = 1 to 2, 'R^mu_{mu mu mu} = 0') is sloppy.** — Novices cannot reproduce the result and may misread which components vanish. *Suggestion:* Derive Christoffels (Exercise 15.2) first, or give a short coordinate derivation; write the sum as over j != i and note R^i_{iii} = 0 by antisymmetry.
- **Hubble's law is given as an exact vector relation with no mention that linearity holds only at low redshift and that recession 'velocities' can exceed c at large distance.** — Later confusion over superluminal recession and over which distance and velocity are meant. *Suggestion:* State v = H d exactly for proper distance and its rate of change at fixed cosmic time, and explain the observational version is only linear at small z.
- **H0 = 70 km/s/Mpc 'to within about 10%' is dated; measurements now cluster near 67 (CMB) and 73 (distance ladder) km/s/Mpc.** — Outdated sense of the precision and tension in cosmology. *Suggestion:* Present H0 with current values and a brief mention of the Hubble tension.
- **The orthogonality argument (Example 15.2) and the statement that the conditions for comoving coordinates 'are met' are asserted rather than shown; the cosmic-time argument in margin note 14 is terse.** — Learners may not see why g_0i = 0 follows and what exactly is being checked. *Suggestion:* Show explicitly that a nonzero shift vector in a slice defines a direction; compute Gamma^i_00 for eqn 15.11 in one line.
- **Exercise 15.4 calls a positive-definite 2D metric a 'spacetime'.** — Mixes Riemannian and Lorentzian geometry. *Suggestion:* Describe it as a 2D surface (Chebyshev net) and note the Lorentzian analogue separately.
- **Book uses 'proper motion' for peculiar velocity and does not mention the Milne form of the empty universe.** — Terminology clashes with astronomy texts; misses the lesson that an empty universe can look expanding in suitable coordinates. *Suggestion:* Introduce 'peculiar velocity' and show Minkowski in Milne coordinates as an optional aside.
- **The claim that the de Sitter spacetime has constant curvature (summary) is not demonstrated in the text, only in Exercise 15.3.** — Unsupported summary bullet. *Suggestion:* Point out that with a = e^{Ht} all orthonormal Riemann components equal H^2, making constant curvature immediate.
- **When reading vacuum energy as a fluid, the book writes the perfect-fluid components as a four-vector T^mu = (rho, p, p, p) rather than the diagonal rest-frame tensor T^{mu nu} = diag(rho, p, p, p).** — Suggests the energy-momentum tensor has vector components and hides why comparing -Lambda g/8pi with it gives p = -rho (one needs g_00 = -1 flipping the sign of the 00 entry only). *Suggestion:* Write T^{mu nu} = diag(rho,p,p,p) in a local Minkowski frame, lower both indices with eta, and compare entry by entry with -Lambda eta_{mu nu}/8pi.
- **Margin note 10 states the comoving condition as the time derivative of r_ij(t0) vanishing, and margin note 14 contains a slip where S(t) = S'(t') is followed by S(t) = S(t') before concluding t = t'.** — Notation is confusing: r(t0) is a constant by construction, and the scalar-clock argument reads as circular. *Suggestion:* Define comoving labels x^i = r/a and say dx^i/dt = 0 for galaxies on the Hubble flow; restate the clock argument as: S is a scalar, so S'(t') = S(t') at the same event, and monotonic S forces t = t'.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'Every galaxy is running away from us. Does that mean we are at the centre?' Let the learner try, then build the stretching-lattice argument.
- Second opener for stronger learners: 'Why should the weakest force decide the fate of the Universe?' Aim for range plus no cancellation.
- Order: scale ladder and cosmological principle -> uniform scaling and H = a-dot/a -> comoving versus physical -> slices and fundamental observers -> metric form -> Universe 0 -> curvature of flat expanding metric -> Lambda source and exponential solution.
- Check understanding: ask the learner to explain why the Solar System does not expand, and why the CMB dipole does not contradict relativity.
- Check: 'If slices are flat, is spacetime flat?' Expect the answer no, backed by R = 6(ä/a + ȧ^2/a^2), which equals 12H^2 for de Sitter.
- For index-shy learners, skip the orthonormal route and use the Christoffel symbols of eqn 15.33 to get R_00 and R_ii directly; for advanced learners, make them track each eta sign in 15.22.
- When presenting negative pressure, avoid 'it pushes outward'; use the rho + 3p rule and flag that ch17 will derive it.
- Common learner question: 'What is space expanding into?' Answer: nothing; a(t) describes changing distances within spacetime, and Fig. 15.5's outside is an artefact of drawing.
- Common question: 'Is Hubble's constant really constant?' Answer: constant in space at fixed t, generally not in time; exactly constant only in de Sitter.
- Common question: 'Where is the Big Bang in Universe 1?' a = e^{Ht} never vanishes; de Sitter has no initial singularity in these coordinates (though they do not cover all of de Sitter).
- Use quick numbers to anchor scales: 1 pc ~ 3 ly, galaxy spacing ~ Mpc, homogeneity ~ 100 Mpc, Hubble time ~ 14 Gyr (derived from H0 = 70).
- Correct the book's typos when writing equations aloud: -dt^2 in 15.32 and a(t0)^2 for the slice metric.
