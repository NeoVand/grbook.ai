# GA ch36 · Cartan's method

> Builds Élie Cartan's exterior-calculus shortcut to curvature: the connection is packaged as a matrix of 1-forms, the torsion-free and metric-compatibility rules are derived from d^2 = 0 and the product rule, and the chain metric -> orthonormal coframe -> connection 1-forms -> curvature 2-forms -> Riemann components becomes a five-step recipe, run on the flat plane, the 2-sphere, a spatially flat expanding universe and the static spherical star metric.

**Pages:** printed 374–385 · pdf 391–402 · **Difficulty:** math 4/5, conceptual 4/5, novice-friendliness 2/5

The recipe in §36.4 is accessible once exterior derivatives are routine, but §§36.1-36.2 lean hard on Chapter 34's vector-valued forms, the heuristic dP argument and dense index manipulation, which the authors themselves flag as skippable. Novices meet several traps: time-index signs in Lorentzian frames, the |αβ| ordering, and an unstated uniqueness theorem behind 'guesswork'. Printed slips (36.17-36.18 factors, 'curvature 1-forms', de Sitter label, Einstein components in 36.81, index and Λ' errors in 36.85, inverted time vielbein in the Example 36.6 margin) plus OCR damage in the reading copy raise the risk of confusion.

## Role in the book

Part V revisits geometry with forms. Chapter 33 supplied the exterior derivative and wedge product, Chapter 34 rebuilt the connection and let d act on vectors as the covariant derivative, and Chapter 35 recast Riemann curvature as a commutator operator. This chapter converts that abstract machinery into a practical tool: a far less laborious way to obtain Riemann, Ricci and Einstein components than the Christoffel-symbol grind of Chapters 11 and 21. It re-derives the static-star curvature of Chapter 21 and the expanding-universe curvature behind the cosmology part (Chapters 15-18), and it hands later chapters (e.g. Exercise 38.4) orthonormal-frame connection data, before Chapters 37-38 finish the toolkit with Hodge duality, volume forms and Stokes' theorem.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can define connection 1-forms through de_μ = ω^ν_μ ⊗ e_ν and convert them to and from connection coefficients via ω^ν_μ = Γ^ν_{λμ} ω^λ.
- Reader can explain why the second exterior derivative of a vector field need not vanish and show that it produces the curvature 2-form dω + ω∧ω.
- Reader can derive the first structure equation dω^μ = -ω^μ_ν ∧ ω^ν from d^2 P = 0 with dP = e_μ ⊗ ω^μ, and recognize it as the torsion-free condition.
- Reader can derive the compatibility rule dg_{μν} = ω_{μν} + ω_{νμ} from the product rule and deduce antisymmetry of lowered connection forms in an orthonormal frame, including the sign flip for time-space forms in Lorentzian signature.
- Reader can write the orthonormal coframe, dual basis vectors and vielbein components for any diagonal metric by taking square roots of the metric components.
- Reader can execute the five-step Cartan recipe: coframe, exterior derivatives, connection forms by inspection, curvature 2-forms, Riemann components with the |αβ| ordering.
- Reader can confirm with Cartan's method that the polar-coordinate plane is flat despite non-zero connection forms, and that a sphere of radius a has R = 2/a^2.
- Reader can convert orthonormal Riemann components to coordinate components with vielbeins and explain why the Ricci scalar is frame-independent.
- Reader can compute Riemann, Ricci, Ricci scalar and Einstein components for a spatially flat Robertson-Walker metric with arbitrary a(t).
- Reader can obtain the six independent curvature 2-forms of the static spherically symmetric metric in terms of E, Ē, F, F̄ and assemble Einstein components from sectional-curvature sums.
- Reader can spot and correct sign and index slips in Cartan computations by checking every structure equation and known limits.

## Teaching approach

A compact computational chapter that deliberately trades generality for a mechanical procedure. Two derivation sections (flagged as skippable) establish the curvature 2-form from the double exterior derivative of a vector and the two structure rules from the product rule applied to Cartan's point-displacement object dP and to the metric. The results are boxed as 'Ideas 1-3', reframed through the moving-frame picture, reduced to a five-step flowchart, and then exercised on four metrics of rising difficulty, with basis vectors and vielbeins parked in the margin beside each example. The authors openly describe finding connection forms as guesswork and closely follow Misner, Thorne and Wheeler.

**Style:** computational-recipe, derivation-heavy, index-free-abstract, example-driven, spiral-revisit, conversational-informal

**Narrative arc**

1. Motivation: brute-force curvature from Christoffel symbols is laborious; forms in an orthonormal frame (metric η) promise a leaner route; epigraph puns on 'frames'
2. Navigation aid: a margin box tells readers they may skip the derivations of §§36.1-36.2 and jump to the rules box and the recipe
3. Model: let d act on vector fields as the connection and expand the change of basis vectors as de_μ = ω^ν_μ ⊗ e_ν, defining connection 1-forms; link to Γ (36.7) and check in Example 36.1
4. Formalism: differentiate a vector twice; the component pieces die by d^2 = 0 on functions, leaving a term linear in v that defines the curvature 2-form dω + ω∧ω and the compact law d^2 v = R(v)
5. Check: Example 36.2 evaluates the curvature 2-form on two basis vectors and recovers the Riemann-from-Γ formula of Chapter 35, giving the expansion R^μ_ν = R^μ_{ν|αβ|} ω^α∧ω^β
6. New problem: the connection forms must come from the metric without Γ; Cartan's dP = e_μ⊗ω^μ and d^2 P = 0 give the symmetry rule; the product rule on e_μ·e_ν gives the compatibility rule; orthonormality makes ω_{μ̂ν̂} antisymmetric
7. Consolidation: grey box of Ideas 1-3 in the order they are used
8. Geometric reinterpretation: a field of orthonormal frames, Cartan's repère mobile, whose point-to-point rotation is the connection-form field; coframe and vielbein for any diagonal metric
9. Algorithm: five-step recipe with the vertical flowchart Fig. 36.1
10. Applications of increasing difficulty: flat polar plane (control giving zero), 2-sphere (R = 2/a^2 plus coordinate conversion), spatially flat expanding universe (Ricci, R, Einstein), static spherical star metric (six curvature 2-forms, Einstein components via Chapter 21 sum rules)
11. Close: chapter summary restating the three equations; exercises on further surfaces, closed FRW and a time-dependent star, with a pointer to MTW for pulsation and collapse

**Signature moves**

- Locates curvature in the failure of d^2 to vanish on vector fields: because dv is built with a tensor product rather than a wedge, a second exterior derivative survives and is exactly the curvature.
- Uses Cartan's displacement-of-a-point object dP = e_μ ⊗ ω^μ as a direction-less 'proto-vector' (an identity map awaiting a direction), so that d^2 P = 0 delivers the torsion-free structure equation almost for free.
- Derives both structure rules from one idea, the Leibniz product rule applied to a contraction or dot product, instead of from Christoffel formulas.
- Advertises the extraction of connection forms as honest pattern-matching guesswork: compare dω^μ̂ with -ω^μ̂_ν̂ ∧ ω^ν̂ and fill in partners with antisymmetry.
- Packages the logic twice for different readers: a grey 'Ideas 1-3' box for the rules and a six-box flowchart (Fig. 36.1) for the procedure.
- Runs a flat-space control computation before any curved case, so the learner sees that non-zero connection forms can coexist with zero curvature.
- Keeps basis vectors and vielbein components for every example in the margin so frame-to-coordinate conversions are always adjacent to the calculation.
- Imports the Chapter 21 shortcut expressing Einstein components as negative sums of sectional curvatures to skip the Ricci stage in the stellar example.

### §36.1 Connection 1-forms — p.374 (pdf 391)

Exploits the similarity between the exterior derivative and the connection when acting on vectors. The change of basis vectors across space is expanded as de_μ = ω^ν_μ ⊗ e_ν, defining a matrix of connection 1-forms tied to connection coefficients by ω^ν_μ = Γ^ν_{λμ} ω^λ (checked in Example 36.1; a note reads ω^ν_μ(e_α) as a frame rotation rate). Differentiating a vector field twice kills the component terms by d^2 = 0 but leaves a piece proportional to v, which defines the curvature 2-form dω^μ_ν + ω^μ_α ∧ ω^α_ν and the compact statement d^2 v = R(v). Example 36.2 evaluates the curvature 2-form on two basis vectors, recovering the Chapter 35 Riemann formula, whence R^μ_ν = R^μ_{ν|αβ|} ω^α ∧ ω^β.

**Concepts:** exterior covariant derivative of a vector field, vector-valued 1-form, connection 1-forms, connection coefficients, curvature 2-form, Cartan's second structure equation, curvature as the second exterior covariant derivative, curvature operator, Riemann components in terms of connection coefficients, ordered index sum notation |αβ|

**Key moves**
- dv = dv^μ ⊗ e_μ + v^μ de_μ (36.4): d acts on components and on basis vectors separately
- Define connection 1-forms by de_μ = ω^ν_μ ⊗ e_ν (36.5)
- Contract with e_α to identify ω^ν_μ = Γ^ν_{λμ} ω^λ (36.6-36.7), verified in Example 36.1
- Margin note 3: ω^ν_μ(e_α) measures how fast e_μ turns toward e_ν when moving along e_α
- Write dv = e_μ ⊗ (dv^μ + ω^μ_ν v^ν) (36.8) and differentiate again (36.9)
- Margin note 4: d^2 need not vanish on vectors because dv involves a tensor product, not a wedge
- Use d^2 v^μ = 0 to cancel terms, leaving d^2 v = e_μ ⊗ (dω^μ_ν + ω^μ_α ∧ ω^α_ν) v^ν (36.10)
- Define the curvature 2-form (36.12), called the chapter's most important equation, and the operator form d^2 v = R(v) (36.13-36.14)
- Example 36.2: evaluate dω and ω∧ω on (e_α, e_β) to reproduce R^μ_{ναβ} in terms of Γ (36.15-36.19)

### §36.2 Two rules — p.377 (pdf 394)

Addresses how to get connection forms from the metric without Christoffel symbols. Both constraints come from the product rule d(u·v) = du·v + u·dv. Cartan regards a point's displacement dP = e_μ ⊗ ω^μ as a (1,1) object that becomes any chosen vector when fed a direction; since P behaves like a function, d^2 P = 0 yields the symmetry condition dω^μ + ω^μ_ν ∧ ω^ν = 0 (Cartan's first structural equation, equivalent to a torsion-free connection). Differentiating g_{μν} = e_μ · e_ν gives the compatibility condition dg_{μν} = ω_{μν} + ω_{νμ}, equivalent to nabla g = 0. In an orthonormal frame the left side vanishes, so ω_{μ̂ν̂} = -ω_{ν̂μ̂}. A grey box lists Ideas 1-3 (rules, curvature 2-form, Riemann extraction) and promises modest guesswork suffices.

**Concepts:** displacement of a point as identity tensor, Cartan's first structure equation, torsion-free connection, metric compatibility of connection 1-forms, metric compatibility of the connection, nilpotency of the exterior derivative, graded Leibniz rule for the exterior derivative, orthonormal frame

**Key moves**
- Product rule for a dot product (36.22) is the single source of both rules
- dP = e_μ ⊗ ω^μ (36.23), justified because feeding it v returns v (36.24)
- d^2 P = 0 expanded with de_μ = ω^ν_μ ⊗ e_ν gives rule 1 (36.25, 36.29)
- Margin note 8 names rule 1 Cartan's first structural equation and links it to Chapter 34's dω^μ = Γ ω∧ω (36.26-36.28)
- d(e_μ·e_ν) with de = ω⊗e gives dg_{μν} = ω^σ_μ g_{σν} + g_{μσ} ω^σ_ν (36.30-36.31); lower indices for rule 2 (36.32)
- Rule 2 identified with nabla g = 0
- Orthonormal basis: dη = 0 forces antisymmetric lowered connection forms
- Ideas 1-3 boxed (36.33-36.35)

### §36.3 Le repère mobile — p.379 (pdf 396)

Reinterprets 'the' orthonormal frame as a field of frames, one erected and normalized at every event, whose change from point to point is encoded in the connection-form field; this is Cartan's moving frame. Local orthonormal frames use η to raise and lower indices yet generally have non-zero connection coefficients; the default choice is the frame of an observer at rest in the coordinates. For a diagonal metric the coframe is obtained by attaching square roots of the metric components to the coordinate differentials, which also gives the dual basis vectors and the vielbein components. A margin note warns that frame connection coefficients, if extracted, are unnecessary for curvature, do not transform as tensors, and are not symmetric in their lower indices.

**Concepts:** moving frame, orthonormal frame, orthonormal basis 1-forms, vielbein, asymmetry of connection coefficients in a non-coordinate basis, orthonormal frame for a diagonal metric

**Key moves**
- Plural 'frames': an orthonormal frame at each point forms a field of frames
- Connection forms ω^μ_ν(x) encode how neighbouring frames are related
- Margin note 9: French se repérer (to find one's bearings) suits navigating curved spaces
- Margin note 10: this generalizes Chapter 10's normalization of a diagonal metric
- Diagonal metric (36.37) matched to -(ω^t̂)^2 + ... (36.38) gives ω^t̂ = sqrt(-g_tt) dt etc. (36.39)
- Dual basis vectors (36.40) and vielbein components (36.41) read off
- Margin caution on extracting non-tensorial, non-symmetric frame connection coefficients

### §36.4 Example computations — p.380 (pdf 397)

Lays out the five-step recipe (identify the orthonormal coframe; take exterior derivatives and read off connection forms using rule 1 and antisymmetry; differentiate the connection forms; assemble curvature 2-forms; extract Riemann components and convert frames if needed), summarized by Fig. 36.1. The static spherical metric gives four connection forms, six curvature 2-forms with coefficient functions E, Ē, F, F̄, and Einstein components from Chapter 21's sum rules.

## Concepts

### Cartan's method for curvature

*technique · core* · also: Cartan's recipe, turn-the-handle method, tetrad formalism for curvature, moving-frame computation of curvature

A procedure for computing the Riemann tensor from a metric without Christoffel symbols: choose an orthonormal coframe, use the first structure equation and antisymmetry of the lowered connection forms to find the connection 1-forms, build curvature 2-forms from dω + ω∧ω, and read off Riemann components from their expansion in ordered wedge products.

**How introduced:** Motivated by the tedium of Christoffel-based curvature; derived in §§36.1-36.2, boxed as Ideas 1-3, stated as Steps I-V with a flowchart (Fig. 36.1), and exercised on four metrics.

**Prerequisites:** orthonormal basis 1-forms, connection 1-forms, Cartan's first structure equation, Cartan's second structure equation, metric compatibility of connection 1-forms

$$
g \to d\omega^{\hat\mu} \to \omega^{\hat\mu}{}_{\hat\nu} \to d\omega^{\hat\mu}{}_{\hat\nu} \to \mathcal{R}^{\hat\mu}{}_{\hat\nu} \to R^{\hat\mu}{}_{\hat\nu\hat\alpha\hat\beta}
$$

**Notes:** Authors follow MTW (Gravitation, ch.14) closely; the method is most efficient for diagonal metrics.

**Where:** GA p.374 (pdf 391); GA §36.2 p.379 (pdf 396); GA §36.4 p.380 (pdf 397); GA p.384 (pdf 401)

### connection 1-forms

*mathematical-object · core* · also: connection one-forms, ω^ν_μ, matrix of connection forms, spin connection (orthonormal-frame version), curvature 1-forms (book's slip in Idea 1 and summary)

The 1-forms ω^ν_μ defined by de_μ = ω^ν_μ ⊗ e_ν, recording how each basis vector changes as one moves; evaluated on a direction e_α they give ∇_{e_α} e_μ = ω^ν_μ(e_α) e_ν, so ω^ν_μ = Γ^ν_{λμ} ω^λ. In an orthonormal frame the lowered forms ω_{μ̂ν̂} are antisymmetric, acting like an infinitesimal rotation/boost generator for the frame.

**How introduced:** Introduced by expanding the vector-valued 1-form de_μ after letting d act on vectors as the connection (Chapter 34), then connected to Γ by contracting with e_α; margin note 3 interprets ω^ν_μ(e_α) as the rate at which e_μ turns toward e_ν along e_α.

**Prerequisites:** vector-valued 1-form, exterior covariant derivative of a vector field, connection coefficients

$$
d\mathbf{e}_\mu = \omega^\nu{}_\mu \otimes \mathbf{e}_\nu
$$

$$
\omega^\nu{}_\mu = \Gamma^\nu{}_{\lambda\mu}\,\omega^\lambda
$$

**Notes:** Idea 1 heading and the chapter summary call these 'curvature 1-forms'; they are connection forms.

**Where:** GA §36.1 p.375 (pdf 392); GA §36.2 p.378 (pdf 395); GA §36.3 p.379 (pdf 396); GA §36.4 p.381 (pdf 398)

### curvature 2-form

*mathematical-object · core* · also: Cartan curvature 2-form, script R^μ_ν, matrix of curvature 2-forms

The matrix of 2-forms R^μ_ν = dω^μ_ν + ω^μ_α ∧ ω^α_ν built from the connection 1-forms; its expansion R^μ_ν = (1/2) R^μ_{ναβ} ω^α ∧ ω^β = R^μ_{ν|αβ|} ω^α ∧ ω^β carries the Riemann tensor components, one 2-form per pair of frame indices.

**How introduced:** Emerges from taking the exterior derivative of a vector field twice; the term surviving after d^2 v^μ = 0 is linear in v^ν and is named the curvature 2-form, then shown equivalent to Riemann in Example 36.2.

**Prerequisites:** connection 1-forms, wedge product, curvature as the second exterior covariant derivative

$$
\mathcal{R}^\mu{}_\nu = d\omega^\mu{}_\nu + \omega^\mu{}_\alpha\wedge\omega^\alpha{}_\nu
$$

$$
\mathcal{R}^\mu{}_\nu = R^\mu{}_{\nu|\alpha\beta|}\,\omega^\alpha\wedge\omega^\beta
$$

**Notes:** The book describes the two terms as the change of the connection forms and their 'tube-like structure'; a clearer reading is a derivative term plus a commutator-like term, echoing ∂Γ + ΓΓ.

**Where:** GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393); GA §36.1 p.377 (pdf 394); GA §36.2 p.379 (pdf 396); GA p.384 (pdf 401)

### Cartan's second structure equation

*identity · core* · also: curvature structure equation, Idea 2, R = dω + ω∧ω

The relation defining curvature from the connection in the language of forms: R^μ_ν = dω^μ_ν + ω^μ_α ∧ ω^α_ν. It is the forms version of R = ∂Γ - ∂Γ + ΓΓ - ΓΓ.

**How introduced:** Derived from d^2 v in §36.1 (eqn 36.12), boxed as Idea 2 and repeated in the summary; the book does not use the name 'second structure equation' but names the first one in a margin note.

**Prerequisites:** connection 1-forms, curvature 2-form

$$
\mathcal{R}^\mu{}_\nu = d\omega^\mu{}_\nu + \omega^\mu{}_\alpha\wedge\omega^\alpha{}_\nu
$$

**Where:** GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393); GA §36.2 p.379 (pdf 396); GA p.384 (pdf 401)

### curvature as the second exterior covariant derivative

*identity · core* · also: d^2 v = R(v), double exterior derivative of a vector

For a vector field v, applying the exterior covariant derivative twice gives a vector-valued 2-form that depends only on v at the point (no derivatives of v survive) and equals the curvature operator acting on v: d^2 v = e_μ ⊗ R^μ_ν v^ν. Flat connections are exactly those for which d^2 v = 0 for all v.

**How introduced:** The authors ask whether the second covariant derivative defining curvature in Chapter 35 can be re-expressed with d; they differentiate v = v^μ e_μ twice and watch the derivative-of-component terms cancel.

**Prerequisites:** exterior covariant derivative of a vector field, nilpotency of the exterior derivative, graded Leibniz rule for the exterior derivative

$$
d^2\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{e}_\mu\otimes(d\omega^\mu{}_\nu+\omega^\mu{}_\alpha\wedge\omega^\alpha{}_\nu)v^\nu = \mathcal{R}(\mathbf{v})
$$

**Notes:** Margin note 4 stresses that d^2 = 0 holds for forms and functions, not for vectors.

**Where:** GA §36.1 p.375 (pdf 392); GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393)

### exterior covariant derivative of a vector field

*operation · developed* · also: dv, d acting on vectors as nabla, gradient of a vector field

The extension of d to vector fields that coincides with the covariant derivative: dv is a vector-valued 1-form whose value on a direction u is ∇_u v, computed by the product rule dv = dv^μ ⊗ e_μ + v^μ de_μ = e_μ ⊗ (dv^μ + ω^μ_ν v^ν).

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapter 34 (d on functions equals ∇ without its direction; for vectors d ≡ ∇), then applied piecewise to components and basis vectors.

**Prerequisites:** vector-valued 1-form, covariant derivative, exterior derivative

$$
d\mathbf{v} = (dv^\mu)\otimes\mathbf{e}_\mu + v^\mu d\mathbf{e}_\mu = \mathbf{e}_\mu\otimes(dv^\mu+\omega^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu)
$$

**Where:** GA §36.1 p.374 (pdf 391); GA §36.1 p.375 (pdf 392)

### vector-valued 1-form

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: (1,1) object from a vector and a 1-form

An object that eats one vector (a direction) and returns a vector, e.g. de_μ or dv; componentwise a sum of tensor products of basis vectors with 1-forms.

**How introduced:** Carried over from Chapter 34 as the type of dv and de_μ; used to motivate why its second derivative need not vanish.

**Prerequisites:** exterior derivative

**Where:** GA §36.1 p.375 (pdf 392); GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393)

### connection coefficients

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: Christoffel symbols (coordinate basis), Γ^ν_{αμ}, Ricci rotation coefficients (orthonormal frame)

The components Γ^ν_{αμ} of the connection in a basis, defined in GA by ∇_{e_α} e_μ = Γ^ν_{αμ} e_ν (differentiation index first). They are the components of the connection 1-forms: ω^ν_μ = Γ^ν_{λμ} ω^λ.

**How introduced:** Re-derived as components of the connection forms (36.6-36.7); in §36.3 a margin note explains how to extract them in an orthonormal frame if desired, with cautions.

**Prerequisites:** connection 1-forms, affine connection

$$

### displacement of a point as identity tensor

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: dP, Cartan's point derivative, proto-vector, identity (1,1) tensor e_μ ⊗ ω^μ

Cartan's object dP = e_μ ⊗ ω^μ: the infinitesimal displacement of a point regarded as a vector-valued 1-form that has not yet been given a direction; inserting any vector v returns v, so it is the identity map on tangent vectors. Because P is like a function, d^2 P = 0.

**How introduced:** Presented as Cartan's view that the most primitive vector is the movement of a point; its form is fixed by requiring ⟨dP, v⟩ = v, and its second derivative is used to derive the first structure equation.

**Prerequisites:** vector-valued 1-form, orthonormal basis 1-forms, nilpotency of the exterior derivative

$$
d\mathcal{P} = \mathbf{e}_\mu\otimes\omega^\mu
$$

$$
\langle d\mathcal{P},\mathbf{v}\rangle = \mathbf{v}

**Where:** GA §36.2 p.377 (pdf 394); GA §36.2 p.378 (pdf 395)

### Cartan's first structure equation

*identity · core* · also: symmetry condition, rule 1, Idea 1 (first part), Cartan's first structural equation, no-torsion condition in forms

For a torsion-free connection the basis 1-forms satisfy dω^μ = -ω^μ_ν ∧ ω^ν. Given a coframe, this fixes the connection forms (together with metric compatibility) and is the step used to read them off by inspection.

**How introduced:** Derived by applying the product rule to d^2 P = d(e_μ ⊗ ω^μ) = 0; a margin note shows it reproduces Chapter 34's dω^μ = Γ^μ_{βν} ω^ν ∧ ω^β, which requires symmetric Γ in a coordinate basis.

**Prerequisites:** displacement of a point as identity tensor, connection 1-forms, graded Leibniz rule for the exterior derivative, torsion-free connection

$$
d\omega^\mu + \omega^\mu{}_\nu\wedge\omega^\nu = 0
$$

**Notes:** The book's name 'symmetry condition' refers to the symmetry of Γ in its lower indices in a coordinate basis, i.e. zero torsion.

**Where:** GA §36.2 p.378 (pdf 395); GA §36.2 p.379 (pdf 396); GA §36.4 p.380 (pdf 397); GA p.384 (pdf 401)

### torsion-free connection

*definition · mention* · also: symmetric connection, zero torsion

A connection with vanishing torsion, ∇_u v - ∇_v u = [u, v]; in a coordinate basis its coefficients are symmetric in the lower indices. In forms, it is equivalent to the first structure equation.

**How introduced:** Implicit: the chapter's 'symmetry condition' is the forms statement of zero torsion, linked back to Chapter 34.

**Prerequisites:** affine connection

**Notes:** Not named as torsion in this chapter.

**Where:** GA §36.2 p.378 (pdf 395)

### metric compatibility of connection 1-forms

*identity · core* · also: compatibility condition, rule 2, antisymmetry of ω_{μ̂ν̂}, Idea 1 (second part)

Compatibility of the connection with the metric expressed in forms: dg_{μν} = ω_{μν} + ω_{νμ}, with ω_{μν} = g_{μσ} ω^σ_ν. In an orthonormal frame g_{μ̂ν̂} = η_{μ̂ν̂} is constant, so ω_{μ̂ν̂} = -ω_{ν̂μ̂}: purely spatial forms are antisymmetric in index position ω^î_ĵ = -ω^ĵ_î, while time-space forms satisfy ω^t̂_î = +ω^î_t̂.

**How introduced:** Derived by applying the product rule to g_{μν} = e_μ · e_ν and substituting de = ω ⊗ e; identified with nabla g = 0 of Chapter 34; in the examples it supplies the partner of each connection form 'for free'.

**Prerequisites:** connection 1-forms, metric compatibility of the connection, orthonormal frame

$$
dg_{\mu\nu} = \omega_{\mu\nu}+\omega_{\nu\mu}
$$

$$
\omega_{\hat\mu\hat\nu} = -\omega_{\hat\nu\hat\mu}
$$

**Notes:** Exercise 36.1 asks for the two index-position consequences.

**Where:** GA §36.2 p.378 (pdf 395); GA §36.2 p.379 (pdf 396); GA §36.4 p.380 (pdf 397); GA §36.4 p.382 (pdf 399); GA p.385 (pdf 402)

### Levi-Civita connection

*theorem · mention* · also: uniqueness of the metric-compatible torsion-free connection

On a (pseudo-)Riemannian manifold there is exactly one connection that is both torsion-free and metric-compatible. In Cartan language: given an orthonormal coframe, there is a unique antisymmetric matrix of 1-forms ω_{μ̂ν̂} solving dω^μ̂ = -ω^μ̂_ν̂ ∧ ω^ν̂, which is why a guessed solution satisfying both rules is the correct one.

**How introduced:** Not stated in the chapter; it is the unspoken justification for the authors' 'guesswork' method of finding connection forms.

**Prerequisites:** torsion-free connection, metric compatibility of the connection

**Notes:** Dossier addition to support tutoring; the book appeals to guesswork without invoking uniqueness.

**Where:** GA §36.2 p.377 (pdf 394); GA §36.2 p.379 (pdf 396)

### graded Leibniz rule for the exterior derivative

*identity · developed* · also: product rule for d, Leibniz rule

d(α ∧ β) = dα ∧ β + (-1)^p α ∧ dβ for a p-form α, extended to products of vectors and forms, e.g. d(e_μ ⊗ α) = de_μ ∧ α + e_μ ⊗ dα, and to dot products d(u·v) = du·v + u·dv.

**How introduced:** The engine of every derivation in the chapter: the double derivative of v, d^2 P, and d(e_μ · e_ν).

**Prerequisites:** exterior derivative, wedge product

$$
d(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}) = (d\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}\cdot(d\mathbf{v})
$$

**Notes:** The sign in -ω^μ_ν ∧ dv^ν in eqn 36.9 comes from moving a 1-form past d.

**Where:** GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393); GA §36.2 p.377 (pdf 394); GA §36.2 p.378 (pdf 395)

### wedge product

*operation · revisited* · also: exterior product, ∧

The antisymmetric product of forms; for 1-forms α ∧ β = -β ∧ α, so α ∧ α = 0.

**How introduced:** Relied on throughout for building 2-forms and for spotting vanishing terms (e.g. dθ ∧ dθ = 0).

**Prerequisites:** p-form

### orthonormal frame

*definition · revisited* · also: local orthonormal frame, orthonormal tetrad, vierbein frame, hatted basis

A basis of vectors at each point that is orthonormal with respect to the metric, g(e_μ̂, e_ν̂) = η_μ̂ν̂, so that indices are raised and lowered with η; it is generally a non-coordinate basis and its connection coefficients need not vanish.

**Notes:** Should not be confused with a local inertial (freely falling) frame where connection coefficients vanish at a point.

**Symbols:** ω^ν_μ connection 1-forms

### (36.6) · supporting · GA §36.1 p.375 (pdf 392)

$$
\Gamma^\nu{}_{\alpha\mu}\mathbf{e}_\nu = \nabla_\alpha\mathbf{e}_\mu = \langle\nabla\mathbf{e}_\mu,\mathbf{e}_\alpha\rangle \equiv \langle d\mathbf{e}_\mu,\mathbf{e}_\alpha\rangle = \langle\omega^\nu{}_\mu\otimes\mathbf{e}_\nu,\mathbf{e}_\alpha\rangle
$$

Feeding a direction into de_μ reproduces the covariant derivative of the basis vector, linking forms to connection coefficients.

**Symbols:** Γ connection coefficients (derivative index first)

### (36.7) Connection forms in terms of Γ · central · GA §36.1 p.375 (pdf 392)

$$
\omega^\nu{}_\mu = \Gamma^\nu{}_{\lambda\mu}\,\omega^\lambda
$$

Connection coefficients are the components of the connection 1-forms in the basis ω^λ.

### (36.8) · derivation-step · GA §36.1 p.375 (pdf 392)

$$
d\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{e}_\mu\otimes(dv^\mu+\omega^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu)
$$

Covariant derivative of a vector as a vector-valued 1-form; the bracket is the forms version of v^μ_{;α}.

### (36.9) · derivation-step · GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393)

$$
d^2\mathbf{v} = d\mathbf{e}_\mu\wedge(dv^\mu+\omega^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu)+\mathbf{e}_\mu\otimes(d^2v^\mu+d\omega^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu-\omega^\mu{}_\nu\wedge dv^\nu) = \mathbf{e}_\mu\otimes(\omega^\mu{}_\nu\wedge dv^\nu+\omega^\mu{}_\alpha\wedge\omega^\alpha{}_\nu v^\nu+d^2v^\mu+d\omega^\mu{}_\nu v^\nu-\omega^\mu{}_\nu\wedge dv^\nu)
$$

Second exterior derivative of a vector: the dv terms cancel pairwise.

### (36.10)-(36.11) · derivation-step · GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393)

$$
d^2\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{e}_\mu\otimes(d\omega^\mu{}_\nu+\omega^\mu{}_\alpha\wedge\omega^\alpha{}_\nu)\,v^\nu = (\mathbf{e}_\mu\otimes\mathcal{R}^\mu{}_\nu)\,v^\nu
$$

After d^2 v^μ = 0 only an undifferentiated v survives, so the result is a tensorial (curvature) operation on v.

### (36.12) Curvature 2-form (Cartan's second structure equation) · central · GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393)

$$
\mathcal{R}^\mu{}_\nu = d\omega^\mu{}_\nu+\omega^\mu{}_\alpha\wedge\omega^\alpha{}_\nu
$$

Curvature is the exterior derivative of the connection forms plus their wedge product; the chapter's most important equation.

**Symbols:** script R^μ_ν curvature 2-form

### (36.13) Curvature 2-form operator · supporting · GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393)

$$
\mathcal{R}(\ ) = \omega^\nu(\ )\otimes\mathbf{e}_\mu\otimes\mathcal{R}^\mu{}_\nu
$$

Packages the curvature 2-forms into a machine that accepts a vector.

### (36.14) · central · GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393)

$$
d^2\mathbf{v} = \mathcal{R}(\mathbf{v})
$$

Memorable form: differentiating a vector twice equals the curvature acting on it.

### (36.15)-(36.16) · derivation-step · GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393)

$$
\omega^\mu{}_\nu = \Gamma^\mu{}_{\sigma\nu}\omega^\sigma,\qquad d\omega^\mu{}_\nu = \frac{\partial\Gamma^\mu{}_{\sigma\nu}}{\partial x^\lambda}\,\omega^\lambda\wedge\omega^\sigma
$$

Exterior derivative of a connection form; dropping the Γ dω^σ term is valid only in a coordinate basis where ω^σ = dx^σ.

### (36.17) · derivation-step · GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393)

$$
d\omega^\mu{}_\nu(\mathbf{e}_\alpha,\mathbf{e}_\beta) = \frac12\left(\frac{\partial\Gamma^\mu{}_{\beta\nu}}{\partial x^\alpha}-\frac{\partial\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\nu}}{\partial x^\beta}\right)
$$

Derivative part of the Riemann formula. Printed last line omits the parentheses so the 1/2 appears to multiply only the first term; the factor 1/2 itself reflects a wedge normalization different from eqn 34.38.

### (36.18) · derivation-step · GA §36.1 p.377 (pdf 394)

$$
\omega^\mu{}_\sigma\wedge\omega^\sigma{}_\nu(\mathbf{e}_\alpha,\mathbf{e}_\beta) = \frac12\left(\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\sigma}\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\beta\nu}-\Gamma^\mu{}_{\beta\sigma}\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\alpha\nu}\right)
$$

Quadratic part of the Riemann formula. The printed final line drops the factor 1/2 present in the preceding lines; it is restored in 36.19.

### (36.19) · supporting · GA §36.1 p.377 (pdf 394)

$$
\mathcal{R}^\mu{}_\nu(\mathbf{e}_\alpha,\mathbf{e}_\beta) = \frac12\left(\partial_\alpha\Gamma^\mu{}_{\beta\nu}-\partial_\beta\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\nu}+\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\sigma}\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\beta\nu}-\Gamma^\mu{}_{\beta\sigma}\Gamma^\sigma{}_{\alpha\nu}\right) = \frac12 R^\mu{}_{\nu\alpha\beta}
$$

Evaluating the curvature 2-form on two basis vectors recovers the Riemann components (the 1/2 follows the example's wedge normalization; with eqn 34.38's convention it is absent).

### (36.20) Riemann components from the curvature 2-form · central · GA §36.1 p.377 (pdf 394)

$$
\mathcal{R}^\mu{}_\nu = R^\mu{}_{\nu|\alpha\beta|}\,\omega^\alpha\wedge\omega^\beta
$$

Riemann components are the coefficients of the curvature 2-form in ordered wedge products; this is the extraction rule (Idea 3).

**Symbols:** |αβ| sum over one ordering only

### (36.21) Bivector form of the curvature operator · supporting · GA §36.1 p.377 (pdf 394)

$$
\mathcal{R}(\ ) = \frac14(\mathbf{e}_\mu\wedge\mathbf{e}_\nu)\,R^{\mu\nu}{}_{\alpha\beta}\,(\omega^\alpha\wedge\omega^\beta)
$$

Curvature as a bivector-valued 2-form (MTW style), given in margin note 6.

### (36.22) Product rule for a dot product · supporting · GA §36.2 p.377 (pdf 394)

$$
d(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}) = (d\mathbf{u})\cdot\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{u}\cdot(d\mathbf{v})

Single source of both structure rules.

### (36.23)-(36.24) Cartan's displacement of a point · supporting · GA §36.2 p.377 (pdf 394)

$$
d\mathcal{P} = \mathbf{e}_\mu\otimes\omega^\mu,\qquad \mathbf{e}_\mu\otimes\omega^\mu(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{e}_\mu v^\mu = \mathbf{v}
$$

The displacement of a point is the identity map on vectors: it returns whatever direction is inserted.

**Symbols:** P a point of the manifold

### (36.25) · derivation-step · GA §36.2 p.378 (pdf 395)

$$
0 = d^2\mathcal{P} = d\mathbf{e}_\mu\wedge\omega^\mu+\mathbf{e}_\mu\otimes d\omega^\mu = \mathbf{e}_\mu\otimes(\omega^\mu{}_\nu\wedge\omega^\nu+d\omega^\mu)
$$

Because P behaves like a function its second derivative vanishes, constraining the connection forms.

### (36.29) Symmetry condition (Cartan's first structure equation, rule 1) · central · GA §36.2 p.378 (pdf 395)

$$
d\omega^\mu+\omega^\mu{}_\nu\wedge\omega^\nu = 0
$$

The exterior derivatives of the basis 1-forms determine the connection forms; equivalent to zero torsion.

### (36.26)-(36.28) · supporting · GA §36.2 p.378 (pdf 395)

$$
d\omega^\mu = \Gamma^\mu{}_{\beta\nu}\,\omega^\nu\wedge\omega^\beta,\quad d\mathbf{e}_\mu = \Gamma^\nu{}_{\beta\mu}\,\omega^\beta\otimes\mathbf{e}_\nu,\quad \nabla\omega^\mu = -\Gamma^\mu{}_{\beta\nu}\,\omega^\nu\otimes\omega^\beta

Margin note 8 comparison: the first structure equation in Γ form (as claimed in Chapter 34) versus the derivatives of basis vectors and the covariant derivative of basis 1-forms.

### (36.30)-(36.31) · derivation-step · GA §36.2 p.378 (pdf 395)

$$
dg_{\mu\nu} = d\mathbf{e}_\mu\cdot\mathbf{e}_\nu+\mathbf{e}_\mu\cdot d\mathbf{e}_\nu = \omega^\sigma{}_\mu g_{\sigma\nu}+g_{\mu\sigma}\omega^\sigma{}_\nu

Product rule applied to the metric components as dot products of basis vectors.

### (36.32) Compatibility condition (rule 2) · central · GA §36.2 p.378 (pdf 395)

$$
dg_{\mu\nu} = \omega_{\mu\nu}+\omega_{\nu\mu}
$$

Metric compatibility in forms; equivalent to ∇g = 0.

**Symbols:** ω_{μν} = g_{μσ} ω^σ_ν

### (36.33) Ideas 1: Cartan's two rules in an orthonormal frame · central · GA §36.2 p.379 (pdf 396)

### (36.34)-(36.35) Ideas 2 and 3 · supporting · GA §36.2 p.379 (pdf 396)

## Worked examples

### Example 36.1 · intro · GA §36.1 p.375 (pdf 392)

**Problem:** Confirm that writing connection forms as ω^ν_μ = Γ^ν_{λμ} ω^λ is consistent with the definition ∇_α e_μ = Γ^ν_{αμ} e_ν.

**Method:** Substitute the proposed expansion into ⟨ω^ν_μ ⊗ e_ν, e_α⟩, insert e_α into the 1-form slot, and use the duality ω^λ(e_α) = δ^λ_α.

**Key insight:** Connection coefficients are simply the components of the connection 1-forms in the dual basis; nothing new is hidden in the forms.

**Result:** The contraction returns Γ^ν_{αμ} e_ν, matching the starting expression.

**Concepts:** connection 1-forms, connection coefficients

### Example 36.2 · standard · GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393)

**Problem:** Show that the curvature 2-form dω + ω∧ω carries the same information as the Riemann tensor defined earlier via connection coefficients.

**Method:** Write ω^μ_ν = Γ^μ_{σν} ω^σ, take d (in a coordinate basis so only Γ is differentiated), expand wedges as antisymmetrized tensor products, and feed in e_α, e_β using Kronecker deltas; repeat for the ω∧ω term and add.

**Key insight:** The derivative term produces the ∂Γ - ∂Γ part and the wedge term the ΓΓ - ΓΓ commutator part of the Riemann formula, so expanding the 2-form in ordered wedge products reads off R^μ_{ναβ}.

**Result:** R^μ_ν(e_α, e_β) = (1/2) R^μ_{ναβ} with the example's wedge normalization, hence R^μ_ν = R^μ_{ν|αβ|} ω^α ∧ ω^β (36.20). Printed 36.17 lacks parentheses and 36.18 drops a 1/2.

**Concepts:** curvature 2-form, Riemann components in terms of connection coefficients, wedge product, ordered index sum notation |αβ|

### Example · standard · GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393)

**Problem:** Derive a forms expression for curvature by differentiating a vector field v = v^μ e_μ twice (eqns 36.8-36.14).

**Method:** Use dv = e_μ ⊗ (dv^μ + ω^μ_ν v^ν), apply the Leibniz rule again with de_μ = ω^ν_μ ⊗ e_ν, note the cancellation of the ω∧dv terms and d^2 v^μ = 0.

**Key insight:** Everything involving derivatives of the components cancels, so d^2 v depends on v only pointwise, the defining property of a tensor, and that tensor is the curvature.

**Result:** d^2 v = e_μ ⊗ (dω^μ_ν + ω^μ_α ∧ ω^α_ν) v^ν = R(v).

**Concepts:** curvature as the second exterior covariant derivative, curvature 2-form, graded Leibniz rule for the exterior derivative, nilpotency of the exterior derivative

### Example · standard · GA §36.2 p.378 (pdf 395)

**Problem:** Derive the two rules that fix the connection forms from the metric (eqns 36.23-36.32).

**Method:** For rule 1, write dP = e_μ ⊗ ω^μ, differentiate with the product rule, substitute de_μ, and set d^2 P = 0. For rule 2, apply the product rule to g_{μν} = e_μ · e_ν, substitute de = ω ⊗ e, and lower indices.

**Key insight:** Both constraints are consequences of the Leibniz rule; one encodes zero torsion and the other metric compatibility, and in an orthonormal frame the second collapses to antisymmetry.

**Result:** dω^μ + ω^μ_ν ∧ ω^ν = 0 and dg_{μν} = ω_{μν} + ω_{νμ}, giving ω_{μ̂ν̂} = -ω_{ν̂μ̂} in orthonormal frames.

**Concepts:** displacement of a point as identity tensor, Cartan's first structure equation, metric compatibility of connection 1-forms

### Example · intro · GA §36.3 p.380 (pdf 397)

**Problem:** Find the orthonormal coframe, dual basis vectors and vielbeins for a general diagonal (3+1) metric in spherical polars (eqns 36.37-36.41).

**Method:** Match g_tt dt^2 + g_rr dr^2 + ... to -(ω^t̂)^2 + (ω^r̂)^2 + ... and take square roots; invert for the vectors.

**Key insight:** For diagonal metrics Step I is immediate and the vielbein matrices are diagonal, so conversions between frames are single multiplicative factors.

**Result:** ω^t̂ = sqrt(-g_tt) dt, ω^r̂ = sqrt(g_rr) dr, ω^θ̂ = sqrt(g_θθ) dθ, ω^φ̂ = sqrt(g_φφ) dφ, with reciprocal factors on the basis vectors.

**Concepts:** orthonormal frame for a diagonal metric, vielbein, orthonormal basis 1-forms

### Example 36.3 · intro · GA §36.4 p.381 (pdf 398)

**Problem:** Use Cartan's method to verify that the plane, written in polar coordinates ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2 dθ^2, has zero curvature.

**Method:** Coframe ω^r̂ = dr, ω^θ̂ = r dθ; compute dω^r̂ = 0 and dω^θ̂ = dr ∧ dθ; match to -ω^θ̂_r̂ ∧ ω^r̂ to find ω^θ̂_r̂ = dθ; differentiate and check wedge products.

**Key insight:** A non-zero connection form (the polar frame rotates as θ changes) does not imply curvature; curvature needs dω + ω∧ω ≠ 0.

**Result:** dω^θ̂_r̂ = d(dθ) = 0 and the ω∧ω terms vanish, so every curvature 2-form is zero.

**Concepts:** Cartan's method for curvature, connection 1-forms, nilpotency of the exterior derivative

### Example 36.4 · standard · GA §36.4 p.381 (pdf 398)

**Problem:** Compute the Riemann, Ricci and scalar curvature of a sphere of radius a, ds^2 = a^2 dθ^2 + a^2 sin^2 θ dφ^2, in orthonormal and coordinate frames.

**Method:** Coframe ω^θ̂ = a dθ, ω^φ̂ = a sin θ dφ; dω^φ̂ gives ω^φ̂_θ̂ = cos θ dφ, antisymmetry gives ω^θ̂_φ̂ = -cos θ dφ; differentiate to get R^θ̂_φ̂; read off with |αβ| ordering; use Riemann symmetries for the partner component; convert with margin vielbeins; contract.

**Key insight:** In two dimensions one curvature 2-form carries all the curvature, and its coefficient is the Gaussian curvature; coordinate components look different (sin^2 θ, 1) but the scalar R agrees.

**Result:** R^θ̂_φ̂ = (1/a^2) ω^θ̂ ∧ ω^φ̂; R^θ̂_{φ̂θ̂φ̂} = R^φ̂_{θ̂φ̂θ̂} = 1/a^2; R^θ_{φθφ} = sin^2 θ, R^φ_{θφθ} = 1; R_{θ̂θ̂} = R_{φ̂φ̂} = 1/a^2; R = 2/a^2. Side result: Γ^θ̂_{φ̂φ̂} = -cot θ / a, Γ^φ̂_{φ̂θ̂} = cot θ / a.

**Concepts:** curvature of the 2-sphere, vielbein transformation of tensor components, symmetries of the Riemann tensor, Ricci scalar

### Example 36.5 · standard · GA §36.4 p.382 (pdf 399)

**Problem:** Find the curvature of the spatially flat expanding universe ds^2 = -dt^2 + a(t)^2(dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2): Riemann, Ricci, Ricci scalar and Einstein components in the orthonormal frame.

**Method:** Coframe ω^t̂ = dt, ω^k̂ = a dx^k; dω^t̂ = 0 suggests ω^t̂_k̂ ∝ ω^k̂; dω^k̂ = (ȧ/a) ω^t̂ ∧ ω^k̂ fixes ω^k̂_t̂ = (ȧ/a) ω^k̂ and ω^k̂_l̂ = 0; use the time-index sign rule; build R^t̂_k̂ from dω and R^k̂_l̂ from ω∧ω; sum components for Ricci with summation suspended; form G.

**Key insight:** Even with flat spatial slices the spacetime is curved: time-space curvature comes from the acceleration ä/a and space-space curvature arises solely from the product of boost connection forms, (ȧ/a)^2.

**Result:** R^t̂_{k̂t̂k̂} = ä/a, R^k̂_{l̂k̂l̂} = (ȧ/a)^2; R_t̂t̂ = -3ä/a, R_îî = ä/a + 2(ȧ/a)^2, R = 6(ä/a + ȧ^2/a^2); correctly G_t̂t̂ = 3(ȧ/a)^2 and G_îî = -2ä/a - (ȧ/a)^2 (printed 36.81 contains typos). For a = e^{Ht} (true de Sitter) every sectional curvature equals H^2.

**Concepts:** spatially flat Robertson-Walker spacetime, Lorentzian sign rule for hatted time indices, Ricci tensor, Einstein tensor

### Example 36.6 · challenging · GA §36.4 p.383 (pdf 400)

**Problem:** Compute the curvature 2-forms, Riemann components and Einstein tensor for the general static spherically symmetric metric with Φ(r) and Λ(r).

**Method:** Coframe e^Φ dt, e^Λ dr, r dθ, r sin θ dφ; state the four connection forms; differentiate them; assemble six curvature 2-forms keeping only surviving dω and ω∧ω terms; name coefficient functions; extract Riemann components respecting |αβ| ordering; get Einstein components from Chapter 21's sums of sectional curvatures.

**Key insight:** Spherical symmetry leaves only four curvature functions (radial tidal E, transverse tidal Ē, angular F, radial-angular F̄), and Einstein components are simple sums of them, which is much lighter than the Christoffel route.

**Result:** E = -e^{-2Λ}(Φ'' + Φ'^2 - Φ'Λ'), Ē = -(1/r)e^{-2Λ}Φ', F = (1 - e^{-2Λ})/r^2, F̄ = (1/r)e^{-2Λ}Λ'; G^t̂_t̂ = -(F + 2F̄), G^r̂_r̂ = -(F + 2Ē), G^θ̂_θ̂ = G^φ̂_φ̂ = -(E + Ē + F̄). Printed 36.85 and the margin time vielbein contain errors (see gaps).

**Concepts:** general static spherically symmetric metric, Riemann tensor components for static spherical symmetry, Einstein tensor from sectional curvatures, curvature 2-form


## Analogies and intuitions

### Connection form as a frame's rotation rate: ω^ν_μ(e_α) says how quickly basis vector e_μ tips toward e_ν as you step in direction e_α (margin note 3). → connection 1-forms · strong · GA §36.1 p.375 (pdf 392)

Gives the abstract matrix of 1-forms a kinematic meaning, akin to an angular-velocity matrix for a rigid body whose 'time' is displacement; antisymmetry in orthonormal frames then matches the antisymmetry of an infinitesimal rotation.

**Where it breaks down:** In Lorentzian frames the time-space forms are boosts (hyperbolic rotations), and the rate depends on direction, so it is a rotation rate per unit displacement, not a single angular velocity.

**App idea:** Walk a tripod of frame arrows along a latitude on a sphere; a dial shows ω^φ̂_θ̂(e_φ̂) = cot θ / a as the rate the arrows turn relative to parallel-transported ones.

### Le repère mobile: a traveller carries a set of orthonormal axes and watches them appear to change as position changes; the French se repérer (finding one's bearings) fits navigation in curved space. → moving frame · strong · GA §36.3 p.379 (pdf 396)

Recasts 'the orthonormal frame' as a field of frames whose point-to-point relation is the connection.

**Where it breaks down:** The apparent rotation depends on the arbitrary choice of frame field; only the curvature built from it is frame-independent (the polar plane rotates frames but has no curvature).

**App idea:** Field-of-frames viewer: learner paints a frame field on a surface (e.g. aligned with coordinates or twisted) and sees connection forms change while the computed curvature stays the same.

### A point's displacement dP is a 'proto-vector' that points nowhere until a direction is inserted, then becomes exactly that vector. → displacement of a point as identity tensor · useful · GA §36.2 p.377 (pdf 394)

Motivates writing dP = e_μ ⊗ ω^μ (the identity map) and then applying d^2 P = 0 to get the first structure equation.

**Where it breaks down:** P is not literally a function with values in a vector space on a curved manifold; the argument is heuristic and relies on the formalism of vector-valued forms.

**App idea:** Show dP as a small 'loaded arrow' icon at a point; dragging a direction vector into its slot produces an arrow identical to the input, making the identity map tangible.

### Curvature has two ingredients: how the connection forms change from place to place (dω) and how they combine with each other (ω∧ω), which the authors call their tube-like structure. → curvature 2-form · weak · GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393)

Explains why neither term alone suffices, previewing the flat-FRW case where space-space curvature is entirely from ω∧ω and the polar plane where both vanish despite non-zero ω.

**Where it breaks down:** 'Tube-like structure' is vague; a sharper picture is the non-commutation of small rotations/boosts (a matrix commutator), exactly as in gauge-field strength F = dA + A∧A.

**App idea:** Split-bar display of the two contributions to each curvature 2-form coefficient as a parameter (a, ȧ, ä, r) changes.

### Cartan's method as a crank-handle machine: once the rules are in place you feed in the metric and turn the handle until Riemann components come out. → Cartan's method for curvature · useful · GA p.374 (pdf 391)

Reassures readers that the heavy derivation can be skipped and the recipe used mechanically.

**Where it breaks down:** Step II still requires judgment (pattern matching) and consistency checks; the machine can jam for non-diagonal metrics.

### The epigraph: Davy's praise of Paris galleries as the finest collection of frames he had seen, punning on reference frames. → moving frame · weak · GA p.374 (pdf 391)

Light-hearted hook for a chapter built on fields of frames.

**Where it breaks down:** Purely a pun; no conceptual content.

### The exterior derivative and the covariant derivative are the same operator when acting on vectors, so the forms rules about d can be borrowed for connections. → exterior covariant derivative of a vector field · useful · GA §36.1 p.374 (pdf 391)

Lets the authors differentiate vectors twice with the familiar Leibniz and d^2 rules.

**Where it breaks down:** d^2 = 0 does not carry over to vectors; that breakdown is precisely where curvature lives.


## Misconceptions addressed

### Since d^2 = 0, the second exterior derivative of a vector field must vanish too. · GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393)

**Correction:** d^2 = 0 holds for functions and forms. dv is built with a tensor product of a vector and a 1-form, so d^2 v survives and equals the curvature acting on v.

**Why tempting:** Learners have just absorbed d^2 = 0 as a universal rule in Chapter 33.

### Non-zero connection forms (or Christoffel symbols) mean the space is curved. · GA §36.4 p.381 (pdf 398)

**Correction:** Connection forms reflect the chosen frame field; the polar plane has ω^θ̂_r̂ = dθ but zero curvature 2-form.

**Why tempting:** Γ ≠ 0 looks like 'gravity' and the rotating polar frame looks like a geometric effect.

### You need connection coefficients before you can compute curvature. · GA §36.3 p.380 (pdf 397)

**Correction:** Cartan's route goes from the coframe directly to connection forms and curvature 2-forms; Γ can be read off afterwards if wanted but is not required.

**Why tempting:** Earlier chapters always computed Γ first.

### Connection coefficients are symmetric in their lower indices in every basis. · GA §36.3 p.380 (pdf 397)

**Correction:** Symmetry holds in coordinate bases for a torsion-free connection; in non-coordinate (orthonormal) frames it fails, e.g. on the sphere Γ^φ̂_{φ̂θ̂} = cot θ / a but Γ^φ̂_{θ̂φ̂} = 0.

**Why tempting:** Christoffel symbols were introduced as symmetric.

### An orthonormal frame is the same as a local inertial frame, so its connection coefficients vanish. · GA §36.3 p.379 (pdf 396)

**Correction:** Orthonormal frames have constant metric components η but generally non-zero connection coefficients; only a freely falling frame kills them at a point.

**Why tempting:** Both frames have η as metric at a point.

### Connection forms in an orthonormal frame always satisfy ω^μ̂_ν̂ = -ω^ν̂_μ̂. · GA §36.4 p.382 (pdf 399)

**Correction:** Antisymmetry holds for both indices lowered. With a hatted time index, lowering flips sign, so ω^t̂_k̂ = +ω^k̂_t̂ while spatial forms obey ω^î_ĵ = -ω^ĵ_î.

**Why tempting:** In Euclidean examples up and down indices are interchangeable.

### Guessing connection forms is not rigorous; a different guess might give different curvature. · GA §36.2 p.377 (pdf 394)

**Correction:** The torsion-free, metric-compatible connection is unique, so any set of antisymmetric forms that satisfies every first structure equation is the answer; the guess must simply be checked against all of them.

**Why tempting:** The book openly calls the step guesswork without citing uniqueness.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### If space is flat, spacetime must be flat. · GA §36.4 p.382 (pdf 399)

**Correction:** The spatially flat expanding universe has curvature components ä/a and (ȧ/a)^2 because the slices change with time.

**Why tempting:** Euclidean spatial geometry of the flat FRW model suggests no curvature.

### Riemann components and the Ricci scalar differ between orthonormal and coordinate frames in the same way. · GA §36.4 p.382 (pdf 399)

**Correction:** Components change by vielbein factors (1/a^2 versus sin^2 θ on the sphere), but the Ricci scalar is invariant.

**Why tempting:** After seeing components change, learners expect all curvature numbers to be frame-dependent.

### Once one structure equation gives a connection form, all curvature 2-forms come from dω alone. · GA §36.4 p.382 (pdf 399)

**Correction:** Other components can arise purely from the ω∧ω term, e.g. R^k̂_l̂ in the expanding universe; the book explicitly warns not to forget them.

**Why tempting:** In the sphere example the wedge term vanished.

### The objects found in Step II are 'curvature 1-forms'. · GA §36.2 p.379 (pdf 396)

**Correction:** They are connection 1-forms; curvature lives in the 2-forms built from them.

**Why tempting:** The book's Idea 1 heading and chapter summary use the phrase 'curvature 1-forms'.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Cartan's traveller with a moving frame · GA §36.3 p.379 (pdf 396)

**Setup:** Imagine moving through a space while carrying an orthonormal set of axes that is re-erected at every point, and watch how the axes seem to turn as position changes.

**Lesson:** The change of the frame from point to point is the connection, recorded by the 1-forms ω^μ_ν; curvature is what remains when that change cannot be undone by re-choosing frames.

**App idea:** First-person walk on a sphere or saddle with an attached tripod; compare the carried frame with a parallel-transported one around a loop to reveal holonomy equal to the enclosed integral of the curvature 2-form.

### A vector as the motion of a point · GA §36.2 p.377 (pdf 394)

**Setup:** Treat the most primitive vector as the infinitesimal displacement dP of a point, an object waiting for a direction to be specified.

**Lesson:** dP must be the identity map e_μ ⊗ ω^μ, and because P is function-like, d^2 P = 0 constrains the connection (zero torsion).

**App idea:** Tiny parallelogram built from two displacements; show that closing it (d^2 P = 0) forces the symmetric/torsion-free relation, and let learners add a torsion slider that opens the gap.


## Applications and observations

- **Curvature of an expanding universe and the Friedmann equations** (astrophysical-system, GA §36.4 p.382 (pdf 399)): Example 36.5 yields the Einstein tensor for a spatially flat Robertson-Walker spacetime, whose components set equal to 8πT give the Friedmann equations of the cosmology part; Exercise 36.7 extends this to closed spatial slices (answer: R = 6(ä/a + ȧ^2/a^2 + 1/a^2), per GA Appendix E and our check). For exponential expansion a = e^{Ht} every sectional curvature equals H^2 and G_μ̂ν̂ = -3H^2 η_μ̂ν̂, i.e. a cosmological constant Λ = 3H^2 (our derivation). Key numbers: Our estimate: with H_0 ≈ 70 km/s/Mpc ≈ 2.3×10^-18 s^-1, the curvature scale H_0^2/c^2 is about 6×10^-53 m^-2, a radius of curvature near 1.3×10^26 m (the Hubble length).
- **Curvature of static stars and the Schwarzschild exterior** (astrophysical-system, GA §36.4 p.384 (pdf 401)): Example 36.6 gives all Riemann and Einstein components of a static spherical star in terms of E, Ē, F, F̄. G_t̂t̂ = F + 2F̄ = r^{-2} d[r(1 - e^{-2Λ})]/dr feeds the mass function and TOV equation of Chapter 21. Setting e^{2Φ} = e^{-2Λ} = 1 - 2M/r gives E = F = 2M/r^3 and Ē = F̄ = -M/r^3 with all Einstein components zero (our check). Key numbers: Our estimate for Earth's surface: GM/(c^2 r^3) ≈ 1.7×10^-23 m^-2, so the radial tidal component 2GM/(c^2 r^3) ≈ 3.4×10^-23 m^-2 (equivalently 2GM/r^3 ≈ 3.1×10^-6 s^-2).
- **Stellar pulsation and gravitational collapse** (astrophysical-system, GA p.385 (pdf 402)): Exercise 36.8 sets up curvature 2-forms for a time-dependent spherical metric with functions Φ(R,T), Λ(R,T), r(R,T), with a new mixed function H; the authors point to MTW chapters 14, 26 and 32 for applications to pulsating stars and collapse.
- **Gaussian curvature of familiar surfaces** (numerical-estimate, GA p.385 (pdf 402)): Exercises apply the method to a surface of revolution, a paraboloid, a torus and the Poincaré half-plane. Results (GA Appendix E, confirmed by our own calculation): K = -r''/r for ds^2 = dσ^2 + r(σ)^2 dφ^2; K = a^2/(1 + a^2 r^2)^2 for the paraboloid; K = cos v/[a(c + a cos v)] for the torus (positive outside, negative inside); K = -1 for the half-plane. Key numbers: Sphere R = 2/a^2; paraboloid R = 2a^2 at the tip; half-plane R = -2.

## Notation and conventions

- **Metric signature:** (-,+,+,+); η_μ̂ν̂ = diag(-1,1,1,1) in orthonormal frames; Euclidean 2D examples use diag(1,1) so up and down indices coincide. (GA p.374 (pdf 391))
- **Hats on indices:** A hatted index (A_μ̂) marks components in an orthonormal frame; unhatted indices are coordinate-frame components. — Margin note 1. (GA p.374 (pdf 391))
- **Connection 1-form index placement:** de_μ = ω^ν_μ ⊗ e_ν: the lower index names the basis vector being differentiated, the upper index the direction it changes into; ω^ν_μ = Γ^ν_{λμ} ω^λ with the differentiation index first among Γ's lower indices (∇_α e_μ = Γ^ν_{αμ} e_ν). — MTW writes ω^ν_μ = Γ^ν_{μλ} ω^λ with the derivative index last; compare carefully when switching sources. (GA §36.1 p.375 (pdf 392))
- **Lowered connection forms:** ω_{μν} = g_{μσ} ω^σ_ν (first index lowered); in orthonormal frames ω_μ̂ν̂ = -ω_ν̂μ̂. (GA §36.2 p.378 (pdf 395))
- **Bold d on vectors:** Bold d acting on vector fields means the covariant derivative ∇, producing vector-valued forms. — Carried over from Chapter 34. (GA §36.1 p.374 (pdf 391))
- **Curvature 2-form and operator symbols:** Script R^μ_ν for the curvature 2-forms, script R( ) for the operator; bold R or R^μ_{ναβ} for the Riemann tensor. (GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393))
- **Restricted sum |αβ|:** Vertical bars around indices mean sum over one ordering only (the order appearing in the wedge product), removing the factor 1/2. — Margin notes 6, 13, 19. (GA §36.1 p.377 (pdf 394))
- **Wedge normalization:** Chapter 34 (eqn 34.38) evaluates dx^α ∧ dx^μ on vectors as dx^α ⊗ dx^μ - dx^μ ⊗ dx^α; Example 36.2 inserts an extra factor 1/2. — Forms-level identities (36.12, 36.20) are unaffected; only 'evaluate on two vectors' statements change by a factor 2. (GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393))
- **Riemann index order and sign:** R^μ_{ναβ} = ∂_α Γ^μ_{βν} - ∂_β Γ^μ_{αν} + Γ^μ_{ασ} Γ^σ_{βν} - Γ^μ_{βσ} Γ^σ_{αν}; the last two indices are the plane of the 2-form; spheres have positive curvature. — Same as MTW. (GA §36.1 p.377 (pdf 394))
- **Squares of 1-forms:** (ω^μ)^2 abbreviates ω^μ ⊗ ω^μ in line elements; dx^2 likewise. (GA §36.3 p.380 (pdf 397))
- **Vielbein notation:** (e_α)^μ̂ converts coordinate to frame components and (e_μ̂)^α the reverse; listed in the margin of each example. (GA §36.3 p.380 (pdf 397))
- **Primes and dots:** Primes are radial derivatives (d/dr or d/dR); dots are time derivatives (d/dt or d/dT). (GA §36.4 p.383 (pdf 400))
- **Suspended summation:** In the FRW example repeated indices in single components such as R^t̂_{k̂t̂k̂} are not summed; explicit Σ marks sums. (GA §36.4 p.383 (pdf 400))

## Teaching gems

### Differentiate a vector twice and watch everything that depends on derivatives of its components cancel: what survives is linear in v at a point, hence a tensor, hence curvature. · GA §36.1 p.376 (pdf 393)

**Why it works:** It explains why curvature is tensorial and why it is built from dω + ω∧ω in one short calculation, and it turns the surprise 'd^2 ≠ 0 for vectors' into the punchline.

**App idea:** Animated term-by-term expansion of d^2 v where matching terms fly together and annihilate, leaving the two curvature terms highlighted; a toggle shows the same cancellation for a function, where nothing survives.

### Treat a point's displacement dP = e_μ ⊗ ω^μ as the identity map and let d^2 P = 0 produce the torsion-free structure equation. · GA §36.2 p.377 (pdf 394)

**Why it works:** Derives a key constraint from a single intuitive fact (closing an infinitesimal parallelogram) rather than from Christoffel algebra.

**App idea:** Parallelogram-closure demo: two small displacements composed in either order; with the correct connection forms the figure closes, with a perturbed (torsionful) choice a gap appears whose size equals dω + ω∧ω applied to the pair.

### Read connection forms off by inspection: write dω^μ̂ in terms of hatted wedges, match to -ω^μ̂_ν̂ ∧ ω^ν̂, then get partners free from antisymmetry. · GA §36.4 p.380 (pdf 397)

**Why it works:** Transforms a dreaded computation into pattern recognition with a built-in consistency check, making curvature of real metrics feasible by hand.

**App idea:** Matching puzzle: the app shows dω^μ̂ for a metric and a palette of candidate connection forms; the learner drags candidates into slots and every structure equation turns green only when all are satisfied.

### Run a flat control first: the polar plane has a non-zero connection form yet zero curvature. · GA §36.4 p.381 (pdf 398)

**Why it works:** Separates frame rotation (connection) from genuine geometry (curvature) before any curved example, blocking a common misconception.

**App idea:** Side-by-side plane and sphere with polar/latitude frames drawn; both show rotating frames, but only the sphere's loop-transport holonomy is non-zero, displayed as a live angle.

### Keep a margin panel of basis vectors and vielbeins beside each example so orthonormal and coordinate components can be compared (1/a^2 versus sin^2 θ) while the scalar R agrees. · GA §36.4 p.382 (pdf 399)

**Why it works:** Makes frame dependence of components visible and reinforces invariance of scalars.

**App idea:** Component inspector: hover any Riemann component to toggle between orthonormal and coordinate values with the vielbein factors multiplying in, alongside a fixed R readout.

### In the flat expanding universe, space-space curvature (ȧ/a)^2 comes entirely from ω∧ω even though the slices are flat. · GA §36.4 p.382 (pdf 399)

**Why it works:** A concrete, physical case where the quadratic term is the whole story, and a memorable answer to 'flat space but curved spacetime'.

**App idea:** Scale-factor sandbox: drag a(t) (linear, power law, exponential, decelerating); plots of ä/a and (ȧ/a)^2 update with the Einstein components and inferred density and pressure.

### Get Einstein components as minus sums of orthonormal sectional curvatures (Chapter 21 rule) instead of forming Ricci first. · GA §36.4 p.384 (pdf 401)

**Why it works:** Short, geometric and fast; makes G_t̂t̂ read as the total spatial curvature felt by a static observer.

**App idea:** Star builder: edit Φ(r) and Λ(r) (or a density profile); bars for E, Ē, F, F̄ and the resulting G components update, and a Schwarzschild preset shows G collapse to zero.

### Condense the whole method into a six-box flowchart (Fig. 36.1) plus three boxed 'Ideas'. · GA §36.4 p.380 (pdf 397)

**Why it works:** Gives a retrievable mental checklist that learners can follow on any diagonal metric.

**App idea:** Checklist overlay in the pipeline workbench that ticks off each stage and flags skipped consistency checks.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Printed eqn 36.81 gives G_t̂t̂ = 3(ä/a)^2 and G_îî = -2ä/a - ȧ/a; the correct values from 36.78-36.80 are 3(ȧ/a)^2 and -2ä/a - (ȧ/a)^2.** — Learners checking against the Friedmann equations will think their own correct result is wrong, or will propagate dimensionally inconsistent expressions. *Suggestion:* Show the substitution G = Ric - (1/2)ηR explicitly and check dimensions (every term must scale as 1/time^2).
- **Example 36.5 is labelled 'de Sitter' but keeps a(t) arbitrary; it is the general spatially flat Robertson-Walker metric, which is de Sitter only for a ∝ e^{Ht}. The text also cites both Chapter 18 and 'Universe 1 in Chapter 15'.** — Confuses de Sitter spacetime with any flat FRW model and obscures the special maximally symmetric case. *Suggestion:* Call it flat FRW, then specialize to a = e^{Ht} to show all sectional curvatures equal H^2 and G = -3H^2 η.
- **In eqn 36.85 the first line ends with ω^t̂ ∧ ω^θ̂ (should be ω^t̂ ∧ ω^r̂), and the third line lumps two different 2-forms under ω^φ̂ ∧ ω^θ̂ and writes Λ for Λ'; the correct form is -(e^{-Λ} cos θ)/(r^2 sin θ) ω^φ̂∧ω^θ̂ + (Λ' e^{-2Λ}/r) ω^φ̂∧ω^r̂.** — The final curvature 2-forms (36.87) are right, so learners following line by line cannot reconcile the intermediate step. *Suggestion:* Present corrected intermediate derivatives, and teach the habit of computing dω in coordinate differentials before converting to hatted wedges.
- **Margin vielbeins for Example 36.6 invert the time entries: printed e_t̂ = (1/e^{-Φ}) ∂_t, (e_t)^t̂ = e^{-φ}, (e_t̂)^t = e^Φ; correct are e_t̂ = e^{-Φ} ∂_t, (e_t)^t̂ = e^Φ, (e_t̂)^t = e^{-Φ}.** — Any conversion of time components to coordinates picks up wrong exponential factors. *Suggestion:* Derive vielbeins from ω^t̂ = e^Φ dt each time rather than copying tables; verify e_t̂ · e_t̂ = -1.
- **Example 36.2 evaluates wedges with an extra factor 1/2, unlike GA's own eqn 34.38; printed 36.17 also lacks parentheses around the derivative difference and the final line of 36.18 drops the 1/2.** — Learners comparing R^μ_ν(e_α, e_β) with Chapter 34 or MTW will find factor-of-2 mismatches and suspect the curvature formula itself. *Suggestion:* State one wedge convention, note that 36.12 and 36.20 are convention-independent as form identities, and restore the parentheses and factor.
- **Idea 1's heading and the chapter summary say 'curvature 1-forms' for what are connection 1-forms; eqn 36.13's operator is called a (2,2) object although, as written, it has one vector and three covector slots.** — Blurs the crucial distinction between connection (1-forms) and curvature (2-forms), and muddles tensor-type bookkeeping. *Suggestion:* Use 'connection 1-forms' consistently; explain that the (2,2) count applies to the bivector-valued form of eqn 36.21 while R^μ_{ναβ} is (1,3).
- **The 'guesswork' step is not justified by the uniqueness of the torsion-free metric-compatible connection, and the examples do not always show checking the remaining structure equations (e.g. dω^r̂ = 0 in the polar plane).** — Learners either distrust the method or accept a guess that satisfies only one equation. *Suggestion:* State the uniqueness theorem and require verifying every dω^μ̂ equation with the full antisymmetric set before moving on.
- **The dP argument treats a point of a manifold as if it had vector-valued differentials, and §36.1 depends on Chapter 34's identification of d with ∇ on vectors; both are heuristic and terse.** — Readers without firm Chapter 34 background may lose the logic and conclude the rules are arbitrary. *Suggestion:* Offer the alternative derivation: in a coordinate basis rule 1 is exactly Γ^μ_{[αβ]} = 0 and rule 2 is ∇g = 0; follow the authors' advice to learn the recipe first.
- **Eqn 36.16 drops the term Γ dω^σ, valid only when ω^σ = dx^σ; this restriction is not stated, while the rest of the chapter works in non-coordinate frames.** — Learners may try to apply 36.16-36.19 directly with orthonormal-frame Γ and get wrong curvature. *Suggestion:* Flag Example 36.2 as a coordinate-basis check; in frames use dω + ω∧ω directly.
- **Sign conventions for hatted time indices and the |αβ| ordering are spread across margin notes 13, 16, 17 and 19 rather than stated once.** — Sign errors in Lorentzian examples, e.g. a wrong sign on R^{φ̂r̂}_{r̂φ̂} or on ω^t̂_k̂. *Suggestion:* Give a one-line rule: lower all indices, apply antisymmetry, raise again with η; and always write the wedge in the order used to read components.
- **Step I's template uses χ̂ (from the opening's hyperspherical example) while §36.3 and the recipe's surroundings use r̂; the conversion in 36.66 shows only the two vielbein factors that do not cancel.** — Minor confusion about which coordinates are meant and how the coordinate component 1 arises. *Suggestion:* Write all four vielbein factors in 36.66 and use consistent coordinate labels.

## Tutor notes

- Open with a picture question: 'Carry a set of arrows pointing north and east as you walk along a line of latitude. Do they turn? How fast?' Use the answer to introduce connection 1-forms as rotation rates per unit step before any formalism.
- Diagnose prerequisites early: can the learner compute d(f dx) = df ∧ dx, explain α ∧ α = 0, and write an orthonormal coframe for a diagonal metric? If not, detour to GA ch33 and ch10 material first.
- For most learners follow the authors' own advice: teach the recipe on the polar plane and sphere first, then return to §§36.1-36.2 to justify the rules. For mathematically inclined learners, derive the rules first via d^2 v and d^2 P.
- Always pair the flat polar plane with the sphere; ask 'the plane's connection form is dθ, not zero, so is the plane curved?' and let the learner find that dω + ω∧ω vanishes.
- When learners 'guess' connection forms, insist on two checks: every first structure equation holds with the full set, and lowered forms are antisymmetric. Mention uniqueness so guessing feels legitimate.
- Check understanding of Lorentzian signs with a quick quiz: given ω^k̂_t̂ = (ȧ/a) ω^k̂, what is ω^t̂_k̂? What is ω^ĵ_î given ω^î_ĵ? (Answers: the same; minus it.)
- Emphasize the |αβ| rule concretely: write the 2-form coefficient in the same wedge order as the component indices; if you swap the wedge, the component changes sign.
- Warn about printed errors before the learner hits them: Einstein components in 36.81, index and Λ' slips in 36.85, the inverted time vielbein in the Example 36.6 margin, the 1/2 issues in 36.17-36.18, and 'curvature 1-forms'.
- Use dimensional checks: 2D surface curvature ~ 1/length^2; FRW components ~ 1/time^2 (c = 1); any term like ȧ/a added to ä/a signals an error.
- Connect results to physics: for Schwarzschild E = 2M/r^3 is the radial tidal stretch, Ē = -M/r^3 the transverse squeeze; G_t̂t̂ = F + 2F̄ = 8πρ gives the mass function; FRW G components give the Friedmann equations.
- Common learner questions to prepare for: Why does a 1-form carry two extra indices? Why is d^2 v not zero? What does 'torsion-free' have to do with a 'symmetry condition'? Why do coordinate and frame Riemann components differ while R does not? How does this relate to gauge fields (F = dA + A∧A)?
- For 2D surfaces, stress that the single curvature 2-form coefficient is the Gaussian curvature K and R = 2K; use Exercises 36.3-36.6 (surface of revolution, paraboloid, torus, half-plane) as a graded ladder with visual intuition for sign.
