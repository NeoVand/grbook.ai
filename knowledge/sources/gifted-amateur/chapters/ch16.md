# GA ch16 · Robertson–Walker spaces

> Drops the spatial-flatness assumption of Chapter 15: symmetry alone fixes the curvature of each constant-time slice to a single constant, which yields the Robertson–Walker metric with k = +1, 0, -1; the chapter then explores the three geometries through proper distance, circumference-to-radius ratios, triangles and embeddings, derives the cosmological redshift 1+z = a(t_ob)/a(t_em) from null rays, and flags the Big-Bang singularity where a(t) vanishes.

**Pages:** printed 169–180 · pdf 186–197 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

The symmetry argument and the transformations are short but lean on unproved assertions (uniqueness of the Riemann form and of the metric) and on Appendix D and Chapter 30 for geometry. The three-case tour is gentle and pictorial; the redshift derivation is clean. Stumbling blocks are the multiplicity of radial coordinates (R, r, chi, xi, and chi used as an r value), the change in the meaning and dimensions of a(t) mid-chapter, and several printed typos in margin formulas and in Exercise 16.4. Component lists appear without derivation.

## Role in the book

Second chapter of Part III (Cosmology). Chapter 15 argued from homogeneity and isotropy for a metric of the form -dt^2 + a^2 gamma_ij dx^i dx^j and studied two spatially flat toy universes (Universe 0 and the de Sitter Universe 1). This chapter supplies the general spatial geometry allowed by those symmetries while deliberately leaving the matter side of Einstein's equation untouched: it is pure kinematics and geometry. It hands Chapter 17 (Friedmann equations) the metric, the curvature index k and the Ricci components needed to insert matter, gives Chapter 18 the redshift as the link between models and observation, and plants the initial singularity and the conformal (Poincaré disc) picture that Chapter 19 develops. The classical curvature of spheres and hyperbolic surfaces is treated properly only in Part V (Chapter 30) and Appendix D, so here those ideas are used intuitively.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can explain why homogeneity and isotropy, not Einstein's equation, force the spatial Riemann tensor into the form K(gamma_ik gamma_jl - gamma_il gamma_jk) with a single constant K.
- Reader can contract the constant-curvature Riemann tensor to obtain (3)R_jl = 2K gamma_jl and (3)R = 6K.
- Reader can transform the isotropic form of the constant-curvature 3-metric into the areal-radius form dr^2/(1-Kr^2) + r^2 dOmega^2 by choosing r so that circumferences are 2 pi a r.
- Reader can rescale K and r so that the curvature index k takes only the values +1, 0, -1 and a(t) carries dimensions of length, with K = k/a^2.
- Reader can rewrite the Robertson–Walker metric using the hyperspherical coordinate chi with Sigma(chi) = sin chi, chi, sinh chi and state the allowed coordinate ranges.
- Reader can compute the proper distance to a comoving galaxy for each k and derive Hubble's law v_p = H d_p from its time derivative.
- Reader can use the ratio of proper circumference to proper radius, and triangle angle sums, to diagnose positive, zero or negative spatial curvature.
- Reader can describe the embeddings of the k = +1 space as a 3-sphere in 4D Euclidean space and of the k = -1 space as a hyperboloid in 4D Minkowski space, and compute the volume 2 pi^2 a^3 of the closed universe.
- Reader can distinguish flat space from flat spacetime: k = 0 with a varying a(t) is still curved spacetime.
- Reader can derive the cosmological redshift 1+z = a(t_ob)/a(t_em) by comparing two successive null rays between comoving observers, and interpret it as wavelength stretching with a(t).
- Reader can explain why a(t) -> 0 signals a curvature singularity (the Big Bang) at which classical GR ceases to apply.

## Teaching approach

A compact geometry-first chapter. It starts from what symmetry permits (one curvature number), finds the metric that realizes it, and then spends most of its effort making the three cases tangible with low-dimensional pictures: circles whose circumference falls short of or exceeds 2 pi times the radius, triangles with angle sums above or below pi, a sphere and a hyperboloid as embeddings, and the Poincaré disc. Much of the calculation is parked in boxed Examples and margin lists (connection coefficients, Ricci components) so that the main line stays short. The single physical payoff, the cosmological redshift, is derived with a visual integral-splitting trick. Matter and dynamics are explicitly deferred.

**Style:** geometric-first, diagram-driven, analogy-driven, example-driven, derivation-heavy, conversational-informal, spiral-revisit

**Narrative arc**

1. Motivation: relax the spatial flatness of Chapter 15's toy universes while keeping homogeneity and isotropy; warn that time dependence can shrink space to a singular point.
2. Symmetry argument: the only tensor structure available for the spatial Riemann tensor is built from gamma_ij, so it must be K(gamma gamma - gamma gamma) with constant K; contract to Ricci and scalar.
3. Find the metric: quote the Robertson–Walker result for gamma_ij in isotropic coordinates, then (Example 16.1) change to a circumference-defined radius r to get dr^2/(1-Kr^2).
4. Normalize: rescale to k in {+1, 0, -1} and absorb |K| into a length-valued a(t); record the final RW line element.
5. Tabulate machinery: metric, vielbein, connection coefficients and Ricci components in the margin; introduce chi and Sigma(chi) in a boxed summary with coordinate ranges.
6. Measure distances: proper distance to a comoving galaxy for each k (Example 16.3, Fig. 16.1), differentiate to obtain Hubble's law.
7. Explore the three geometries in turn with a circumference-to-radius test, triangle area rules (Girard, Lambert), embeddings (Fig. 16.2), the Poincaré disc (Figs 16.3, 16.5), closed-universe volume and the round-trip light ray (Fig. 16.4).
8. Turn to observables: proper distance is not measurable, so derive the redshift from two successive null rays (Example 16.8, Fig. 16.6), interpret as wavelength stretching, define z.
9. Close by running time backwards: a -> 0 gives diverging density and curvature, the initial singularity, deferred to Chapter 19 and later.

**Signature moves**

- Fixing the form of the curvature tensor by symmetry before ever writing down a metric, then asking which metric produces it.
- Choosing the radial coordinate by an operational demand (circumference equals 2 pi times scale factor times r) rather than by algebraic convenience.
- Using the same diagnostic, circumference over radius, for all three k values so the geometry becomes a comparison of one number with 2 pi.
- Recycling two-dimensional results (Girard's theorem from Chapter 3, embeddings from Appendix D) as stand-ins for the three-dimensional spaces.
- Reading Lambert's hyperbolic triangle formula as Girard's formula on a sphere of imaginary radius to make negative curvature feel like a continuation of positive curvature.
- A playful hook (seeing the back of your own head) to make the angle-like radial coordinate of the closed universe memorable, with a margin caveat that real models do not allow it.
- Splitting two integrals so their common middle cancels, illustrated by a two-ray timing diagram, to reduce the redshift derivation to one line.
- Relegating component lists (connection coefficients, Ricci) to margins so the prose argument stays uncluttered while the data remain available for the next chapter.

## Section by section

### Chapter opening: beyond spatially flat universes — p.169 (pdf 186)

After an epigraph from C. S. Peirce, the authors explain that they will now allow the spatial slices of a homogeneous, isotropic universe to be curved, producing geometries that are only the left-hand side of Einstein's equation; matter comes next chapter. They preview that the curvature is constant and can be positive, zero or negative, and that time dependence lets all three shrink to a point where spacetime curvature diverges, which they connect to the Big Bang. Margin notes define constant curvature and recall how a ball's circles tear or a saddle's circles fold when flattened.

**Concepts:** space of constant curvature, curvature index k, initial singularity, cosmological principle

**Key moves**
- Frame the chapter as geometry only, deferring matter to Chapter 17.
- Preview the three curvature classes by sign.
- Announce the singularity as a consequence of a shrinking scale factor rather than of any specific matter content.

### §16.1 Spaces with constant curvature — p.169 (pdf 186)

Starting from the symmetry-based metric form of Chapter 15, the section argues that the Riemann tensor of the spatial slices can only be K times an antisymmetrized product of the 3-metric, contracts it to (3)R_jl = 2K gamma_jl and (3)R = 6K, and quotes Robertson and Walker's unique metric in isotropic coordinates. Example 16.1 changes to a radius defined by circumference, rescales to k = +1, 0, -1 with a(t) now a length, and produces the Robertson–Walker line element. Example 16.2 motivates the chi coordinate by analogy with the 2-spaces of Appendix D, and margins list metric, vielbein, connection coefficients and orthonormal Ricci components. A boxed summary gives the chi form with Sigma(chi) and coordinate ranges, emphasizing that chi is bounded only for k = +1. A margin note separates constant spatial curvature from constant spacetime curvature.

**Concepts:** three-dimensional Riemann tensor of spatial slices, Gaussian curvature, space of constant curvature, spatial Ricci tensor and scalar, isotropic coordinates for constant-curvature space, areal radial coordinate, curvature index k, scale factor, Robertson–Walker metric, hyperspherical radial coordinate chi, connection coefficients of the Robertson–Walker metric, spacetime of constant curvature, comoving coordinates, cosmic time

**Key moves**
- Only gamma_ij can build an isotropic, homogeneous rank-4 tensor with Riemann symmetries, giving eqn 16.2.
- Contract with gamma^ik using gamma^ik gamma_ik = 3 to get Ricci 2K gamma_jl.
- Substitute r = R/(1 + KR^2/4) so that the angular part reads a^2 r^2 dOmega^2.
- Set k = K/|K|, r-bar = |K|^{1/2} r and redefine a -> a/sqrt|K|, so K = k/a^2.
- Replace r by chi via dchi^2 = dr^2/(1-kr^2): r = sin chi, chi, sinh chi.
- Note that chi runs only to pi for k = +1, hinting at a finite space.

### §16.1 Proper distance and Hubble's law (Example 16.3) — p.172 (pdf 189)

With coordinates comoving and t the cosmic time, the proper distance on a constant-time slice from the origin to a galaxy at fixed coordinate xi is a(t) times arcsin xi, xi, or arsinh xi for k = +1, 0, -1 (Fig. 16.1). For k = +1 the distance reaches a finite value pi a/2 as xi approaches 1. Differentiating in time, the xi-dependent factor is just l/a in every case, so the rate of change of proper distance is H times proper distance: Hubble's law, valid for all three geometries.

**Concepts:** proper distance in a Robertson–Walker spacetime, Hubble's law, Hubble parameter, comoving coordinates

**Key moves**
- Integrate the radial line element at fixed t.
- Observe that the comoving factor is time independent, so d l/dt = (da/dt)(l/a).
- Identify H = (1/a) da/dt and read the result as v_p = H d_p.

### §16.2 Three Robertson–Walker spaces: the circumference test and k = 0 — p.173 (pdf 190)

The section proposes to compare the proper circumference of a circle with its proper radius for each k, reusing the tearing-versus-folding picture from the opening margin note. For k = 0 the metric is Chapter 15's Universe 1 form; with constant a the spacetime is Minkowski, but with time-varying a the flat space expands and the spacetime is curved. Example 16.4 confirms the ratio is exactly 2 pi and ties the case to Euclidean parallels and angle sums of pi.

**Concepts:** flat Robertson–Walker universe, circumference-to-radius curvature test, flat space versus flat spacetime

**Key moves**
- Circumference of a coordinate circle of radius xi is 2 pi a xi for every k.
- Proper radius differs by k, so the ratio encodes curvature.
- Distinguish spatial flatness from spacetime flatness through the time dependence of a.

### §16.2 The case k = +1: closed spherical space — p.174 (pdf 191)

The k = +1 spatial metric embeds in four-dimensional Euclidean space as a 3-sphere of radius a (margin eqn 16.28, Fig. 16.2a). Example 16.5 connects the geometry to the surface of a 2-sphere: parallel geodesics meet, triangle angles exceed pi with Girard's area rule, and the circumference-to-radius ratio is below 2 pi. The volume integral over 0 <= chi <= pi gives 2 pi^2 a^3, so the space is closed yet has no edge or centre, like a 2-sphere experienced from within. Example 16.6 notes that chi acts like an angle, so a radial light ray can circle the whole universe and in principle return to its origin, a possibility later ruled out for realistic models.

**Concepts:** closed spherical universe, embedding of spatial sections, Girard's theorem, angle sum of geodesic triangles, volume of the closed universe, circumference-to-radius curvature test, hyperspherical radial coordinate chi

**Key moves**
- Embed the slice in flat R^4 with the fourth coordinate equal to a cos chi and the other three equal to a sin chi times the usual unit-vector direction factors.
- Ratio 2 pi xi / arcsin xi <= 2 pi.
- Volume element 4 pi a^3 sin^2 chi dchi integrated to 2 pi^2 a^3.
- Closed means finite volume, not bounded by an edge.
- Radial null rays wrap around the chi circle.

### §16.2 The case k = -1: open hyperbolic space — p.175 (pdf 192)

The k = -1 spatial metric embeds, as a whole, only in four-dimensional Minkowski space, as one sheet of the hyperboloid w^2 - x^2 - y^2 - z^2 = a^2 (margin eqn 16.33, Fig. 16.2b). Example 16.7 names the geometry hyperbolic: parallels diverge, triangle angles fall short of pi and Lambert's formula gives the area, which looks like Girard's rule on a sphere of imaginary radius. The Poincaré disc (Figs 16.3b, 16.5) is introduced as a projection of the hyperboloid in which geodesics hit the rim at right angles. The circumference ratio exceeds 2 pi; the volume diverges because chi is unbounded, so the space is called open, with a margin caution that k = 0 is also open. (The book twice calls the embedded surface a '2-sheet hyperboloid'; only one sheet, w > 0, is the spatial slice.)

**Concepts:** open hyperbolic universe, embedding of spatial sections, Lambert's formula for hyperbolic triangles, Poincaré disc model, angle sum of geodesic triangles, circumference-to-radius curvature test

**Key moves**
- Embed via w = a cosh chi, spatial coordinates a sinh chi times angular factors.
- Lambert area = R^2(pi - sum of angles), read as a sphere with R^2 -> -R^2.
- Ratio 2 pi xi / arsinh xi >= 2 pi.
- Infinite volume since chi ranges to infinity.

### §16.3 Redshift and cosmic expansion — p.176 (pdf 193)

Because proper distances cannot be measured directly, the section turns to the redshift, the third kind after Doppler and gravitational shifts. A comoving observer at the origin receives light from a comoving galaxy; radial null rays obey dt = a dr/sqrt(1-kr^2). Example 16.8 writes this for two successive wave crests, splits the time integrals so that the long common interval cancels (Fig. 16.6), and uses the shortness of the period to conclude delta t_em / a(t_em) = delta t_ob / a(t_ob). Converting periods to frequencies gives omega_ob/omega_em = a(t_em)/a(t_ob), depending only on the scale factor at the two events; Example 16.9 notes redshift for expansion and blueshift for contraction. Wavelength therefore scales with a, which (in a margin note) also holds for neutrinos and gravitational waves. The redshift parameter z is defined, giving 1+z = a(t_ob)/a(t_em), or 1/a(t_em) with today's a set to one.

**Concepts:** cosmological redshift, redshift parameter z, radial null geodesic in a Robertson–Walker spacetime, comoving distance, wavelength stretching with the scale factor, three causes of redshift

**Key moves**
- Set ds^2 = 0 for a radial ray and separate variables.
- The comoving coordinate integral is the same for both crests because emitter and observer are comoving.
- Split the time integrals and cancel the shared piece from t_em + delta t_em to t_ob.
- Treat 1/a as constant over one period to linearize.
- Invert periods to frequencies, then to wavelengths, and define z.

### §16.4 The initial singularity — p.178 (pdf 195)

A short qualitative closing section. Once matter is added (next chapter), density falls as the universe expands; running the models backwards, most reach a(t) = 0 a finite time ago, where the density grows without bound. Because matter sources the metric, this is not only a density blow-up (as in a Newtonian model) but a divergence of spacetime curvature that classical GR cannot handle, so the point must be excised: the Big Bang, the initial singularity. The authors defer its treatment as an edge of spacetime to Chapter 19.

**Concepts:** initial singularity, curvature singularity

**Key moves**
- Reverse the direction of time in an expanding model.
- Contrast a Newtonian density singularity with a GR curvature singularity.
- Excise the singular point from the spacetime description.

### Chapter summary — p.179 (pdf 196)

Three bullet points restate that there are three homogeneous, isotropic Robertson–Walker geometries labelled by k, that their evolution through a(t) is tied to observed redshift by 1+z = a(t_ob)/a(t_em), and that these universes may begin in a Big-Bang singularity.

**Concepts:** Robertson–Walker metric, cosmological redshift, initial singularity

**Key moves**
- Restate eqn 16.49 (metric) and eqn 16.50 (redshift).

## Concepts

### cosmological principle

*principle · revisited* · also: homogeneity and isotropy, spatial homogeneity and isotropy

The assumption that, on large scales, space looks the same at every location (homogeneity) and in every direction (isotropy) at a given cosmic time.

**How introduced:** Carried over from Chapter 15, where it produced the general form of the cosmological metric; here it is used to forbid any preferred direction in the spatial curvature tensor.

**Prerequisites:** metric, spacelike hypersurface

$$
ds^2 = -dt^2 + a(t)^2\gamma_{ij}dx^i dx^j
$$

**Notes:** The authors stress that the metric form follows from symmetry alone, independently of GR.

**Where:** GA §16.1 p.169 (pdf 186); GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187)

### Robertson–Walker metric

*solution-or-model · core* · also: RW metric, FLRW metric, Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric, Robertson–Walker spacetime

The most general metric of a spacetime whose constant-time spatial slices are homogeneous and isotropic: ds^2 = -dt^2 + a(t)^2 [dr^2/(1-kr^2) + r^2 dOmega^2], with k = +1, 0 or -1 and scale factor a(t).

**How introduced:** Derived by first fixing the spatial curvature by symmetry, quoting the unique metric with that curvature, changing to a circumference-based radius and rescaling so k takes three values.

**Prerequisites:** cosmological principle, space of constant curvature, scale factor, curvature index k

$$
ds^2=-dt^2+a(t)^2\left[\frac{dr^2}{1-kr^2}+r^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)\right]
$$

$$
ds^2=-dt^2+a(t)^2\left[d\chi^2+\Sigma(\chi)^2 d\Omega^2\right]
$$

**Notes:** The book does not mention Friedmann or Lemaître in this chapter; the geometry is independent of the field equations.

**Where:** GA §16.1 p.171 (pdf 188); GA §16.1 p.172 (pdf 189); GA §16.2 p.173 (pdf 190); GA p.179 (pdf 196)

### scale factor

*physical-quantity · core* · also: expansion factor, radius of the universe, cosmological radius, effective radius, a(t)

The function a(t) multiplying the spatial part of the Robertson–Walker metric; proper distances between comoving points are proportional to it. After rescaling for k = ±1 it has dimensions of length and the spatial curvature is K = k/a^2.

**How introduced:** Inherited from Chapter 15 as a dimensionless factor, then redefined in Example 16.1 by absorbing 1/sqrt|K| so that it becomes a length, which motivates calling it the radius of the universe by analogy with a sphere.

**Prerequisites:** Robertson–Walker metric, Gaussian curvature

$$
a(t):=a(t)/\sqrt{|K|}\ (K\neq0)
$$

$$
K=k/a(t)^2
$$

**Notes:** For k = 0 there is no natural normalization; later the book sets a(t_ob) = 1.

**Where:** GA §16.1 p.171 (pdf 188); GA §16.1 p.173 (pdf 190); GA §16.3 p.177 (pdf 194)

### cosmic time

*definition · developed* · also: cosmological time, t

The time coordinate t of the Robertson–Walker metric, equal to the proper time kept by comoving observers (g_tt = -1), labelling the homogeneous spatial slices.

**How introduced:** Stated in passing when the authors note the RW coordinates are comoving; relied on in the redshift derivation where coordinate intervals are treated as proper periods.

**Prerequisites:** comoving coordinates, proper time

$$
g_{tt}=-1
$$

**Where:** GA §16.1 p.172 (pdf 189); GA §16.3 p.177 (pdf 194)

### comoving coordinates

*convention · developed* · also: comoving frame, galaxy-fixed coordinates

Coordinates in which galaxies moving with the cosmic expansion have fixed spatial labels (r, theta, phi) or (chi, theta, phi); all expansion is carried by a(t).

**How introduced:** Asserted for the RW coordinates with a margin reminder of the Chapter 15 conditions on metric derivatives; used to define fixed comoving separation xi and to argue that two light crests traverse the same coordinate interval.

**Prerequisites:** cosmic time, Robertson–Walker metric

$$
g_{00,i}=g_{0i,0}=0
$$

**Where:** GA §16.1 p.171 (pdf 188); GA §16.1 p.172 (pdf 189); GA §16.3 p.176 (pdf 193)

### three-dimensional Riemann tensor of spatial slices

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: (3)R, spatial curvature tensor, intrinsic curvature of the constant-t hypersurface

The Riemann tensor built from the 3-metric gamma_ij of a constant-time hypersurface alone, describing the intrinsic curvature of space (not spacetime) at that instant.

**How introduced:** Constructed to classify spatial geometries: homogeneity and isotropy leave gamma_ij as the only building block, and Riemann symmetries then allow a single arrangement with one constant.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann curvature tensor, symmetries of the Riemann tensor, cosmological principle

$$
{}^{(3)}R_{ijkl}=K(\gamma_{ik}\gamma_{jl}-\gamma_{il}\gamma_{jk})
$$

**Notes:** A margin note mentions that the Levi-Civita symbol is the only other isotropic 3-tensor, but it is not needed.

**Where:** GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187)

### space of constant curvature

*definition · core* · also: maximally symmetric space, constant-curvature space, isotropic curved space

A space whose Riemann tensor has the form K(g_ik g_jl - g_il g_jk) with the same K at every point, so its curvature is characterized by a single number and looks identical in all places and directions.

**How introduced:** Reached by the symmetry argument for the spatial slices; named once the matching metric (eqn 16.5) is found. Exercise 16.5 asks for the proof (via the Bianchi identity) that isotropy at each point forces the coefficient to be constant.

**Prerequisites:** three-dimensional Riemann tensor of spatial slices, Gaussian curvature

$$
R_{ijkl}=K(g_{ik}g_{jl}-g_{il}g_{jk})
$$

**Notes:** The constancy result is Schur's theorem, though the book does not name it.

**Where:** GA p.169 (pdf 186); GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187); GA p.180 (pdf 197)

### Gaussian curvature

*physical-quantity · core* · also: K, sectional curvature of the spatial slice

For these spaces, the constant K in the constant-curvature Riemann tensor, with dimensions of inverse length squared; for a 2-sphere of radius r it equals +1/r^2, and it is negative for hyperbolic geometry.

**How introduced:** Introduced as the coefficient in eqn 16.2 with only a pointer to Chapter 30 for its classical meaning; its sign is identified with spherical, flat or hyperbolic geometry using the sphere example.

**Prerequisites:** three-dimensional Riemann tensor of spatial slices

$$
K=+1/r^2\ \text{(sphere)}
$$

$$
K=k/a(t)^2
$$

**Notes:** The classical (surface) meaning of Gaussian curvature is deferred to Chapter 30, Classical curvature; in this chapter K is used as a label whose sign sets the geometry.

**Where:** GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187); GA §16.1 p.171 (pdf 188)

### curvature index k

*convention · core* · also: curvature parameter, rescaled curvature, k

The sign of the spatial curvature, k = K/|K| for K nonzero and k = 0 otherwise, taking values +1 (spherical, closed), 0 (flat) and -1 (hyperbolic, open) after rescaling r and a.

**How introduced:** Produced by rescaling in Example 16.1 to strip dimensions from the bracketed part of the metric.

**Prerequisites:** Gaussian curvature, scale factor

$$
k=K/|K|
$$

$$
\bar r=|K|^{1/2}r
$$

**Where:** GA §16.1 p.171 (pdf 188); GA §16.2 p.173 (pdf 190)

### spatial Ricci tensor and scalar

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: (3)R_ij, three-dimensional Ricci scalar, (3)R = 6K

Contractions of the spatial Riemann tensor: for constant curvature, (3)R_jl = 2K gamma_jl and (3)R = 6K.

**How introduced:** Obtained by a one-line contraction using gamma^ik gamma_ik = 3; a margin caution stresses these are not the spacetime quantities that enter Einstein's equation.

**Prerequisites:** three-dimensional Riemann tensor of spatial slices, Ricci tensor

$$
{}^{(3)}R_{jl}=2K\gamma_{jl}
$$

$$
{}^{(3)}R=6K
$$

**Where:** GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187); GA p.180 (pdf 197)

### isotropic coordinates for constant-curvature space

*technique · introduced* · also: conformally flat form, stereographic coordinates

A coordinate system in which the constant-curvature 3-metric is a position-dependent multiple of the Euclidean metric: gamma_ij = [1 + K(x^2+y^2+z^2)/4]^{-2} delta_ij.

**How introduced:** Quoted as Robertson and Walker's unique solution matching eqn 16.2, then converted to polar form (eqn 16.8) before the change to an areal radius.

**Prerequisites:** space of constant curvature, metric

$$
\gamma_{ij}=\left[1+\tfrac{K}{4}(x^2+y^2+z^2)\right]^{-2}\delta_{ij}
$$

**Notes:** Geometrically this is stereographic projection; the book does not use that name.

**Where:** GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187)

### areal radial coordinate

*convention · developed* · also: circumferential radius, r coordinate, reduced circumference coordinate

A radial coordinate r chosen so that the angular part of the metric is a^2 r^2 dOmega^2, making the proper circumference of a coordinate circle equal to 2 pi a r (and sphere area 4 pi a^2 r^2), at the cost of a non-trivial g_rr.

**How introduced:** Motivated operationally in Example 16.1 by demanding that circumferences look Euclidean, leading to r = R/(1 + KR^2/4).

**Prerequisites:** isotropic coordinates for constant-curvature space

$$
r=\frac{R}{1+KR^2/4}
$$

**Notes:** For k = +1 this r reaches a maximum of 1 and then retraces, so it covers only half of the 3-sphere at a time.

**Where:** GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187)

### hyperspherical radial coordinate chi

*convention · developed* · also: chi, angular radial coordinate, comoving radial distance coordinate, Sigma(chi)

A dimensionless radial coordinate defined by dchi = dr/sqrt(1-kr^2), so that r = Sigma(chi) with Sigma = sin chi, chi, sinh chi for k = +1, 0, -1; the spatial metric becomes dchi^2 + Sigma^2 dOmega^2. It ranges over [0, pi] for k = +1 and [0, infinity) otherwise.

**How introduced:** Motivated in Example 16.2 by the 2-space analysis of Appendix D and presented in a boxed summary as the most useful coordinates for applications.

**Prerequisites:** areal radial coordinate, curvature index k

$$
d\chi^2=\frac{dr^2}{1-kr^2}
$$

$$
\Sigma(\chi)=\sin\chi,\ \chi,\ \sinh\chi
$$

**Notes:** Proper radial distance on a slice is simply a chi; for k = +1, chi behaves like a polar angle on a 3-sphere.

**Where:** GA §16.1 p.171 (pdf 188); GA §16.1 p.172 (pdf 189); GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192)

### connection coefficients of the Robertson–Walker metric

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: Christoffel symbols of RW, RW Ricci components

The non-zero Christoffel symbols and orthonormal Ricci components of the RW metric, e.g. Gamma^t_rr = a adot/(1-kr^2), Gamma^i_tj = (adot/a) delta^i_j, R_tt-hat = -3 addot/a, R_ii-hat = addot/a + 2 adot^2/a^2 + 2k/a^2, R = 6(addot/a + adot^2/a^2 + k/a^2).

**How introduced:** Listed without derivation in the margin of Example 16.2 as the data needed to insert the metric into Einstein's equation; Exercise 16.3 asks for verification.

**Prerequisites:** Robertson–Walker metric, Christoffel symbols, orthonormal frame

$$
\Gamma^t_{rr}=\frac{a\dot a}{1-kr^2}
$$

$$
R_{\hat t\hat t}=-3\frac{\ddot a}{a}
$$

$$
R=6\left(\frac{\ddot a}{a}+\frac{\dot a^2}{a^2}+\frac{k}{a^2}\right)
$$

**Notes:** The printed spatial Ricci component has 2k/a where 2k/a^2 is required by dimensions and by the trace; see gaps.

**Where:** GA §16.1 p.171 (pdf 188)

### spacetime of constant curvature

*definition · mention* · also: maximally symmetric spacetime, constant curvature spacetime

A four-dimensional spacetime whose Riemann tensor is a fixed constant C times the antisymmetrized product of two copies of the spacetime metric (the same structure as the spatial case), which implies Ricci equal to one quarter of the Ricci scalar times the metric; de Sitter spacetime is an example.

**How introduced:** Raised in a margin note to warn that the constant spatial curvature of RW slices is a different statement from the constant spacetime curvature of Universe 1; linked via Exercises 16.5 and 16.6.

**Prerequisites:** space of constant curvature, Ricci tensor

$$
R_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}=C(g_{\mu\alpha}g_{\nu\beta}-g_{\mu\beta}g_{\nu\alpha})
$$

$$
R_{\mu\nu}=\tfrac14 R g_{\mu\nu}
$$

**Where:** GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187); GA p.180 (pdf 197)

### flat Robertson–Walker universe

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: k = 0 universe, spatially flat universe, Euclidean spatial sections

The k = 0 case, whose constant-time slices are ordinary Euclidean 3-space scaled by a(t); it is infinite (open) and its circles have circumference exactly 2 pi times their radius.

**How introduced:** The first of the three cases in §16.2, identified with Chapter 15's Universe 1 metric form and with the Euclidean plane (Example 16.4).

**Prerequisites:** Robertson–Walker metric, circumference-to-radius curvature test

$$
ds^2=-dt^2+a(t)^2[dr^2+r^2d\Omega^2]
$$

**Where:** GA §16.2 p.173 (pdf 190); GA §16.2 p.174 (pdf 191)

### flat space versus flat spacetime

*principle · developed* · also: spatial flatness is not spacetime flatness

Spatial slices can be intrinsically flat while the four-dimensional spacetime is curved: for k = 0 the Riemann tensor vanishes only if a(t) is constant.

**How introduced:** Stated when discussing k = 0: constant a gives Minkowski space, time-dependent a gives an expanding flat space in a curved spacetime.

**Prerequisites:** flat Robertson–Walker universe, Riemann curvature tensor

$$
k=0,\ \dot a\neq0\Rightarrow R\neq0
$$

**Notes:** Visible directly in the Ricci scalar 6(addot/a + adot^2/a^2 + k/a^2).

**Where:** GA §16.2 p.173 (pdf 190); GA §16.2 p.174 (pdf 191)

### closed spherical universe

*solution-or-model · core* · also: k = +1 universe, elliptic geometry, spherical space, 3-sphere universe

The k = +1 case: each spatial slice is a 3-sphere of radius a(t), with finite volume 2 pi^2 a^3, positive curvature, circles whose circumference is less than 2 pi times their radius, and no edge or centre.

**How introduced:** Explored in §16.2 via embedding in 4D Euclidean space, analogies with the 2-sphere (Example 16.5), the finite range of chi and the round-the-universe light ray (Example 16.6).

**Prerequisites:** Robertson–Walker metric, hyperspherical radial coordinate chi, embedding of spatial sections

$$
w^2+x^2+y^2+z^2=a^2
$$

$$
V=2\pi^2a(t)^3
$$

**Notes:** The book equates closed with k = +1; nontrivial topologies (e.g. a flat torus) are not discussed.

**Where:** GA §16.2 p.174 (pdf 191); GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192)

### open hyperbolic universe

*solution-or-model · core* · also: k = -1 universe, hyperbolic geometry, Lobachevskian geometry, open universe

The k = -1 case: each slice is a 3-dimensional hyperbolic space of curvature -1/a^2, infinite in volume, in which parallel geodesics diverge, triangle angles sum to less than pi and circles have circumference exceeding 2 pi times their radius.

**How introduced:** Explored in §16.2 via embedding as a hyperboloid in 4D Minkowski space, Lambert's triangle formula and the Poincaré disc (Example 16.7).

**Prerequisites:** Robertson–Walker metric, embedding of spatial sections, Poincaré disc model

$$
w^2-x^2-y^2-z^2=a^2
$$

$$
\Sigma(\chi)=\sinh\chi
$$

**Notes:** A margin caution notes that 'open' is ambiguous because the flat case is also infinite.

**Where:** GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192); GA §16.2 p.176 (pdf 193)

### embedding of spatial sections

*technique · developed* · also: embedding diagram, hypersurface embedding

Representing a curved 3-space as a hypersurface in a flat 4-dimensional space whose induced metric reproduces the spatial RW metric: a sphere in Euclidean R^4 for k = +1, a hyperboloid in Minkowski space for k = -1.

**How introduced:** Given via explicit parametrizations in margin eqns 16.28 and 16.33 and drawn with one dimension suppressed in Fig. 16.2; full treatment deferred to Appendix D.

**Prerequisites:** hyperspherical radial coordinate chi, metric

(w,x,y,z)=a(\cos\chi,\ \sin\chi\sin\theta\cos\phi,\ \sin\chi\sin\theta\sin\phi,\ \sin\chi\cos\theta)
$$

**Notes:** Only portions of hyperbolic space embed in Euclidean space; the whole needs a Minkowski ambient space.

**Where:** GA §16.2 p.174 (pdf 191); GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192)

### circumference-to-radius curvature test

*technique · developed* · also: ratio of circumference to radius, C/r test

An intrinsic diagnostic of curvature: compare the proper circumference of a circle with its proper radius; the ratio equals 2 pi in flat space, is smaller for positive curvature and larger for negative curvature.

**How introduced:** Introduced by the opening margin note (a ball tears when flattened, a saddle folds) and applied to all three RW spaces in eqns 16.26, 16.30 and 16.35.

**Prerequisites:** proper distance in a Robertson–Walker spacetime, areal radial coordinate

$$
\frac{2\pi a\xi}{a\sin^{-1}\xi}\le2\pi
$$

$$
\frac{2\pi a\xi}{a\sinh^{-1}\xi}\ge2\pi
$$

**Where:** GA p.169 (pdf 186); GA §16.2 p.173 (pdf 190); GA §16.2 p.174 (pdf 191); GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192)

### angle sum of geodesic triangles

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: angle excess, angle deficit

In a curved 2-geometry the interior angles of a triangle made from geodesics sum to more than pi (positive curvature), exactly pi (flat) or less than pi (negative curvature), with the difference proportional to area.

**How introduced:** Used as the second qualitative marker of each geometry in Examples 16.4, 16.5 and 16.7, with pictures in Fig. 16.3.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic, Gaussian curvature

**Where:** GA §16.2 p.174 (pdf 191); GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192)

### Girard's theorem

*theorem · introduced* · also: spherical excess formula

The area of a geodesic triangle on a sphere of radius R is R^2 times the excess of its angle sum over pi.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapter 3 in Example 16.5 as the 2D model for positive curvature.

**Prerequisites:** angle sum of geodesic triangles

$$
\text{Area}=R^2(\alpha_1+\alpha_2+\alpha_3-\pi)
$$

**Where:** GA §16.2 p.174 (pdf 191)

### Lambert's formula for hyperbolic triangles

*theorem · introduced* · also: hyperbolic angle defect formula

The area of a geodesic triangle in a hyperbolic plane with curvature -1/R^2 equals R^2 times the deficit of the angle sum below pi.

**How introduced:** Given in Example 16.7 and interpreted as Girard's theorem on a sphere of imaginary radius sqrt(-R^2), signalling negative curvature.

**Prerequisites:** Girard's theorem, open hyperbolic universe

$$
\text{Area}=-R^2(\alpha_1+\alpha_2+\alpha_3-\pi)
$$

**Where:** GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192)

### Poincaré disc model

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: Poincaré disc, Beltrami–Poincaré disc, conformal disc model

A representation of the entire hyperbolic plane inside a unit disc, obtained by projecting the hyperboloid; it preserves angles (conformal), and geodesics are diameters or circular arcs meeting the boundary circle at right angles.

**How introduced:** Shown in Fig. 16.3(b) to display a hyperbolic triangle and in Fig. 16.5 as a projection of the hyperboloid; margin note flags it as conformal and credits Beltrami.

**Prerequisites:** open hyperbolic universe, embedding of spatial sections

**Notes:** Conformal representations return in Chapter 19 (causal structure).

### volume of the closed universe

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: finite volume of the 3-sphere

The total proper volume of a k = +1 spatial slice, 2 pi^2 a(t)^3, finite because chi runs from 0 to pi.

**How introduced:** Computed by integrating 4 pi a^3 sin^2 chi dchi; used to justify the name closed and contrasted with the divergent k = -1 volume.

**Prerequisites:** closed spherical universe, hyperspherical radial coordinate chi

V=\int_0^\pi4\pi a^3\sin^2\chi\,d\chi=2\pi^2a^3

### proper distance in a Robertson–Walker spacetime

*physical-quantity · core* · also: proper length, l(t), d_p, geometrical distance

The length measured along a constant-cosmic-time slice between two comoving points, l(t) = a(t) chi, which equals a arcsin xi, a xi or a arsinh xi in terms of the areal coordinate xi for k = +1, 0, -1.

**How introduced:** Built in Example 16.3 by integrating the radial line element; plotted as l/a in Fig. 16.1 and later declared not directly measurable.

**Prerequisites:** Robertson–Walker metric, comoving coordinates, areal radial coordinate

$$
l(t)=\int_0^\xi\frac{a(t)\,dr}{\sqrt{1-kr^2}}
$$

**Notes:** It is defined on a hypersurface of simultaneity that no single observer can survey.

**Where:** GA §16.1 p.172 (pdf 189); GA §16.1 p.173 (pdf 190); GA §16.2 p.176 (pdf 193)

### Hubble parameter

*physical-quantity · revisited* · also: H(t), expansion rate

The fractional rate of change of the scale factor, H = (1/a) da/dt.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapter 15 and identified as the proportionality constant in the time derivative of proper distance.

**Prerequisites:** scale factor

$$
H(t)=\frac{\dot a}{a}
$$

**Where:** GA §16.1 p.173 (pdf 190); GA p.180 (pdf 197)

### Hubble's law

*law · developed* · also: velocity-distance relation, Hubble–Lemaître law

In any RW spacetime, the rate of change of proper distance to a comoving galaxy is proportional to that distance: v_p = H(t) d_p.

**How introduced:** Derived by differentiating the proper distances of Example 16.3 and noticing that the comoving factor equals l/a for all k.

**Prerequisites:** proper distance in a Robertson–Walker spacetime, Hubble parameter

$$
\frac{dl}{dt}=\frac{\dot a}{a}l
$$

$$
v_p=H(t)d_p
$$

**Notes:** The book does not discuss that v_p exceeds c beyond d = c/H; this is not a violation of relativity.

**Where:** GA §16.1 p.173 (pdf 190)

### radial null geodesic in a Robertson–Walker spacetime

*technique · developed* · also: light-ray equation, ds^2 = 0 radial ray

A light signal moving radially (fixed theta, phi) satisfies dt = ± a(t) dr/sqrt(1-kr^2), so the comoving coordinate it covers is the integral of dt/a.

**How introduced:** Written in §16.3 for crests travelling from a comoving galaxy to an observer at the origin.

**Prerequisites:** null geodesic, Robertson–Walker metric

$$
dt^2-a(t)^2\frac{dr^2}{1-kr^2}=0
$$

**Where:** GA §16.3 p.176 (pdf 193)

### comoving distance

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: eta, comoving radial coordinate distance

The time-independent coordinate separation covered by light, eta = integral of dt/a from emission to observation, equal to chi for the source; multiplying by a(t) gives the (time-dependent) proper distance.

**How introduced:** Introduced in margin note 18 while setting up the redshift derivation.

**Prerequisites:** radial null geodesic in a Robertson–Walker spacetime, hyperspherical radial coordinate chi

$$
\eta=\int_{t_{em}}^{t_{ob}}\frac{dt}{a(t)}=\int_0^{\chi}\frac{dr}{\sqrt{1-kr^2}}
$$

**Notes:** Many texts use eta for conformal time; here it denotes the comoving distance the ray covers, numerically the same integral.

**Where:** GA §16.3 p.176 (pdf 193)

### cosmological redshift

*phenomenon · core* · also: expansion redshift, cosmic redshift

The shift of light to lower frequency (for expansion) between comoving emitter and observer, fixed only by the scale factor at emission and observation: omega_ob/omega_em = a(t_em)/a(t_ob).

**How introduced:** Motivated as the only practical way to measure a(t) since rulers cannot measure expansion; derived by comparing two successive crests along radial null rays and cancelling a shared interval.

**Prerequisites:** radial null geodesic in a Robertson–Walker spacetime, comoving coordinates, scale factor

$$
\frac{\delta t_{em}}{a(t_{em})}=\frac{\delta t_{ob}}{a(t_{ob})}
$$

$$
\frac{\omega_{ob}}{\omega_{em}}=\frac{a(t_{em})}{a(t_{ob})}
$$

**Notes:** Contracting universes give a blueshift by the same formula.

**Where:** GA §16.3 p.176 (pdf 193); GA §16.3 p.177 (pdf 194); GA §16.3 p.178 (pdf 195)

### redshift parameter z

*definition · core* · also: z, redshift

The fractional increase in wavelength, z = (lambda_ob - lambda_em)/lambda_em; in RW cosmology 1+z = a(t_ob)/a(t_em), or 1/a(t_em) when today's scale factor is set to one.

**How introduced:** Defined immediately after the frequency result; the zero of time is often chosen where z would be infinite, a = 0.

**Prerequisites:** cosmological redshift, wavelength stretching with the scale factor

$$
z=\frac{\lambda_{ob}-\lambda_{em}}{\lambda_{em}}
$$

$$
1+z=\frac{a(t_{ob})}{a(t_{em})}
$$

**Where:** GA §16.3 p.178 (pdf 195); GA p.179 (pdf 196); GA p.180 (pdf 197)

### wavelength stretching with the scale factor

*principle · developed* · also: lambda proportional to a, frozen-in wavelength

Wavelengths of freely propagating radiation grow in proportion to a(t), as if printed on space that stretches; the same scaling holds for neutrinos and gravitational waves.

**How introduced:** Offered as an intuitive rereading of the frequency ratio; a margin note extends it beyond photons.

**Prerequisites:** cosmological redshift

$$
\lambda\propto a(t)
$$

**Where:** GA §16.3 p.178 (pdf 195)

### three causes of redshift

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: Doppler, gravitational and cosmological redshift

Frequency shifts of light can arise from relative motion (Doppler), from climbing out of a gravitational potential, and from the expansion of the universe between emission and reception.

**How introduced:** Listed at the start of §16.3 to position the cosmological shift as new; a margin box points forward to Chapter 22 for the gravitational case.

**Prerequisites:** Doppler effect, gravitational redshift, cosmological redshift

**Where:** GA §16.3 p.176 (pdf 193)

### initial singularity

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: Big Bang, Big-Bang singularity

The moment, a finite time in the past for most expanding models, at which a(t) = 0, the density diverges and spacetime curvature becomes infinite, so the point is removed from the spacetime and no known law applies.

**How introduced:** Previewed in the opening and discussed qualitatively in §16.4 by running an expanding, matter-filled model backwards; its treatment as an edge of spacetime is deferred to Chapter 19.

**Prerequisites:** scale factor, curvature singularity

$$
a(t_0)=0
$$

**Notes:** Revisited in Chapter 50, 'The Big-Bang singularity'.

**Where:** GA p.169 (pdf 186); GA §16.4 p.178 (pdf 195); GA p.179 (pdf 196)

### curvature singularity

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: singularity in the geometry, infinite curvature

A place where curvature invariants of spacetime diverge, so the metric field itself breaks down and cannot be continued by a coordinate change.

**How introduced:** Contrasted in §16.4 with a Newtonian singularity, where only density blows up: in GR the density sources the metric, so the geometry itself fails.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann curvature tensor

$$
R=6\left(\frac{\ddot a}{a}+\frac{\dot a^2}{a^2}+\frac{k}{a^2}\right)\to\infty
$$

**Where:** GA p.169 (pdf 186); GA §16.4 p.178 (pdf 195)

### redshift drift

*phenomenon · mention* · also: time derivative of redshift, Sandage–Loeb effect

The slow change in a source's redshift as the observer's time advances, related to the expansion rates at emission and observation by H(t_em) = H(t_ob)(1+z) - dz/dt_ob.

**How introduced:** Appears only in Exercise 16.9.

**Prerequisites:** redshift parameter z, Hubble parameter

$$
H(t_{em})=H(t_{ob})(1+z)-\frac{\partial z}{\partial t_{ob}}
$$

**Where:** GA p.180 (pdf 197)

### (16.1) Homogeneous isotropic metric form · central · GA §16.1 p.169 (pdf 186)

$$
ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+a(t)^{2}\gamma_{ij}\,dx^{i}dx^{j}
$$

Starting form of the cosmological metric, dictated by symmetry; the task is to find gamma_ij.

**Symbols:** a scale factor; gamma_ij spatial 3-metric; t cosmic time

### (16.2) Constant-curvature spatial Riemann tensor · central · GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187)

$$
{}^{(3)}R_{ijkl}=K(\gamma_{ik}\gamma_{jl}-\gamma_{il}\gamma_{jk})
$$

The only form of the spatial curvature tensor compatible with homogeneity, isotropy and Riemann symmetries.

**Symbols:** K Gaussian curvature (1/length^2)

### (16.3) · derivation-step · GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187)

$$
\gamma^{ik}(\gamma_{ik}\gamma_{jl}-\gamma_{il}\gamma_{jk})=3\gamma_{jl}-\gamma_{jl}=2\gamma_{jl}
$$

Contraction identity in three dimensions used to get the Ricci tensor.

**Symbols:** gamma^ik gamma_ik = 3

### (16.4) Spatial Ricci tensor · supporting · GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187)

$$
{}^{(3)}R_{jl}=2K\gamma_{jl},\qquad {}^{(3)}R=6K
$$

Ricci tensor and scalar of a 3-space of constant curvature (the scalar is unlabelled in the text).

### (16.5) Constant-curvature 3-metric in isotropic coordinates · central · GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187)

$$
\gamma_{ij}=\left[1+\frac{K}{4}(x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2})\right]^{-2}\delta_{ij}
$$

Robertson and Walker's metric that realizes eqn 16.2.

### (16.6) Constant-curvature spacetime · supporting · GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187)

$$
R_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}=C(g_{\mu\alpha}g_{\nu\beta}-g_{\mu\beta}g_{\nu\alpha})

Four-dimensional analogue (margin), e.g. de Sitter; distinct from constant spatial curvature.

**Symbols:** C constant

### (16.7) · derivation-step · GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187)

## Worked examples

### Example 16.1 · standard · GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187)

**Problem:** Put the constant-curvature RW metric (isotropic coordinates) into a form whose angular part is Euclidean-looking, then normalize the curvature to three values.

**Method:** Write in Cartesian and polar isotropic form; define r = R/(1 + KR^2/4) so the angular term is a^2 r^2 dOmega^2; verify g_rr becomes 1/(1-Kr^2); rescale r-bar = |K|^{1/2} r, k = K/|K| and absorb 1/sqrt|K| into a.

**Key insight:** Choosing the radial coordinate by the circumference it gives makes curvature appear only in g_rr, and the curvature magnitude can be hidden inside a length-valued a(t), leaving just its sign k.

**Result:** ds^2 = -dt^2 + a^2[dr^2/(1-kr^2) + r^2 dOmega^2] with k = +1, 0, -1 and K = k/a^2.

**Concepts:** areal radial coordinate, isotropic coordinates for constant-curvature space, curvature index k, scale factor

### Example 16.2 · standard · GA §16.1 p.171 (pdf 188)

**Problem:** Relate the three RW spaces to the 2-spaces of Appendix D, remove the factor 1-kr^2 with a new radial variable, and record metric, vielbein, connection and Ricci data.

**Method:** Analogy with sphere and two-sheet hyperboloid; substitute dchi^2 = dr^2/(1-kr^2) with r = sin chi, chi, sinh chi; list components in the margin (both r and chi forms).

**Key insight:** The same radial function Sigma(chi) encodes all three curvatures, and the RW Ricci components depend only on addot/a, (adot/a)^2 and k/a^2.

**Result:** Boxed chi form (16.15)-(16.17); R = 6(addot/a + adot^2/a^2 + k/a^2).

**Concepts:** hyperspherical radial coordinate chi, connection coefficients of the Robertson–Walker metric, embedding of spatial sections

### Example 16.3 · intro · GA §16.1 p.172 (pdf 189)

**Problem:** Find the proper distance at cosmic time t to a comoving galaxy at areal coordinate xi, and how fast it changes.

**Method:** Integrate a dr/sqrt(1-kr^2) from 0 to xi for each k, then differentiate with respect to t holding xi fixed.

**Key insight:** Because the comoving part is time independent, every proper distance grows at the same fractional rate adot/a regardless of curvature, which is Hubble's law.

**Result:** l = a arcsin xi, a xi, a arsinh xi; dl/dt = H l, i.e. v_p = H d_p.

**Concepts:** proper distance in a Robertson–Walker spacetime, Hubble's law, Hubble parameter

### Example 16.4 · intro · GA §16.2 p.174 (pdf 191)

**Problem:** Characterize the k = 0 spatial geometry.

**Method:** Compare with the Euclidean plane; compute circumference 2 pi a xi over proper radius a xi.

**Key insight:** Flat slices obey Euclid: parallels stay parallel, angle sums are pi, and the ratio is exactly 2 pi.

**Result:** Ratio = 2 pi.

**Concepts:** flat Robertson–Walker universe, circumference-to-radius curvature test

### Example 16.5 · intro · GA §16.2 p.174 (pdf 191)

**Problem:** Characterize the k = +1 spatial geometry.

**Method:** Use the 2-sphere as the model: meeting parallels, angle excess via Girard's theorem; compute circumference over proper radius with l = a arcsin xi.

**Key insight:** Since arcsin xi exceeds xi, circles are shorter than Euclid predicts, the hallmark of positive curvature.

**Result:** Ratio = 2 pi xi / arcsin xi <= 2 pi.

**Concepts:** closed spherical universe, Girard's theorem, circumference-to-radius curvature test

### Example 16.6 · intro · GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192)

**Problem:** Interpret the angle-like radial coordinate of the closed universe for light propagation.

**Method:** Treat chi as an angle on a circle and follow radial light rays around it (Fig. 16.4).

**Key insight:** A closed space lets light from one source arrive by two routes and, in principle, lets you see your own back, though realistic models run out of time (Chapter 19). The example also reminds the reader that with a growing a(t) the proper separation of fixed comoving points on the circle keeps increasing.

**Concepts:** closed spherical universe, hyperspherical radial coordinate chi

### Example 16.7 · intro · GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192)

**Problem:** Characterize the k = -1 spatial geometry.

**Method:** Hyperbolic model: diverging parallels, Lambert's area formula read as a sphere of imaginary radius, Poincaré disc picture; compute circumference over radius with l = a arsinh xi.

**Key insight:** arsinh xi is smaller than xi, so circles are longer than Euclid predicts: negative curvature.

**Result:** Ratio = 2 pi xi / arsinh xi >= 2 pi.

**Concepts:** open hyperbolic universe, Lambert's formula for hyperbolic triangles, Poincaré disc model

### Example 16.8 · standard · GA §16.3 p.176 (pdf 193)

**Problem:** Relate the observed period of light from a distant comoving galaxy to the emitted period.

**Method:** Integrate the radial null condition for two crests; equate since the comoving distance is the same; split the time integrals and cancel the common part; approximate 1/a as constant over one period.

**Key insight:** The comoving distance light covers in one period at emission equals that at reception, so periods scale with a.

**Result:** delta t_em / a(t_em) = delta t_ob / a(t_ob), hence omega_ob/omega_em = a(t_em)/a(t_ob).

**Concepts:** cosmological redshift, radial null geodesic in a Robertson–Walker spacetime, comoving distance

### Example 16.9 · intro · GA §16.3 p.177 (pdf 194)

**Problem:** Decide the sign of the shift for expanding and contracting universes, including light around the closed universe of Fig. 16.4.

**Method:** Apply eqn 16.46 with a(t_ob) larger or smaller than a(t_em).

**Key insight:** Only the ratio of scale factors matters, not the route or distance; expansion reddens, contraction blues.

**Result:** Expanding: omega_ob < omega_em (redshift); contracting: blueshift.

**Concepts:** cosmological redshift

### Example · intro · GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187)

**Problem:** Obtain the spatial Ricci tensor and scalar from the constant-curvature Riemann tensor.

**Method:** Contract eqn 16.2 with gamma^ik using gamma^ik gamma_ik = 3 (margin eqn 16.3), then contract again.

**Key insight:** In n dimensions the factor would be (n-1)K; in 3D it is 2K, and the scalar is 6K.

**Result:** (3)R_jl = 2K gamma_jl, (3)R = 6K.

**Concepts:** spatial Ricci tensor and scalar, space of constant curvature

### Example · intro · GA §16.2 p.174 (pdf 191)

**Problem:** Compute the total proper volume of a k = +1 spatial slice.

**Method:** Integrate the shell volume 4 pi (a sin chi)^2 a dchi over 0 <= chi <= pi.

**Key insight:** The bounded range of chi turns the angular coordinate range into finite volume without any boundary.

**Result:** V = 2 pi^2 a^3.

**Concepts:** volume of the closed universe, closed spherical universe

### Example · intro · GA §16.3 p.178 (pdf 195)

**Problem:** Express the frequency result as a redshift parameter.

**Method:** Convert omega ratio to wavelength ratio, define z, and set a(t_ob) = 1.

**Key insight:** Measuring z directly measures the scale factor at emission relative to today.

**Result:** 1 + z = a(t_ob)/a(t_em) = 1/a(t_em).

**Concepts:** redshift parameter z, wavelength stretching with the scale factor


## Analogies and intuitions

### Pressing a curved skin flat: a positively curved cap (like a ball) must tear, a negatively curved patch (a saddle is our example; the book just says negative curvature) must overlap and fold → circumference-to-radius curvature test · strong · GA p.169 (pdf 186)

Opening margin note sets up the idea that positive curvature makes circles too short and negative curvature too long; §16.2 explicitly reuses it for all three RW spaces.

**Where it breaks down:** Uses 2D surfaces seen from outside; the 3D slices must be diagnosed intrinsically with spheres or circles inside them.

**App idea:** Peel a spherical cap and a saddle patch onto a plane in an animation, showing gaps or overlaps, then show the corresponding circle ratio.

### A sphere's Gaussian curvature is 1/r^2, so a(t) is 'the radius of the universe' → scale factor · useful · GA §16.1 p.171 (pdf 188)

Justifies calling the length-valued scale factor a radius after the rescaling K = k/a^2.

**Where it breaks down:** Meaningful only for k = +1; for k = 0 a has no geometric size and the 'radius' of a hyperbolic space is imaginary in this sense.

### Two-dimensional creatures living on a globe's surface can find no middle and no rim → closed spherical universe · strong · GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192)

Explains why a finite-volume closed universe has neither boundary nor centre; those only appear from the fictitious embedding space.

**Where it breaks down:** The embedding picture itself suggests a centre inside the ball, which the analogy must then disown.

**App idea:** Flatland walker on a sphere: first-person view where walking straight always returns to the start and every point looks the same.

### The radial coordinate of a closed universe is like an angle around a circle; light can lap the universe and let you see the back of your own head → hyperspherical radial coordinate chi · strong · GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192)

Example 16.6 with Fig. 16.4 makes the topology of the 3-sphere concrete through light propagation.

**Where it breaks down:** A margin note says realistic expanding models do not allow enough time for a full lap; the circle picture suppresses two dimensions.

**App idea:** Animated photon lapping a circle universe with an expansion-history slider showing whether the lap completes.

### Lambert's hyperbolic area formula is Girard's spherical formula for a sphere of imaginary radius → open hyperbolic universe · useful · GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192)

Presents negative curvature as the analytic continuation of positive curvature, R^2 -> -R^2, and sin -> sinh.

**Where it breaks down:** Formal; no actual surface with imaginary radius exists in Euclidean space.

**App idea:** Slider for curvature K from positive through zero to negative with Sigma(chi) morphing from sin to chi to sinh and triangle angle sums updating.

### The Poincaré disc as a shadow of the hyperboloid → Poincaré disc model · useful · GA §16.2 p.176 (pdf 193)

Fig. 16.5 shows the infinite hyperbolic sheet squeezed into a finite disc by projection.

**Where it breaks down:** Distances are badly distorted near the rim; only angles are faithful.

**App idea:** Linked hyperboloid-disc projection view.

### A photon's wavelength is frozen into space and stretches with it → wavelength stretching with the scale factor · strong · GA §16.3 p.178 (pdf 195)

Rereads the frequency result as a stretching of the wave pattern by the factor a(t_ob)/a(t_em).

**Where it breaks down:** Can suggest that all lengths (atoms, galaxies, rulers) stretch, or that the redshift is caused locally by space pulling on photons; bound systems do not expand.

**App idea:** Sine wave drawn on a stretching rubber strip with a comoving grid, next to an atom that keeps its size.

### A ruler big enough to measure the expansion would expand with it → cosmological redshift · weak · GA §16.3 p.176 (pdf 193)

Motivates why redshift, not direct length measurement, is the observable.

**Where it breaks down:** Physically misleading as stated: rigid bound rulers do not follow the Hubble flow; the real problem is that we cannot lay a ruler over cosmological distances at an instant of cosmic time.


## Misconceptions addressed

### The Robertson–Walker metric is a solution of Einstein's equation. · GA §16.1 p.169 (pdf 186)

**Correction:** Its form follows from homogeneity and isotropy alone; Einstein's equation with matter only determines a(t) and relates k to content (Chapter 17).

**Why tempting:** Most metrics met earlier (e.g. Schwarzschild) were derived by solving the field equations.

### If space is flat (k = 0) then spacetime is flat. · GA §16.2 p.174 (pdf 191)

**Correction:** With a time-dependent a(t) the spacetime Riemann tensor is non-zero; only constant a gives Minkowski space.

**Why tempting:** The word flat is used for the k = 0 case without qualification.

### The spatial curvature quantities (3)R_ij = 2K gamma_ij go straight into Einstein's equation. · GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187)

**Correction:** They describe only the slices; Einstein's equation needs the four-dimensional Ricci tensor, which also contains adot and addot terms.

**Why tempting:** Both are called Ricci tensors and look similar.

### Constant spatial curvature means the spacetime has constant curvature. · GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187)

**Correction:** RW slices have constant curvature at each time, but the spacetime generally does not; de Sitter is a special case where it does.

**Why tempting:** The phrase 'space of constant curvature' is used for both.

### A closed (finite) universe must have an edge or a centre. · GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192)

**Correction:** A 3-sphere is finite yet boundaryless and homogeneous; the centre of the embedding ball is not part of the universe.

**Why tempting:** Every finite object in everyday life has a boundary, and embedding pictures show a ball with a middle.

### 'Open universe' means the k = -1 case only. · GA §16.2 p.176 (pdf 193)

**Correction:** Both k = 0 and k = -1 have infinite volume; the book therefore prefers the terms flat and hyperbolic.

**Why tempting:** Older literature pairs open/flat/closed with k = -1/0/+1.

### The whole hyperbolic space can be drawn as a surface in ordinary Euclidean space. · GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192)

**Correction:** Only pieces can; the full space needs a Minkowski ambient space or a model such as the Poincaré disc.

**Why tempting:** Saddle surfaces look like hyperbolic geometry.

### In a closed universe you really could see the back of your own head. · GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192)

**Correction:** It is geometrically possible in a static closed space, but in physically realistic models light does not have time to go around (Chapter 19).

**Why tempting:** The geometric picture of Fig. 16.4(b) seems to guarantee it.

### Cosmological redshift is just a Doppler shift from galaxies moving through space. · GA §16.3 p.176 (pdf 193)

**Correction:** It is a distinct effect fixed entirely by a(t_em)/a(t_ob) for comoving sources, not by a relative velocity at any instant; the book lists it as a third cause of redshift.

**Why tempting:** Hubble's law reads like a velocity law, and small-z redshifts match v/c.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Redshift is set directly by the source's distance or by the route the light takes. · GA §16.3 p.177 (pdf 194)

**Correction:** The shift is fixed by the scale factor at the emission and reception events alone. Distance and route matter only because they determine how long ago the light left (t_em); two sources at different distances whose light left at the same cosmic time show the same z, and light reaching A by the long and short routes of Fig. 16.4(a) is reddened according to its own emission time.

**Why tempting:** Distance and redshift correlate observationally.

### Hubble's law implies nothing can recede faster than light. · GA §16.1 p.173 (pdf 190)

**Correction:** v_p = H d_p is a rate of change of proper distance on a slice, not a local velocity; it exceeds c beyond d_p = c/H without contradicting relativity.

**Why tempting:** It is written as a velocity.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Everything, including atoms, planets and rulers, expands with the universe. · GA §16.3 p.176 (pdf 193)

**Correction:** Only systems not bound together follow the Hubble flow; bound systems keep their size, which is exactly what lets us compare wavelengths.

**Why tempting:** The chapter's ruler remark and the stretched-wavelength picture suggest universal stretching.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The Big Bang was an explosion at a point in a pre-existing space. · GA §16.4 p.178 (pdf 195)

**Correction:** a -> 0 everywhere at once; every point of an infinite k = 0 or -1 universe is at the singularity, which is a boundary of spacetime rather than an event inside space.

**Why tempting:** The chapter's opening talks of geometries shrinking to a point.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Seeing the back of your own head · GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192)

**Setup:** In a closed universe, light scattered backwards off your head travels radially all the way around the chi circle and returns to your eyes.

**Lesson:** The 3-sphere is finite and edgeless; straight lines close on themselves, but real expansion histories limit this.

**App idea:** Animated lap of a photon on an expanding circle universe with a completion meter.

### Two crests from a distant galaxy · GA §16.3 p.176 (pdf 193)

**Setup:** A comoving galaxy emits two successive wave crests separated by one period; both travel along radial null rays to a comoving observer.

**Lesson:** Equal comoving distances force the received period to be stretched by a(t_ob)/a(t_em).

**App idea:** Spacetime diagram with shaded 1/a areas for the two rays (see Fig. 16.6 redesign).

### Measuring circles in the universe · GA §16.2 p.173 (pdf 190)

**Setup:** Galaxies at fixed comoving radius xi from us form a circle; compare its measured circumference with its measured radius.

**Lesson:** The ratio relative to 2 pi reveals the sign of spatial curvature from inside.

**App idea:** Circle-growing tool on sphere, plane, and hyperbolic disc with a ratio meter.

### Measuring expansion with a ruler · GA §16.3 p.176 (pdf 193)

**Setup:** Try to measure cosmic expansion by laying a gigantic ruler between galaxies.

**Lesson:** Direct length measurement at an instant is impossible in practice; the accessible observable is the frequency shift of light.

### Running the expanding universe backwards · GA §16.4 p.178 (pdf 195)

**Setup:** Follow a matter-filled expanding model back in time as the density rises.

**Lesson:** a reaches zero a finite time ago; density and curvature diverge and the classical description must stop.

**App idea:** Reverse-time animation of comoving grid shrinking with a curvature-scalar gauge heading to infinity.


## Applications and observations

- **Redshift as the observable of cosmic expansion** (observation, GA §16.3 p.178 (pdf 195)): Measuring z for a source gives the ratio of today's scale factor to the scale factor when the light left, so a catalogue of redshifts (with independent distance or time information) maps out a(t). Key numbers: 1+z = a(t_ob)/a(t_em); with a(t_ob) = 1, a(t_em) = 1/(1+z). Own illustration: the cosmic microwave background at z about 1100 was emitted when the universe was about 1/1100 of its present linear size.
- **Hubble's law in curved RW universes** (observation, GA §16.1 p.173 (pdf 190)): The proportionality of recession rate to proper distance holds in all three geometries, so Hubble's observed linear law does not by itself determine k. Key numbers: v_p = H d_p with H = adot/a. Own estimate: for H_0 about 70 km/s/Mpc, v_p reaches c at d_p = c/H_0 of roughly 4.3 Gpc.
- **Finite size of a closed universe** (numerical-estimate, GA §16.2 p.174 (pdf 191)): A k = +1 universe of radius a has volume 2 pi^2 a^3, and the greatest proper distance from any point (the antipode) is pi a. Key numbers: V = 2 pi^2 a^3 (about 19.7 a^3); antipodal distance pi a; the areal coordinate r reaches 1 at proper distance pi a/2.
- **Redshift of neutrinos and gravitational waves** (observation, GA §16.3 p.178 (pdf 195)): The wavelength-proportional-to-a scaling applies not only to photons but also to cosmological neutrinos and gravitational waves. Key numbers: lambda proportional to a
- **Redshift drift as a probe of expansion history** (data-analysis, GA p.180 (pdf 197)): Exercise 16.9 shows that the rate of change of a source's redshift with observer time encodes the expansion rate at emission relative to today. Key numbers: H(t_em) = H(t_ob)(1+z) - dz/dt_ob

## Historical notes

- **Howard P. Robertson, Arthur G. Walker:** Showed that the constant-curvature metric in eqn 16.5 is the only metric compatible with homogeneity and isotropy (the book gives no date; historically their work dates to 1935-1936). — Establishes that cosmology on large scales has only three geometric options, labelled by k. (GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187))
- **Albert Girard:** Spherical triangle area formula (angle excess), recalled from Chapter 3. — Two-dimensional template for positive curvature. (GA §16.2 p.174 (pdf 191))
- **Nikolai I. Lobachevsky:** One of several independent discoverers of hyperbolic geometry, which is sometimes named after him. — Provides the geometry of the k = -1 universe; margin note adds the Tom Lehrer song anecdote. (GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192))
- **Johann Heinrich Lambert:** Formula for the area of a hyperbolic triangle from its angle deficit. — Two-dimensional template for negative curvature. (GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192))
- **Eugenio Beltrami, Henri Poincaré (1868):** Beltrami formulated the disc model of hyperbolic geometry; Poincaré rediscovered it fourteen years later and it bears his name. — Conformal representation of hyperbolic space used in Figs 16.3(b) and 16.5, and a precursor of the conformal methods of Chapter 19. (GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192))
- **Tom Lehrer:** Satirical song about Lobachevsky, with Lehrer noting the name was chosen for its rhythm. — Humorous aside humanizing the history of non-Euclidean geometry. (GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192))
- **Charles Sanders Peirce:** Chapter epigraph about the universe being too large to have any particular character. — Sets the tone that on large scales the universe is featureless, i.e. homogeneous and isotropic. (GA p.169 (pdf 186))

## Notation and conventions

- **Metric signature and units:** Signature (-, +, +, +) with c = 1; the RW line element begins -dt^2. (GA §16.1 p.169 (pdf 186))
- **(3) prefix:** A left superscript (3) marks tensors built from the spatial 3-metric of a constant-t slice, e.g. (3)R_ijkl, (3)R. — Latin indices i, j, k, l run over spatial coordinates; Greek over spacetime. (GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187))
- **gamma_ij versus g_mu nu:** gamma_ij is the comoving spatial metric without the a^2 factor; the full spatial metric components are a^2 gamma_ij. — The curvature K in (3)R_ijkl refers to gamma_ij, so after rescaling the physical curvature of a slice is k/a^2. (GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187))
- **K, k and C:** K is the dimensional Gaussian curvature (1/length^2); k in {+1, 0, -1} its sign after rescaling; C is used for the constant in a constant-curvature spacetime to avoid confusion with K. — Many texts call C by K as well. (GA §16.1 p.171 (pdf 188))
- **Scale factor dimensions:** a(t) starts dimensionless and is redefined as a length (a/sqrt|K|) for k = ±1, making r dimensionless; later normalizations set a(t_ob) = 1. — Mixed conventions within the book: dimensionful a here versus a(today) = 1 in the redshift margin note. (GA §16.1 p.171 (pdf 188))
- **Bar dropped from rescaled r:** After rescaling, r-bar is written simply as r. (GA §16.1 p.171 (pdf 188))
- **Radial coordinates r, R, chi, xi:** R isotropic radius; r areal (circumference) radius; chi hyperspherical radius with r = Sigma(chi); xi a fixed comoving value of r for a particular galaxy. — In §16.3 the source is placed at 'r = chi', using chi as a label for the r value while the upper limit of the integral is written chi; a learner may confuse this with the chi coordinate. (GA §16.1 p.172 (pdf 189))
- **Sigma(chi):** sin chi, chi, sinh chi for k = +1, 0, -1. — Other books write S_k(chi) or f_k(chi). (GA §16.1 p.172 (pdf 189))
- **Coordinate ranges:** phi runs from -pi to pi; chi from 0 to pi for k = +1 and 0 to infinity otherwise. (GA §16.1 p.172 (pdf 189))
- **Hatted indices:** Hats denote orthonormal-frame components obtained with vielbeins (e_mu)^{mu-hat}. (GA §16.1 p.171 (pdf 188))
- **Proper distance symbols:** l(t) or d_p for proper distance, v_p = dl/dt for proper velocity; exercises use a script ell with subscripts em and ob. (GA §16.1 p.173 (pdf 190))
- **em/ob subscripts and eta:** t_em, t_ob emission and observation times; eta denotes the comoving distance integral of dt/a. — Elsewhere eta often denotes conformal time. (GA §16.3 p.176 (pdf 193))
- **Universe labels:** Universe 0 and Universe 1 (de Sitter) refer to the spatially flat toy models of Chapter 15. (GA §16.1 p.169 (pdf 186))
- **Terminology for k:** The book prefers spherical/flat/hyperbolic over closed/flat/open for k = +1/0/-1. — k = +1 is also called elliptic. (GA §16.2 p.176 (pdf 193))
- **t_0 in Section 16.4:** Here t_0 labels the moment at which a(t) vanishes (the initial singularity), and the text also mentions choosing the time origin so that a(0) = 0. — Many cosmology texts, and later usage, reserve t_0 for the present epoch; flag the clash so learners do not read t_0 as 'today'. (GA §16.4 p.178 (pdf 195))

## Teaching gems

### Decide what the curvature tensor must look like from symmetry before searching for any metric. · GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187)

**Why it works:** Shows learners that the three cosmological geometries are forced by the cosmological principle, not chosen, and gives a template (symmetry fixes tensor form) that recurs throughout physics.

**App idea:** Tensor 'symmetry filter': learners toggle candidate building blocks (gamma_ij, a vector n_i, epsilon_ijk) and see which survive rotations and Riemann symmetries, leaving the single K structure.

### Pick the radial coordinate so that circumferences come out as 2 pi a r. · GA §16.1 p.170 (pdf 187)

**Why it works:** Coordinate choice becomes a physical demand rather than an algebra trick, and it explains why curvature then shows up only in g_rr.

**App idea:** On a sphere, drag a circle and display three candidate radii (isotropic R, areal r, arc length chi) with the circumference; learners see which label matches 2 pi r.

### One number, circumference over radius compared with 2 pi, distinguishes all three geometries. · GA §16.2 p.173 (pdf 190)

**Why it works:** Reduces an abstract classification to a measurement a learner could imagine doing with galaxies.

**App idea:** Three side-by-side worlds with a shared radius slider and a ratio gauge that dips below, sits at, or rises above 2 pi.

### Hyperbolic formulas are spherical formulas with an imaginary radius (Girard -> Lambert, sin -> sinh). · GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192)

**Why it works:** Unifies the three cases under a single curvature parameter, so learners remember one formula family instead of three.

**App idea:** Continuous curvature slider from +1/R^2 through 0 to -1/R^2 driving Sigma(chi), triangle angle sums, and volume growth plots.

### Treat the closed universe's radial coordinate as an angle, so light can lap the universe and show you your own back. · GA §16.2 p.175 (pdf 192)

**Why it works:** A vivid, slightly absurd image fixes the topology of the 3-sphere in memory, and the margin caveat invites a later payoff.

**App idea:** Photon-lap animation on an expanding circle with a 'did it make it?' result depending on a(t).

### Split the two null-ray integrals so the long shared interval cancels, leaving one period at each end. · GA §16.3 p.177 (pdf 194)

**Why it works:** Turns an apparently global calculation into a local comparison, and the timing diagram makes the cancellation visible.

**App idea:** Area-under-1/a spacetime diagram with two draggable emission times and live cancellation shading.

### Redshift depends only on a at emission and at observation: the wavelength is stretched with space. · GA §16.3 p.178 (pdf 195)

**Why it works:** Gives an instantly usable rule (1+z = a_ob/a_em) and a picture that transfers to neutrinos and gravitational waves.

**App idea:** Wave on a stretching grid, with a photon, a neutrino wave packet and a GW strain pattern all scaling identically.

### Flat space is not flat spacetime: k = 0 with a changing a is curved. · GA §16.2 p.174 (pdf 191)

**Why it works:** Pre-empts a persistent confusion about 'flat universe' language and connects directly to the Ricci scalar formula.

**App idea:** Curvature readout panel showing (3)R and the 4D Ricci scalar separately as the learner changes k and the expansion history.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Printed margin Ricci component R_ii-hat ends with 2k/a; it must be 2k/a^2 (dimensions, and consistency with the stated Ricci scalar).** — Anyone deriving the Friedmann equations from the margin list gets a dimensionally inconsistent curvature term. *Suggestion:* Show the corrected component and verify -R_tt + 3R_ii = R as a check.
- **Exercise 16.4: printed R_rr = Lambda'/r should be 2 Lambda'/r, so the first condition in (d) should be Lambda' = Kr e^{2 Lambda}; the second condition prints e^{-Lambda} where e^{-2 Lambda} is needed.** — Following the printed equations does not lead to the stated answer dr^2/(1-Kr^2), causing confusion about what went wrong. *Suggestion:* Provide corrected equations; check with Lambda = -ln(1-Kr^2)/2.
- **Fig. 16.6 caption writes the second observation time as t_ob + delta t_em (should be delta t_ob) and 'comment' for 'common'.** — Suggests periods are unchanged, contradicting the result being derived. *Suggestion:* Label the diagram with delta t_ob and highlight that it differs from delta t_em.
- **The claims that eqn 16.2 is the only possible arrangement and that 16.5 is the only matching metric are asserted, not derived, in the text.** — Learners may accept the uniqueness without understanding the role of isotropy about every point (Schur's theorem) or of local vs global uniqueness. *Suggestion:* Point to Exercises 16.4-16.5 and mention that topology is not fixed by local geometry.
- **The areal coordinate r for k = +1 only covers half the 3-sphere (r = sin chi reaches 1 at the equator); g_rr diverges at r = 1 and Fig. 16.1 stops there.** — The finite limit pi a/2 in Example 16.3 can be mistaken for the size of the universe, and the divergence for a physical singularity. *Suggestion:* Prefer the chi form for global statements; show that the maximum proper distance is pi a.
- **At the start of §16.2 the text recalls l(t) = a xi for a galaxy at coordinate xi, which is only the k = 0 result.** — Makes the circumference-to-radius comparison look trivially equal to 2 pi for all k. *Suggestion:* State that the circumference is 2 pi a xi in all cases while the proper radius depends on k.
- **Setting K = 0 is said to give 'the flat spacetime of the de Sitter model'; in fact it gives the spatially flat RW family with arbitrary a(t), of which de Sitter (exponential a) is one case and which is curved spacetime unless a is constant.** — Mixes spatial and spacetime flatness and ties the flat case to one specific expansion law. *Suggestion:* Say 'spatially flat metric of the form used for Universe 1'.
- **In §16.3 the source is placed at 'r = chi' and the integrals use chi as their upper limit, while chi is also the hyperspherical coordinate whose value is the integral itself.** — Notation collision obscures that the comoving distance eta equals the chi coordinate of the source. *Suggestion:* Call the source's areal coordinate r_1 and write eta = chi(r_1).
- **The ruler argument for why expansion cannot be measured directly implies measuring devices expand; bound objects do not follow the Hubble flow.** — Reinforces the misconception that everything expands, which would make redshift undetectable. *Suggestion:* Replace with: proper distances live on a constant-time slice that no observer can survey; only light signals connect us to distant galaxies.
- **The redshift derivation silently assumes comoving source and observer and uses coordinate time as proper time (g_tt = -1); peculiar velocities and the combination with Doppler shifts are not discussed.** — Learners may apply 1+z = a_ob/a_em to objects with large peculiar motions or wonder why t can be used as a clock. *Suggestion:* State the assumptions explicitly and note that total redshift multiplies (1+z_total) = (1+z_cos)(1+z_pec).
- **Closed/open is equated with k = +1 versus k <= 0, and the opening says the geometries 'shrink to a point'; topology and dynamics are conflated with local geometry.** — Learners may think flat universes must be infinite, or that the singularity is a point in space. *Suggestion:* Mention that flat or hyperbolic spaces can be compactified, and that a -> 0 happens everywhere simultaneously; whether it happens depends on the Friedmann dynamics, not the metric form.
- **Hubble's law via proper velocity is given without noting superluminal recession beyond c/H or the distinction from measured velocities.** — Common confusion about faster-than-light recession and about interpreting redshift as velocity. *Suggestion:* Add a short note and a numerical example with the Hubble distance.
- **The k = -1 slice is described (main text and Example 16.2) as a '2-sheet hyperboloid'; the spatial universe is a single sheet of the two-sheeted surface w^2 - x^2 - y^2 - z^2 = a^2, as Fig. 16.2(b) correctly draws.** — A learner may picture two disconnected copies of space, or think the open universe has two components. *Suggestion:* Say 'one sheet (w > 0) of a two-sheeted hyperboloid' and point to the single bowl in Fig. 16.2(b).

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'If the universe looks the same everywhere and in every direction, how many different shapes could space have?' Let the learner guess before revealing the answer: three, labelled by the sign of one curvature number.
- Check that the learner remembers from Chapter 15 why the metric has the -dt^2 + a^2 gamma_ij form; emphasize that no field equation has been used yet.
- Teach the three geometries in 2D first (sphere, plane, saddle or Poincaré disc) using circles and triangles, then promote to 3D by analogy. Ask: 'On a sphere, is a circle's circumference bigger or smaller than 2 pi times its radius measured along the surface?'
- Keep the radial coordinates straight explicitly: R (isotropic), r (areal, circumference-defined), chi (proper radial distance divided by a), xi (a particular galaxy's r). Write r = Sigma(chi) on the board and return to it whenever confusion arises.
- When a(t) changes from dimensionless to a length, flag it: after rescaling the curvature of a slice is k/a^2, so a genuinely is a radius of curvature for k = +1.
- Check understanding with: 'Is a k = 0 universe with growing a(t) curved?' The correct answer is that space is flat but spacetime is curved; point to the addot and adot terms in the Ricci scalar.
- For the redshift, draw the two-ray timing picture first and ask the learner to identify which time interval is shared; then let them write delta t/a = constant themselves. Follow up: 'Does the path or the distance enter the final formula?'
- Use a numerical anchor: if light left when the universe was half its present size, z = 1; the CMB at z of about 1100 left when distances were about 1100 times smaller (own illustration).
- Anticipate the questions 'Can galaxies recede faster than light?', 'Does my ruler expand?', and 'Where did the Big Bang happen?' and answer with the proper-distance-on-a-slice idea, bound systems, and a -> 0 everywhere at once.

- For advanced learners, connect the Ricci components here to the Friedmann equations of Chapter 17 and have them verify the trace R = -R_tt + 3R_ii.
- For beginners, skip the connection coefficient list and Exercise 16.4; the essential takeaways are the metric with k, proper distance and Hubble's law, the three geometries, and 1+z = a_ob/a_em.
