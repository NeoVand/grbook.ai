# GA ch46 · Gravitational waves

> Builds gravitational waves by replaying the electromagnetic gauge-theory argument with one extra index: vacuum plane waves of the trace-reversed perturbation, transverse-traceless gauge and the + and x polarizations read off from geodesic deviation of a ring of test masses, a conservation-law argument that gravity radiates first at quadrupole order, the Einstein quadrupole formula and the quadrupole luminosity, binary inspiral and the Hulse-Taylor pulsar, an exact plane-wave check in light-cone coordinates, and the interferometric detection of GW150914 with a survey of future detectors.

**Pages:** printed 494–511 · pdf 511–528 · **Difficulty:** math 4/5, conceptual 4/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

The EM-first framing and ring-of-masses pictures are friendly, and the numerical estimates are accessible. The TT gauge construction and geodesic-deviation algebra need solid index skills; the energy-flux section quotes the Isaacson tensor and relies on two long exercises; Section 46.5 needs connection and curvature computation in null coordinates. Several printed slips (eqn 46.24 missing terms, J formula, Gamma^v_xx, dropped c factors and signs in 46.68 and 46.77, historical dates) and OCR losses of dots in the reading copy can confuse a careful reader.

## Role in the book

Chapter 45 linearized the Einstein equation and, in Lorenz gauge, reduced it to a sourced wave equation; this chapter cashes that in. It sits in Part VI because the whole treatment is framed as field theory: Chapter 42's electromagnetic potentials, gauge freedom and retarded solutions supply the template, and each step for light is repeated for the metric perturbation. Geodesic deviation (Chapters 11 and 35) turns the abstract solution into measurable tidal motion, and the energy-momentum tensor of Chapter 12 underlies the radiated-power calculation. The chapter closes the classical account of gravitational radiation and hands over to Chapter 47, which quantizes the same waves into gravitons and explains the spin-2 character hinted at by the 45-degree polarization rotation.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can reproduce the electromagnetic chain (Lorenz gauge, wave equation, Coulomb gauge, transverse polarization vectors) and say which step gains an index in gravity.
- Reader can substitute a plane-wave ansatz into the vacuum wave equation and Lorenz condition to derive k.k = 0 and k^mu A_{mu nu} = 0, and interpret them as propagation at c and transversality.
- Reader can use a residual gauge transformation with d^2 xi = 0 to impose tracelessness and A_{mu nu}u^nu = 0, reaching transverse-traceless gauge and the 2x2 amplitude matrix with two free entries.
- Reader can explain why a free test mass keeps fixed TT coordinates while the proper distance to a neighbour oscillates, and compute that oscillation to first order in the amplitude.
- Reader can derive the relative accelerations of test masses from geodesic deviation with weak-field Riemann components, and draw the + and x deformation patterns of a ring of particles.
- Reader can explain why the two gravitational polarizations are 45 degrees apart while electromagnetic ones are 90 degrees apart, linking this to the rank of the field.
- Reader can argue from conservation of mass, momentum and angular momentum that gravitational radiation has no monopole or dipole part and starts at quadrupole order.
- Reader can state the Einstein quadrupole formula, estimate the strain from a stellar-mass binary at 100 Mpc (about 1e-21), and explain the role of retarded time.
- Reader can explain why gravitational-wave energy is only defined after averaging over several wavelengths, and quote the effective stress-energy t_{mu nu} in TT gauge.
- Reader can state the quadrupole luminosity L = (G/5c^5)<dddot I_ij dddot I_ij>, apply it to an equal-mass circular binary, and derive the period decay dP/dt.
- Reader can explain why a bound binary speeds up (spin-up) as it radiates, and connect this to the Hulse-Taylor pulsar timing data.
- Reader can set up the light-cone metric -du dv + F(u)^2 dx^2 + G(u)^2 dy^2, obtain the vacuum condition F''/F + G''/G = 0, and recognize the linearized + wave inside it.
- Reader can explain how a Michelson interferometer converts h_+ into a phase difference proportional to arm length, and why LIGO needs km arms and Fabry-Perot cavities.
- Reader can place ground interferometers, LISA, pulsar timing arrays and the primordial background on the gravitational-wave frequency spectrum.

## Teaching approach

An analogy-first, derivation-heavy chapter that treats gravity as a second gauge field. Every move is first rehearsed for electromagnetism (gauge fixing, plane waves, polarization) and then repeated for the metric perturbation, with the extra index being the recurring source of novelty. Physical meaning is extracted operationally from the motion of free test masses, with a deliberately staged false start (the coordinate of a mass does not move) to teach coordinate suspicion. Formal results are carried mostly in boxed Examples; heavy algebra (Isaacson stress-energy, TT projection, the conservation-law route to the quadrupole formula) is pushed to exercises. The second half pivots to order-of-magnitude astrophysics and a descriptive survey of detection.

**Style:** analogy-driven, derivation-heavy, example-driven, physical-motivation-first, observational-astrophysics, conversational-informal, survey-overview

**Narrative arc**

1. Motivate with history: prediction, the 1993 and 2017 Nobel prizes, and the promise of describing LIGO.
2. Rehearse electromagnetism: sourceless wave equation, Lorenz gauge, plane waves, Coulomb gauge leaving two transverse polarizations.
3. Replay for gravity: Lorenz-gauge wave equation for hbar, plane-wave ansatz, constraints k.k=0 and transversality; margin argument that the wave rides with a light ray.
4. Exhaust the residual gauge freedom with an oscillatory xi to reach transverse-traceless gauge; write the amplitude matrix and the oscillating line element; split into two polarizations related by 45 degrees.
5. Stage a paradox: a single test mass does not move in these coordinates; resolve it with proper distance between two masses.
6. Use geodesic deviation with weak-field Riemann components to get relative accelerations, integrate to the displacement formula, and draw the ring-of-masses and acceleration-field pictures; define the tidal field and polarization tensors.
7. Ask what sources can radiate: multipole expansion for electromagnetism, then conservation laws killing gravitational monopole and dipole terms.
8. Solve the sourced equation with retarded Green's function, apply the compact-source limit and energy-momentum conservation to reach the Einstein quadrupole formula; estimate the strain for a black-hole binary.
9. Confront the smallness of h and the problem of defining wave energy: background split, second-order Einstein tensor, averaging to get t_{mu nu}.
10. Derive radiated power by flux through a large sphere and angular integration; obtain quadrupole luminosity and angular-momentum loss.
11. Apply to an equal-mass Newtonian binary: luminosity, energy-period relation, period decay; connect to Hulse-Taylor spin-up; warn that Newtonian and linear assumptions fail at merger and that gravity is nonlinear.
12. Show waves survive nonlinearity with an exact plane-wave metric in light-cone coordinates, recovering the + polarization at small amplitude.
13. Describe detection: Michelson geometry, phase shift proportional to arm length times strain, LIGO parameters, GW150914; survey ET, Cosmic Explorer, LISA, pulsar timing arrays and the primordial background; point ahead to gravitons.

**Signature moves**

- Parallel-derivation pedagogy: the gravitational argument is literally a rerun of the electromagnetic one with square matrices replacing column vectors.
- Deliberate dead end ('the particle doesn't move') used to teach that coordinate values in a chosen gauge carry no physics, followed by the proper-distance rescue.
- Physical meaning of polarization given by deformations of a circle of free masses and by quadrupole-magnet-like acceleration field lines.
- Radiation selection rules argued from conservation laws (charge for EM; mass, momentum, angular momentum for gravity) rather than from a full multipole calculation.
- Immediate numerical sanity checks after each formula (strain 5e-21, luminosity 1e47 W, Earth potential 1e-9) to give scale.
- Heavy but standard algebra (Isaacson average, TT projector, conservation identities for the quadrupole formula) outsourced to long guided exercises.
- Short exact-solution section to reassure that the linear picture is not an artefact, done with light-cone coordinates and a two-function metric.
- Ends with a narrative survey of experiments and a forward pointer to the quantum (graviton) view.

The plan is to review electromagnetic waves first and then repeat the reasoning for a weak gravitational field.

**Concepts:** gravitational wave, Hulse-Taylor binary pulsar, laser interferometer gravitational-wave detector

**Key moves**
- Announce the electromagnetism-first strategy.
- Anchor the topic in the two Nobel prizes to motivate the observational finale.

### §46.1 Waves in a gauge theory — p.494 (pdf 511)

Starts from the sourceless Maxwell equation for the potential, fixes Lorenz gauge to obtain a plain wave equation with plane-wave solutions, then uses the leftover freedom (a gauge function obeying the wave equation) to also set A^0 = 0. The Coulomb condition makes k perpendicular to the polarization vector, leaving two transverse linear polarizations along x and y for a wave moving along z. The same steps are then applied to the trace-reversed metric perturbation: the Lorenz condition gives the vacuum wave equation, a plane-wave ansatz with a symmetric amplitude matrix is inserted, and the two constraints k.k = 0 and k^mu A_{mu nu} = 0 follow. A margin argument shows the wave phase is constant along a light ray, so the wave moves with light. The section ends by writing the surviving components as functions of t - z and flagging that a further gauge choice should remove the time components.

**Concepts:** Lorenz gauge, residual gauge freedom, Coulomb gauge, transverse electromagnetic plane wave, linearized vacuum wave equation for hbar, plane gravitational wave ansatz, null wavevector and propagation at the speed of light, transversality condition

**Key moves**
- Lorenz gauge removes the divergence term in eqn 46.1, leaving d^2 A = 0 (46.2).
- Residual shift by a function with d^2 xi = 0 allows A_0 = 0; div A = 0 then gives k.epsilon = 0 (46.4).
- Count: four potential components, two physical polarizations (46.6).
- Gravity: Lorenz condition on hbar gives d^2 hbar = 0 (46.8); polarizations become matrices.
- Insert hbar = Re[A e^{ik.x}] to get i k^nu A_{mu nu} = 0 and k.k = 0 (46.10-46.12).
- Light ray x = k lambda + l keeps k.x constant (46.13-46.14), so phase surfaces move with light.

### §46.2 Lorenz gauge for gravitational waves — p.496 (pdf 513)

Uses the residual gauge freedom x -> x + xi with d^2 xi = 0 and an oscillatory xi = B e^{ik.x} to shift the amplitude by terms built from B and k while preserving transversality; B is chosen to make the amplitude traceless and orthogonal to a chosen 4-velocity, which defines transverse-traceless gauge (where h = hbar). In the rest frame of u with the wave along z only A_xx = -A_yy and A_xy survive, giving an oscillating line element that splits into two plane waves related by a 45 degree rotation. A test mass at rest keeps constant coordinates because Gamma^i_00 vanishes, but the proper distance to a second mass oscillates with fractional amplitude A_xx/2. Geodesic deviation with the weak-field Riemann tensor yields relative accelerations proportional to second time derivatives of h, integrating to a displacement of half h_ij times the separation, which produces the + and x ring deformations of Fig. 46.1 and the quadrupolar acceleration fields of Fig. 46.2; the tidal field E_ij = R_0i0j and polarization tensors e_+, e_x are defined.

**Concepts:** residual gauge transformation in linearized gravity, transverse-traceless gauge, plus and cross polarizations, TT coordinates of free test masses, proper-distance oscillation between test masses, geodesic deviation equation, weak-field Riemann tensor, gravitational-wave displacement formula, tidal field tensor, polarization tensors, 45 degree polarization rotation and field rank, quadrupolar acceleration pattern

**Key moves**
- Residual gauge with d^2 xi = 0 (46.16) and xi = B e^{ik.x} shifts A by -i(B k + k B - eta B.k) (46.19).
- Choose B so A^alpha_alpha = 0 and A u = 0 (46.20): transverse-traceless gauge.
- In the u rest frame with k along z, A has only A_xx = -A_yy and A_xy (46.21); line element 46.22 splits into 46.23 and 46.24.
- Geodesic equation gives zero coordinate acceleration (46.25): the 'oh dear' moment.
- Proper length integral gives L + (L A_xx/2) cos(omega t - kz) (46.26).
- Geodesic deviation with u = (1,0,0,0) and R_{x0x0} = -(1/2) d_t^2 h_xx etc. (46.28-46.30) gives relative accelerations (46.31-46.32).
- Integrate twice: n^i = (1/2) h^TT_ij x^j (46.33); read off + and x patterns.
- Define E_ij = R_0i0j = -(1/2) ddot h^TT_ij and e_+, e_x (46.34-46.36).

### 46.2 (cont.) Example 46.8: multipoles and why gravity radiates at quadrupole order — p.500 (pdf 517)

A boxed detour compares sources. For electromagnetism, a bounded charge distribution is expanded into monopole, dipole, quadrupole and magnetic moments; charge conservation freezes the monopole, so antennas radiate by oscillating a dipole. For gravity, the mass monopole is fixed by mass conservation, the mass dipole (centre of mass) by momentum conservation, and the current dipole (angular momentum) by angular-momentum conservation, so the lowest radiating multipole is the mass quadrupole. Expansions of g_00 in mass moments and g_0j in current moments, with dimensional scaling I_l ~ M L^l and S_l ~ M v L^l, support the claim that the mass quadrupole dominates the time-varying field.

**Concepts:** multipole expansion of a source, absence of monopole and dipole gravitational radiation, mass and current multipole moments

**Key moves**
- Charge conservation forbids electromagnetic monopole radiation; dipole is the leading term.
- Mass, momentum and angular-momentum conservation freeze the three lowest gravitational moments.
- Metric expansions 46.37-46.38 plus scaling show the mass quadrupole is the leading time-dependent term.

### §46.3 Quadrupolar radiation — p.501 (pdf 518)

Restores the source and G in the linearized equation and writes the retarded Green's-function solution by direct analogy with the electromagnetic retarded potential. For a compact source far away the spatial components reduce to an integral of T_ij at retarded time; energy-momentum conservation converts this into the second time derivative of the second mass moment, giving the Einstein quadrupole formula hbar_ij = (2G/R) ddot I_ij(t - R). The moment of inertia tensor differs from the quadrupole moment only by its trace, which the TT description ignores. A numerical estimate for two 30 solar-mass black holes at 100 Mpc gives a strain of a few times 1e-21. The section then notes that such tiny, rapidly varying ripples ride on a curved background, that the split between background and wave is ambiguous, and that the waves carry energy that itself gravitates; this is modelled by moving the quadratic part of the Einstein tensor to the source side and averaging over several wavelengths and periods to get a meaningful effective stress-energy t_{mu nu}.

**Concepts:** retarded solution of the linearized field equation, compact-source far-zone approximation, Einstein quadrupole formula, second mass moment and reduced quadrupole moment, background-plus-ripple decomposition, effective energy-momentum tensor of gravitational waves, non-locality of gravitational field energy, averaging over wavelengths

**Key moves**
- Retarded solution 46.41 copied from the electromagnetic version 46.40.
- Compact source at distance R: pull 1/R out and evaluate at t - R (46.42).
- Use d_mu T^{mu nu} = 0 (Exercise 46.3) to trade the integral of T_ij for (1/2) d_t^2 of the integral of y_i y_j T^00 (46.43).
- Name the result the Einstein quadrupole formula (46.44, with c restored in 46.45).
- Estimate |hbar| ~ 32 pi^2 G M a^2 f^2/(c^4 R) ~ 5e-21 (46.46).
- Compare with the Earth's |h_00| ~ 1e-9; introduce g = g^b + h (46.47).
- Move G^(2) to the right-hand side as t_{mu nu} (46.48-46.51); average to restore gauge invariance (46.52).

### §46.4 Radiated energy and power — p.503 (pdf 520)

Quotes the averaged stress-energy in TT gauge (derived in Exercise 46.2), inserts the quadrupole formula to get the energy density in terms of third time derivatives of I_ij, and computes the power as flux through a large sphere using energy-momentum conservation and the divergence theorem. Because the field depends on t - R/c, the radial flux equals the energy density. An identity for the TT part of a traceless tensor plus angular averages of products of unit vectors (Exercise 46.5) gives the quadrupole luminosity G/(5c^5) times the averaged square of the third derivative of I_ij, with an extremely small prefactor. The black-hole binary of the previous example radiates about 1e47 W but delivers under a milliwatt per square metre at Earth. An analogous flux integral gives the angular-momentum loss rate.

**Concepts:** effective energy-momentum tensor of gravitational waves, energy flux through a distant sphere, transverse-traceless projection operator, quadrupole luminosity formula, angular momentum carried by gravitational waves, retarded time, non-radiating symmetric sources

**Key moves**
- t_{mu nu} = (c^4/32 pi G)<d_mu h^TT d_nu h^TT> (46.53); t_00 in terms of dddot I^TT (46.54).
- Energy conservation plus divergence theorem gives dE/dt as a surface integral (46.55-46.56).
- Outgoing wave: d_r hbar = -d_0 hbar, so t^{0r} = t^{00} (46.57).
- dE/dt = (G/8 pi c^5) integral <dddot I^TT dddot I^TT> dOmega (46.58).
- TT identity (46.59) and angular integrals yield L = (G/5c^5)<dddot I_ij dddot I_ij> (46.60).
- Numbers for the binary: L ~ 1e47 W (46.61).
- Angular momentum flux (46.62) gives dJ/dt = (2G/5c^5) eps_ijk <ddot I_jl dddot I_kl> (46.63).

### 46.4 (cont.) Binary inspiral, spin-up and the Hulse-Taylor pulsar — p.504 (pdf 521)

For two equal Newtonian masses in a circular orbit, the virial energy E = -GM^2/(4a) and Kepler's law convert the luminosity into a function of separation (proportional to 1/a^5) and the energy into a function of period, so that the period derivative follows. Since the bound energy is negative, radiating makes |E| larger, shrinks the orbit and shortens the period: the orbit speeds up. This is tied to the Hulse-Taylor pulsar (59 ms pulsar, 7.75 h orbit, period falling by roughly 76.5 microseconds per year) and Fig. 46.3. The angular-momentum loss is computed both from the radiation formula and from the orbital relation between E and J, and the two agree. The subsection warns that Newtonian dynamics and linearization fail near merger, and contrasts electromagnetism (uncharged photons, linear in vacuum) with gravity (waves carry energy that gravitates, so the theory is nonlinear).

**Concepts:** binary inspiral driven by gravitational radiation, negative binding energy and spin-up, Hulse-Taylor binary pulsar, angular momentum carried by gravitational waves, nonlinearity of gravity

**Key moves**
- Newtonian circular orbit: omega^2 = GM/(4a^3), E = -GM^2/(4a) = -M a^2 omega^2 (46.64).
- L = (2/5) G^4 M^5/(a^5 c^5) (46.65).
- E proportional to P^{-2/3} (46.66), so dP/dt = -(3/2)(P/E) dE/dt (46.67), giving 46.68.
- Negative E means losing energy speeds up the orbit; compare Hulse-Taylor data.
- dJ_z/dt from eqn 46.63 (46.69) equals the result from E = -G^2 M^5/(4J^2) (46.70).

### §46.5 An exact solution — p.505 (pdf 522)

Asks whether waves survive in the full nonlinear theory. Introduces light-cone coordinates u = t - z and v = t + z, takes a metric -du dv + F(u)^2 dx^2 + G(u)^2 dy^2, lists its nonzero connection coefficients and Riemann components, and finds the vacuum Einstein equation reduces to F''/F + G''/G = 0. Setting F = 1 + epsilon and G = 1 - epsilon satisfies it for small epsilon and reproduces the + polarized wave, which the authors take as evidence that wave solutions persist beyond linear order.

**Concepts:** light-cone coordinates, exact plane gravitational wave, nonlinearity of gravity, plus and cross polarizations

**Key moves**
- Minkowski metric in null coordinates: ds^2 = -du dv + dx^2 + dy^2 (46.71).
- Ansatz with two profile functions of u only (46.72).
- Riemann components -F''/F and -G''/G (46.74) give the single vacuum condition 46.75.
- Small-amplitude check with F = 1 + eps, G = 1 - eps (46.76) recovers h_xx = -h_yy = eps.

### §46.6 The discovery of gravitational waves — p.506 (pdf 523)

Describes ground-based detection in the 10 Hz to 10 kHz band dominated by compact binary coalescences. Free mirrors along two perpendicular arms of a Michelson interferometer register the + pattern; strains of order 1e-21 demand that light stay in the arms for about half a gravitational-wave period (milliseconds), hence very long arms. A boxed example relates the output phase to (L_x + L_y) h_+. LIGO's twin 4 km-class detectors with Fabry-Perot arm cavities (effective length about 1200 km) detected GW150914 on 14 September 2015 (roughly 36 and 29 solar masses merging to 62, with 3 solar masses radiated), shown in Fig. 46.5. The section then surveys the Einstein Telescope and Cosmic Explorer, the space-based LISA (100 microhertz to 100 millihertz, massive black-hole binaries), pulsar timing arrays (nanohertz to microhertz, supermassive black-hole remnants) and the primordial stochastic background, and points ahead to gravitons.

**Concepts:** laser interferometer gravitational-wave detector, Fabry-Perot arm cavities, GW150914, gravitational-wave frequency spectrum and detector bands, pulsar timing array, stochastic primordial gravitational-wave background, graviton

**Key moves**
- Map the + ring deformation onto two perpendicular interferometer arms.
- Photon storage time must be comparable to half a wave period; arms must be long.
- Phase difference Delta phi proportional to (L_x + L_y) h_+ (46.77-46.78): sensitivity grows with arm length.
- Fabry-Perot cavities multiply effective optical length; GW150914 masses and energy budget.
- Frequency-band survey: ground (10 Hz-10 kHz), LISA (1e-4 to 1e-1 Hz), pulsar timing (nHz-microHz), primordial background.

## Concepts

### gravitational wave

*phenomenon · core* · also: ripple in spacetime, gravitational radiation

A propagating, source-free perturbation of the spacetime metric that travels at the speed of light and produces oscillating tidal strains between freely falling masses transverse to its direction of travel.

**How introduced:** Framed historically (Poincare, Einstein, two Nobel prizes) and then derived as the vacuum solution of the linearized Einstein equation in Lorenz gauge, explicitly as the gravitational counterpart of an electromagnetic wave.

**Prerequisites:** linearized gravity, linearized vacuum wave equation for hbar

$$
\partial^2 \bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0
$$

**Where:** GA p.494 (pdf 511); GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512)

### Lorenz gauge

*convention · revisited* · also: Lorenz condition, harmonic gauge, de Donder gauge (gravity)

The gauge condition d_mu A^mu = 0 for the electromagnetic potential, or d^nu hbar_{mu nu} = 0 for the trace-reversed metric perturbation, which reduces the respective field equations to wave equations.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapter 42 as the step that removes the divergence term in the sourceless Maxwell equation, then transplanted to gravity where the analogous condition removes the extra derivative terms of the linearized Einstein tensor.

**Prerequisites:** gauge freedom, electromagnetic 4-potential

$$
\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0
$$

$$
\partial^\nu \bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §46.1 p.494 (pdf 511); GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512)

### residual gauge freedom

*convention · developed* · also: remaining gauge freedom

The gauge transformations that preserve an already imposed gauge condition; for Lorenz gauge these are shifts generated by functions (EM) or vector fields (gravity) that themselves satisfy the wave equation.

**How introduced:** In electromagnetism a further shift by a function with d^2 xi = 0 keeps Lorenz gauge and allows A_0 = 0; in gravity the coordinate shift xi^mu with d^2 xi_alpha = 0 is used the same way (margin note 10 stresses the extra index).

**Prerequisites:** Lorenz gauge, gauge transformation

$$
\partial^2 \xi = 0
$$

$$
\partial^2 \xi_\alpha = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512); GA §46.2 p.496 (pdf 513)

### Coulomb gauge

*convention · revisited* · also: radiation gauge (with A_0 = 0)

The electromagnetic gauge condition div A = 0; in a source-free region combined with A_0 = 0 it leaves only transverse potential components.

**How introduced:** Imposed on top of Lorenz gauge using the residual freedom, making div A = 0 and turning into the transversality condition k.epsilon = 0 for plane waves.

**Prerequisites:** residual gauge freedom

$$
A_0 = 0,\quad \vec\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512)

### transverse electromagnetic plane wave

*solution-or-model · revisited* · also: light wave in potential form

A vacuum solution A = epsilon e^{i k.x} with null k whose polarization vector is perpendicular to the propagation direction, leaving two independent linear polarizations.

**How introduced:** Used as the template: plane waves solve d^2 A = 0, the Coulomb condition gives k.epsilon = 0, and for a wave along z the basis polarizations are unit vectors along x and y.

**Prerequisites:** Lorenz gauge, Coulomb gauge

$$
\vec k\cdot\vec\epsilon = 0
$$

$$
\vec\epsilon_1 = (1,0,0)^T,\ \vec\epsilon_2 = (0,1,0)^T
$$

**Notes:** Margin note 7 ties the polarization vectors to photon spin states, pointing to Chapter 47.

**Where:** GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512)

### linearized gravity

*solution-or-model · revisited* · also: weak-field gravity, linearized Einstein theory

The approximation g = eta + h with |h| << 1 in which the Einstein equation is kept only to first order in h, making it a linear field theory on flat spacetime.

**How introduced:** Assumed from Chapter 45 and used throughout; later the chapter questions its limits (curved background, energy carried by waves, nonlinearity).

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor

$$
g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}
$$

**Where:** GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512); GA §46.3 p.502 (pdf 519)

### trace-reversed metric perturbation

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: hbar, h-bar field

The combination hbar_{mu nu} = h_{mu nu} - (1/2) eta_{mu nu} h, whose trace is minus that of h; in Lorenz gauge it obeys a simple wave equation, and in TT gauge it coincides with h.

**How introduced:** Carried over from Chapter 45; in TT gauge the tracelessness condition makes the distinction disappear, which the chapter uses to write the line element directly.

**Prerequisites:** linearized gravity

$$
\bar h_{\mu\nu} = h_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12 \eta_{\mu\nu} h
$$

**Where:** GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512); GA §46.2 p.497 (pdf 514)

### linearized vacuum wave equation for hbar

*law · core* · also: gravitational wave equation

In Lorenz gauge and away from sources, each component of the trace-reversed perturbation satisfies the flat-space wave equation; with sources the right-hand side is -16 pi G T_{mu nu}.

**How introduced:** Presented as looking just like the electromagnetic wave equation but carrying two indices, so polarizations are matrices rather than vectors.

**Prerequisites:** Lorenz gauge, trace-reversed metric perturbation

$$
\partial^2 \bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0
$$

$$
-\partial^2 \bar h_{\mu\nu} = 16\pi G T_{\mu\nu}
$$

**Where:** GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512); GA §46.3 p.501 (pdf 518)

### plane gravitational wave ansatz

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: monochromatic plane wave

A trial solution hbar_{mu nu} = Re[A_{mu nu} e^{i k.x}] with constant symmetric amplitude matrix A and constant wavevector k.

**How introduced:** Written by analogy with the electromagnetic plane wave and substituted into the Lorenz condition and wave equation in Example 46.2.

**Prerequisites:** linearized vacuum wave equation for hbar

$$
\bar h_{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{Re}[A_{\mu\nu} e^{i k\cdot x}]
$$

**Where:** GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512)

### null wavevector and propagation at the speed of light

*principle · developed* · also: k.k = 0

Plane-wave solutions of the vacuum wave equation require k_mu k^mu = 0, so the wave's phase fronts move at c and are carried along with light rays in the same direction.

**How introduced:** Obtained from the wave equation (46.11) and reinforced by margin note 9: a light ray x = k lambda + l keeps k.x constant, so photon and wave share constant-phase surfaces.

**Prerequisites:** plane gravitational wave ansatz

$$
k_\sigma k^\sigma = 0
$$

$$
k_\mu x^\mu(\lambda) = k_\mu l^\mu
$$

**Where:** GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512); GA §46.1 p.496 (pdf 513)

### transversality condition

*principle · developed* · also: Lorenz condition for plane waves

For a plane wave in Lorenz gauge the amplitude contracted with the wavevector vanishes, k^mu A_{mu nu} = 0, so the perturbation has no component along the direction of propagation (once the time components are also removed).

**How introduced:** Derived from the Lorenz gauge condition applied to the ansatz (46.10) and compared to k.epsilon = 0 for light.

**Prerequisites:** plane gravitational wave ansatz, Lorenz gauge

$$
A_{\alpha\mu}k^\mu = 0
$$

**Notes:** The book writes hbar_{mu z} = 0 already in eqn 46.15, but in Lorenz gauge alone this only fixes A_{alpha z} = -A_{alpha 0}; zero z-components follow after A_{alpha 0} = 0 in TT gauge.

**Where:** GA §46.1 p.496 (pdf 513); GA §46.2 p.497 (pdf 514)

### residual gauge transformation in linearized gravity

*technique · developed* · also: oscillatory gauge shift

An infinitesimal coordinate change generated by a vector field that itself solves the wave equation, taken to oscillate like the wave with constant amplitude B; it alters the wave amplitude by a symmetric combination of B and k (eqn 46.19) without spoiling the Lorenz condition, so its four components can be spent on further simplifications.

**How introduced:** Example 46.3 transforms h and hbar under the shift and shows the new amplitude still satisfies transversality; B is then tuned to impose extra conditions.

**Prerequisites:** residual gauge freedom, trace-reversed metric perturbation

$$
\bar h'_{\alpha\beta} = \bar h_{\alpha\beta} - \xi_{\alpha,\beta} - \xi_{\beta,\alpha} + \eta_{\alpha\beta}\xi^\mu{}_{,\mu}
$$

$$
A'_{\alpha\beta} = A_{\alpha\beta} - i(B_\alpha k_\beta + B_\beta k_\alpha - \eta_{\alpha\beta}B^\mu k_\mu)
$$

**Where:** GA §46.2 p.496 (pdf 513)

### transverse-traceless gauge

*convention · core* · also: TT gauge

The gauge in which a gravitational wave's amplitude is traceless, orthogonal to a chosen observer 4-velocity u, and transverse to k; in the observer's frame only spatial components transverse to the propagation direction survive, and h = hbar.

**How introduced:** Reached by choosing the four components of B to impose A^alpha_alpha = 0 and A_{alpha beta}u^beta = 0; Example 46.4 then evaluates it in the rest frame of u with the wave along z.

**Prerequisites:** residual gauge transformation in linearized gravity, transversality condition

$$
A^\alpha{}_\alpha = 0,\quad A_{\alpha\beta}u^\beta = 0
$$

$$
A_{\alpha\beta} = \begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0\\0&A_{xx}&A_{xy}&0\\0&A_{xy}&-A_{xx}&0\\0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

**Notes:** The book does not count constraints explicitly (10 components - 4 Lorenz - 4 residual = 2) or explain why the five conditions in 46.20 are compatible with only four B components.

**Where:** GA §46.2 p.496 (pdf 513); GA §46.2 p.497 (pdf 514)

### plus and cross polarizations

*definition · core* · also: + polarization, x polarization, h_+ and h_x

The two independent linear polarization states of a gravitational wave along z: h_+ = h_xx = -h_yy stretches along x while squeezing along y and vice versa, and h_x = h_xy produces the same pattern rotated by 45 degrees.

**How introduced:** Read off from the TT amplitude matrix and line elements 46.23-46.24, then made physical through the ring-of-masses deformations in Fig. 46.1 and the acceleration fields of Fig. 46.2.

**Prerequisites:** transverse-traceless gauge

$$
h^{TT}_{xx} = -h^{TT}_{yy} = h_+,\quad h^{TT}_{xy} = h^{TT}_{yx} = h_\times
$$

**Where:** GA §46.2 p.497 (pdf 514); GA §46.2 p.499 (pdf 516)

### TT coordinates of free test masses

*principle · developed* · also: coordinates comoving with free particles

In TT gauge a freely falling particle initially at rest keeps constant spatial coordinates as the wave passes, because Gamma^i_00 vanishes; the coordinates are attached to free masses, so coordinate values alone show no effect.

**How introduced:** Staged as a disappointment in Example 46.5: the geodesic equation gives zero coordinate acceleration, prompting the lesson to distrust coordinates and look at invariant distances.

**Prerequisites:** transverse-traceless gauge, geodesic equation

$$
\ddot x^i + \Gamma^i_{00} = 0,\quad \Gamma^i_{00} = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §46.2 p.497 (pdf 514); GA §46.2 p.498 (pdf 515)

### proper-distance oscillation between test masses

*phenomenon · developed* · also: strain between free masses

The physical separation between two free masses a coordinate distance L apart along x oscillates as L[1 + (1/2) h_+ cos(omega t - kz)], giving a fractional length change of half the wave amplitude.

**How introduced:** Computed right after the coordinate paradox by integrating sqrt(g_xx) between the masses; offered as the basis of detection.

**Prerequisites:** TT coordinates of free test masses

$$
\int_0^L \sqrt{g_{xx}}\,dx \approx L + \tfrac{L A_{xx}}{2}\cos(\omega t - |k|z)
$$

**Where:** GA §46.2 p.498 (pdf 515)

### geodesic deviation equation

*law · revisited* · also: Jacobi equation

The relative acceleration of neighbouring geodesics is given by the Riemann tensor acting on the separation vector: D^2 n^mu/dtau^2 = -R^mu_{alpha beta nu} u^alpha n^beta u^nu.

**How introduced:** Recalled in index-free form and evaluated in the rest frame of the particles, where only R^mu_{0 alpha 0} matters, to turn the wave metric into measurable tidal accelerations.

**Prerequisites:** weak-field Riemann tensor, geodesic equation

$$
\frac{D^2\mathbf n}{d\tau^2} + \mathbf R(\ ,\mathbf u,\mathbf n,\mathbf u) = 0
$$

$$
\frac{D^2 n^\mu}{d\tau^2} = -R^\mu{}_{0\alpha0}n^\alpha
$$

**Where:** GA §46.2 p.498 (pdf 515)

### weak-field Riemann tensor

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: linearized Riemann tensor

To first order in h, the Riemann tensor is a combination of second derivatives of the metric perturbation and is gauge invariant; for a TT wave the relevant components are R_{i0j0} = -(1/2) d_t^2 h_ij.

**How introduced:** Taken from the previous chapter (46.29) and specialized in Example 46.6 to components R^x_{0x0}, R^y_{0x0}, R^y_{0y0}.

**Prerequisites:** linearized gravity

$$
R_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu} = \tfrac12(h_{\alpha\nu,\beta\mu} - h_{\alpha\mu,\beta\nu} + h_{\beta\mu,\alpha\nu} - h_{\beta\nu,\alpha\mu})
$$

$$
R_{x0x0} = -\tfrac12 \partial_t^2 h_{xx}
$$

**Where:** GA §46.2 p.498 (pdf 515)

### gravitational-wave displacement formula

*law · core* · also: TT strain formula, n^i = (1/2) h_ij x^j

A free mass at transverse position x^j relative to a reference mass is displaced by (1/2) h^TT_ij x^j, so displacements are linear in the separation and set by the local TT strain.

**How introduced:** Obtained by integrating the geodesic-deviation equations 46.31-46.32 twice in time (starting from rest, dropping integration constants), then used to explain Fig. 46.1 and the interferometer phase in Example 46.13.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic deviation equation, plus and cross polarizations

$$
\delta n^i = \tfrac12 h^{TT}_{ij}x^j
$$

**Notes:** The book writes n^i on the left; it is the oscillating change of the separation, not the full separation, and assumes the separation is small compared with the wavelength.

**Where:** GA §46.2 p.499 (pdf 516); GA §46.6 p.507 (pdf 524)

### tidal field tensor

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: electric part of the Weyl/Riemann tensor, calligraphic E

The spatial tensor E_ij = R_0i0j that gives the relative acceleration of nearby free particles; for a TT wave it equals minus one half of the second time derivative of h^TT.

**How introduced:** Defined in Example 46.7 and decomposed on two polarization tensors to show the wave's tidal action directly.

**Prerequisites:** weak-field Riemann tensor, geodesic deviation equation

$$
\mathcal E = -\tfrac12 \ddot{\mathbf h}^{TT} = -\tfrac12(\ddot h_+ \mathbf e_+ + \ddot h_\times \mathbf e_\times)
$$

**Notes:** The reading copy renders the dots as an overbar; the printed page has second time derivatives.

**Where:** GA §46.2 p.499 (pdf 516)

### polarization tensors

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: e_+ and e_x

The basis tensors e_+ = e_x (x) e_x - e_y (x) e_y and e_x = e_x (x) e_y + e_y (x) e_x on the plane transverse to a wave along z; any TT wave is h_+ e_+ + h_x e_x.

**How introduced:** Introduced alongside the tidal field as the gravitational analogue of the polarization vectors epsilon_1, epsilon_2 for light.

**Prerequisites:** plus and cross polarizations

$$
\mathbf e_+ = \mathbf e_x\otimes\mathbf e_x - \mathbf e_y\otimes\mathbf e_y
$$

$$
\mathbf e_\times = \mathbf e_x\otimes\mathbf e_y + \mathbf e_y\otimes\mathbf e_x
$$

**Where:** GA §46.2 p.499 (pdf 516)

### multipole expansion of a source

*technique · introduced* · also: multipole moments

Representation of the far field of a bounded source as a series of monopole, dipole, quadrupole and higher terms, each weighted by a moment of the source distribution and falling off faster with distance.

**How introduced:** Example 46.8 reviews it for electromagnetism (charge and current moments, antennas as oscillating dipoles) before applying it to masses.

**Where:** GA §46.2 p.500 (pdf 517)

### absence of monopole and dipole gravitational radiation

*principle · core* · also: quadrupole as lowest radiating order

Because total mass-energy, total momentum (fixing the centre-of-mass dipole) and angular momentum (the current dipole) are conserved for an isolated source, none of these moments can oscillate, so the leading gravitational radiation is quadrupolar.

**How introduced:** Argued in parallel with charge conservation forbidding electromagnetic monopole radiation; margin note 20 adds that a spherically symmetric source has no changing quadrupole and so does not radiate.

**Prerequisites:** multipole expansion of a source

**Where:** GA §46.2 p.500 (pdf 517); GA §46.4 p.503 (pdf 520)

### mass and current multipole moments

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: I_l and S_l moments

Moments of the mass density (I_l ~ M L^l) and of the mass current (S_l ~ M v L^l) that control the expansion of g_00 and g_0j far from a source; current moments are suppressed by a factor v relative to mass moments of the same order.

**How introduced:** Written schematically with unspecified constants in eqns 46.37-46.38 and used in a dimensional argument that the time-varying field is dominated by I_2.

**Prerequisites:** multipole expansion of a source

$$
g_{00} = -1 + a_0\frac{\mathcal I_0}{r} + a_1\frac{\mathcal I_1}{r^2} + a_2\frac{\mathcal I_2}{r^3} + \dots
$$

$$
g_{0j} = b_1\frac{\mathcal S_1}{r^2} + b_2\frac{\mathcal S_2}{r^3} + \dots
$$

**Where:** GA §46.2 p.500 (pdf 517)

### retarded solution of the linearized field equation

*solution-or-model · revisited* · also: retarded Green's function solution

The solution of -d^2 hbar = 16 pi G T that sums contributions of the source at the earlier times from which a light signal could reach the field point: hbar(t, x) = 4G integral T(t - |x - y|, y)/|x - y| d^3y.

**How introduced:** Written by analogy with the electromagnetic retarded potential in margin note 12, repeating the argument of Chapter 45.

**Prerequisites:** linearized vacuum wave equation for hbar, retarded time

$$
\bar h_{\mu\nu}(t,\vec x) = 4G\int d^3y\,\frac{T_{\mu\nu}(t - |\vec x - \vec y|,\vec y)}{|\vec x - \vec y|}
$$

**Where:** GA §46.3 p.501 (pdf 518)

### retarded time

*definition · revisited* · also: t_r = t - R/c

The emission time t - R/c at which a source a distance R away must have acted to influence the field at time t, accounting for propagation at the speed of light.

**How introduced:** Appears in the retarded solution and is emphasized after the luminosity and angular-momentum formulas, where the source derivatives are evaluated at t - R/c.

$$
t_r = t - R/c
$$

**Where:** GA §46.3 p.501 (pdf 518); GA §46.4 p.504 (pdf 521)

### compact-source far-zone approximation

*technique · developed* · also: slow-motion, far-field approximation

For a source of size r much smaller than the distance R (and moving slowly), 1/|x - y| can be replaced by 1/R and the retarded time by t - R throughout the source volume.

**How introduced:** Applied to the spatial components to get eqn 46.42 as an integral of T_ij over the source.

**Prerequisites:** retarded solution of the linearized field equation

$$
\bar h_{ij}(t) \approx \frac{4G}{R}\int_\Sigma d^3y\,T_{ij}(t - R,\vec y)
$$

**Notes:** The slow-motion condition (source size much smaller than the emitted wavelength) is needed for evaluating all of the source at one retarded time but is not stated.

**Where:** GA §46.3 p.501 (pdf 518)

### Einstein quadrupole formula

*law · core* · also: quadrupole formula for the field

Far from a slowly moving compact source, the spatial wave field equals 2G/(c^4 R) times the second time derivative of the source's second mass moment evaluated at the retarded time.

**How introduced:** Derived from the far-zone integral of T_ij by using energy-momentum conservation to rewrite it as (1/2) d_t^2 of the integral of y_i y_j T^00 (worked out in Exercise 46.3), then named and restored with factors of c in margin note 14.

**Prerequisites:** compact-source far-zone approximation, second mass moment and reduced quadrupole moment, conservation of energy-momentum

$$
\bar h_{ij}(t) \approx \frac{2G}{R}\ddot I_{ij}(t - R)
$$

$$
\bar h_{ij}(t) \approx \frac{2G}{c^4 R}\ddot I_{ij}(t - R/c)
$$

**Notes:** The physical radiation field is the TT projection of this expression; the text glosses this by saying TT gauge is insensitive to the trace.

**Where:** GA §46.3 p.501 (pdf 518)

### second mass moment and reduced quadrupole moment

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: moment of inertia tensor I_ij, quadrupole moment Q_ij

I_ij is the integral of rho y_i y_j over the source; the reduced (traceless) quadrupole moment Q_ij = I_ij - (1/3) delta_ij Tr I differs only by a trace term, which does not contribute to the TT radiation field.

**How introduced:** Introduced just after the quadrupole formula; the book calls I_ij the moment of inertia tensor and notes its trace is irrelevant in TT gauge.

$$
I_{ij} = \int_\Sigma d^3y\,\rho\,y_i y_j
$$

$$
Q_{ij} = \int_\Sigma d^3y\,\rho\left(y_iy_j - \tfrac13 r^2\delta_{ij}\right) = I_{ij} - \tfrac13\delta_{ij}\mathrm{Tr}\,I
$$

**Notes:** Not the rigid-body inertia tensor of mechanics, which is the integral of rho(r^2 delta_ij - y_i y_j). The printed Q_ij expression omits delta_ij on the trace term.

**Where:** GA §46.3 p.501 (pdf 518)

### conservation of energy-momentum

*law · revisited* · also: d_mu T^{mu nu} = 0

In the linearized theory on flat spacetime the matter stress-energy tensor is divergence-free, which links time derivatives of T^00 and T^0i to spatial divergences.

**How introduced:** Used twice: to convert the integral of T_ij into moments of T^00 (Exercise 46.3) and to express energy loss from a volume as a surface flux.

$$
\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0
$$

$$
\partial_0 T^{00} = -\partial_k T^{k0}
$$

**Where:** GA §46.3 p.501 (pdf 518); GA §46.4 p.503 (pdf 520)

### effective energy-momentum tensor of gravitational waves

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: Isaacson stress-energy tensor, t_{mu nu}

The averaged second-order part of the Einstein tensor, moved to the source side, that acts as the energy-momentum carried by gravitational waves; in TT gauge it is (c^4/32 pi G) times the average of d_mu h^TT_ab d_nu h^TT_ab.

**How introduced:** Built by expanding G = G^(1) + G^(2) + ..., identifying t = -(1/8 pi)G^(2), noting it is not gauge invariant, and fixing this by averaging over several wavelengths and periods; the TT result is quoted with the derivation left to Exercise 46.2.

**Prerequisites:** background-plus-ripple decomposition, averaging over wavelengths, Einstein tensor

$$
t_{\mu\nu} = -\frac{c^4}{8\pi G}\langle G^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}\rangle
$$

$$
t_{\mu\nu} = \frac{c^4}{32\pi G}\langle\partial_\mu h^{TT}_{\alpha\beta}\partial_\nu h_{TT}^{\alpha\beta}\rangle
$$

**Where:** GA §46.3 p.502 (pdf 519); GA §46.4 p.503 (pdf 520)

### energy flux through a distant sphere

*technique · developed* · also: radiated power as surface integral

The power emitted by a source is found by integrating the radial energy flux of the wave, c t^{0r}, over a large sphere of radius R; for an outgoing wave t^{0r} = t^{00}, so power = c R^2 integral t^00 dOmega.

**How introduced:** Derived by differentiating the energy in a volume, using the conservation law and Stokes' (divergence) theorem, then noting that fields depending on t - R/c have radial derivative equal to minus the time derivative.

**Prerequisites:** conservation of energy-momentum, effective energy-momentum tensor of gravitational waves

\frac{dE}{dt} = cR^2\int t^{0r}\,d\Omega

$$
\partial_r \bar h_{ij} = -\partial_0\bar h_{ij}
$$

**Where:** GA §46.4 p.503 (pdf 520)

### transverse-traceless projection operator

*operation · introduced* · also: TT projector, Lambda tensor

For a propagation direction n, P_ij = delta_ij - n_i n_j projects onto the transverse plane, and M^TT_kl = (P_ik P_jl - (1/2) P_kl P_ij) M_ij extracts the transverse, traceless part of a symmetric tensor.

P_{ij} = \delta_{ij} - n_in_j

M^{TT}_{k\ell} = (P_{ik}P_{j\ell} - \tfrac12 P_{k\ell}P_{ij})M_{ij}

### quadrupole luminosity formula

*law · core* · also: Einstein quadrupole formula for power, gravitational-wave luminosity

The total power radiated in gravitational waves by a slowly moving source is G/(5c^5) times the time average of the sum of squares of the third time derivatives of its (traceless) quadrupole moment.

**How introduced:** Obtained by inserting the field quadrupole formula into t_00, integrating the TT identity over solid angle, and immediately checked for scale: the prefactor G/5c^5 is tiny, so only extreme sources radiate appreciably.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein quadrupole formula, energy flux through a distant sphere, transverse-traceless projection operator

$$
L = \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{G}{5c^5}\langle\dddot I_{ij}\dddot I_{ij}\rangle
$$

**Notes:** The angular integral in Exercise 46.5 assumes a traceless tensor, so strictly Q_ij should appear; for sources with constant Tr I (like a circular binary) the results coincide. The reading copy loses a dot; the printed page has third derivatives.

**Where:** GA §46.4 p.503 (pdf 520); GA p.509 (pdf 526)

### Hulse-Taylor binary pulsar

*experiment-or-observation · developed* · also: PSR B1913+16

A binary of two neutron stars, one seen as a 59 ms radio pulsar, in a 7.75 h eccentric orbit whose period decreases at the rate predicted by gravitational-wave emission, giving the first (indirect) evidence for gravitational radiation.

**How introduced:** Cited as observational confirmation of spin-up, with the cumulative periastron-time shift plotted in Fig. 46.3 against the general-relativity curve.

**Prerequisites:** binary inspiral driven by gravitational radiation

**Notes:** The book's circular equal-mass formula underestimates the decay rate for this highly eccentric orbit (eccentricity about 0.62) by an order of magnitude; the plotted prediction uses the full elliptical parameters.

**Where:** GA p.494 (pdf 511); GA §46.4 p.505 (pdf 522)

### nonlinearity of gravity

*principle · introduced* · also: gravitational waves gravitate

Unlike electromagnetic waves, which carry no charge, gravitational waves carry energy and momentum that themselves source curvature, so the full field equations are nonlinear and superposition holds only approximately.

**How introduced:** Raised at the end of the binary discussion and used to motivate checking for exact wave solutions.

**Prerequisites:** effective energy-momentum tensor of gravitational waves

**Where:** GA §46.4 p.505 (pdf 522); GA §46.5 p.505 (pdf 522)

### light-cone coordinates

*convention · introduced* · also: null coordinates u = t - z, v = t + z

Coordinates u = t - z and v = t + z adapted to waves moving along plus or minus z, in which the Minkowski line element becomes -du dv + dx^2 + dy^2.

**How introduced:** Introduced in Section 46.5 because a wave along +z depends only on u.

$$
ds^2 = -du\,dv + dx^2 + dy^2
$$

**Where:** GA §46.5 p.505 (pdf 522)

### exact plane gravitational wave

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: plane wave in Rosen form

An exact vacuum solution ds^2 = -du dv + F(u)^2 dx^2 + G(u)^2 dy^2 whose only nontrivial Einstein equation is F''/F + G''/G = 0; for small deviations from 1 it reduces to a linear + polarized wave.

**How introduced:** Example 46.12 computes connection coefficients and Riemann components for the ansatz and checks the small-amplitude case F = 1 + eps, G = 1 - eps.

**Prerequisites:** light-cone coordinates, plus and cross polarizations

$$
ds^2 = -du\,dv + F(u)^2dx^2 + G(u)^2dy^2
$$

$$
\frac{F''}{F} + \frac{G''}{G} = 0
$$

**Notes:** For any chosen F the condition is a linear ODE for G, so exact solutions exist for arbitrary profiles; the book's check is only to first order in eps. Rosen-form coordinates typically develop coordinate singularities as the wave focuses free particles.

**Where:** GA §46.5 p.506 (pdf 523)

### laser interferometer gravitational-wave detector

*experiment-or-observation · developed* · also: Michelson-type GW detector, LIGO

A Michelson interferometer whose end mirrors are nearly free test masses; a passing wave changes the two arm lengths oppositely by fractions of order h, producing a phase difference at the output proportional to arm length times strain.

**How introduced:** Built from the + polarization picture: masses along x and y form the arms; Example 46.13 derives the phase shift and concludes the arms must be as long as possible, with Fig. 46.4 showing the arm changes.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational-wave displacement formula, plus and cross polarizations

$$
\Delta\phi(t) \propto (L_x + L_y)h_+(t)
$$

**Where:** GA §46.6 p.506 (pdf 523); GA §46.6 p.507 (pdf 524)

### Fabry-Perot arm cavities

*experiment-or-observation · mention* · also: optical cavity arms

Partially reflecting mirrors that make light bounce many times along each interferometer arm, multiplying the effective optical path (to about 1200 km in LIGO) and hence the phase response to a strain.

**How introduced:** Mentioned as LIGO's fix for arms that are still too short, with the requirement that light stay in the arms for about half a gravitational-wave period.

**Prerequisites:** laser interferometer gravitational-wave detector

**Where:** GA §46.6 p.507 (pdf 524)

### GW150914

*experiment-or-observation · introduced* · also: first direct detection

The first directly detected gravitational-wave signal, recorded on 14 September 2015 by both LIGO detectors, produced by the inspiral, merger and ringdown of black holes of about 36 and 29 solar masses leaving a 62 solar-mass remnant and radiating about 3 solar masses of energy.

**How introduced:** Described with its parameters and shown as strain versus time in Fig. 46.5.

**Prerequisites:** laser interferometer gravitational-wave detector, binary inspiral driven by gravitational radiation

**Where:** GA §46.6 p.507 (pdf 524); GA §46.6 p.508 (pdf 525)

### gravitational-wave frequency spectrum and detector bands

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: GW spectrum

Different sources dominate different frequencies: stellar-mass compact binaries at 10 Hz-10 kHz (ground interferometers), massive black-hole binaries at 1e-4 to 1e-1 Hz (LISA), supermassive black-hole binaries at nanohertz to microhertz (pulsar timing), and a primordial background extending to about 1e-18 Hz.

**How introduced:** Assembled in the closing survey of current and planned observatories.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational wave

**Where:** GA §46.6 p.506 (pdf 523); GA §46.6 p.508 (pdf 525); GA §46.6 p.509 (pdf 526)

### pulsar timing array

*experiment-or-observation · introduced* · also: PTA

A network of millisecond pulsars whose extremely regular pulse arrival times at Earth are monitored for years; a gravitational wave perturbs the arrival times through spacetime changes at the pulsar and at Earth, giving sensitivity at nanohertz to microhertz frequencies.

**How introduced:** Described as the lowest-frequency detection technique, contrasted with laser interferometry.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational-wave frequency spectrum and detector bands

**Where:** GA §46.6 p.508 (pdf 525); GA §46.6 p.509 (pdf 526)

### stochastic primordial gravitational-wave background

*phenomenon · mention* · also: relic gravitational waves

A random superposition of gravitational waves produced in the very early Universe, expected to dominate the spectrum at extremely low frequencies and possibly reach bands accessible to current technologies.

**How introduced:** Mentioned as an open question at the end of the survey.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational-wave frequency spectrum and detector bands

**Where:** GA §46.6 p.509 (pdf 526)

### graviton

*definition · mention* · also: quantum of the gravitational field

The hypothetical force-carrying quantum of the gravitational field, obtained by quantizing gravitational waves, with spin 2.

**How introduced:** Named only as the subject of the next chapter.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational wave

**Where:** GA §46.6 p.509 (pdf 526)

### gauge transformation

*operation · revisited* · also: gauge change

A change of the field variables that leaves all physics unchanged: A_mu -> A_mu - d_mu chi for electromagnetism, and h_{mu nu} -> h_{mu nu} - xi_{mu,nu} - xi_{nu,mu} under x -> x + xi for linearized gravity.

**How introduced:** Recalled at the start of Section 46.1 and applied to the gravitational amplitude in Example 46.3.

$$
A_\mu \to A_\mu - \partial_\mu\chi
$$

$$
h'_{\alpha\beta} = h_{\alpha\beta} - \xi_{\alpha,\beta} - \xi_{\beta,\alpha}
$$

**Where:** GA §46.1 p.494 (pdf 511); GA §46.2 p.496 (pdf 513)

## Key equations

### (46.1) Sourceless Maxwell equation for the potential · supporting · GA §46.1 p.494 (pdf 511)

$$
-\partial_\nu(\partial_\mu A^\mu) + \partial^2 A_\nu = 0
$$

The electromagnetic field has its own wave-like dynamics even without charges; margin note 2 adds -J_nu on the right when sources are present.

**Symbols:** A_mu 4-potential; d^2 = eta^{mu nu} d_mu d_nu

### (46.2) EM wave equation in Lorenz gauge · supporting · GA §46.1 p.494 (pdf 511)

$$
\partial^2 A'^\mu = 0
$$

Lorenz gauge removes the divergence term, leaving a pure wave equation.

**Symbols:** A' gauge-fixed potential

### (46.3) EM plane wave · supporting · GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512)

$$
A^\mu = \mathrm{Re}\left[\epsilon^\mu(\mathbf k)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}\right],\quad \omega = |\vec k|
$$

Plane-wave solutions with polarization vector epsilon and null wavevector.

**Symbols:** epsilon polarization; k.x = k_mu x^mu

### (46.4) EM transversality · supporting · GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512)

$$
\vec k\cdot\vec A = \vec k\cdot\vec\epsilon = 0
$$

The Coulomb condition forces the polarization perpendicular to propagation: light is transverse.

### (46.5) EM wave components along z · derivation-step · GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512)

$$
A^t = 0,\ A^x = A^x(-\omega t + |\vec k|z),\ A^y = A^y(-\omega t + |\vec k|z),\ A^z = 0
$$

Only transverse components survive for a wave along z.

### (46.6) EM basis polarizations · supporting · GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512)

$$
\vec\epsilon_1 = (1,0,0)^T,\quad \vec\epsilon_2 = (0,1,0)^T
$$

Two linear polarizations along x and y, related by a 90 degree rotation.

### (46.7) Sourced linearized equation (margin) · supporting · GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512)

$$
-\partial^2\bar h_{\mu\nu} = 16\pi T_{\mu\nu}
$$

Reminder of the Lorenz-gauge linearized Einstein equation with matter, in G = 1 units.

**Symbols:** hbar trace-reversed perturbation; T stress-energy

### (46.8) Vacuum gravitational wave equation · central · GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512)

$$
\partial^2\bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0
$$

Away from sources each component of hbar obeys the flat wave equation; the extra index makes polarizations matrices.

### (46.9) Plane gravitational wave ansatz · central · GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512)

$$
\bar h_{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{Re}\left[A_{\mu\nu}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}\right]
$$

Trial solution with constant symmetric amplitude matrix.

**Symbols:** A_{mu nu} amplitude

### (46.10) Lorenz condition on the plane wave · derivation-step · GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512)

$$
\bar h_{\mu\nu}{}^{,\nu} = i k^\nu A_{\mu\nu}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x} = 0
$$

Gauge condition becomes an algebraic constraint on the amplitude.

### (46.11) Wave equation on the plane wave · derivation-step · GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512)

$$
-k_\sigma k^\sigma A_{\mu\nu}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x} = 0
$$

Nontrivial waves need a null wavevector.

### (46.12) Plane-wave constraints · central · GA §46.1 p.496 (pdf 513)

$$
A_{\alpha\mu}k^\mu = 0,\quad \mathbf k\cdot\mathbf k = 0
$$

Transversality of the amplitude and propagation at the speed of light.

### (46.13) Light ray along k (margin note 9) · derivation-step · GA §46.1 p.496 (pdf 513)

$$
x^\mu(\lambda) = k^\mu\lambda + l^\mu
$$

Straight null line followed by a photon moving with the wave.

**Symbols:** lambda parameter; l constant offset

### (46.14) Constant phase along the ray (margin note 9) · supporting · GA §46.1 p.496 (pdf 513)

$$
k_\mu x^\mu = k_\mu l^\mu = \text{const}
$$

The wave phase does not change along the photon's path, so the wave keeps pace with light.

### (46.15) Gravitational wave components along z · derivation-step · GA §46.1 p.496 (pdf 513)

$$
\bar h_{xx},\ \bar h_{xy} = \bar h_{yx},\ \bar h_{yy}\ \text{functions of } (-\omega t + |k|z),\quad \bar h_{\mu z} = 0
$$

Transverse components depend only on the retarded phase.

### (46.16) Residual gauge condition · supporting · GA §46.2 p.496 (pdf 513)

$$
\partial^2\xi_\alpha = 0
$$

Coordinate shifts that keep Lorenz gauge intact.

**Symbols:** xi_alpha gauge vector

### (46.17) Gauge change of h · derivation-step · GA §46.2 p.496 (pdf 513)

$$
h'_{\alpha\beta} = h_{\alpha\beta} - \xi_{\alpha,\beta} - \xi_{\beta,\alpha}
$$

Linearized effect of x -> x + xi on the metric perturbation.

### (46.18) Gauge change of hbar · derivation-step · GA §46.2 p.496 (pdf 513)

$$
\bar h'_{\alpha\beta} = \bar h_{\alpha\beta} - \xi_{\alpha,\beta} - \xi_{\beta,\alpha} + \eta_{\alpha\beta}\xi^\mu{}_{,\mu}
$$

Trace reversal adds the divergence term.

### (46.19) Gauge change of the amplitude · supporting · GA §46.2 p.496 (pdf 513)

$$
A'_{\alpha\beta} = A_{\alpha\beta} - i\left(B_\alpha k_\beta + B_\beta k_\alpha - \eta_{\alpha\beta}B^\mu k_\mu\right)
$$

With xi = B e^{ik.x} the amplitude shifts by terms built from B and k; transversality is preserved.

**Symbols:** B_alpha gauge amplitude

### (46.20) Transverse-traceless conditions · central · GA §46.2 p.496 (pdf 513)

$$
A^\alpha{}_\alpha = 0,\quad A_{\alpha\beta}u^\beta = 0
$$

Choice of B making the wave traceless and orthogonal to observer velocity u.

**Symbols:** u fixed 4-velocity

### (46.21) TT amplitude for a wave along z · central · GA §46.2 p.497 (pdf 514)

$$
A_{\alpha\beta} = \begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0\\0&A_{xx}&A_{xy}&0\\0&A_{xy}&-A_{xx}&0\\0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

Only two independent numbers remain: the two polarizations.

### (46.22) Line element of a TT plane wave · central · GA §46.2 p.497 (pdf 514)

$$
ds^2 = -dt^2 + \left(1 + A_{xx}e^{-i(\omega t - |k|z)}\right)dx^2 + 2A_{xy}e^{-i(\omega t - |k|z)}dx\,dy + \left(1 - A_{xx}e^{-i(\omega t - |k|z)}\right)dy^2 + dz^2
$$

Spatial distances transverse to z oscillate; real part understood.

### (46.23) Plus-polarized line element · supporting · GA §46.2 p.497 (pdf 514)

$$
ds^2 = -dt^2 + \left(1 + A_{xx}e^{-i(\omega t - |k|z)}\right)dx^2 + \left(1 - A_{xx}e^{-i(\omega t - |k|z)}\right)dy^2 + dz^2
$$

x stretched while y squeezed, and vice versa half a period later.

### (46.24) Cross-polarized line element · supporting · GA §46.2 p.497 (pdf 514)

$$
ds^2 = -dt^2 + 2A_{xy}e^{-i(\omega t - |k|z)}dx\,dy + dz^2
$$

Same physics rotated by 45 degrees. As printed, the dx^2 + dy^2 terms are omitted; with A_xx = 0 the line element should still contain dx^2 + dy^2.

### (46.25) Geodesic equation for a mass at rest · derivation-step · GA §46.2 p.497 (pdf 514)

$$
\ddot x^i + \Gamma^i_{00}\dot x^0\dot x^0 = \ddot x^i + \Gamma^i_{00} = 0
$$

Since Gamma^i_00 = 0 in TT gauge, the mass keeps fixed coordinates.

### (46.26) Oscillating proper distance · central · GA §46.2 p.498 (pdf 515)

$$
\int_0^L\sqrt{g_{xx}}\,dx = \mathrm{Re}\left[L\sqrt{1 + A_{xx}e^{-i(\omega t - |k|z)}}\right] \approx L + \frac{LA_{xx}}{2}\cos(\omega t - |k|z)
$$

Physical separation oscillates with fractional amplitude A_xx/2: the measurable effect.

**Symbols:** L coordinate separation

### (46.27) Geodesic deviation (index-free) · supporting · GA §46.2 p.498 (pdf 515)

$$
\frac{D^2\mathbf n}{d\tau^2} + \mathbf R(\ ,\mathbf u,\mathbf n,\mathbf u) = 0
$$

Relative acceleration of neighbouring free particles is set by curvature.

**Symbols:** n separation; u 4-velocity

### (46.28) Geodesic deviation in rest frame · derivation-step · GA §46.2 p.498 (pdf 515)

$$
\frac{D^2 n^\mu}{d\tau^2} = -R^\mu{}_{0\alpha0}n^\alpha
$$

Only the R^mu_{0 alpha 0} components act on particles at rest.

### (46.29) Linearized Riemann tensor · supporting · GA §46.2 p.498 (pdf 515)

$$
R_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\left(h_{\alpha\nu,\beta\mu} - h_{\alpha\mu,\beta\nu} + h_{\beta\mu,\alpha\nu} - h_{\beta\nu,\alpha\mu}\right)
$$

Curvature to first order in h.

### (46.30) Riemann components for a TT wave · supporting · GA §46.2 p.498 (pdf 515)

$$
R^x{}_{0x0} = -\tfrac12\partial_t^2h_{xx},\quad R^y{}_{0x0} = -\tfrac12\partial_t^2h_{xy},\quad R^y{}_{0y0} = -\tfrac12\partial_t^2h_{yy} = -R^x{}_{0x0}
$$

Tidal components are second time derivatives of the strain.

### (46.31) Relative acceleration, separation along x · derivation-step · GA §46.2 p.498 (pdf 515)

$$
\partial_t^2n^x = \tfrac12 a\,\partial_t^2h_{xx},\quad \partial_t^2n^y = \tfrac12 a\,\partial_t^2h_{xy}
$$

Masses separated along x move along x under h_+ and along y under h_x.

**Symbols:** a initial separation; tau = t to first order

### (46.32) Relative acceleration, separation along y · derivation-step · GA §46.2 p.499 (pdf 516)

$$
\partial_t^2n^y = -\tfrac12 a\,\partial_t^2h_{xx},\quad \partial_t^2n^x = \tfrac12 a\,\partial_t^2h_{xy}
$$

The y-separated pair responds oppositely to h_+.

### (46.33) Displacement formula · central · GA §46.2 p.499 (pdf 516)

$$
n^i = \tfrac12 h^{TT}_{ij}x^j,\quad h^{TT}_{xx} = -h^{TT}_{yy} = h_+,\ h^{TT}_{xy} = h^{TT}_{yx} = h_\times
$$

Displacement of a free mass relative to the origin is half the strain times its position (the left side is the oscillating change of separation).

### (46.34) Tidal field of a wave · supporting · GA §46.2 p.499 (pdf 516)

$$
\boldsymbol{\mathcal E} = -\tfrac12\ddot{\mathbf h}^{TT} = -\tfrac12\left(\ddot h_+\mathbf e_+ + \ddot h_\times\mathbf e_\times\right),\quad \mathcal E_{ij} = R_{0i0j}
$$

Tidal tensor expressed through the two polarization amplitudes. The reading copy shows a bar where the printed page has two dots.

### (46.35)-(46.36) Polarization tensors · supporting · GA §46.2 p.499 (pdf 516)

$$
\mathbf e_+ = \mathbf e_x\otimes\mathbf e_x - \mathbf e_y\otimes\mathbf e_y,\quad \mathbf e_\times = \mathbf e_x\otimes\mathbf e_y + \mathbf e_y\otimes\mathbf e_x
$$

Tensor basis for the two polarizations.

### (46.37) Mass-moment expansion of g_00 · supporting · GA §46.2 p.500 (pdf 517)

$$
g_{00} = -1 + a_0\frac{\mathcal I_0}{r} + a_1\frac{\mathcal I_1}{r^2} + a_2\frac{\mathcal I_2}{r^3} + \dots

Schematic multipole expansion; conserved I_0 and I_1 cannot radiate.

**Symbols:** I_l l-th mass moment; a_l constants

### (46.38) Current-moment expansion of g_0j · supporting · GA §46.2 p.500 (pdf 517)

g_{0j} = b_1\frac{\mathcal S_1}{r^2} + b_2\frac{\mathcal S_2}{r^3} + \dots

Mass-current moments, suppressed by v relative to mass moments.

**Symbols:** S_l current moments

Starting point for radiation from sources.

$$

Surface integral for angular momentum carried off.

### (46.63) Radiated angular momentum · central · GA §46.4 p.504 (pdf 521)

$$
\frac{dJ^i}{dt} = \frac{2G}{5c^5}\epsilon_{ijk}\langle\ddot I_{j\ell}\dddot I_{k\ell}\rangle
$$

Rate of angular momentum loss, derivatives at retarded time; zero for axisymmetric sources.

### (46.64) Orbital frequency of equal-mass binary · supporting · GA §46.4 p.504 (pdf 521)

$$
\omega = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{4a^3}},\quad E = -\frac{GM^2}{4a} = -Ma^2\omega^2
$$

Newtonian circular orbit of two masses M at radius a about the centre of mass.

**Symbols:** a orbital radius (separation 2a)

### (46.65) Luminosity versus separation · central · GA §46.4 p.504 (pdf 521)

$$
L = \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{128G}{5c^5}M^2a^4\omega^6 = \frac25\frac{G^4M^5}{a^5c^5}
$$

Power rises steeply as the orbit shrinks. Here L is the power radiated, so the orbital energy changes at -L.

### (46.66) Energy versus period · derivation-step · GA §46.4 p.504 (pdf 521)

$$
E = -\left(\frac{G^2M^5\pi^2}{4}\right)^{1/3}P^{-2/3}
$$

Kepler's law recast for the bound energy.

**Symbols:** P = 2 pi/omega

### (46.67) Period change from energy change · derivation-step · GA §46.4 p.504 (pdf 521)

$$
\frac{dP}{dt} = -\frac32\frac PE\frac{dE}{dt}
$$

Logarithmic derivative of E proportional to P^{-2/3}; dE/dt here must be the orbital energy change (negative) for the sign to come out right.

### (46.68) Orbital period decay · central · GA §46.4 p.504 (pdf 521)

$$
\frac{dP}{dt} = -\frac35\,4^{11/3}\pi^{8/3}\left(\frac{GM}{P}\right)^{5/3}
$$

Period shortens (spin-up). A factor 1/c^5 is needed for dimensional consistency in SI units.

### (46.69) Angular-momentum loss of the binary · supporting · GA §46.4 p.505 (pdf 522)

$$
\frac{dJ^z}{dt} = \frac{128GM^2a^4\omega^5}{5c^5} = \frac{4G^{7/2}M^{9/2}}{5a^{7/2}c^5}
$$

From eqn 46.63; equals L/omega as expected for a circular orbit.

### (46.70) Consistency check from orbital relations · supporting · GA §46.4 p.505 (pdf 522)

$$
\frac{dJ}{dt} = -\frac{4G^{7/2}M^{9/2}}{5a^{7/2}c^5},\quad J = 2Ma^2\omega = \sqrt{GM^3a} = -\frac{2E}\omega,\quad E = -\frac{G^2M^5}{4J^2}
$$

Orbital angular momentum lost equals that carried by waves. The book prints J = sqrt(G M a^3) = -E omega/2, which is dimensionally wrong; the corrected forms are given here.

### (46.71) Minkowski metric in light-cone coordinates · supporting · GA §46.5 p.505 (pdf 522)

$$
ds^2 = \eta_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu = -du\,dv + dx^2 + dy^2,\quad u = t - z,\ v = t + z
$$

Null coordinates suited to waves along z.

### (46.72) Exact plane-wave ansatz · central · GA §46.5 p.506 (pdf 523)

$$
ds^2 = -du\,dv + F(u)^2dx^2 + G(u)^2dy^2
$$

Two profile functions of the null coordinate u.

### (46.73) Connection coefficients of the ansatz · derivation-step · GA §46.5 p.506 (pdf 523)

$$
\Gamma^x{}_{xu} = \frac{F'}{F},\quad \Gamma^y{}_{yu} = \frac{G'}{G},\quad \Gamma^v{}_{xx} = 2FF',\quad \Gamma^v{}_{yy} = 2GG'
$$

Nonzero Christoffel symbols. The book prints Gamma^v_xx = 2F'/F and Gamma^v_yy = 2G'/G; direct computation with g^{uv} = -2 gives 2FF' and 2GG'.

**Symbols:** prime = d/du

### (46.74) Riemann components of the ansatz · derivation-step · GA §46.5 p.506 (pdf 523)

$$
R^x{}_{uxu} = -\frac{F''}{F},\quad R^y{}_{uyu} = -\frac{G''}{G}
$$

Curvature is controlled by the second derivatives of the profiles.

### (46.75) Vacuum Einstein equation for the plane wave · central · GA §46.5 p.506 (pdf 523)

$$
\frac{F''}{F} + \frac{G''}{G} = 0
$$

Single condition (R_uu = 0) for an exact vacuum plane wave.

### (46.76) Small-amplitude check · derivation-step · GA §46.5 p.506 (pdf 523)

$$
\frac{\varepsilon''}{1 + \varepsilon} - \frac{\varepsilon''}{1 - \varepsilon} \approx \varepsilon''(1 - \varepsilon) - \varepsilon''(1 + \varepsilon) \approx 0
$$

F = 1 + eps, G = 1 - eps satisfies eqn 46.75 to first order, recovering h_xx = -h_yy = eps (+ polarization).

### (46.77) Interferometer phase shift · supporting · GA §46.6 p.507 (pdf 524)

$$
\Delta\phi(t) = (L_x + L_y)h_+(t)
$$

Output phase tracks h_+ with sensitivity proportional to arm length; as printed the laser frequency omega_0 (over c) and an overall sign are dropped from Delta phi = omega_0(2 delta y - 2 delta x).

**Symbols:** L_x, L_y arm lengths

### (46.78) Equal-arm response · supporting · GA §46.6 p.507 (pdf 524)

$$
I \propto \Delta\phi(t) \approx 2Lh_+(t)
$$

Longer arms mean bigger signal; the linear intensity-phase relation holds only near a chosen operating point.

### (46.83) Averaged second-order Einstein tensor (Exercise 46.2) · supporting · GA p.510 (pdf 527)

$$
\langle G^{(2)}_{\mu\nu}\rangle = -\frac14\langle\partial_\mu h_{\alpha\beta}\partial_\nu h^{\alpha\beta}\rangle
$$

Combined with 46.52 gives eqn 46.53.

### (46.90) Tensor virial identity (Exercise 46.3) · supporting · GA p.510 (pdf 527)

$$
\ddot I_{ij} = \int_\Sigma d^3y\,\ddot{\mathfrak I}_{ij} = 2\int_\Sigma d^3y\,T_{ij}
$$

Key step behind eqn 46.43: the stress integral equals half the second derivative of the second moment.

**Symbols:** frak I^{ij} = x^i x^j T^00

### (46.96) TT projection (Exercise 46.5) · supporting · GA p.511 (pdf 528)

$$
M^{TT}_{k\ell} = \left(P_{ik}P_{j\ell} - \tfrac12P_{k\ell}P_{ij}\right)M_{ij},\quad P_{ij} = \delta_{ij} - n_in_j
$$

Extracts the transverse-traceless part for propagation along n.

### (46.98)-(46.99) Angular averages · supporting · GA p.511 (pdf 528)

$$
\int n_in_jd\Omega = \frac{4\pi}3\delta_{ij},\quad \int n_in_jn_kn_\ell d\Omega = \frac{4\pi}{15}(\delta_{ij}\delta_{k\ell} + \delta_{ik}\delta_{j\ell} + \delta_{i\ell}\delta_{jk})
$$

Isotropic integrals used to evaluate the total power.

### (46.100) Integrated TT square · supporting · GA p.511 (pdf 528)

$$
\int\langle\dddot I^{TT}_{ij}\dddot I^{TT}_{ij}\rangle d\Omega = \frac{24\pi}{15}\langle\dddot I_{ij}\dddot I_{ij}\rangle
$$

Multiplying by G/(8 pi c^5) gives G/(5c^5). The reading copy shows T for I.

### (46.103) Second mass moment of the circular binary (Exercise 46.6) · supporting · GA p.511 (pdf 528)

$$
I^{ij} = Ma^2\begin{pmatrix}1 + \cos2\omega t&\sin2\omega t&0\\\sin2\omega t&1 - \cos2\omega t&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

Time dependence at twice the orbital frequency.

### (46.104) Wave from the binary along the orbital axis · supporting · GA p.511 (pdf 528)

$$
\bar h_{\mu\nu}(t,R) = -\frac{8GMa^2\omega^2}{R}\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0\\0&\cos2\omega t_r&\sin2\omega t_r&0\\0&\sin2\omega t_r&-\cos2\omega t_r&0\\0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

Circularly polarized (+ and x a quarter cycle apart) at twice the orbital frequency.

**Symbols:** t_r = t - R

### (46.106) Squared third derivative for the binary · supporting · GA p.511 (pdf 528)

$$
\langle\dddot I^{ij}\dddot I_{ij}\rangle = 128M^2a^4\omega^6

Feeds eqn 46.61.

## Worked examples

### Example 46.1 · intro · GA §46.1 p.494 (pdf 511)

**Problem:** Simplify the sourceless Maxwell equation for the potential by exploiting gauge freedom.

**Method:** Shift to a potential obeying d_mu A^mu = 0, which deletes the divergence term.

**Key insight:** Gauge choice turns a coupled equation into a plain wave equation for each component.

**Result:** d^2 A^mu = 0 with plane-wave solutions Re[epsilon e^{ik.x}], omega = |k|.

**Concepts:** Lorenz gauge, transverse electromagnetic plane wave

### Example 46.2 · intro · GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512)

**Problem:** Find the conditions a plane gravitational wave must satisfy in Lorenz gauge.

**Method:** Differentiate the ansatz: the gauge condition gives i k^nu A_{mu nu} = 0 and the wave equation gives -k.k A = 0.

**Key insight:** Both gauge and field equation become algebra on the amplitude; nontrivial waves need null k.

**Result:** A_{alpha mu}k^mu = 0 and k.k = 0 (eqn 46.12).

**Concepts:** plane gravitational wave ansatz, null wavevector and propagation at the speed of light, transversality condition

### Example 46.3 · standard · GA §46.2 p.496 (pdf 513)

**Problem:** Determine how an oscillatory residual gauge transformation changes the wave amplitude.

**Method:** Take xi_alpha = B_alpha e^{ik.x}, apply the linearized transformation to h, trace-reverse, and substitute.

**Key insight:** The amplitude shifts by combinations of B and k, and transversality survives, so B is free to impose further conditions.

**Result:** A' = A - i(B_alpha k_beta + B_beta k_alpha - eta_{alpha beta} B.k) (eqn 46.19).

**Concepts:** residual gauge transformation in linearized gravity

### Example 46.4 · standard · GA §46.2 p.497 (pdf 514)

**Problem:** Write the TT-gauge amplitude and line element for a wave travelling along z, seen by an observer at rest.

**Method:** Use A u = 0 with u = (1,0,0,0) to kill time components, transversality with k along z to kill z components, tracelessness for A_yy = -A_xx and symmetry for A_yx = A_xy.

**Key insight:** Only two numbers survive; the metric shows oscillating transverse distances, and setting either number to zero gives two waves related by 45 degrees.

**Result:** Amplitude matrix 46.21 and line elements 46.22-46.24.

**Concepts:** transverse-traceless gauge, plus and cross polarizations

### Example 46.5 · intro · GA §46.2 p.497 (pdf 514)

**Problem:** Does a single test mass at rest move when the wave passes?

**Method:** Geodesic equation for a mass at rest reduces to x-double-dot = -Gamma^i_00, which vanishes because h_0a = 0 in TT gauge.

**Key insight:** The coordinate position is frozen, which says nothing physical: TT coordinates are tied to free masses.

**Result:** Coordinate acceleration zero (eqn 46.25); resolved afterwards by the proper-distance integral 46.26.

**Concepts:** TT coordinates of free test masses, proper-distance oscillation between test masses

### Example 46.6 · standard · GA §46.2 p.498 (pdf 515)

**Problem:** Find the relative motion of two free masses initially separated along x by a small distance a.

**Method:** Specialize geodesic deviation to R^mu_{0 alpha 0}, compute the needed linearized Riemann components from h, replace tau by t to first order, and read off second-order ODEs; repeat for a y separation.

**Key insight:** Relative acceleration equals half the separation times the second time derivative of the strain, so integrating twice gives displacement linear in h.

**Result:** n^i = (1/2) h^TT_ij x^j (eqn 46.33) with h_+ and h_x identified.

**Concepts:** geodesic deviation equation, weak-field Riemann tensor, gravitational-wave displacement formula

### Example 46.7 · intro · GA §46.2 p.499 (pdf 516)

**Problem:** Express the tidal effect of the wave as a tensor decomposed into polarizations.

**Method:** Define E_ij = R_0i0j, use the TT Riemann components, and expand on tensor-product bases.

**Key insight:** The wave's tidal field is just minus half the second derivative of h^TT, split cleanly onto e_+ and e_x.

**Result:** E = -(1/2)(ddot h_+ e_+ + ddot h_x e_x) with e_+ and e_x in 46.35-46.36.

**Concepts:** tidal field tensor, polarization tensors

### Example 46.8 · intro · GA §46.2 p.500 (pdf 517)

**Problem:** Which multipoles of a source can emit electromagnetic and gravitational radiation?

**Method:** List multipole moments; ask which are fixed by conservation laws (charge; mass, momentum, angular momentum); use metric expansions and dimensional scaling of mass and current moments.

**Key insight:** Conservation laws, not dynamics, forbid monopole and dipole gravitational radiation, so the mass quadrupole leads.

**Result:** Lowest gravitational radiation is quadrupolar; current moments are suppressed by v.

**Concepts:** multipole expansion of a source, absence of monopole and dipole gravitational radiation, mass and current multipole moments

### Example 46.9 · intro · GA §46.3 p.501 (pdf 518)

**Problem:** Estimate the strain at Earth from two 30 solar-mass black holes orbiting at 10 Hz with separation 2000 km, 100 Mpc away.

**Method:** Insert ddot I ~ 4 M a^2 omega^2 into the quadrupole formula with c restored.

**Key insight:** Even an extreme source gives strain of a few parts in 1e21.

**Result:** |hbar| ~ 32 pi^2 G M a^2 f^2/(c^4 R) ~ 5e-21 (eqn 46.46). Own check: with a = 1000 km the arithmetic gives 5.0e-21; Kepler's law for these masses gives an orbital frequency near 5 Hz, so 10 Hz is best read as the wave frequency.

**Concepts:** Einstein quadrupole formula

### Example 46.10 · intro · GA §46.4 p.504 (pdf 521)

**Problem:** Estimate the gravitational luminosity of the same binary and the flux at Earth.

**Method:** Use <dddot I dddot I> = 128 M^2 a^4 omega^6 (Exercise 46.6) in the quadrupole luminosity.

**Key insight:** Enormous total power but tiny flux at cosmological distance; the omega^6 dependence favours tight, fast orbits.

**Result:** L ~ 1e47 W (eqn 46.61); flux about a milliwatt per square metre (own recomputation with the book's inputs: L ~ 1.5e47 W, flux ~ 1.3 mW m^-2).

**Concepts:** quadrupole luminosity formula

### Example 46.11 · standard · GA §46.4 p.504 (pdf 521)

**Problem:** How fast does the orbital period of an equal-mass circular binary decay because of gravitational radiation?

**Method:** Newtonian orbit: force balance gives omega; virial energy E = -GM^2/(4a); express L and E in terms of a and P; differentiate E proportional to P^{-2/3}.

**Key insight:** Because E is negative, losing energy shortens the period: radiation speeds the orbit up.

**Result:** L = (2/5) G^4 M^5/(a^5 c^5) (46.65) and dP/dt = -(3/5) 4^{11/3} pi^{8/3} (GM/P)^{5/3}/c^5 (46.68, c^5 omitted in print).

**Concepts:** binary inspiral driven by gravitational radiation, negative binding energy and spin-up, quadrupole luminosity formula

### Example 46.12 · standard · GA §46.5 p.506 (pdf 523)

**Problem:** Show that the full nonlinear Einstein equation admits plane-wave solutions.

**Method:** Take ds^2 = -du dv + F(u)^2 dx^2 + G(u)^2 dy^2, compute connections and Riemann components, impose vacuum, test F = 1 + eps, G = 1 - eps.

**Key insight:** The vacuum equations collapse to one ODE linking F and G; its small-amplitude limit is the linearized + wave.

**Result:** F''/F + G''/G = 0 (eqn 46.75), satisfied to first order by F = 1 + eps, G = 1 - eps.

**Concepts:** exact plane gravitational wave, light-cone coordinates

### Example 46.13 · intro · GA §46.6 p.507 (pdf 524)

**Problem:** Relate the interferometer output to the wave amplitude.

**Method:** Phase difference from round-trip arm-length changes, with displacements from eqn 46.33.

**Key insight:** Signal is proportional to arm length times strain, so long arms are essential.

**Result:** Delta phi = (L_x + L_y) h_+ and I proportional to 2 L h_+ (eqns 46.77-46.78), with omega_0/c and sign suppressed.

**Concepts:** laser interferometer gravitational-wave detector, gravitational-wave displacement formula

### Example · intro · GA §46.1 p.496 (pdf 513)

**Problem:** Margin note 9: show that a gravitational plane wave travels at the speed of light.

**Method:** Follow a photon along x = k lambda + l with null k and dot with k_mu.

**Key insight:** The wave phase is constant along a light ray, so photon and wave move in lockstep.

**Result:** k_mu x^mu = k_mu l^mu = const (eqns 46.13-46.14).

**Concepts:** null wavevector and propagation at the speed of light

### Example · intro · GA §46.2 p.498 (pdf 515)

**Problem:** Compute the physical distance between two free masses on the x axis as a + wave passes.

**Method:** Integrate sqrt(g_xx) along x, take the real part and expand the square root to first order.

**Key insight:** What oscillates is proper distance, even though coordinates do not.

**Result:** L + (L A_xx/2) cos(omega t - kz) (eqn 46.26).

**Concepts:** proper-distance oscillation between test masses

### Example · challenging · GA §46.4 p.503 (pdf 520)

**Problem:** Derive the total power radiated in gravitational waves by a slowly moving source.

**Method:** Energy density from the TT Isaacson tensor plus quadrupole formula; flux through a sphere via energy conservation and the divergence theorem; outgoing-wave relation t^{0r} = t^{00}; TT identity and isotropic angular integrals.

**Key insight:** The direction-dependent TT projection averages over the sky to a fixed fraction (2/5 of the naive sum) of the full quadrupole squared.

**Result:** dE/dt = (G/5c^5)<dddot I_ij dddot I_ij> (eqn 46.60).

**Concepts:** quadrupole luminosity formula, energy flux through a distant sphere, transverse-traceless projection operator

### Example · standard · GA §46.4 p.505 (pdf 522)

**Problem:** Check that angular momentum lost by the binary equals that carried by the waves.

**Method:** Evaluate eqn 46.63 with the binary's quadrupole (Exercise 46.7); independently write E in terms of J and use dJ/dt = -(J/2E) dE/dt.

**Key insight:** Two routes agree, and dJ/dt = L/omega as required for circular orbits.

**Result:** |dJ/dt| = 4 G^{7/2} M^{9/2}/(5 a^{7/2} c^5) (eqns 46.69-46.70). The printed J = sqrt(G M a^3) = -E omega/2 should read sqrt(G M^3 a) = -2E/omega.

**Concepts:** angular momentum carried by gravitational waves, binary inspiral driven by gravitational radiation


## Analogies and intuitions

### Gravity as a second gauge field: every step for electromagnetic waves repeated with one more index → linearized vacuum wave equation for hbar · strong · GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512)

Section 46.1 walks through Lorenz gauge, plane waves, residual gauge and transversality for A_mu, then reruns the same argument for hbar_{mu nu}; polarization vectors become polarization matrices.

**Where it breaks down:** Gravity's gauge freedom is a coordinate change with a vector parameter, the field is symmetric rank-2, and gravity is nonlinear because the waves carry energy; there is no gravitational analogue of opposite charges, which changes the radiation selection rules.

**App idea:** Split-screen derivation where each EM line has a gravity twin; learners click a line to see which object gained an index and why.

### Boats bobbing on ocean waves → TT coordinates of free test masses · useful · GA §46.2 p.497 (pdf 514)

Raised as the naive expectation for how suspended masses respond, then shown to be misleading: coordinates stay put and the real effect is transverse relative stretching.

**Where it breaks down:** Water waves displace floats vertically (along the gradient of a potential); gravitational waves have no single-particle displacement and act only on separations, perpendicular to travel.

**App idea:** Contrast animation: bobbing boats on a water wave next to a ring of beads in a gravitational wave, with a single bead highlighted to show it has no absolute motion.

### Field of a quadrupole magnet → quadrupolar acceleration pattern · useful · GA §46.2 p.499 (pdf 516)

The acceleration field lines of Fig. 46.2 are compared with the field of a four-pole magnet, setting up the question of quadrupole sources.

**Where it breaks down:** The magnet field is static and sourced by poles at the corners; the gravitational tidal pattern oscillates in time and is a property of the passing wave, not of nearby sources.

**App idea:** Morph slider between a quadrupole magnet's field and the tidal field of a + wave.

### Radio transmitter as an oscillating dipole → absence of monopole and dipole gravitational radiation · strong · GA §46.2 p.500 (pdf 517)

Antennas radiate by driving charge back and forth; the gravitational analogue fails because the centre of mass cannot oscillate for an isolated system, so gravity must shake a quadrupole.

**Where it breaks down:** Mass has one sign, so there is no way to build a gravitational dipole oscillator; the analogy works by showing where it breaks.

**App idea:** Toy sources: drag charges or masses; a meter shows which moments oscillate and a far-field panel shows radiation appearing only for allowed moments.

### A photon surfing with the gravitational wave → null wavevector and propagation at the speed of light · useful · GA §46.1 p.496 (pdf 513)

A light ray moving along k keeps a constant phase of the gravitational wave, so the two travel together.

**Where it breaks down:** Shows consistency of the phase fronts with light rays for a plane wave in flat background; says nothing about dispersion or propagation through curved backgrounds.

**App idea:** Spacetime diagram with wave-crest lines and a photon worldline that stays on one crest.

### Charged versus uncharged messengers → nonlinearity of gravity · useful · GA §46.4 p.505 (pdf 522)

Photons carry no charge, so light does not source more electromagnetism in vacuum; gravitational waves carry energy, which is the 'charge' of gravity, so they gravitate and the theory is nonlinear.

**Where it breaks down:** Energy of gravitational waves is only defined after averaging, unlike electric charge which is local.

### Michelson-Morley apparatus scaled up → laser interferometer gravitational-wave detector · useful · GA §46.6 p.507 (pdf 524)

The detector is the same interferometer geometry that tested the ether, but with arms of kilometres rather than about a metre, and extended further by optical cavities.

**Where it breaks down:** Michelson-Morley compared light speeds along rigid arms; LIGO measures time-varying proper-length changes between free mirrors.

### Pulsars as cosmic clocks → pulsar timing array · useful · GA §46.6 p.508 (pdf 525)

Millisecond pulsars tick so regularly that tiny timing residuals reveal passing low-frequency waves, and a binary pulsar's clock reveals its orbital decay.

**Where it breaks down:** Timing residuals mix wave effects with spin-down, dispersion and noise; years of data are needed.

**App idea:** Pulse train with arrival-time residuals plotted as a slow gravitational wave passes between pulsar and Earth.


## Misconceptions addressed

### A passing gravitational wave makes each free mass bob back and forth, like a boat on the sea. · GA §46.2 p.497 (pdf 514)

**Correction:** No single free mass has a measurable absolute motion; in TT coordinates it keeps fixed coordinates, and the physical effect is an oscillation of distances between masses, transverse to propagation.

**Why tempting:** Everyday waves (water, sound) move matter at each point, and the line element visibly oscillates.

### If the geodesic equation gives zero coordinate acceleration, the wave has no physical effect. · GA §46.2 p.498 (pdf 515)

**Correction:** Coordinates in a chosen gauge carry no physical meaning; the invariant proper distance between masses oscillates and geodesic deviation gives nonzero tidal accelerations.

**Why tempting:** In Newtonian physics and in simple frames, coordinate acceleration equals physical acceleration.

### The two gravitational polarizations are perpendicular, like x and y polarized light. · GA §46.2 p.499 (pdf 516)

**Correction:** The + and x patterns are related by a 45 degree rotation; rotating by 90 degrees turns a + wave into minus itself.

**Why tempting:** Linear polarizations of light are 90 degrees apart, and the chapter builds explicitly on that analogy.

### Gravitational radiation, like electromagnetic radiation, can come from an oscillating dipole. · GA §46.2 p.500 (pdf 517)

**Correction:** Momentum conservation fixes the mass dipole and angular-momentum conservation fixes the current dipole; the leading radiation is quadrupolar.

**Why tempting:** Dipole antennas are the textbook radiators and gravity and electromagnetism share inverse-square laws.

### Any rotating or pulsating massive body emits gravitational waves. · GA §46.4 p.503 (pdf 520)

**Correction:** Only a time-varying quadrupole radiates; a spherically symmetric star, even if spinning or pulsating radially, emits none, and an axisymmetric rotator emits no angular momentum.

**Why tempting:** Motion of mass feels like it should disturb the field, and accelerating charges always seem to radiate.

### Gravitational field energy has a definite density at each point, like electromagnetic energy. · GA §46.3 p.502 (pdf 519)

**Correction:** In a freely falling frame gravity vanishes locally, so the energy of gravitational waves is meaningful only after averaging over several wavelengths and periods.

**Why tempting:** Electromagnetic energy density E^2 + B^2 is local, and waves obviously carry energy.

### A binary that loses energy slows down and drifts apart. · GA §46.4 p.505 (pdf 522)

**Correction:** Bound orbits have negative total energy; losing energy makes it more negative, the orbit shrinks and the stars speed up, so the period decreases.

**Why tempting:** In daily life losing energy means slowing down (friction), and kinetic energy is positive.

### Gravitational waves are an artefact of linearizing Einstein's equation. · GA §46.5 p.505 (pdf 522)

**Correction:** Exact plane-wave solutions of the full vacuum equation exist and reduce to the linear waves at small amplitude.

**Why tempting:** The chapter derives everything in the linear approximation and admits gravity is nonlinear.

### The + and x polarizations differ by a 45 degree phase in time. · GA p.509 (pdf 526)

**Correction:** They are two independent spatial patterns related by a 45 degree rotation; their temporal phase relation is free (a quarter-cycle offset gives circular polarization).

**Why tempting:** The chapter summary itself uses the phrase 'out of phase' for the rotation.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The I_ij in the quadrupole formula is the rigid-body moment of inertia tensor from mechanics. · GA §46.3 p.501 (pdf 518)

**Correction:** It is the second mass moment, the integral of rho y_i y_j; the mechanics tensor is the integral of rho(r^2 delta_ij - y_i y_j). They differ by sign and a trace term.

**Why tempting:** The book calls it the moment of inertia tensor.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Gravitational waves are longitudinal compressions like sound waves. · GA §46.2 p.497 (pdf 514)

**Correction:** They are transverse: in TT gauge nothing happens along the propagation direction, and the stretching occurs in the perpendicular plane.

**Why tempting:** Stretch-and-squeeze language evokes compression waves.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### A detector responds equally to waves from every direction and polarization. · GA §46.6 p.507 (pdf 524)

**Correction:** An L-shaped interferometer measures the difference of strains along its arms, so its response depends on the arrival direction and polarization angle and can vanish (for example a x wave aligned with the arms from overhead).

**Why tempting:** The chapter only treats a + wave arriving perpendicular to the detector plane.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Ring of free test masses · GA §46.2 p.499 (pdf 516)

**Setup:** A circle of freely floating masses in the plane transverse to an incoming plane gravitational wave.

**Lesson:** The ring deforms into oscillating ellipses (+ along the axes, x along the diagonals), making polarization tangible and showing displacements grow with distance from the centre.

**App idea:** Draggable rings of different radii and orientations; measure diameters as the wave passes.

### The mass that refuses to move · GA §46.2 p.497 (pdf 514)

**Setup:** A single test mass at rest at the origin in TT coordinates while a wave passes.

**Lesson:** Its coordinates never change, forcing the move from coordinate positions to invariant distances between two masses.

**App idea:** Coordinate grid that stretches with the wave so a bead stays on its grid point while a ruler between beads visibly changes length.

### Photon riding a wave crest · GA §46.1 p.496 (pdf 513)

**Setup:** A photon launched along the wavevector of a gravitational plane wave.

**Lesson:** It stays on a surface of constant wave phase forever, so the wave moves at the speed of light.

### Trying to make a monopole radiate · GA §46.2 p.500 (pdf 517)

**Setup:** Imagine making the total charge (or total mass) of a bounded object oscillate to produce spherical waves.

**Lesson:** Conservation laws forbid it; for gravity the dipole is frozen as well, leaving the quadrupole as the first radiator.

**App idea:** Attempt-to-radiate sandbox where forbidden moves are blocked with a message naming the conservation law.

### Shaking a fist in anger · GA p.511 (pdf 528)

**Setup:** Exercise 46.4 asks for the gravitational power radiated by the solar system, a black-hole binary, and a shaken fist, using P ~ G^4 m^5/(r^5 c^5).

**Lesson:** Everyday and planetary sources radiate absurdly little because of the G/c^5 prefactor; only compact relativistic systems matter.

**App idea:** Log-scale power ladder from a waving hand to merging black holes with a slider for mass, size and speed.


## Applications and observations

- **Strain from a stellar-mass black-hole binary** (numerical-estimate, GA §46.3 p.501 (pdf 518)): Quadrupole formula applied to two 30 solar-mass black holes 100 Mpc away. Key numbers: M = 30 M_sun each, 2a = 2000 km, f = 10 Hz, R = 100 Mpc gives |h| ~ 5e-21.
- **Static potential of the Earth versus wave strain** (numerical-estimate, GA §46.3 p.502 (pdf 519)): Compares the wave amplitude with the Newtonian metric perturbation at the Earth's surface to motivate a background-plus-ripple split. Key numbers: |h_00| = 2GM_E/(R_E c^2) ~ 1e-9 versus h ~ 1e-21; detected frequencies tens of Hz to kHz, wavelengths tens to thousands of km.
- **Luminosity of the black-hole binary** (numerical-estimate, GA §46.4 p.504 (pdf 521)): Quadrupole luminosity for the same system; L grows as omega^6. Key numbers: L ~ 1e47 W; flux at Earth under about 1 mW m^-2 (own recomputation: 1.5e47 W and 1.3 mW m^-2 with the book's inputs).
- **Hulse-Taylor binary pulsar PSR B1913+16** (astrophysical-system, GA §46.4 p.505 (pdf 522)): Radio pulses from one neutron star time the orbit; the orbital period shrinks as predicted, shown as cumulative periastron-time shift in Fig. 46.3. Key numbers: Pulsar period 59 ms; orbital period about 7.75 h; period decrease about 76.5 microseconds per year. Own estimate: the circular equal-mass formula (46.68) with M = 1.4 M_sun gives dP/dt ~ -2e-13, about 12 times smaller than observed (-2.4e-12), the difference being the eccentricity (e ~ 0.62) enhancement.
- **Ground-based interferometers (LIGO)** (technology, GA §46.6 p.507 (pdf 524)): Two L-shaped detectors in Hanford, Washington and Livingston, Louisiana with Fabry-Perot arm cavities; light must stay in the arms for about half a wave period. Key numbers: Band about 10 Hz to 10 kHz; arms given as 4.2 km (actual LIGO arms are 4 km); effective optical length ~1200 km; fractional displacement ~1e-21; Michelson-Morley arms ~1.3 m.
- **GW150914** (detection-event, GA §46.6 p.507 (pdf 524)): Binary black-hole inspiral, merger and ringdown seen by both LIGO detectors; Fig. 46.5 shows the strain traces and numerical-relativity fits. Key numbers: 14 September 2015, 09:50:45 UTC; masses ~36 and 29 M_sun; remnant ~62 M_sun; ~3 M_sun c^2 radiated; peak strain ~1e-21.
- **Next-generation ground observatories** (technology, GA §46.6 p.508 (pdf 525)): Einstein Telescope and Cosmic Explorer are planned to improve sensitivity about tenfold and reach compact objects in the early Universe. Key numbers: ~10x sensitivity gain.
- **LISA** (technology, GA §46.6 p.508 (pdf 525)): Proposed space interferometer for low-frequency waves from seed, intermediate-mass and lighter supermassive black-hole binaries at high redshift. Key numbers: Band ~100 microHz to 100 mHz; masses 1e2 to 1e7 M_sun.
- **Pulsar timing arrays** (technology, GA §46.6 p.508 (pdf 525)): Arrival times of pulses from many millisecond pulsars are monitored for years; a wave perturbs timing through its effect at the pulsar and at Earth. Key numbers: Band nanohertz to microhertz; sources are supermassive black-hole binaries.
- **Primordial stochastic background** (observation, GA §46.6 p.509 (pdf 526)): Relic waves from the Big Bang expected to dominate the spectrum at the lowest frequencies; whether any part is detectable depends on the cosmological model. Key numbers: Extends down to ~1e-18 Hz.
- **Gravitational power of everyday and solar-system sources** (numerical-estimate, GA p.511 (pdf 528)): Exercise 46.4 sets up the estimate P ~ G^4 m^5/(r^5 c^5) for the solar system, a black-hole binary and a shaken fist. Key numbers: Own estimate: Sun-Jupiter radiates about 5 kW; Sun-Earth about 200 W; a waved arm far below 1e-40 W.

## Historical notes

- **Henri Poincare (1905):** Suggested that gravitational disturbances propagate as waves. The book names him but gives no date; 1905 comes from outside the book. — Anticipated gravitational waves before general relativity existed. (GA p.494 (pdf 511))
- **Albert Einstein (1916):** Predicted gravitational waves from linearized general relativity (the book prints 1906); the quadrupole formula followed in 1918. — Theoretical origin of the chapter's central result. (GA p.494 (pdf 511))
- **Russell Hulse, Joseph Taylor (1974):** Discovered the binary pulsar PSR B1913+16, whose measured orbital decay later matched the gravitational-wave prediction; Nobel Prize 1993. — First, indirect, evidence that gravitational waves carry energy away. (GA §46.4 p.505 (pdf 522))
- **Rainer Weiss, Kip Thorne, Barry Barish, LIGO Scientific Collaboration (2015):** Direct detection of GW150914 from a binary black-hole merger by both LIGO detectors on 14 September 2015; Nobel Prize 2017. — Opened gravitational-wave astronomy and provided strong-field tests of general relativity. (GA §46.6 p.507 (pdf 524))
- **Albert A. Michelson, Edward Morley, Hendrik Lorentz (1887):** Margin note 24: the Michelson-Morley null result tested a v^2/c^2 ether effect predicted by Lorentz and led him to propose length contraction; its interferometer geometry is reused by LIGO. — Links the oldest precision interferometry in relativity to modern detectors, and notes it was not central to Einstein's motivation. (GA §46.6 p.507 (pdf 524))

## Notation and conventions

- **Metric signature and units:** eta = diag(-1, 1, 1, 1); geometrized units G = c = 1 for most derivations, with G and c restored in quadrupole, power and numerical results. — Mixed use: eqn 46.68 lacks the 1/c^5 needed in SI; eqns 46.42-46.44 use t - R for t - R/c. (GA §46.2 p.498 (pdf 515))
- **Plane-wave phase:** k.x means k_mu x^mu, so e^{ik.x} = e^{-i(omega t - |k| z)} for a wave along z; Re[ ] is understood and often dropped. (GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512))
- **Trace-reversed perturbation:** hbar_{mu nu} = h_{mu nu} - (1/2) eta_{mu nu} h; in TT gauge hbar = h. — The chapter sometimes switches between h and hbar without comment once in TT gauge. (GA §46.2 p.497 (pdf 514))
- **Gauge transformation sign:** A coordinate shift by +xi subtracts the symmetrized gradient of xi from the metric perturbation (eqn 46.17), and trace reversal adds an eta times divergence term (eqn 46.18). — Some texts use the opposite sign for xi. (GA §46.2 p.496 (pdf 513))
- **Comma and d-squared notation:** Commas denote partial derivatives; d^2 = eta^{mu nu} d_mu d_nu is the d'Alembertian, so the sourced equation reads -d^2 hbar = 16 pi G T. (GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512))
- **Polarization amplitudes:** h^TT_xx = -h^TT_yy = h_+, h^TT_xy = h^TT_yx = h_x for a wave along z; basis tensors e_+ and e_x. (GA §46.2 p.499 (pdf 516))
- **Tidal field:** Calligraphic E with E_ij = R_0i0j. — Same object often called the electric part of the Weyl tensor in other texts. (GA §46.2 p.499 (pdf 516))
- **Riemann index convention:** The linearized Riemann tensor is half an alternating sum of four second derivatives of h (eqn 46.29, taken from Chapter 45), and geodesic deviation for particles at rest carries an explicit minus sign in front of the R^mu_{0 alpha 0} contraction (eqn 46.28), so that R_{x0x0} = -(1/2) times the second time derivative of h_xx. (GA §46.2 p.498 (pdf 515))
- **Second mass moment:** I_ij denotes the integral of rho y_i y_j (called the moment of inertia tensor); Q_ij its traceless part; dots are time derivatives, three dots in the power formula. — The reading copy frequently drops the third dot. (GA §46.3 p.501 (pdf 518))
- **Spatial index placement:** For purely spatial tensors indices are all written down and repeated indices are summed (margin note 17). (GA §46.4 p.503 (pdf 520))
- **Averaging brackets:** Angle brackets denote averaging over several wavelengths and periods. (GA §46.3 p.502 (pdf 519))
- **Wave stress-energy symbol:** Lower-case t_{mu nu} for the effective energy-momentum of gravitational waves, capital T_{mu nu} for matter. (GA §46.3 p.502 (pdf 519))
- **Light-cone coordinates:** u = t - z, v = t + z with ds^2 = -du dv + dx^2 + dy^2. — Some texts include a factor 1/sqrt(2) or define u = (t - z)/2. (GA §46.5 p.505 (pdf 522))
- **Retarded time:** t_r = t - R/c. (GA §46.4 p.504 (pdf 521))

## Teaching gems

### Teach gravitational waves as a replay of electromagnetic waves: same gauge-fixing sequence, same plane-wave algebra, with each vector becoming a symmetric matrix. · GA §46.1 p.495 (pdf 512)

**Why it works:** Learners reuse a derivation they already trust and can locate exactly where gravity differs (extra index, 45 degree polarization, quadrupole sources, nonlinearity).

**App idea:** Two-column stepper: EM step on the left, gravity step on the right, with differences highlighted and a counter tracking independent components (4 -> 2 for EM, 10 -> 2 for gravity).

### Stage the 'the mass does not move' paradox before revealing proper distance. · GA §46.2 p.497 (pdf 514)

**Why it works:** A productive failure makes the gauge meaning of coordinates memorable and motivates geodesic deviation as the invariant tool.

**App idea:** Prediction prompt: learner guesses how a single bead moves, sees its fixed TT coordinates, then drags a ruler to a second bead and watches the length oscillate.

### Ring of free masses as the definition of polarization. · GA §46.2 p.499 (pdf 516)

**Why it works:** Turns abstract amplitude matrices into shapes; the 45 degree relation and the linear growth of displacement with distance are visible at a glance.

**App idea:** Polarization mixer with h_+ and h_x sliders and relative phase, showing linear and circular polarization of the ring.

### Conservation laws as radiation selection rules: charge kills EM monopole; mass, momentum and angular momentum kill gravitational monopole and both dipoles. · GA §46.2 p.500 (pdf 517)

**Why it works:** Explains why gravitational radiation is so weak and quadrupolar without solving the multipole problem in full.

**App idea:** Source sandbox with moment meters (I_0, I_1, S_1, I_2) that stay frozen or oscillate as the learner moves masses, with the radiated power readout lighting only for I_2.

### Put numbers in immediately: 5e-21 strain, 1e47 W, milliwatts per square metre, compared with Earth's 1e-9 potential. · GA §46.3 p.501 (pdf 518)

**Why it works:** The scale shock explains decades of detector development and justifies the background-plus-ripple approximation.

**App idea:** Strain calculator with sliders for masses, separation, frequency and distance, and a comparison bar showing proton widths over the LIGO arm.

### Negative energy makes radiating orbits speed up, linking a one-line energy argument to the Hulse-Taylor data. · GA §46.4 p.505 (pdf 522)

**Why it works:** Resolves a strong everyday intuition and connects theory to a Nobel-winning measurement.

**App idea:** Energy-well animation: a binary sliding deeper into its potential well as it radiates, with speed and period gauges and a live cumulative periastron-shift plot.

### Photon on a wave crest proves c-propagation in two lines. · GA §46.1 p.496 (pdf 513)

**Why it works:** A geometric picture replaces an abstract dispersion relation and ties k.k = 0 to light rays.

**App idea:** Spacetime diagram with draggable wave crests and a photon worldline locked to a crest.

### Exact plane wave in null coordinates reduces the full Einstein equation to one ODE. · GA §46.5 p.506 (pdf 523)

**Why it works:** Offers a rare nonlinear calculation small enough to do by hand, reassuring learners that waves are not an approximation artefact.

**App idea:** Profile designer: sketch F(u), the app integrates G'' = -(F''/F) G numerically and shows free particles focusing, including where the Rosen coordinates break down.

### Detector sensitivity argument: strain is fractional, so the signal scales with arm length and photon storage time must match the wave period. · GA §46.6 p.507 (pdf 524)

**Why it works:** Explains in plain terms why LIGO is kilometres long and uses optical cavities.

**App idea:** Arm-length slider from 1 m to 1000 km showing phase signal against a fixed noise floor, with a cavity-bounce counter.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Historical slips: Einstein's prediction dated 1906 instead of 1916, Michelson's birth year printed as 1952, spin-up said to be observed in 1974 (the pulsar was found then; decay was reported around 1978-79), and LIGO's arms given as 4.2 km (they are 4 km).** — Learners may repeat wrong dates or numbers. *Suggestion:* Correct silently in app text; if quoting, use 1916 (and 1918 for the quadrupole formula), 1852-1931, 1974 discovery and 1979 decay measurement, 4 km arms.
- **Printed eqn 46.24 (pure x wave) omits the dx^2 + dy^2 terms.** — Suggests the x wave removes transverse distances entirely, contradicting the 45 degree rotation claim. *Suggestion:* Show ds^2 = -dt^2 + dx^2 + dy^2 + 2 h_x dx dy + dz^2 and verify the rotation explicitly.
- **Eqn 46.15 sets hbar_{mu z} = 0 before TT gauge; Lorenz gauge alone only gives A_{alpha z} = -A_{alpha 0}.** — Blurs which conditions come from gauge fixing and which from the field equations. *Suggestion:* Track constraints explicitly: Lorenz (4), residual gauge (4), leaving 2; show A_{alpha z} = 0 only after A_{alpha 0} = 0.
- **Eqn 46.33 writes n^i for what is the change in separation, and the integration step silently drops constants and assumes separations much smaller than the wavelength.** — Confusion between total separation and displacement; misapplication to detectors longer than a wavelength. *Suggestion:* Write delta x^i = (1/2) h_ij x^j and state the long-wavelength assumption.
- **The quadrupole formula gives hbar_ij, not its TT part; the text says TT gauge makes the trace irrelevant without mentioning the transverse projection that is only introduced in Exercise 46.5.** — Learners may use non-transverse components (e.g. along the line of sight) as physical strain. *Suggestion:* Introduce the TT projector Lambda_{ij,kl}(n) alongside eqn 46.44 and show the binary example viewed edge-on and face-on.
- **I_ij is called the moment of inertia tensor, and the Q_ij formula omits delta_ij; the luminosity derivation needs a traceless tensor but uses I_ij.** — Confusion with the mechanics inertia tensor and errors for sources whose trace varies (e.g. radial pulsation of a non-spherical body). *Suggestion:* Use the reduced quadrupole Q_ij throughout the power formula and note it equals I_ij when Tr I is constant.
- **Example 46.9 inputs are not mutually consistent: two 30 solar-mass holes at 2000 km separation orbit at about 5 Hz, so the quoted 10 Hz is really the wave frequency; with the book's numbers the flux at Earth is about 1.3 mW m^-2, not below 1 (own calculation).** — Careful learners checking with Kepler's law get a mismatch and lose trust. *Suggestion:* Present orbital and wave frequency separately (f_GW = 2 f_orb) and recompute; use the discrepancy as a teaching moment.
- **Sign bookkeeping in Example 46.11 and eqn 46.70: L is defined as dE/dt (positive radiated power) but then used as the orbital energy change, and dP/dt picks up its minus sign only if dE_orb/dt = -L.** — Sign errors when deriving decay rates independently. *Suggestion:* Name the two quantities: L_GW > 0 and dE_orb/dt = -L_GW; carry them through.
- **Printed J = sqrt(G M a^3) = -E omega/2 is dimensionally wrong; correct is J = 2 M a^2 omega = sqrt(G M^3 a) = -2E/omega. Eqn 46.68 also omits the 1/c^5 factor.** — Learners following the algebra will not reproduce eqn 46.70. *Suggestion:* Use the corrected expressions and check units at each step.
- **Hulse-Taylor is discussed with a formula for circular equal-mass black holes, but the system is an eccentric neutron-star binary; the circular formula underestimates the decay by roughly a factor of 12.** — Learners who plug in the pulsar numbers get a wrong answer and may doubt general relativity. *Suggestion:* Introduce the Peters-Mathews eccentricity enhancement factor f(e) and show that it closes the gap.
- **Printed Gamma^v_xx = 2F'/F and Gamma^v_yy = 2G'/G; correct values are 2FF' and 2GG'.** — Learners recomputing curvature from these connections will get inconsistent Riemann components. *Suggestion:* Provide a symbolic-algebra check of the metric 46.72.
- **The 'exact solution' demonstration only verifies eqn 46.75 to first order in eps, which is just the linear theory again.** — The claim that waves survive nonlinearity is not actually demonstrated. *Suggestion:* Note that for any F the equation is a linear ODE for G, so exact solutions exist; exhibit one numerically and mention the coordinate singularity where G reaches zero (wave focusing).
- **Example 46.13 drops the laser frequency factor and sign from the phase and says intensity is proportional to phase, which holds only near a chosen operating point.** — Misunderstanding of how interferometric readout works (a dark port has intensity quadratic in small phase). *Suggestion:* Write Delta phi = (2 omega_0 L/c) h_+ and explain operating-point offsets and cavity gain.
- **Chapter summary says the polarizations are '45 degrees out of phase'.** — Confuses spatial rotation with temporal phase. *Suggestion:* Phrase as related by a 45 degree rotation; show circular polarization as the quarter-cycle phase case.
- **Energy of gravitational waves is introduced via a quoted Isaacson result and exercises; the scale-separation requirement (wavelength much smaller than background curvature radius) is not stated.** — The averaging looks like an arbitrary trick. *Suggestion:* State the two-scale condition and illustrate averaging with a rapidly oscillating function on a slowly varying background.
- **Reading copy OCR drops one time-derivative dot in the quadrupole power formulas (46.54, 46.58, 46.60, 46.61, 46.105, summary) and in Example 46.9, shows a bar for two dots in 46.34, and loses eqns 46.3-46.11 labels and margin notes 9-10.** — Anyone working only from the reading copy would get wrong dimensions. *Suggestion:* Use the corrected equations recorded in this dossier.
- **Detector response is treated only for a + wave arriving perpendicular to the detector plane; antenna patterns, the two-detector time delay and sign flip visible in Fig. 46.5 are not explained.** — Learners cannot interpret why the H1 trace is shifted and inverted. *Suggestion:* Add an antenna-pattern discussion and point out the roughly 7 ms light-travel delay between sites and their different arm orientations.

## Tutor notes

- Open by asking: 'If gravity propagates at a finite speed, what should happen far away when two stars orbit each other?' Then ask what the learner remembers about how light waves come out of Maxwell's equations.
- Order of explanation: EM wave recap -> gravity wave equation -> plane-wave constraints -> TT gauge and the two polarizations -> test masses and geodesic deviation -> ring pictures -> why quadrupole -> quadrupole formula and numbers -> energy and luminosity -> binary inspiral and Hulse-Taylor -> exact plane wave -> detectors and GW150914.
- For novices, skip the gauge algebra and start from the ring of masses and the displacement formula delta x = (1/2) h x; return to TT gauge only when they ask why there are exactly two polarizations.
- Check understanding: ask the learner to sketch the ring for a + wave, then rotate the sketch by 45 and by 90 degrees and say what polarization each corresponds to.
- Check understanding: 'A single free mass in TT coordinates does not move. Does that mean LIGO cannot work?' Look for an answer about distances between masses.
- Check understanding: ask why a perfectly spherical star collapsing or pulsating radially emits no gravitational waves, expecting a conservation or symmetry argument about the quadrupole.
- Common question: 'If waves stretch space, why don't the laser wavelengths stretch too and cancel the effect?' Answer: light in the arms is affected, but the measurement compares round-trip times in two arms whose storage time is a significant part of the wave period; the effect does not cancel for gravitational-wave frequencies well below the inverse light travel time.
- Common question: 'Where does the energy of the waves live?' Emphasize that it cannot be pinned to a point; only averages over many wavelengths make sense, which is also why t_{mu nu} is not a normal tensor field.
- Use the numbers: have the learner compute h ~ 1e-21 and turn it into an arm-length change dL = (1/2) h L for a 4 km arm, which is about 2e-18 m. That is roughly 1/400 of a proton radius, so thinking of it as 'a thousandth of a proton' is only the right order of magnitude.
- When discussing inspiral, ask the learner to predict whether the orbit speeds up or slows down before showing E = -GM^2/(4a); many will say slows down.
- Flag the printed errors (eqn 46.24, J formula, Gamma^v_xx, 46.68 c^5, 46.77 factors, dates) proactively if the learner is working from the book.
- Advanced learners: connect the 45 degree rotation to spin 2 (patterns repeat under rotation by 180/s degrees) and preview gravitons; mention circular polarization along the axis of a binary from eqn 46.104.
- If the learner asks about eccentric orbits or Hulse-Taylor numbers, introduce the eccentricity enhancement factor rather than letting the circular formula be taken as exact.
