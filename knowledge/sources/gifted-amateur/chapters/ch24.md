# GA ch24 · Photons in the Schwarzschild geometry

> Runs light through the same conserved-quantity machinery already used for planets: the null condition becomes an effective-energy equation with a photon potential W_eff = (1 - 2M/r)/r^2 that does not care about angular momentum, whose single peak at r = 3M marks the unstable photon sphere and the capture threshold b = sqrt(27) M; the orbit equation u'' + u = 3u^2, perturbed about a straight line, gives the 4M/b deflection (quoted as about 1.8 arcsec for a ray grazing the Sun, 1.75 with precise solar values; the 1919 eclipse test); a point mass is shown to be an imperfect lens with Einstein rings; and photon momenta transferred to a static observer's frame give a critical emission angle that turns, by time reversal, into a dark patch on the sky.

**Pages:** printed 254–261 · pdf 271–278 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

Algebra is light for readers who did Chapters 22-23, since each step mirrors the massive case, and the perturbative deflection uses only a forced oscillator. Harder spots: the index rearrangement in Example 24.3, the m -> 0 limit in Example 24.7, vielbein conversions, and the two-branch critical angle, which the book does not explain. The text has several slips (eqn 24.22, margin note 3, the massive dr/dtau entry of the summary table, inconsistent Delta phi labelling) and leaves lensing mostly to exercises.

## Role in the book

Third of the four Schwarzschild chapters (21 metric, 22 constants of motion and redshift, 23 massive orbits and perihelion precession). It reuses Chapter 22's E-tilde and L-tilde and Chapter 23's effective-potential and u-substitution recipes almost line for line, now for null geodesics, so that the second classical solar-system test (light bending) can be computed and compared with Soldner's Newtonian value. Its closing section, on what a static observer sees, supplies the critical angle and the photon sphere that Chapter 25 (black holes, especially 25.4 on looking around near a hole and the Event Horizon Telescope image) builds on, and Exercise 24.4 on the affine parameter along a light ray is explicitly reused in Exercise 26.4. The chapter-end table unifies the coordinate-time equations for massive and massless motion as a reference for the black-hole chapters.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can explain why a light ray that is a null geodesic of spacetime can still trace a bent path in space, using the position-dependent coordinate speed of light and a Fermat-principle picture.
- Reader can derive the photon effective-energy equation 1/b^2 = (dr/dlambda)^2/L^2 + W_eff from the Schwarzschild null condition and the conserved E-tilde and L-tilde.
- Reader can show that b = L-tilde/E-tilde equals the geometric impact parameter of a ray that starts at infinity.
- Reader can sketch W_eff(r), locate its maximum at r = 3M with value 1/(27M^2), and classify incoming rays as scattered (b > sqrt(27) M), captured (b < sqrt(27) M) or marginally orbiting on the unstable photon sphere.
- Reader can convert the null condition into the orbit equation (du/dphi)^2 = M^2/b^2 - u^2 + 2u^3 and u'' + u = 3u^2, and identify 3u^2 as the relativistic correction to straight-line motion.
- Reader can perturb about the straight-line solution u0 = (M/b) sin phi, solve for u1, and extract the total deflection 4M/b from the asymptotic directions where u = 0.
- Reader can evaluate 4GM/(c^2 b) for a ray grazing the Sun (about 1.75 arcsec), state that it is twice the Newtonian corpuscular value, and summarize the 1919 eclipse test and later radio confirmations with their caveats.
- Reader can show that a point mass focuses rays at a distance D = b^2/4M that grows with b, explain why this means no single focus, and describe when an Einstein ring forms.
- Reader can derive the small-angle point-lens equation theta_i = theta_s + theta_E^2/theta_i from a ray diagram and define the Einstein angle.
- Reader can build photon four-momentum components in Schwarzschild coordinates as the massless limit of m dx/dtau at fixed energy at infinity, and express them in terms of epsilon and b.
- Reader can use static-observer vielbeins to get sin psi = (b/r)(1 - 2M/r)^{1/2} and the critical angle sin psi_c = (sqrt(27) M/r)(1 - 2M/r)^{1/2}, choosing the correct branch above and below r = 3M.
- Reader can use time-reversal symmetry of the static geometry to argue that directions inside the capture cone look black to a static observer, anticipating the black-hole shadow.

## Teaching approach

A compact, reuse-driven chapter. Instead of solving the null geodesic equation, the authors recycle the Chapter 22-23 template (conserved quantities plus a normalization condition gives an energy equation, then an orbit equation in u) and rename the pieces for light, so almost every new result is a small modification of something the reader has just done for planets. Physics is kept in front: an optics heuristic opens the chapter, the potential plot is read qualitatively before any orbit is solved, solar numbers and the eclipse story follow the perturbative deflection formula immediately, and the lens analogy is both used and deliberately broken. The final section changes the question from 'where does the ray go' to 'what does an observer see', using vielbeins from Chapter 10 and a time-reversal argument to set up the black-hole chapter. Most calculations sit in shaded Example boxes; the chapter ends with a two-part reference table.

**Style:** physical-motivation-first, components-first, derivation-heavy, example-driven, analogy-driven, observational-astrophysics, conversational-informal

**Narrative arc**

1. Motivating question: in Newtonian gravity as developed so far light ignores gravity, yet Soldner's corpuscular reasoning already predicted a small bending near the Sun; what does GR say?
2. Heuristic (Example 24.1): in a static metric the coordinate speed of light varies with position, so gravity acts like a graded refractive index and a Fermat least-time principle bends spatial rays.
3. Model: photons follow null geodesics labelled by an affine parameter; the conserved E-tilde and L-tilde from Chapter 22 still apply because they came from symmetry, not from proper time.
4. Formalism: rewrite the null condition as an effective-energy equation with 1/b^2 as the energy and W_eff(r) as a potential independent of L-tilde.
5. Interpretation: identify b as the impact parameter (Example 24.2) and read Fig. 24.2 for scattering, capture and the unstable photon sphere at r = 3M.
6. Orbit equation (Example 24.3): switch to u = M/r and differentiate to get u'' + u = 3u^2, contrasting with Newtonian straight lines u'' + u = 0.
7. Check against experiment (Examples 24.4-24.5): perturb around a straight line, find a 4M/b deflection, plug in solar numbers, compare with Soldner, recount Eddington 1919 with caveats and later radio confirmation.
8. Extension and critique (Example 24.6): treat the mass as a lens, show the focal distance depends on b so there is no focus, and introduce the Einstein ring (exercises develop the lens equation).
9. New question (Section 24.2): what does an observer at radius r see? Build photon momentum as the m -> 0 limit at fixed energy at infinity (Example 24.7).
10. Measurement: convert to the static observer's orthonormal frame with vielbeins to get the emission angle psi, then the critical angle psi_c and its shrinking escape cone as r -> 2M.
11. Hand-off: time-reversal symmetry turns captured emission directions into a black region of the observer's sky, setting up black holes; the summary table collects massive and photon equations side by side.

**Signature moves**

- Dictionary-style reuse of the massive-particle energy equation: 1/b^2 plays total energy, (dr/dlambda)^2/L^2 plays kinetic energy, W_eff plays the potential.
- Opening with an optics picture (coordinate light speed as a refractive index, Fermat's principle) before any geodesic calculation, while flagging it only as partial justification.
- Isolating the relativistic physics in a single extra term (3u^2) by writing the photon orbit equation next to its Newtonian straight-line limit.
- Perturbing about the flat-space straight line and reading the deflection off the two asymptotic angles where 1/r vanishes, instead of integrating an elliptic integral.
- Immediate numbers and history after the formula: solar grazing ray, factor of two over Soldner, Eddington's eclipse, and a note that his systematics were later questioned.
- Stress-testing an analogy: a mass deflects like a lens but its focal distance scales as b^2, so the analogy fails and rings rather than points appear.
- Defining photon momentum as a limit of the massive formula with energy at infinity held fixed, so the undefined dtau for light never appears.
- Translating coordinate components into what a static observer measures via vielbeins, yielding an angle whose form r sin(psi) = b(1 - 2M/r)^{1/2} echoes flat-space geometry.
- Using time reversal of a static spacetime to convert an emission statement (photons that fall in) into an observation statement (a black region of sky).
- Ending with a side-by-side table of massive and massless equations of motion in the equatorial plane.

## Section by section

### Chapter opening: can gravity bend light? — p.254 (pdf 271)

Notes that in the Newtonian picture used so far light moves in straight lines, then mentions Soldner's early corpuscular estimate of about 0.8 arcsec for starlight skimming the Sun (with margin credit to Cavendish, Michell and Newton). Example 24.1 gives a first reason light should bend in GR: for a static diagonal metric the null condition makes the light speed measured in coordinate time depend on g_00, which behaves like a refractive index, and the spatial path obeys a Fermat least-time principle built from g_ij/(-g_00).

**Concepts:** coordinate speed of light, Fermat's principle in a static spacetime, gravitational refractive index, Newtonian corpuscular light deflection

**Key moves**
- Set ds^2 = 0 for a static diagonal metric and solve for the coordinate speed c'^2 = -c^2 g_00 (eqn 24.2).
- Link g_00 to the Newtonian potential (margin note 3) so c' visibly depends on position.
- Separate the ideas 'null geodesic in spacetime' and 'geodesic in space': the former need not be the latter.
- State without proof that spatial rays extremize the integral of sqrt(g_ij dx^i dx^j/(-g_00)) (eqn 24.3) as partial justification for bending.

### §24.1 Photon trajectories: effective energy, impact parameter and the photon potential — p.254 (pdf 271)

Photons have no proper time, so world lines are labelled with an affine parameter and obey the null condition. Writing that condition in Schwarzschild spacetime with the conserved E-tilde and L-tilde and rescaling gives an effective-energy equation whose energy is 1/b^2 and whose potential W_eff = (1 - 2M/r)/r^2 does not involve L-tilde. Example 24.2 shows b = L/E is the impact parameter of an incoming ray. The potential has one maximum, 1/(27M^2) at r = 3M, and no minimum: rays with b above sqrt(27) M bounce off the hill and are deflected, rays below it plunge to r = 0, and exactly at the peak there are circular but unstable orbits on the photon sphere; no stable light orbits exist.

**Concepts:** null geodesic, affine parameter, conserved energy and angular momentum along null geodesics, photon effective-energy equation, photon effective potential, impact parameter, photon sphere, critical impact parameter, photon capture

**Key moves**
- Replace proper time by an affine parameter and impose u.u = 0 (eqn 24.4).
- Substitute u^t = E/(1 - 2M/r) and u^phi = L/r^2 into the null condition (eqn 24.5).
- Multiply by (1 - 2M/r) and divide by L^2 to reach 1/b^2 = (dr/dlambda)^2/L^2 + W_eff with W_eff = (1 - 2M/r)/r^2 (eqns 24.6-24.7).
- Evaluate L/E far from the mass for a straight ray at offset d, using phi ~ d/r and dr/dt ~ -1, to show b = d (eqns 24.8-24.9).
- Set dW_eff/dr = 0 to get r0 = 3M and W_eff(r0) = 1/(27M^2); compare 1/b^2 with this peak to sort rays into scattered, captured and orbiting.
- Note that the absence of a minimum rules out stable circular photon orbits.

### §24.1 Photon trajectories (continued): the orbit equation — p.256 (pdf 273)

Example 24.3 derives the shape equation for equatorial rays. Dividing the null condition by (u^phi)^2 turns it into an equation for dr/dphi; the time term is rewritten with lowered components so that 1/b^2 appears; substituting u = M/r gives (du/dphi)^2 = M^2/b^2 - u^2 + 2u^3, and differentiating gives u'' + u = 3u^2. Setting the particle mass to zero in the Newtonian orbit equation of Chapter 20 gives u'' + u = 0 (straight lines), so the 3u^2 term carries all of the relativistic bending.

**Concepts:** photon orbit equation, Binet substitution, null geodesic

**Key moves**
- Write dr/dphi = u^r/u^phi (eqn 24.10) and divide the null condition by (u^phi)^2 (eqns 24.11-24.12).
- Use raised and lowered components (margin note 5) to replace the time ratio by -(1 - 2M/r)^{-1} r^4/b^2.
- Substitute u = M/r to reach the first integral (eqn 24.15) and differentiate to reach eqn 24.16.
- Compare with the massless limit of the Newtonian orbit equation (eqn 20.33) to isolate the correction term.

### §24.1 Photon trajectories (continued): deflection of starlight by the Sun — p.257 (pdf 274)

Light bending is introduced as the second classical solar-system test. Example 24.4 treats M/b as small, starts from the straight line u0 = (M/b) sin phi, and solves the forced equation for the first correction u1, obtaining a trajectory whose two zeros of u sit slightly beyond phi = 0 and phi = pi; the extra 2M/b at each end adds to a total deflection of 4M/b. Example 24.5 restores G and c for a ray grazing the Sun to get about 8.6e-6 rad, roughly 1.8 arcsec and about twice Soldner's figure, then recounts Eddington's 1919 eclipse measurement, its role in making Einstein famous, later doubts about its systematic errors, and modern radio-source measurements that agree well with GR. A rubber-sheet cartoon (Fig. 24.4) shows the apparent shift of a star.

**Concepts:** perturbation about the straight-line solution, gravitational light deflection, Newtonian corpuscular light deflection, classical solar-system tests of general relativity, 1919 eclipse expedition, radio interferometric light-deflection measurements

**Key moves**
- Choose the zeroth-order straight-line solution u0 = (M/b) sin phi of the Newtonian equation (eqn 24.17).
- Insert u = u0 + u1 and keep only 3u0^2 on the right; use sin^2 = (1 - cos 2phi)/2 (eqns 24.18-24.19).
- Solve with a constant plus a cos 2phi particular solution, u1 = (1/2)(M/b)^2 (3 + cos 2phi) (eqns 24.20-24.21).
- Find the asymptotes from u = 0, which gives phi ~ -2M/b and phi ~ pi + 2M/b, so the ray turns through 4M/b in total.
- Restore constants: Delta phi = 4GM/(c^2 b) for b = R_sun gives about 1.8 arcsec (eqn 24.23).

### §24.1 Photon trajectories (continued): gravitational lensing and the Einstein ring — p.258 (pdf 275)

Because masses deflect light they act somewhat like lenses, but Example 24.6 shows the analogy is imperfect: a parallel ray at distance b crosses the axis a distance D = b/theta = b^2/4M behind the mass, so outer rays focus farther away and there is no single focal point. When source, lens and observer are collinear, the symmetric bending produces a ring image (the Einstein ring, Fig. 24.5b); the exercises build the small-angle lens equation and the Einstein angle.

**Concepts:** gravitational lensing, gravitational lens focal distance, Einstein ring, Einstein angle, point-mass lens equation

**Key moves**
- Use the small-angle relation D = b/theta with theta = 4M/b to get D = b^2/4M (eqn 24.24).
- Contrast with a glass lens, for which D would be the same for all b.
- Invoke axial symmetry for an aligned source to turn two images into a ring.

### §24.2 Looking around: photon momentum in Schwarzschild coordinates — p.258 (pdf 275)

To ask what an observer sees, the chapter needs photon four-momentum. Example 24.7 starts from a massive particle, writes p = m (dx/dt) u^t, trades u^t for E-tilde/(1 - 2M/r), and names m E-tilde the energy at infinity epsilon; the resulting expression survives the limit m -> 0 and so defines photon momentum, at the cost of being tied to coordinate time. Using b = r^2 (1 - 2M/r)^{-1} dphi/dt and the null condition divided by dt^2 fixes dr/dt, giving p^t = epsilon/(1 - 2M/r), p^r = +/- epsilon [1 - (b^2/r^2)(1 - 2M/r)]^{1/2}, p^theta = 0 and p^phi = epsilon b/r^2.

**Concepts:** photon four-momentum in Schwarzschild spacetime, energy at infinity, conserved energy and angular momentum along null geodesics, impact parameter

**Key moves**
- Recall the flat-spacetime relation p^mu = E u^mu for photons.
- Write p^mu = m (dx^mu/dt)(dt/dtau) and replace dt/dtau using E-tilde (eqns 24.25-24.27).
- Hold epsilon = m E-tilde fixed and let m -> 0 (eqn 24.28), giving coordinate components (eqn 24.29).
- Divide the null condition by dt^2 and use b to solve for dr/dt (eqns 24.30-24.31), then assemble eqn 24.32.

### §24.2 Looking around (continued): critical emission angle and the dark part of the sky — p.259 (pdf 276)

A static emitter at radius r sends photons at angle psi measured in its own orthonormal frame from the outward radial direction (Fig. 24.6). The sine of psi is the ratio of sideways to time components of momentum in that frame; converting with the vielbeins (e_t)^t-hat = (1 - 2M/r)^{1/2} and (e_phi)^phi-hat = r gives sin psi = (b/r)(1 - 2M/r)^{1/2}. Setting b = sqrt(27) M defines a critical angle beyond which rays have 1/b^2 above the peak of W_eff (Fig. 24.7) and fall in; the escaping fraction shrinks as r falls and vanishes at r = 2M. Because the static geometry is unchanged under time reversal, the same cones describe reception: directions whose backward rays would come from the hole bring no light, so that part of the observer's sky is black, the theme of the next chapter.

**Concepts:** static observer orthonormal frame, locally measured emission angle, critical emission angle, photon capture, time-reversal symmetry of a static spacetime, black-hole shadow

**Key moves**
- Define sin psi = u^phi-hat/u^t-hat = p^phi-hat/p^t-hat in the emitter's local frame (eqn 24.33).
- Convert coordinate components with vielbeins (eqn 24.34, margin note 9) and insert eqn 24.32 to get eqn 24.35.
- Impose the capture threshold b = sqrt(27) M to get sin psi_c = (sqrt(27) M/r)(1 - 2M/r)^{1/2}.
- Track how the capture cone widens as r decreases, until everything is captured at r = 2M.
- Reverse the film: emission cones become viewing cones, and the captured directions appear black.

## Concepts

### null geodesic

*definition · revisited* · also: light-like geodesic, photon world line

A geodesic whose tangent vector has zero length at every point, g_{mu nu} (dx^mu/dlambda)(dx^nu/dlambda) = 0 with lambda affine; freely propagating light follows such curves.

**How introduced:** Presented as the key physical input for photons right after noting that proper time cannot label a light ray; combined with the Schwarzschild metric it replaces the u.u = -1 normalization used for planets.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic, affine parameter, metric tensor

$$
g_{\mu\nu}\frac{dx^\mu}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^\nu}{d\lambda}=0
$$

**Where:** GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272); GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273)

### affine parameter

*definition · revisited* · also: lambda

A parameter along a curve in which the geodesic equation takes its standard form; it is unique up to lambda -> a lambda + b and is needed for null curves, where proper time does not advance.

**How introduced:** Introduced as a stand-in for proper time that ticks off equal steps along a photon world line; Exercise 24.4 later shows how to compute it along a cosmological light ray.

**Prerequisites:** geodesic, proper time

$$
\lambda=\int\frac{1}{C}\frac{a(t)^2\,dr}{(1-kr^2)^{1/2}}
$$

**Notes:** For photons the normalization of lambda is arbitrary, so E-tilde and L-tilde individually have no absolute meaning; only b = L/E is physical.

**Where:** GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272); GA p.261 (pdf 278)

### coordinate speed of light

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: c prime, variable coordinate light speed

The rate at which a light signal covers coordinate distance per unit coordinate time. In a static diagonal metric the null condition gives g_ij v^i v^j = -c^2 g_00, so it varies with position even though any local observer measures c.

**How introduced:** Example 24.1 sets ds^2 = 0 for a static diagonal metric and reads off c'; a margin note ties g_00 to the Newtonian potential so c' visibly changes near a mass.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, static spacetime, Newtonian limit of g_00

$$
-c^2g_{00}=g_{ij}\frac{dx^i}{dt}\frac{dx^j}{dt}=c'^2
$$

**Where:** GA p.254 (pdf 271)

### Fermat's principle in a static spacetime

*principle · introduced* · also: principle of least time, optical metric, Fermat's principle

In a static spacetime the spatial track of a light ray makes the integral of sqrt(g_ij dx^i dx^j/(-g_00)) stationary; equivalently it is a geodesic of the rescaled 3-metric g_ij/(-g_00), which extremizes coordinate travel time.

**How introduced:** Quoted without proof in Example 24.1 as partial justification for bending, after pointing out that a null geodesic of spacetime need not be a geodesic of space.

**Prerequisites:** coordinate speed of light, null geodesic, variational principle

$$
\delta\int\left(\frac{g_{ij}dx^idx^j}{-g_{00}}\right)^{1/2}=0
$$

**Where:** GA p.254 (pdf 271)

### gravitational refractive index

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: effective refractive index of a gravitational field, gravity as an optical medium

The ratio of c to the coordinate speed of light, which lets a static gravitational field be modelled as an inhomogeneous optical medium; in isotropic weak-field coordinates n is about 1 - 2 Phi/c^2, larger nearer the mass, so rays bend toward it.

**How introduced:** Suggested in Example 24.1 by the observation that the g_00 factor in the light-speed formula looks like an index of refraction.

**Prerequisites:** coordinate speed of light, Fermat's principle in a static spacetime

$$
n=\frac{c}{c'}
$$

$$
n\approx1-\frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\ \text{(weak field, isotropic coordinates)}
$$

**Notes:** The book loosely calls sqrt(-g_00) the index; with a flat spatial metric the index is 1/sqrt(-g_00), and keeping only g_00 reproduces just half the full GR deflection (the spatial metric supplies the rest).

**Where:** GA p.254 (pdf 271)

### conserved energy and angular momentum along null geodesics

*law · revisited* · also: E-tilde and L-tilde for photons, constants of the motion for light

Because the Schwarzschild metric does not depend on t or phi, E-tilde = (1 - 2M/r) dt/dlambda and L-tilde = r^2 dphi/dlambda (equatorial plane) are constant along any geodesic, null ones included, when lambda is an affine parameter.

**How introduced:** A margin note stresses that these constants came from symmetry arguments in the previous chapters, not from proper time, so they carry over to photons unchanged.

**Prerequisites:** Killing vector field, affine parameter, Schwarzschild metric

$$
\tilde E=\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)u^t
$$

$$
\tilde L=r^2u^\phi
$$

**Where:** GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272); GA §24.2 p.258 (pdf 275); GA p.260 (pdf 277)

### photon effective-energy equation

*law · core* · also: effective energy equation for photons, radial equation for light

The Schwarzschild null condition rewritten as a one-dimensional energy balance 1/b^2 = (1/L^2)(dr/dlambda)^2 + W_eff(r), where 1/b^2 plays total energy and (dr/dlambda)^2/L^2 plays kinetic energy.

**How introduced:** Derived by writing the null condition in terms of E-tilde and L-tilde, multiplying by (1 - 2M/r) and dividing by L^2, explicitly mirroring the energy equation for massive particles in Chapters 22-23.

**Prerequisites:** null geodesic, conserved energy and angular momentum along null geodesics, relativistic effective potential

$$
\frac{1}{b^2}=\frac{1}{\tilde L^2}\left(\frac{dr}{d\lambda}\right)^2+W_{\rm eff}(r)
$$

**Where:** GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272)

### photon effective potential

*mathematical-object · core* · also: W_eff, effective potential for light

W_eff(r) = (1/r^2)(1 - 2M/r). It is negative inside r = 2M, zero at 2M, rises to a single maximum 1/(27M^2) at r = 3M and falls off as 1/r^2 at large r; it has no minimum and, unlike V_eff for massive particles, contains no angular momentum.

**How introduced:** Defined as the potential term of the photon energy equation and immediately plotted (Fig. 24.2) and compared with the Newtonian and relativistic potentials for massive particles.

**Prerequisites:** photon effective-energy equation, relativistic effective potential

$$
W_{\rm eff}(r)=\frac{1}{r^2}\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)
$$

$$
W_{\rm eff}(3M)=\frac{1}{27M^2}
$$

**Notes:** Because the angular momentum has been scaled into 1/b^2, every ray meets the same hill; which rays pass depends only on b.

**Where:** GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272); GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273); GA §24.2 p.259 (pdf 276)

### impact parameter

*definition · core* · also: b, d

The perpendicular distance between a mass and the straight-line asymptote of an incoming ray; for Schwarzschild null geodesics it equals the ratio b = L-tilde/E-tilde, which is independent of how the affine parameter is normalized.

**How introduced:** Example 24.2 evaluates L/E far from the mass for a ray travelling parallel to the x-axis at height d (Fig. 24.1), using phi ~ d/r and dr/dt ~ -1, and finds b = d; the text adds that b grows with angular momentum and shrinks with energy.

**Prerequisites:** conserved energy and angular momentum along null geodesics, plane polar coordinates

$$
b=\left|\frac{\tilde L}{\tilde E}\right|
$$

$$
b=r^2\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}\frac{d\phi}{dt}
$$

**Where:** GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272); GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273); GA §24.2 p.259 (pdf 276)

### photon sphere

*phenomenon · core* · also: photon orbit, r = 3M

The sphere r = 3M on which circular null geodesics exist, for rays with b = sqrt(27) M sitting exactly at the peak of W_eff; since it is a maximum, any perturbation sends light either inward or out to infinity, so these orbits are unstable.

**How introduced:** Read off Fig. 24.2 by analogy with the unstable circular orbit at the maximum of V_eff for massive particles.

**Prerequisites:** photon effective potential, unstable circular orbit

$$
r_0=3M,\quad b^2=27M^2
$$

**Where:** GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273); GA §24.2 p.259 (pdf 276)

### critical impact parameter

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: b = sqrt(27) M, capture impact parameter

The impact parameter b_c = sqrt(27) M = 3 sqrt(3) M (about 5.2 M) at which 1/b^2 equals the peak of W_eff; incoming rays with larger b are deflected and escape, rays with smaller b are captured.

**How introduced:** Obtained by comparing the photon energy level 1/b^2 with the maximum 1/(27M^2) of W_eff, then reused in Section 24.2 to define the critical emission angle.

**Prerequisites:** photon effective potential, impact parameter

$$
b_c=\sqrt{27}\,M
$$

**Where:** GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273); GA §24.2 p.259 (pdf 276)

### photon capture

*phenomenon · developed* · also: light capture, spiralling into the origin

A ray moving inward whose 1/b^2 exceeds the maximum of W_eff meets no turning point and continues to smaller r, through r = 2M and on to r = 0 in these coordinates; outside 3M an outward-moving ray escapes whatever its b.

**How introduced:** Stated from the potential plot for incoming rays with small impact parameter, then applied to rays emitted near the mass in Section 24.2 (Figs. 24.6 and 24.7).

**Prerequisites:** photon effective potential, critical impact parameter

$$
\frac{1}{b^2}>W_{\rm eff}(3M)=\frac{1}{27M^2}
$$

**Notes:** The book describes captured photons as 'destroyed' at r = 0; the horizon at 2M and the nature of r = 0 are postponed to Chapters 25-26.

**Where:** GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273); GA §24.2 p.259 (pdf 276); GA §24.2 p.260 (pdf 277)

### photon orbit equation

*law · core* · also: shape equation for light rays, u'' + u = 3u^2

The equation for the path r(phi) of an equatorial light ray in Schwarzschild spacetime, in u = M/r: (du/dphi)^2 = M^2/b^2 - u^2 + 2u^3, or after differentiation u'' + u = 3u^2; without the right-hand side its solutions are straight lines.

**How introduced:** Example 24.3 divides the null condition by (u^phi)^2, rewrites the time term via lowered components so that 1/b^2 appears, substitutes u = M/r, and differentiates; the massless Newtonian limit of eqn 20.33 is shown for contrast.

**Prerequisites:** null geodesic, impact parameter, Binet substitution

$$
\left(\frac{du}{d\phi}\right)^2=\frac{M^2}{b^2}-u^2+2u^3
$$

$$
\frac{d^2u}{d\phi^2}+u=3u^2
$$

**Where:** GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273); GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)

### Binet substitution

*technique · revisited* · also: u = M/r substitution, u = 1/r

Replacing the radius by its inverse (here the dimensionless u = M/r) and using phi as the independent variable, which turns central-force orbit equations into forced oscillator equations whose Newtonian solutions are straight lines or conics.

**How introduced:** Reused from the massive-orbit chapters; a margin note also gives the straight line in polar form u = 1/r so the zeroth-order solution is recognizable.

**Prerequisites:** plane polar coordinates, Newtonian orbit equation

$$
u=\frac{1}{r}=-\frac{m\cos\theta-\sin\theta}{c}
$$

$$
u=\frac{M}{r}
$$

**Where:** GA p.254 (pdf 271); GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273)

### perturbation about the straight-line solution

*technique · developed* · also: first-order expansion in M/b, weak-deflection expansion

Writing u = u0 + u1 with u0 = (M/b) sin phi the flat-space straight line, inserting into u'' + u = 3u^2 and keeping only 3u0^2 as the source, so that u1 is a small correction of order (M/b)^2 found by elementary methods.

**How introduced:** Example 24.4 motivates it by the smallness of M/b for starlight passing the Sun, uses the identity sin^2 phi = (1 - cos 2phi)/2 and a constant plus cos 2phi trial solution.

**Prerequisites:** photon orbit equation, linear ODE with forcing

$$
u_1''+u_1\approx3u_0^2=\frac32\left(\frac{M}{b}\right)^2(1-\cos2\phi)
$$

**Where:** GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)

### gravitational light deflection

*phenomenon · core* · also: bending of light, deflection of starlight, light bending

The total change in direction of a ray passing a spherical mass with impact parameter b; to first order in M/b it is 4M/b, that is 4GM/(c^2 b), and it does not depend on the photon's energy.

**How introduced:** Obtained in Example 24.4 from the two angles where u = 0 on the perturbed trajectory; Example 24.5 evaluates it for a ray grazing the Sun and connects it to the eclipse test.

**Prerequisites:** perturbation about the straight-line solution, impact parameter, photon orbit equation

$$
\delta\phi=\frac{4M}{b}=\frac{4GM}{c^2b}
$$

**Notes:** Text and Fig. 24.3 use Delta phi inconsistently: in the figure Delta phi is the total angle swept and delta phi the deflection.

**Where:** GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274); GA §24.1 p.258 (pdf 275); GA p.260 (pdf 277)

### Newtonian corpuscular light deflection

*historical-idea · mention* · also: Soldner deflection, Newtonian prediction for light bending

The deflection obtained by treating light as fast particles in Newtonian gravity, 2GM/(c^2 b), half the GR value; for a grazing solar ray it is a bit under 1 arcsec.

**How introduced:** Mentioned in the chapter opening (Soldner, about 0.8 arcsec) as a pre-relativistic foil, and recalled when the GR result is found to be roughly twice as large.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational light deflection

$$
\delta\phi_{\rm Newton}=\frac{2GM}{c^2b}
$$

**Where:** GA p.254 (pdf 271); GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)

The formation of displaced, magnified, multiple or ring-shaped images of a background source because light from it is deflected by an intervening mass.

### gravitational lens focal distance

*phenomenon · developed* · also: no single focus of a point-mass lens, focal line

For parallel rays passing a point mass, a ray at impact parameter b crosses the symmetry axis a distance D = b^2/4M behind the mass, so different annuli focus at different distances and the lens has a focal line rather than a focal point.

**How introduced:** Example 24.6 applies the small-angle relation D = b/theta to theta = 4M/b (Fig. 24.5a) and contrasts the result with an ideal lens.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational light deflection, gravitational lensing

D=\frac{b^2}{4M}

### Einstein ring

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: ring image

The circular image formed when a point source, a spherically symmetric lens and the observer lie on one line, so that rays deflected by the same angle arrive from every azimuth around the lens.

**How introduced:** Named after Example 24.6 and illustrated as a cone of rays traced back from the observer (Fig. 24.5b); its angular size is left to Exercise 24.3.

**Prerequisites:** gravitational lensing, gravitational light deflection

$$
\theta_{\rm E}^2=\frac{4MD_{\ell s}}{D_\ell D_s}

### Einstein angle

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: theta_E, Einstein radius (angular)

The characteristic angular scale of a point-mass lens, theta_E = sqrt(4M D_ls/(D_l D_s)) with D_l, D_s and D_ls the observer-lens, observer-source and lens-source distances; it is the ring radius for perfect alignment.

**How introduced:** Defined inside Exercise 24.3 while deriving the lens equation from the ray diagram of Fig. 24.8.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein ring, gravitational light deflection

$$
\theta_{\rm E}^2=\frac{4MD_{\ell s}}{D_\ell D_s}
$$

**Where:** GA p.261 (pdf 278)

### point-mass lens equation

*law · introduced* · also: thin-lens equation for gravitational lensing

Small-angle relation between the observed image angle theta_i and the true source angle theta_s for a point-mass lens: theta_i = theta_s + theta_E^2/theta_i, which has two image solutions for theta_s nonzero and a ring for theta_s = 0.

**How introduced:** Set as Exercise 24.3: equate transverse distances in the source plane, using the deflection 4M/b with b = theta_i D_l.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein angle, gravitational light deflection

$$
\theta_{\rm i}=\theta_{\rm s}+\frac{\theta_{\rm E}^2}{\theta_{\rm i}}
$$

**Where:** GA p.261 (pdf 278)

### photon four-momentum in Schwarzschild spacetime

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: momentum components of light, p^mu for photons

Components of a photon's momentum in Schwarzschild coordinates, defined as the massless limit of the massive formula at fixed energy at infinity: p^t = epsilon/(1 - 2M/r), p^r = +/- epsilon [1 - (b^2/r^2)(1 - 2M/r)]^{1/2}, p^theta = 0, p^phi = epsilon b/r^2.

**How introduced:** Example 24.7 starts from m dx/dtau, trades dt/dtau for E-tilde, names m E-tilde the energy at infinity, lets m -> 0, and then removes dr/dt and dphi/dt using b and the null condition.

**Prerequisites:** energy at infinity, impact parameter, conserved energy and angular momentum along null geodesics, photon 4-momentum

$$
p^\mu=\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}\varepsilon\frac{dx^\mu}{dt}
$$

**Notes:** The construction ties p to coordinate time t, which the book flags as the price paid.

**Where:** GA §24.2 p.258 (pdf 275); GA §24.2 p.259 (pdf 276)

### energy at infinity

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: epsilon, Killing energy

The conserved quantity epsilon = m E-tilde (for a photon, the limit of this product) equal to the energy a static observer far from the mass would measure; locally measured energies differ from it by redshift factors.

**How introduced:** Named in Example 24.7 so that a finite parameter remains when the rest mass is sent to zero.

\varepsilon=m\tilde E

### static observer orthonormal frame

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: vielbein for a static Schwarzschild observer, local frame of a hovering observer

The orthonormal basis carried by an observer at fixed r, theta, phi, related to coordinate components by (e_t)^t-hat = (1 - 2M/r)^{1/2}, (e_r)^r-hat = (1 - 2M/r)^{-1/2}, (e_theta)^theta-hat = r and (e_phi)^phi-hat = r in the equatorial plane.

**How introduced:** Invoked in Section 24.2 with a margin list of vielbein components to convert photon momentum into what the emitter measures.

**Prerequisites:** vielbein, local orthonormal frame, Schwarzschild metric

(e_t)^{\hat t}=\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{1/2},\ (e_\phi)^{\hat\phi}=r

**Where:** GA §24.2 p.259 (pdf 276)

### locally measured emission angle

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: psi, angle from the radial direction in the static frame

The angle psi between a photon's direction and the outward radial direction as measured in a static observer's orthonormal frame; sin psi = p^phi-hat/p^t-hat = (b/r)(1 - 2M/r)^{1/2}.

**How introduced:** Defined with the construction in Fig. 24.6, whose flat-space geometry r sin psi = b is corrected by the factor (1 - 2M/r)^{1/2} once the vielbeins are applied.

**Prerequisites:** static observer orthonormal frame, photon four-momentum in Schwarzschild spacetime, impact parameter

\sin\psi=\frac{p^{\hat\phi}}{p^{\hat t}}=\frac{b}{r}\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{1/2}

**Where:** GA §24.2 p.259 (pdf 276)

### critical emission angle

*physical-quantity · core* · also: psi_c, escape cone, capture cone

The value of psi for which the emitted ray has b = sqrt(27) M: sin psi_c = (sqrt(27) M/r)(1 - 2M/r)^{1/2}. It separates emission directions that escape from those that are captured; outside 3M the captured cone surrounds the inward direction, inside 3M only a cone around the outward direction escapes, and at r = 2M nothing escapes.

**How introduced:** Obtained by setting b to its critical value in the emission-angle formula; the text notes the capture region widens as the emitter moves inward (Figs. 24.6-24.7).

**Prerequisites:** locally measured emission angle, critical impact parameter, photon capture

$$
\sin\psi_c=\frac{\sqrt{27}\,M}{r}\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{1/2}
$$

**Notes:** Because sin is not one-to-one on [0, pi], psi_c is obtuse for r > 3M and acute for r < 3M; the book does not spell out the branch choice.

**Where:** GA §24.2 p.259 (pdf 276); GA §24.2 p.260 (pdf 277)

### time-reversal symmetry of a static spacetime

*principle · introduced* · also: time-reversal invariance of Schwarzschild

A static metric is unchanged under t -> -t, so every null geodesic run backwards is again a null geodesic; the rules for emitted rays therefore also describe received rays.

**How introduced:** Used in the last paragraph of Section 24.2 as a film-played-backwards argument turning the capture cone for emission into a region of sky that sends no light.

**Prerequisites:** static spacetime, null geodesic

**Where:** GA §24.2 p.260 (pdf 277)

### black-hole shadow

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: dark region of the sky, capture cone seen by an observer

The patch of an observer's sky, bounded by rays with b = sqrt(27) M, from which no light arrives because tracing those directions backwards leads into the hole rather than to a light source; for a distant observer its angular radius is about sqrt(27) M/r.

**How introduced:** Implied rather than named: the book concludes that directions with psi beyond psi_c are black and defers the consequences to the black-hole chapter.

**Prerequisites:** critical emission angle, time-reversal symmetry of a static spacetime, photon sphere

$$
\sin\psi_c\approx\frac{\sqrt{27}\,M}{r}\quad(r\gg M)
$$

**Notes:** Strictly it is black only if nothing inside the photon sphere emits; an extended star larger than 3M would fill those directions with its own light.

**Where:** GA §24.2 p.260 (pdf 277)

### Schwarzschild metric

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: Schwarzschild geometry, Schwarzschild line element

The static, spherically symmetric vacuum solution ds^2 = -(1 - 2M/r)dt^2 + (1 - 2M/r)^{-1}dr^2 + r^2 dOmega^2 in geometrized units.

**How introduced:** Assumed from Chapter 21; its components are inserted in the null condition and orbit equation.

ds^2=-\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)dt^2+\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}dr^2+r^2d\Omega^2

### relativistic effective potential

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: V_eff for massive particles

The potential V_eff = -M/r + L^2/(2r^2) - M L^2/r^3 in the massive-particle energy equation of Chapter 23, which has a stable minimum and an unstable maximum for large enough L-tilde.

**How introduced:** Recalled for contrast: W_eff shares the maximum but not the minimum and has no L dependence.

**Prerequisites:** Schwarzschild metric, conserved energy and angular momentum along null geodesics

V_{\rm eff}=-\frac{M}{r}+\frac{\tilde L^2}{2r^2}-\frac{M\tilde L^2}{r^3}

### radial light rays in Robertson-Walker spacetime

*technique · mention* · also: chi coordinate for radial null rays

For purely radial motion the Robertson-Walker metric reduces to ds^2 = -dt^2 + a(t)^2 dchi^2 with dchi = dr/(1 - kr^2)^{1/2}; chi is cyclic, so a^2 u^chi is constant along the ray, giving u^chi = C/a^2 and an explicit integral for the affine parameter.

**How introduced:** Appears only as Exercise 24.4, which transplants the conserved-quantity method to cosmology and points ahead to Exercise 26.4.

**Prerequisites:** Robertson-Walker metric, affine parameter, conserved energy and angular momentum along null geodesics

$$
ds^2=-dt^2+a(t)^2d\chi^2

$$
u^\chi=\frac{C}{a(t)^2}
$$

**Where:** GA p.261 (pdf 278)

*convention · mention* · also: G = c = 1

Units in which G and c are set to one, so mass is measured as a length M = GM_phys/c^2; constants are restored when numbers are needed.

## Key equations

### (24.1) Straight line in polar form · supporting · GA p.254 (pdf 271)

$$
u=\frac{1}{r}=-\frac{m\cos\theta-\sin\theta}{c}
$$

A Newtonian photon path y = mx + c written in terms of inverse radius, the form against which relativistic rays are compared.

**Symbols:** m slope, c intercept on y axis (not the speed of light), theta polar angle

### (24.2) Coordinate speed of light in a static metric · supporting · GA p.254 (pdf 271)

$$
-c^2g_{00}=g_{ij}\frac{dx^i}{dt}\frac{dx^j}{dt}=c'^2
$$

Setting ds^2 = 0 for a static diagonal metric shows the coordinate speed of light depends on g_00 and hence on position.

**Symbols:** g_00, g_ij metric components; c' coordinate light speed

### Weak-field g_00 (margin note 3, corrected) · supporting · GA p.254 (pdf 271)

$$
-g_{00}\approx1+2\Phi\quad\Rightarrow\quad\sqrt{-g_{00}}\approx1+\Phi
$$

Relates the coordinate light speed to the Newtonian potential. The margin prints sqrt(-g_00) ~ 1 + 2 Phi, which mixes the two forms; the square root carries only one Phi.

**Symbols:** Phi Newtonian potential in units c = 1

### (24.3) Fermat's principle for static spacetimes · supporting · GA p.254 (pdf 271)

$$
\delta\int\left(\frac{g_{ij}\,dx^idx^j}{-g_{00}}\right)^{1/2}=0
$$

The spatial projection of a light ray is a geodesic of the optical metric g_ij/(-g_00), i.e. it makes coordinate travel time stationary.

**Symbols:** delta variation of the path

### (24.4) Null condition · central · GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272)

$$
\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u=g_{\mu\nu}\frac{dx^\mu}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^\nu}{d\lambda}=0
$$

Photon tangent vectors have zero length; replaces u.u = -1 used for massive particles.

**Symbols:** lambda affine parameter; u tangent vector

### (24.5) Schwarzschild null condition in conserved quantities · derivation-step · GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272)

$$
-\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}\tilde E^2+\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{dr}{d\lambda}\right)^2+\frac{\tilde L^2}{r^2}=0
$$

The null condition in the equatorial plane after substituting u^t = E/(1 - 2M/r) and u^phi = L/r^2.

**Symbols:** E-tilde, L-tilde conserved energy and angular momentum (OCR shows bars)

### (24.6) Photon effective-energy equation · central · GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272)

$$
\frac{1}{b^2}=\frac{1}{\tilde L^2}\left(\frac{dr}{d\lambda}\right)^2+W_{\rm eff}(r)
$$

Radial motion of light is a particle of energy 1/b^2 moving in the potential W_eff.

**Symbols:** b impact parameter; W_eff photon effective potential

### Definition of b · central · GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272)

$$
b\equiv\mathcal E^{-1/2}=\frac{\tilde L}{\tilde E}
$$

The single constant that fixes a photon trajectory; the effective energy is 1/b^2.

**Symbols:** script E effective energy

### (24.7) Photon effective potential · central · GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272)

$$
W_{\rm eff}(r)=\frac{1}{r^2}\left(1-\frac{2M}{r}\right)
$$

Potential hill seen by every photon; it has no angular-momentum dependence.

**Symbols:** M mass in geometrized units

### (24.8) b far from the mass · derivation-step · GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272)

$$
b=\left|\frac{\tilde L}{\tilde E}\right|=\frac{r^2\,d\phi/d\lambda}{dt/d\lambda}=r^2\frac{d\phi}{dt}
$$

For r much larger than 2M the redshift factor is 1, so b reduces to r^2 dphi/dt.

### (24.9) Angular rate of a distant straight ray · derivation-step · GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272)

$$
\frac{d\phi}{dt}=\frac{d\phi}{dr}\frac{dr}{dt}=\frac{d}{r^2}
$$

Using phi ~ d/r and dr/dt ~ -1 for an incoming ray at offset d; combined with 24.8 gives b = d.

**Symbols:** d perpendicular offset of the ray

### Impact parameter identification · central · GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272)

$$
b=d
$$

The ratio L/E is the geometric impact parameter of a ray from infinity.

### Peak of the photon potential · central · GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273)

$$
\frac{dW_{\rm eff}}{dr}=0\ \Rightarrow\ r_0=3M,\qquad W_{\rm eff}(r_0)=\frac{1}{27M^2}

Location and height of the barrier; also the radius of the unstable photon sphere.

**Symbols:** r_0 radius of the maximum (not the r_0 of Chapter 23)

### Scattering versus capture criterion · central · GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273)

$$
\frac{1}{b^2}<\frac{1}{27M^2}\ \text{(deflected)},\qquad\frac{1}{b^2}>\frac{1}{27M^2}\ \text{(captured)},\qquad b^2=27M^2\ \text{(circular, unstable)}

Classification of incoming rays by comparing their energy level with the barrier height.

### (24.17) Zeroth-order straight line · derivation-step · GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)

u_0=\frac{M}{b}\sin\phi
$$

Undeflected ray at distance b from the mass, valid when M/b is small.

### (24.18) · derivation-step · GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)

$$
u_1''+u_1\approx3u_0^2=3\left(\frac{M}{b}\right)^2\sin^2\phi
$$

Linearized equation for the first correction, sourced by the zeroth-order solution.

### (24.19) · derivation-step · GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)

$$
u_1''+u_1\approx\frac32\left(\frac{M}{b}\right)^2(1-\cos2\phi)

Same source written as a constant plus a cos 2phi term.

### (24.20) · derivation-step · GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)

$$
u_1=\frac12\left(\frac{M}{b}\right)^2(3+\cos2\phi)
$$

Particular solution: a constant 3/2 (M/b)^2 plus (1/2)(M/b)^2 cos 2phi.

### (24.21) Perturbed light-ray trajectory · central · GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)

$$
u\approx\frac{M}{b}\sin\phi+\frac12\left(\frac{M}{b}\right)^2(3+\cos2\phi)
$$

Trajectory accurate to second order in M/b.

### (24.22) Asymptote condition (corrected) · derivation-step · GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)

$$
2\sin\phi\approx-\frac{M}{b}\left(3+\cos2\phi\right)
$$

Condition u = 0 for the directions of the incoming and outgoing asymptotes. The printed equation has (3 + 2 cos phi); dividing eqn 24.21 by M/b gives (3 + cos 2phi), which is what yields the quoted roots.

### Deflection angle · central · GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)

$$
\phi\approx-\frac{2M}{b},\quad\phi\approx\pi+\frac{2M}{b}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\delta\phi=\frac{4M}{b}
$$

Each asymptote is rotated by 2M/b beyond the straight-line value, giving a total deflection 4M/b.

**Symbols:** The book writes Delta phi for the deflection here.

### (24.23) Solar light deflection · central · GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)

$$
\Delta\phi=\frac{4GM}{c^2b}\approx8.6\times10^{-6}\ \text{rad}\ (\approx1.8'')
$$

Deflection of a ray grazing the Sun, with b = 6.9e8 m; twice the Newtonian corpuscular value.

**Symbols:** G gravitational constant, c speed of light, M solar mass

### (24.24) Focal distance of a point-mass lens · central · GA §24.1 p.258 (pdf 275)

$$
D=\frac{b^2}{4M}
$$

Distance behind the mass at which a parallel ray with impact parameter b meets the axis; depends on b, so no single focus.

**Symbols:** D distance from lens to crossing point; theta = 4M/b

Starting point recalled for Section 24.2.

Momentum in terms of E-tilde.

Impact parameter expressed with coordinate time, valid at any r.

Sine of the angle from the radial direction equals the sideways velocity in the static observer's orthonormal frame.

**Symbols:** hats denote orthonormal-frame components

## Worked examples

### Example 24.1 · intro · GA p.254 (pdf 271)

**Problem:** Give a qualitative reason why gravity should affect the path of light at all.

**Method:** Apply the null condition to a static diagonal metric to find the coordinate speed of light, relate g_00 to the potential, and state the Fermat-type variational principle with g_ij/(-g_00).

**Key insight:** Since coordinate light speed varies from place to place, space acts like an inhomogeneous optical medium, so a spacetime null geodesic can project onto a bent spatial path.

**Result:** c'^2 = -c^2 g_00 (for the spatial metric along the path) and delta integral of sqrt(g_ij dx^i dx^j/(-g_00)) = 0.

**Concepts:** coordinate speed of light, Fermat's principle in a static spacetime, gravitational refractive index

### Example 24.2 · intro · GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272)

**Problem:** Interpret the constant b = L-tilde/E-tilde geometrically.

**Method:** Take a ray travelling parallel to the x-axis at offset d; far away the redshift factor is 1 so b = r^2 dphi/dt; use phi ~ d/r and dr/dt ~ -1.

**Key insight:** The ratio of the two conserved quantities is the impact parameter, so the energy-like quantity 1/b^2 is fixed by geometry alone.

**Result:** b = d.

**Concepts:** impact parameter, conserved energy and angular momentum along null geodesics

### Reading the photon potential (embedded in 24.1) · intro · GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273)

**Problem:** Use W_eff to classify the possible fates of light rays and find where light can orbit.

**Method:** Differentiate W_eff to find its peak; compare the energy level 1/b^2 with the peak height; note the lack of a minimum.

**Key insight:** A single barrier with no well means scattering or capture but never stable light orbits; the balance point at 3M is unstable.

**Result:** r0 = 3M, W_eff(r0) = 1/(27M^2); b > sqrt(27) M deflected, b < sqrt(27) M captured, b = sqrt(27) M unstable circular orbit.

**Concepts:** photon effective potential, photon sphere, critical impact parameter, photon capture

### Example 24.3 · standard · GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273)

**Problem:** Derive the shape equation for equatorial light rays in Schwarzschild spacetime.

**Method:** Write dr/dphi = u^r/u^phi, divide the null condition by (u^phi)^2, rewrite the time term with lowered components to insert 1/b^2, substitute u = M/r, simplify, and differentiate with respect to phi.

**Key insight:** The photon orbit equation differs from the Newtonian straight-line equation only by the source term 3u^2.

**Result:** (du/dphi)^2 = M^2/b^2 - u^2 + 2u^3 and u'' + u = 3u^2.

**Concepts:** photon orbit equation, Binet substitution, null geodesic

### Example 24.4 · standard · GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)

**Problem:** Find the path of a ray that passes far from the mass (M/b small) and its total deflection.

**Method:** Expand about u0 = (M/b) sin phi, solve u1'' + u1 = 3u0^2 with a constant plus cos 2phi, then set u = 0 and solve for phi near 0 and pi to first order; the text following the box extracts the angle.

**Key insight:** Each end of the trajectory is rotated by 2M/b beyond the straight-line asymptote, so the deflection is 4M/b and independent of photon energy.

**Result:** u ~ (M/b) sin phi + (1/2)(M/b)^2 (3 + cos 2phi); asymptotes at phi ~ -2M/b and pi + 2M/b; deflection 4M/b.

**Concepts:** perturbation about the straight-line solution, gravitational light deflection, photon orbit equation

### Example 24.5 · intro · GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)

**Problem:** Evaluate the deflection of starlight that just grazes the Sun and connect it with observations.

**Method:** Restore G and c in 4M/b, insert the solar mass and b = 6.9e8 m, convert radians to arcseconds, and compare with Soldner's Newtonian estimate.

**Key insight:** The GR prediction is about twice the Newtonian corpuscular value, which is what made an eclipse measurement decisive in principle.

**Result:** About 8.6e-6 rad, roughly 1.8 arcsec; tested by the 1919 eclipse and confirmed precisely by radio sources.

**Concepts:** gravitational light deflection, Newtonian corpuscular light deflection, 1919 eclipse expedition, radio interferometric light-deflection measurements

### Example 24.6 · intro · GA §24.1 p.258 (pdf 275)

**Problem:** Decide whether a point mass focuses parallel light like a lens.

**Method:** For a ray at impact parameter b, use the small-angle crossing distance D = b/theta with theta = 4M/b.

**Key insight:** The crossing distance grows as b^2, so there is no common focus; the analogy with a lens breaks down.

**Result:** D = b^2/4M.

**Concepts:** gravitational lens focal distance, gravitational lensing

### Example 24.7 · standard · GA §24.2 p.258 (pdf 275)

**Problem:** Construct the components of a photon's four-momentum in Schwarzschild coordinates.

**Method:** Write massive p^mu in terms of coordinate velocity and u^t, replace u^t with E-tilde, define epsilon = m E-tilde, take m -> 0; then express dphi/dt with b and solve the null condition divided by dt^2 for dr/dt.

**Key insight:** Holding the energy at infinity fixed gives a finite massless limit, and the whole momentum is fixed by epsilon and b.

**Result:** p^t = epsilon/(1 - 2M/r), p^r = +/- epsilon [1 - (b^2/r^2)(1 - 2M/r)]^{1/2}, p^theta = 0, p^phi = epsilon b/r^2.

**Concepts:** photon four-momentum in Schwarzschild spacetime, energy at infinity, impact parameter

### Critical emission angle (embedded in 24.2) · standard · GA §24.2 p.259 (pdf 276)

**Problem:** Find the range of directions in which a static emitter at radius r can send light that escapes.

**Method:** Define sin psi as the ratio of orthonormal phi and t momentum components, convert with static vielbeins, insert eqn 24.32, then set b = sqrt(27) M.

**Key insight:** The locally measured angle is tied to the conserved b by sin psi = (b/r)(1 - 2M/r)^{1/2}, so the capture threshold in b becomes a cone of directions that widens as r decreases.

**Result:** sin psi_c = (sqrt(27) M/r)(1 - 2M/r)^{1/2}; psi_c = 90 degrees at r = 3M and all light captured as r -> 2M.

**Concepts:** critical emission angle, locally measured emission angle, static observer orthonormal frame, critical impact parameter


## Analogies and intuitions

### A gravitational field as a medium with a position-dependent refractive index, with rays obeying a least-time principle → Fermat's principle in a static spacetime · useful · GA p.254 (pdf 271)

Example 24.1 notes the coordinate light speed depends on g_00, likens this to refraction, and states a Fermat principle so that bending seems natural before any geodesic is solved.

**Where it breaks down:** Only works for static spacetimes and coordinate speeds; the local speed is always c. Using g_00 alone gives half the true deflection; the spatial metric must be included in the index.

**App idea:** Graded-index optics sandbox: a ray in a medium with n(r) = 1/sqrt(-g_00) versus n(r) from the full isotropic metric, overlaid on the true null geodesic, showing the factor of two.

### A photon as a particle with energy 1/b^2 rolling toward a potential hill W_eff → photon effective-energy equation · strong · GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272)

The chapter maps the null condition onto the familiar energy-plus-potential picture so turning points, capture and unstable circular orbits are read off a plot.

**Where it breaks down:** The 'energy' is geometric (an inverse impact parameter squared), the 'time' is the affine parameter, and nothing about the photon's actual energy enters.

**App idea:** Ball-on-hill animation synchronized with the actual ray around the mass.

### Light orbiting on the photon sphere is like a ball balanced on a hilltop → photon sphere · useful · GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273)

Because circular photon orbits sit at the maximum of W_eff, the text concludes they are unstable, just as for the massive-particle maximum.

**Where it breaks down:** Real near-critical rays wind around several times before leaving; the instability grows exponentially in the number of orbits, which the ball picture does not quantify.

**App idea:** Nudge a photon off the 3M circle by a tiny amount and count laps before escape or capture.

### Impact parameter as angular momentum per unit energy, with more energetic projectiles scattered less → impact parameter · weak · GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273)

Used right after Example 24.2 to make b = L/E feel physically sensible.

**Where it breaks down:** For photons L scales with energy, so b is purely geometric and deflection does not depend on photon energy; the scattering-by-energy intuition belongs to massive particles.

### A gravitating mass as a lens → gravitational lensing · useful · GA §24.1 p.258 (pdf 275)

Introduced and immediately tested: the crossing distance D = b^2/4M shows there is no focus, so the analogy is only partial; the aligned case gives a ring.

**Where it breaks down:** No single focal length; image positions obey the lens equation rather than the thin-lens formula; magnification and caustics differ from glass optics.

**App idea:** Wine-glass-foot lens comparison: photograph-like rendering of a background grid seen through a point-mass lens versus a convex lens.

### Rubber-sheet picture of starlight bending around the Sun (Fig. 24.4) → gravitational light deflection · weak · GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)

Offered as a familiar way the effect is illustrated, after the eclipse discussion.

**Where it breaks down:** Shows only curved space; half the deflection comes from the time part of the metric, and a sheet embedded in a gravitating room smuggles in the gravity it claims to explain.

**App idea:** Toggle between rubber-sheet cartoon and a decomposition bar showing space and time contributions of 2M/b each.

### Running the film backwards: rays that an emitter would lose into the hole correspond to directions from which an observer receives nothing → time-reversal symmetry of a static spacetime · strong · GA §24.2 p.260 (pdf 277)

Closes Section 24.2 by turning the capture cone for emitted photons into a black region in the observer's sky.

**Where it breaks down:** Assumes a static geometry and that nothing inside the photon sphere emits light; for collapsing stars or rotating holes the argument must be refined.

**App idea:** Toggle between emission mode (arrows outward from the observer) and reception mode (arrows inward) with the same cone shaded black.


## Misconceptions addressed

### Light is massless, so gravity cannot bend it. · GA p.254 (pdf 271)

**Correction:** In GR light follows null geodesics of curved spacetime and is deflected by 4M/b; even Newtonian corpuscular reasoning gives a nonzero (half-size) effect.

**Why tempting:** Newtonian force is proportional to mass, and in the book's own Newtonian orbit chapter photons move in straight lines.

### Because a light ray follows a geodesic, its path through space must be straight. · GA p.254 (pdf 271)

**Correction:** A geodesic of spacetime projected into space need not be a spatial geodesic; in a static field the spatial path follows the optical metric g_ij/(-g_00).

**Why tempting:** Geodesics are introduced as the curved-space version of straight lines.

### The conserved energy and angular momentum defined with proper time do not apply to photons. · GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272)

**Correction:** They follow from the metric's symmetries and hold along any geodesic with an affine parameter, null ones included.

**Why tempting:** Earlier definitions used d/dtau, which is undefined for light.

### Light can orbit a compact mass stably, like a planet. · GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273)

**Correction:** W_eff has no minimum; the only circular light orbits are at the peak r = 3M and are unstable.

**Why tempting:** Massive particles have stable circular orbits, and the photon sphere is often drawn as a neat ring of light.

### Rays with different angular momenta see different effective potentials, as massive particles do. · GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273)

**Correction:** After scaling by L^2, W_eff is the same function for every photon; only the level 1/b^2 changes.

**Why tempting:** V_eff for massive particles changes shape with L-tilde.

### A gravitational lens brings light to a focus just like a glass lens. · GA §24.1 p.258 (pdf 275)

**Correction:** The crossing distance D = b^2/4M depends on impact parameter, so there is no single focus; aligned systems produce rings and off-axis ones produce multiple images.

**Why tempting:** The name 'gravitational lens' and pictures of converging rays.

### GR merely reproduces the Newtonian prediction for light bending. · GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)

**Correction:** GR predicts about twice Soldner's corpuscular value (1.75 versus about 0.87 arcsec at the solar limb), which is what the eclipse test discriminated.

**Why tempting:** In the perihelion and redshift tests the Newtonian limit is the leading behaviour, and for slow particles GR and Newton agree.

### Eddington's 1919 eclipse data settled the question cleanly and precisely. · GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)

**Correction:** The measurement agreed with GR within its estimated errors, but later analyses questioned its systematics; precise confirmation came from later radio measurements.

**Why tempting:** The expedition is told as a triumphant turning point that made Einstein famous.

### Higher-energy (bluer) photons are deflected less because they scatter less. · GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273)

**Correction:** The trajectory depends only on b = L/E; the deflection 4M/b is the same for all frequencies, as the equivalence principle requires.

**Why tempting:** The text's remark that b decreases with energy borrows an intuition from massive-particle scattering.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The rubber-sheet picture of curved space accounts for the full bending of starlight. · GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)

**Correction:** At first order the time part of the metric (g_tt) and spatial curvature (g_rr) each contribute 2M/b; a picture of curved space alone captures only half.

**Why tempting:** Fig. 24.4 is captioned as curved space bending starlight, and rubber sheets are ubiquitous in popular accounts.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The speed of light really changes near a mass. · GA p.254 (pdf 271)

**Correction:** Only the coordinate speed varies; every local observer using their own rods and clocks measures c.

**Why tempting:** Example 24.1 speaks of a varying speed of light and a refractive index.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Any photon emitted with b below sqrt(27) M falls into the mass, whatever its direction. · GA §24.2 p.259 (pdf 276)

**Correction:** Outside r = 3M an outward-directed photon escapes for any b; capture requires b < sqrt(27) M and an inward heading, which is why the critical angle is obtuse there. Inside 3M outward rays with large b turn back.

**Why tempting:** The text states the capture criterion 1/b^2 > W(r0) without mentioning the direction, and sin psi does not distinguish psi from pi - psi.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Stars next to the eclipsed Sun · GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)

**Setup:** Photograph a star field during a total solar eclipse and again at night months later, and compare positions of stars whose light passed near the solar limb.

**Lesson:** Light passing the Sun is deflected, so stars appear pushed radially outward by up to about 1.75 arcsec, twice the Newtonian corpuscular value.

**App idea:** Blink comparator between eclipse and night images with exaggerated displacement vectors scaling as 1/b.

### Parallel beam past a point mass · GA §24.1 p.258 (pdf 275)

**Setup:** Send a wide parallel beam of light past a single compact mass and look for where the rays cross the axis.

**Lesson:** Each ring of rays crosses at its own distance b^2/4M, so the mass is a lens without a focus.

**App idea:** Slide a screen along the axis behind the mass and watch the illuminated ring shrink and grow.

### Perfect alignment of source, lens and observer · GA §24.1 p.258 (pdf 275)

**Setup:** Place a distant source exactly behind a compact mass as seen by the observer.

**Lesson:** Symmetry turns the two images into a ring whose angular radius is the Einstein angle.

**App idea:** Drag the source through alignment and see arcs merge into a ring.

### A lamp hovering at radius r · GA §24.2 p.259 (pdf 276)

**Setup:** A static observer at fixed r emits photons uniformly in all directions and records which ones reach infinity; repeat for smaller r.

**Lesson:** Beyond a critical angle photons are captured; the captured fraction grows from a few percent far away to half the sky at r = 3M and all of it as r -> 2M.

**App idea:** Lighthouse simulation with shaded escape cone and captured-fraction readout.

### Playing the lamp film backwards · GA §24.2 p.260 (pdf 277)

**Setup:** Reverse the emitter experiment in time: the static observer now receives light from all directions.

**Lesson:** Directions inside the capture cone trace back into the hole, so no light arrives from them and that region of sky is black.

**App idea:** Ray-traced sky view for a hovering observer against a star background, with the dark disk growing as the observer descends.


## Applications and observations

- **Deflection of starlight grazing the Sun** (numerical-estimate, GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)): 4GM/(c^2 b) evaluated at the solar limb; the book uses b = 6.9e8 m and quotes the solar radius as about 696,000 km. Key numbers: Book: 8.6e-6 rad, about 1.8 arcsec. Our check: GM_sun/c^2 = 1477 m, R_sun = 6.957e8 m gives 8.49e-6 rad = 1.75 arcsec.
- **Soldner's Newtonian deflection** (numerical-estimate, GA p.254 (pdf 271)): Corpuscular Newtonian light bending, half the GR value. Key numbers: Book: around 0.8 arcsec; exact half of the GR limb value is 0.875 arcsec (our figure).
- **1919 solar eclipse expedition** (observation, GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)): Star positions near the eclipsed Sun compared with night-time positions; agreed with GR within estimated uncertainties, systematics later questioned. Key numbers: Not given in the book. Commonly quoted results (external): about 1.98 arcsec from Sobral and 1.61 arcsec from Principe, each with uncertainties of order 0.1-0.3 arcsec.
- **Radio-source light-deflection measurements** (observation, GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274)): Very-long-baseline radio interferometry of quasars passing near the Sun confirms the GR deflection with high precision. Key numbers: Not given in the book. External context: VLBI analyses constrain the PPN parameter gamma to about 1 part in 10^4 of the GR value.
- **Photon sphere and critical impact parameter** (numerical-estimate, GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273)): Circular light orbits at 3M require b = sqrt(27) M. Our estimate: for the Sun 3M is 4.4 km and for a 1.4 solar-mass neutron star 6.2 km, both inside the bodies, so photon spheres need objects more compact than about 3M, in practice black holes. Key numbers: r = 3M; b_c = sqrt(27) M = 5.196 M; W_eff peak 1/(27 M^2). Our figures: 3M_sun = 4.4 km; 3(1.4 M_sun) = 6.2 km.
- **Focal distance of the Sun as a gravitational lens** (numerical-estimate, GA §24.1 p.258 (pdf 275)): Our estimate using D = b^2/4M with b at least the solar radius: rays grazing the limb meet the axis around 550 AU from the Sun, and rays with larger b meet farther out, forming a focal line; this is the basis of proposed solar-gravitational-lens telescope missions. Key numbers: Our computation: R_sun^2/(4 GM_sun/c^2) = 8.2e13 m, about 548 AU.
- **Einstein rings and microlensing** (numerical-estimate, GA p.261 (pdf 278)): Einstein angle from Exercise 24.3. Our estimate for a solar-mass star 4 kpc away lensing a source at 8 kpc gives a ring of angular radius about a milliarcsecond, unresolvable, so such events are seen as brightening (microlensing); galaxy-mass lenses give arcsecond rings seen in telescope images. Key numbers: Our computation: theta_E = sqrt(4GM D_ls/(c^2 D_l D_s)) = 1.0 mas (ring radius; diameter about 2 mas) for M = M_sun, D_l = 4 kpc, D_s = 8 kpc.
- **Captured fraction of light emitted by a static observer** (numerical-estimate, GA §24.2 p.260 (pdf 277)): Our evaluation of sin psi_c = (sqrt(27) M/r)(1 - 2M/r)^{1/2}, choosing the obtuse branch outside 3M, with captured solid-angle fraction (1 + cos psi_c)/2. Key numbers: Our computation: r = 10M psi_c = 152 deg, 5.7 percent captured; 6M 135 deg, 14.6 percent; 4M 113 deg, 30 percent; 3M 90 deg, 50 percent; 2.5M 68 deg, 68 percent; 2.2M 45 deg, 85 percent; 2M all captured.
- **Black-hole shadow size (Event Horizon Telescope)** (detection-event, GA §24.2 p.260 (pdf 277)): For a distant observer the dark region has angular radius about sqrt(27) M/D, set by the critical impact parameter rather than by 2M. Our estimates for the EHT targets; the book shows the Sgr A* image in the next chapter (Fig. 25.1). Key numbers: Our computation: shadow diameter 2 sqrt(27) GM/(c^2 D) is about 40 microarcsec for M87* (6.5e9 M_sun, 16.8 Mpc) and about 50 microarcsec for Sgr A* (4.0e6 M_sun, 8.28 kpc); EHT ring diameters were reported as about 42 and 52 microarcsec (external).

## Historical notes

- **Johann Georg von Soldner:** Treated light as Newtonian corpuscles falling in the Sun's field and estimated an apparent displacement of roughly 0.8 arcsec for stars seen next to the solar limb (his paper dates from the early 1800s; the book gives only his life dates, 1776-1833). — Shows light bending was anticipated classically, and supplies the Newtonian value that GR doubles. (GA p.254 (pdf 271))
- **Henry Cavendish, John Michell, Isaac Newton:** Earlier, largely neglected speculations about gravity acting on light; Michell is also credited with first proposing black holes, explaining double stars gravitationally and bringing statistics into cosmology. — Places the GR prediction in a longer tradition and foreshadows the dark stars of the black-hole chapters. (GA p.254 (pdf 271))
- **Arthur Eddington, Albert Einstein (1919):** Eclipse expedition measured shifts of star positions near the Sun, finding agreement with the GR prediction within the estimated uncertainties. — Made Einstein a public celebrity; the book notes that later analyses questioned the systematic errors and points to Earman and Glymour (1980). (GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274))
- **History:** Later high-precision measurements of the deflection of radio sources near the Sun. — Provide the robust confirmation of the GR value that the eclipse plates could not. (GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274))

## Notation and conventions

- **Units:** Geometrized G = c = 1 with M standing for GM/c^2; constants restored in Example 24.5 (4GM/c^2 b). Example 24.1 keeps an explicit c. (GA p.254 (pdf 271))
- **Metric signature:** (-, +, +, +); photons satisfy u.u = 0 and massive particles u.u = -1. (GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272))
- **Conserved quantities:** E-tilde = (1 - 2M/r) dt/dlambda and L-tilde = r^2 dphi/dlambda for photons (dtau for massive particles, per unit rest mass). — The OCR copy renders the tildes as bars in several places. (GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272))
- **b, script E, script T:** b = L-tilde/E-tilde; the effective energy is script E = 1/b^2; script T = (dr/dlambda)^2/L-tilde^2 is the kinetic-like term. (GA §24.1 p.255 (pdf 272))
- **W_eff versus V_eff:** W_eff(r) = (1 - 2M/r)/r^2 is the photon potential; V_eff is reserved for massive particles. The text sometimes drops the subscript and writes W(r0). Plots label the axes W_eff(r-vector) and |r-vector|. (GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273))
- **r0:** Here r0 = 3M is the location of the maximum of W_eff. — In Chapter 23 r0 denoted a circular-orbit or orbital radius; do not carry that meaning over. (GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273))
- **u:** Bold u is the tangent (four-velocity) vector; plain u = M/r (dimensionless) in the orbit equation, and u = 1/r in margin note 1; primes mean d/dphi; u0 and u1 are zeroth- and first-order parts of u. — Triple use of the same letter in two pages. (GA §24.1 p.256 (pdf 273))
- **Deflection angle symbols:** Fig. 24.3 labels the total swept angle Delta phi and the deflection delta phi; the text and eqn 24.23 call the deflection Delta phi. — Treat 4M/b as the deflection whichever symbol is used. (GA §24.1 p.257 (pdf 274))
- **Energy at infinity:** Script epsilon = m E-tilde, the energy measured far away; kept finite as m -> 0 for photons. — Distinct from script E (effective energy) and from the eccentricity epsilon of Chapter 23. (GA §24.2 p.258 (pdf 275))
- **Hatted indices and vielbeins:** Hats mark components in an orthonormal (static observer) frame; (e_mu)^nu-hat are vielbein components converting coordinate components to hatted ones. (GA §24.2 p.259 (pdf 276))
- **Emission angle psi:** Measured in the emitter's local frame from the outward radial direction (the z-axis of Fig. 24.6); psi_c is the critical value. (GA §24.2 p.259 (pdf 276))
- **Lensing symbols:** s source, ell lens, O observer; D_l observer-lens, D_s observer-source, D_ls lens-source distances; theta_i image angle, theta_s source angle, theta_E Einstein angle, alpha deflection; in Example 24.6 theta is the deflection and D the axis-crossing distance. (GA p.261 (pdf 278))

## Teaching gems

## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Printed eqn 24.22 has (3 + 2 cos phi); setting eqn 24.21 to zero gives (3 + cos 2phi).** — With the printed form the roots come out as -5M/(2b), contradicting the stated -2M/b and the 4M/b result. *Suggestion:* Use 2 sin phi = -(M/b)(3 + cos 2phi); near phi = 0 and pi the bracket is 4, giving shifts of 2M/b.
- **Margin note 3 writes sqrt(-g_00) ~ 1 + 2 Phi; the weak-field result is -g_00 ~ 1 + 2 Phi, so the square root is about 1 + Phi.** — Factor-of-two confusion about how strongly clocks and coordinate light speed depend on the potential. *Suggestion:* State g_00 ~ -(1 + 2 Phi) and derive sqrt(-g_00) ~ 1 + Phi explicitly.
- **The text calls sqrt(-g_00) a refractive index; since n = c/c', with flat spatial metric the index is 1/sqrt(-g_00), and g_00 alone yields only half the deflection.** — Learners may build an optics model that bends light the wrong way or gives Soldner's value. *Suggestion:* Use the isotropic weak-field index n ~ 1 - 2 Phi, which includes the spatial metric and reproduces 4M/b.
- **Summary table entry for massive dr/dtau has L-tilde/r^2 instead of L-tilde^2/r^2.** — Copying the table into a numerical integrator gives wrong orbits. *Suggestion:* Use (1 - 2M/r)(1 + L^2/r^2), consistent with the dr/dt row just below.
- **Delta phi means the total swept angle in Fig. 24.3 but the deflection in the text and eqn 24.23.** — Readers comparing figure and text may think the deflection is pi + 4M/b or vice versa. *Suggestion:* Reserve delta for the deflection 4M/b and Delta for the swept angle pi + 4M/b.
- **The critical-angle discussion does not mention that sin psi = s has two solutions; for r > 3M the relevant psi_c is obtuse and the captured cone surrounds the inward direction, for r < 3M it is acute. Capture also depends on whether the ray is initially inward or outward.** — Learners taking arcsin naively conclude that most outward photons fall in, or cannot reconcile 'critical angle becomes smaller' with the formula. *Suggestion:* Tabulate psi_c against r (152 deg at 10M, 135 deg at 6M, 90 deg at 3M, 45 deg near 2.2M) and draw the escape cone explicitly.
- **The remark that b decreases with increasing energy because energetic particles scatter less imports massive-particle intuition; for light b is purely geometric.** — Invites the false belief that deflection is frequency dependent. *Suggestion:* Emphasize that L and E scale together for a photon and that 4M/b is achromatic, a consequence of the equivalence principle.
- **The text describes sin psi as a sideways part of the photon's coordinate velocity, yet eqn 24.33 is built from orthonormal-frame (locally measured) components.** — Blurs the coordinate versus measured distinction that the section depends on. *Suggestion:* Say 'sideways velocity measured by the static observer'.
- **Fig. 24.4's rubber sheet and caption attribute the bending to curved space; the split into time and space contributions is not discussed in this chapter.** — Reinforces a popular picture that yields only half the effect. *Suggestion:* Show that keeping only g_tt gives 2M/b and adding g_rr gives the other 2M/b; link to Chapter 23's note that rubber sheets omit time curvature.
- **Cross-chapter inconsistency: Chapter 6 says its equivalence-principle estimate is a third of the true bending, while this chapter says GR is about twice the Newtonian value; the standard decomposition is half from g_tt and half from spatial curvature.** — Readers who remember Chapter 6 may be confused about which factor is right. *Suggestion:* Tutor should state the factor-of-two relation clearly and treat the Chapter 6 heuristic as order-of-magnitude only.
- **The capture discussion speaks of photons being destroyed at r = 0 and of the critical angle vanishing at r = 2M, without noting that no static observer can exist at or inside 2M and that the horizon is the real point of no return.** — Misleading picture of what happens at 2M and of what 'destroyed' means. *Suggestion:* Restrict the emitter formula to r > 2M and preview Chapter 25's horizon discussion.
- **Perturbative solution silently drops the homogeneous solution and the approximation 3u^2 ~ 3u0^2 is described only briefly; accuracy limits (M/b small) are not quantified.** — Unclear why the answer is unique and when 4M/b fails (strong deflection near b = sqrt(27) M, where deflection diverges). *Suggestion:* Show that a homogeneous term just redefines b and orientation, and plot exact versus first-order deflection as b approaches the critical value.
- **Gravitational lensing, magnification, multiple images and the Einstein angle are relegated to one example and one exercise; the book (published before recent imaging) gives no observational lensing numbers.** — The connection to one of the most used GR tools in astronomy is thin. *Suggestion:* Add worked numbers for microlensing (milliarcsecond rings) and galaxy lenses (arcsecond rings), and a simulated sky.
- **Fig. 24.7 labels the dotted level touching the peak as 1/b^2 while drawing the capture arrow above it.** — Ambiguity whether the arrow's energy or the dotted line is the photon's 1/b^2. *Suggestion:* Label the peak height 1/(27M^2) and put 1/b^2 on the arrow.

## Tutor notes

- Open with a prediction question: 'Light has no mass. Should the Sun bend it at all, and if so would you expect GR to give more or less than Newton?' Record the guess and return to it after Example 24.5.
- Before new algebra, ask the learner to recall the massive-particle energy equation and V_eff from Chapter 23; then have them predict what changes when u.u = -1 becomes 0.
- Order of explanation: null condition -> effective energy equation -> read W_eff plot (peak, no well) -> impact parameter meaning -> orbit equation -> perturbative deflection -> numbers -> lensing -> what an observer sees.
- Stress early that only b matters for a photon's path, so deflection is independent of colour; use this to defuse the text's energy-scattering remark.
- Check for understanding: 'Why can't light orbit stably around a black hole?' (no minimum of W_eff) and 'What happens to a ray with b slightly less than sqrt(27) M?' (winds near 3M, then plunges).
- When deriving the deflection, have the learner find where u = 0 on the straight line first (phi = 0 and pi), then ask how a small positive correction shifts those zeros; point out the typo in printed eqn 24.22.
- Give concrete scales: M_sun is about 1.5 km, the solar limb is 7e5 km, so M/b ~ 2e-6 and the deflection is 8.5e-6 rad, about 1.75 arcsec; Newton gives half.
- If the learner likes optics, use the refractive-index picture but correct it: the index is about 1 - 2 Phi including space curvature; g_tt alone gives the Newtonian half.
- Common question: 'Does light slow down near the Sun?' Answer: coordinate speed changes (this is also the Shapiro delay of Chapter 8), local speed is always c.
- For Section 24.2, make the learner say out loud which components are coordinate and which are measured; then sanity-check sin psi = b/r far from the mass.
- Walk through the critical angle at a few radii (10M, 6M, 3M, 2.5M) and explicitly handle the obtuse versus acute branch; the half-sky result at 3M is memorable.
- Close by reversing time to predict the dark region on the sky, and ask how large it looks from far away (about sqrt(27) M/D, bigger than the 2M horizon radius); bridge to the Event Horizon Telescope image in Chapter 25.
- Common question: 'Is the Einstein ring real?' Yes; galaxy lenses give arcsecond rings, stars in our Galaxy give milliarcsecond rings detected as brightenings.
- For advanced learners, suggest numerically integrating u'' + u = 3u^2 to see the deflection grow without bound as b approaches sqrt(27) M.
