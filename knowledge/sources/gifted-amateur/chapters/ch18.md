# GA ch18 · Universes of the past and future

> A guided tour of a numbered zoo of Friedmann model universes (Universes 2 to 11): flat radiation, dust and dust-plus-Lambda models, closed and open dust models, and the k = 1 family built around Einstein's static universe (its instability, Lemaitre's coasting model, the Eddington-Lemaitre and bouncing models), closed by the historical route from Einstein's 1917 static model through Hubble, the time-scale problem, the Lambda = 0 'standard models' and the CMB.

**Pages:** printed 188–200 · pdf 205–217 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

Mostly separable ODEs, standard integrals and one linearization, well within undergraduate calculus; the catalogue can feel like a parade of cases, several solutions are quoted rather than derived (Universes 4, 5, 7, the sinh solution), and the effective-potential sign convention, the misnamed a_min and a wrong prefactor in eqn 18.35 can trip careful readers. The history is accessible but dated (Lambda = 0 'standard models', 'missing mass'). The de Sitter hyperboloid exercises are markedly harder than the chapter and contain printed slips.

## Role in the book

Payoff chapter of Part III. Chapter 15 introduced the cosmological principle and Universes 0 and 1, Chapter 16 built the three Robertson-Walker geometries, and Chapter 17 derived the Friedmann equations and the a^-3 and a^-4 dilution laws. Here those tools are exercised: matter content (radiation, dust, vacuum energy of either sign) and spatial curvature k are varied one at a time and the resulting a(t) is solved exactly or qualitatively, producing a catalogue summarized in a single grid figure. The chapter deliberately stays with Big-Bang singularities, horizons and de Sitter geometry at a descriptive level; Chapter 19 takes up Penrose diagrams, the de Sitter spacetime proper and the nature of the initial singularity, the exercises preview de Sitter space as a hyperboloid, and the present-day observational verdict (dark energy, flatness) is deferred to Section 49.8. The Lambda = 0 'standard models' and 'missing mass' discussion reflect the pre-1998 picture and must be updated by the tutor.

## Learning objectives

- Reader can derive a(t) for flat radiation-filled (a proportional to t^1/2) and flat dust-filled (a proportional to t^2/3) universes from the first Friedmann equation and state H(t) = 1/(2t) and 2/(3t) respectively.
- Reader can explain why a density that scales as a negative power of a forces a(0) = 0 and an infinite initial density, i.e. a Big-Bang singularity, while pure de Sitter expansion has none.
- Reader can write the combined dust-plus-vacuum first Friedmann equation adot^2 + k = C/a + (Lambda/3) a^2 and use term dominance at small and large a to predict late and early behaviour before solving.
- Reader can show that a negative cosmological constant stops expansion at a^3 = 3C/(-Lambda) and leads to a Big Crunch even in a flat universe.
- Reader can integrate the closed (k = 1) and open (k = -1) Lambda = 0 dust models by the substitution u^2 = a/C and describe their fates (recollapse vs unbounded growth).
- Reader can derive the Einstein static universe conditions k = 1, a0 = Lambda_E^-1/2, rho = Lambda_E/(4 pi) and prove it is unstable by linearizing the acceleration equation with mass conservation.
- Reader can recast the k = 1 dust-plus-Lambda equation as adot^2 + 1 = V_eff(a) and use the shape of V_eff to explain the Lemaitre coasting phase, the Eddington-Lemaitre model, and the bouncing Universe 11.
- Reader can estimate the coasting duration of a Lemaitre universe and explain why it grows only logarithmically as Lambda approaches Lambda_E.
- Reader can read the (k, Lambda) grid of a(t) curves and predict the origin (Big Bang or not) and fate (crunch, unbounded expansion, asymptotic static) of a model from its position.
- Reader can use the tangent-line construction to show that a monotonically decelerating universe is younger than 1/H_now, and explain how the time-scale problem arose and was resolved.
- Reader can outline the historical sequence (Einstein 1917, de Sitter 1917, Friedmann 1922, Lemaitre 1927/1931, Hubble 1929, Eddington 1930, Robertson-Walker 1935, Alpher-Herman 1948, Penzias-Wilson 1964) and say which numbered universe each proposal corresponds to.
- Reader can compute the critical density in SI units from H0 = 100h km/s/Mpc and compare it with the observed density of luminous matter.
- Reader can (via the exercises) represent de Sitter spacetime as a hyperboloid in a higher-dimensional Minkowski space and recognize that different slicings give flat, closed, open or static line elements.

## Teaching approach

A catalogue chapter organized as a controlled experiment: the Friedmann machinery from Chapter 17 is held fixed while the ingredients (radiation, dust, positive or negative vacuum energy) and the curvature constant k are changed one at a time, each resulting model receiving a number and a stacked-slice cartoon of its history. Exact solutions are shown when they are compact, but the authors lean repeatedly on 'which term dominates at small or large a' reasoning, then consolidate in a comparison table and a 5-by-3 grid of a(t) sketches. The k = 1 family is organized around Einstein's static universe, analysed with a linear perturbation; the Lemaitre model is then treated with an effective potential V_eff(a) whose shallow minimum (a point of slowest expansion) produces the coasting phase, while the Eddington-Lemaitre and 0 < Lambda < Lambda_E models are described only qualitatively through initial conditions and sketched radius functions. Only after the logical tour do the authors tell the historical story, deliberately contrasting logical order with discovery order, and use a tangent-line age argument to explain why Lambda was once needed and then abandoned.

**Style:** survey-overview, example-driven, diagram-driven, historical-narrative, conversational-informal, derivation-heavy, observational-astrophysics

**Narrative arc**

1. Recall the RW metric, both Friedmann equations and H = adot/a in a margin box; announce a survey of numbered universes classified by k, starting from a known a(t0).
2. Flat models: recall empty Universe 0 and de Sitter Universe 1 (no Big Bang); insert radiation (Universe 2, a proportional to t^1/2) and dust (Universe 3, Einstein-de Sitter, a proportional to t^2/3), showing both begin at a = 0 with infinite density; tabulate the three.
3. Mix dust and positive Lambda (Universe 4): predict t^2/3 early and exponential late by term dominance, then quote the exact cosh solution; flip the sign of Lambda (Universe 5) to get turnaround and a Big Crunch.
4. Curve space with Lambda = 0 (Universes 6 and 7): integrate the closed case in a worked example by substitution, quote the open case, and summarize that only k = 1 recollapses.
5. Seek a static solution (Universe 8): both Friedmann equations force k = 1 and a tuned dust density; perturb it and find exponential runaway, so the balance is unstable.
6. Push Lambda slightly above Lambda_E (Universe 9): introduce an effective potential, locate the minimum of adot, expand about it and show the coasting time scales as ln(1/epsilon).
7. Complete the k = 1 family: Eddington-Lemaitre (Universe 10, leaving or approaching the static state) and 0 < Lambda < Lambda_E (Universe 11, recollapse or bounce); gather everything in the (k, Lambda) grid of Fig. 18.10.
8. Re-tell the story in historical order: static Einstein model, de Sitter, Friedmann, Lemaitre, Hubble's law, instability, dropping Lambda, Primeval Atom and Hoyle's 'Big Bang' label, Robertson-Walker generality.
9. Pose the time-scale problem with a tangent-line bound on the age (Example 18.7), then show how revised H0 values in the 1950s removed the need for Lambda, making the Lambda = 0 Big-Bang models 'standard'.
10. Close with hot Big Bang nucleosynthesis, CMB prediction and discovery, critical density numbers and the missing mass puzzle, and hand off to the Big-Bang singularity in the next chapter; exercises build de Sitter space as a hyperboloid.

**Signature moves**

- Numbering every model (Universe 0 to 11) so that later text, figures and the summary grid can refer to them as named characters.
- Stacked-slice cartoons that encode both spatial geometry (square grids for flat, circles for 3-spheres, funnel sections for hyperbolic space) and the size history a(t) in one 3D sketch.
- Predicting asymptotic behaviour from which term of the Friedmann equation dominates at small or large a before quoting an exact solution.
- Treating the k = 1 dust-plus-Lambda equation like a particle energy equation, adot^2 + 1 = V_eff(a), so that coasting is explained by V_eff barely exceeding 1 near its minimum (the book uses this device for Universe 9 only; extending it to Universes 8, 10 and 11 is a natural tutor move).
- Linear perturbation of an equilibrium using the conservation law rho a^3 = const to close the equation, borrowed from a problem-book approach.
- A one-page grid (k columns, Lambda rows) that turns the whole chapter into a lookup table of origins and fates.
- Presenting physics in logical order first and history afterwards, with an explicit remark that the two orders differ, then using an elementary geometric construction (tangent line) to make a historical controversy quantitative.
- Exercises that preview advanced geometry (de Sitter hyperboloid, slicings, Poincaré patch, static patch) in a 2D toy version.

## Section by section

### Chapter opening: the toolkit recalled — p.188 (pdf 205)

After a Darwin epigraph about the struggle for existence, the authors restate the Robertson-Walker line element and, in a shaded margin box, both Friedmann equations and the Hubble parameter with its role in proper-distance growth. Universes will be stocked with perfect fluid plus vacuum energy, sorted by k, and each evolved from a known value of a at some reference time t0; Universes 0 and 1 from earlier chapters are flagged as the flat starting point.

**Concepts:** Robertson-Walker metric, Friedmann equations, Hubble parameter, scale factor, curvature constant k

**Key moves**
- Treat a(t) as an effective radius of the universe.
- Classify models first by k, then by matter content.
- Fix initial data by specifying a(t0).

### §18.1 Spatially flat universes — p.188 (pdf 205)

Flat models are built one ingredient at a time. Universe 0 is empty Minkowski space; Universe 1 (de Sitter) expands exponentially with constant H = sqrt(Lambda/3) and has no singularity. Radiation (Universe 2) gives a proportional to t^1/2 and dust (Universe 3, Einstein-de Sitter) gives a proportional to t^2/3; both start at zero size with infinite density and have H decaying as 1/(2t) and 2/(3t) (Example 18.1). A table compares the three flat models. Adding positive Lambda to dust (Universe 4) interpolates from t^2/3 to exponential growth with an exact cosh^(1/3) solution; a negative Lambda (Universe 5) instead halts expansion at a^3 = 3C/(-Lambda) and recollapses to a Big Crunch, with an exact (1 - cos)^(1/3) solution. A footnote introduces the general dust-plus-Lambda equation (18.11) used for the rest of the chapter.

**Concepts:** de Sitter universe, radiation-dominated universe, Einstein-de Sitter universe, Big-Bang singularity, flat dust plus cosmological constant universe, negative cosmological constant universe, Big Crunch, dust-plus-vacuum Friedmann equation, Hubble parameter

**Key moves**
- Substitute rho proportional to a^-4 or a^-3 into the k = Lambda = 0 Friedmann equation and separate variables.
- Differentiate a(t) to show H = 1/(2t), so H0 = 1/(2t0) and rho diverges at t = 0.
- Collect composition, a(t), H(t) and presence of a Big Bang into a comparison table.
- Argue from term dominance that dust controls early times and Lambda controls late times, then quote the exact solution.
- Note that negative vacuum energy acts like attractive positive-pressure matter, find the zero of the right-hand side, and quote the recollapsing exact solution.

### §18.2 Curved universes with Lambda = 0 — p.191 (pdf 208)

With Lambda = 0 the dust Friedmann equation becomes adot^2 + k = C/a. Example 18.2 solves the closed case (Universe 6) by the substitution u^2 = a/C and Big-Bang initial conditions, giving t(a) in closed form (arcsin minus a square-root term): the model rises from zero, reaches a maximum and collapses. The open case (Universe 7) is obtained analogously with arsinh and grows without bound, faster than Einstein-de Sitter. Stacked cartoons show 3-spheres for k = 1 and hyperbolic slices for k = -1, and a three-panel plot compares the k = 1, 0, -1 radius functions.

**Concepts:** closed dust universe, open dust universe, Big-Bang initial conditions, Big Crunch, curvature constant k

**Key moves**
- Change variable u^2 = a/C to reduce the closed-dust equation to a standard integral.
- Impose a = 0 at t = 0 as the integration constant.
- Invert the logic: obtain t(a) rather than a(t) and read the shape from the graph.
- Replace trigonometric by hyperbolic functions for k = -1.
- Conclude that with Lambda = 0 geometry decides fate: only k = 1 recollapses.

### §18.3 Einstein, Lemaître and Eddington — p.192 (pdf 209)

Seeking a time-independent model, Example 18.3 sets adot = addot = 0 in both Friedmann equations for dust plus Lambda_E and finds that k must be +1, with a0 = Lambda_E^-1/2 and rho = Lambda_E/(4 pi): the Einstein static universe (Universe 8). Example 18.4 perturbs a and rho, uses rho a^3 = const, and finds delta a grows like exp(sqrt(Lambda_E) t), so the balance between vacuum push and matter pull is unstable. Universe 9 (Lemaitre) with Lambda slightly above Lambda_E has a coasting period: writing adot^2 + 1 = V_eff(a) (Example 18.5), the expansion rate is smallest at a_min = (1+epsilon)^(1/3)/sqrt(Lambda), and expanding V_eff about that point (Example 18.6) gives a sinh solution whose plateau lasts a time of order ln(1/epsilon)/sqrt(Lambda) before de Sitter-like growth resumes. Universe 10 (Eddington-Lemaitre) either leaves the static state or approaches it asymptotically from a Big Bang; Universe 11 (0 < Lambda < Lambda_E) either recollapses from a Big Bang or bounces from exponential contraction to exponential expansion. Fig. 18.10 gathers representative a(t) curves on a k-versus-Lambda grid.

**Concepts:** Einstein static universe, instability of the Einstein static universe, critical cosmological constant Lambda_E, Lemaitre universe, coasting period, effective potential for the scale factor, Eddington-Lemaitre universe, bouncing universe, linear perturbation analysis

**Key moves**
- Use the second Friedmann equation at rest to get k/a0^2 = Lambda_E, which forces k = +1.
- Subtract the Friedmann equations to isolate addot, perturb about equilibrium, and close with delta rho/rho = -3 delta a/a.
- Rescale dust density in Einstein-universe units and write Lambda = (1+epsilon)^2 Lambda_E.
- Recast the first Friedmann equation as an energy equation with V_eff(a); locate the minimum of V_eff.
- Quadratic expansion of V_eff about its minimum yields a sinh solution; the prefactor sets a logarithmic coasting time.
- Describe the remaining k = 1 cases (Eddington-Lemaitre and 0 < Lambda < Lambda_E) qualitatively, distinguishing branches by initial conditions and pointing to the sketched radius functions of Fig. 18.9(b,c) and the grid of Fig. 18.10.

### §18.4 A brief history of model universes — p.196 (pdf 213)

The models are re-ordered historically: Einstein's 1917 static closed universe with a cosmological term, de Sitter's Lambda-only model, Friedmann's evolving solutions (1922), Weyl's remark on test-particle recession (1923), Lemaitre's independent derivation and expanding model (1927) championed by Eddington, Hubble's distance-velocity law (1929), Eddington's instability proof (1930), Einstein abandoning Lambda (1931), Lemaitre's Primeval Atom (1931), and Robertson and Walker's generality proof (1935). Example 18.7 uses a tangent to a(t) to show a decelerating universe is younger than 1/H_now; with pre-1950 values (about 1.8 billion years) this clashed with uranium-dated rocks at least 3 billion years old, the time-scale problem. Large Lambda or the Eddington-Lemaitre model offered escapes, but 1950s revisions of H0 lengthened the age to about 13 billion years, restoring Lambda = 0 Big-Bang 'standard models' (Universes 3, 6, 7). Nucleosynthesis in a hot early universe, the 1948 Alpher-Herman CMB prediction (about 5 K) and the 1964 Penzias-Wilson discovery (2.73 K) supported the Big Bang. The critical density 1.88e-26 h^2 kg/m^3 is compared with a luminous matter density near 1e-28 kg/m^3 despite evidence for flatness, labelled the missing mass problem.

**Concepts:** Hubble's law, time-scale problem, Hubble time as an age bound, standard models of cosmology, hot Big Bang, cosmic microwave background, critical density, missing mass problem, steady-state theory, dimensionless Hubble constant h

**Key moves**
- Explicitly contrast the logical order of the models with the order in which they were proposed.
- Draw the tangent to a(t) at t_now and show its intercept lies earlier than the true origin for a decelerating curve.
- Compare the Hubble-time bound with radiometric Earth ages to expose a contradiction.
- Show how revised measurements (timeline plot of H0) removed the contradiction and the motivation for Lambda.
- Use T proportional to 1/a to connect the Big Bang to element synthesis and a relic radiation field.
- Express rho_c numerically via h and compare with observed matter density.

### Chapter summary and exercises: Milne and de Sitter geometry — p.199 (pdf 216)

The summary recaps that perfect-fluid RW models mostly start with a Big Bang, that Einstein's static model is unstable while Einstein-de Sitter expands forever and Lemaitre's model coasts, and that the Lambda = 0 standard models all have an initial singularity. The eight exercises cover the empty k = -1 model (Milne universe) as flat spacetime in disguise, substitution checks of the Universe 4 and 5 solutions and of the stability and coasting algebra, and a sequence on de Sitter spacetime embedded as a hyperboloid in 4+1 (and toy 2+1 and 1+1) Minkowski space, where different coordinate choices give closed, open, flat-expanding, Poincaré and static line elements covering different portions of the hyperboloid.

**Concepts:** Milne universe, de Sitter hyperboloid, coordinate slicings of de Sitter spacetime, static de Sitter coordinates

**Key moves**
- Show the empty open universe is Minkowski space via r = t sinh chi, T = t cosh chi.
- Embed hyperbola and hyperboloid in flat Lorentzian space and read off induced metrics.
- Recognize that the same spacetime admits slicings with different spatial curvature, so k is a property of the slicing, not only of the spacetime.
- Identify coordinate patches that cover only part of the hyperboloid.

## Concepts

### Robertson-Walker metric

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: RW line element, FLRW metric

The most general spacetime metric compatible with spatial homogeneity and isotropy: ds^2 = -dt^2 + a(t)^2 [dr^2/(1-kr^2) + r^2 dOmega^2], with a single time-dependent scale factor and constant spatial curvature sign k.

**How introduced:** Restated at the chapter start as the common geometric backdrop of every model; in the history section the authors note Robertson and Walker proved its generality in 1935.

**Prerequisites:** curvature constant k, scale factor

$$
ds^2=-dt^2+a(t)^2\left[\frac{dr^2}{1-kr^2}+r^2 d\Omega^2\right]
$$

**Where:** GA p.188 (pdf 205); GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214)

### Friedmann equations

*law · revisited* · also: Friedmann equation 1 and 2

The time-time and space-space components of Einstein's equation for a Robertson-Walker metric filled with perfect fluid and vacuum energy: (adot/a)^2 + k/a^2 = 8 pi rho/3 + Lambda/3 and 2 addot/a + (adot/a)^2 + k/a^2 = -8 pi p + Lambda (G = c = 1).

**How introduced:** Recalled in a margin box; the first equation drives almost every model, and the second is used to impose staticity and to derive the acceleration equation for the stability analysis.

**Prerequisites:** Robertson-Walker metric, cosmological constant as vacuum energy

$$
\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2+\frac{k}{a^2}=\frac{8\pi}{3}\rho+\frac{\Lambda}{3}
$$

$$
2\frac{\ddot a}{a}+\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2+\frac{k}{a^2}=-8\pi p+\Lambda
$$

**Where:** GA p.188 (pdf 205); GA §18.3 p.192 (pdf 209); GA §18.3 p.193 (pdf 210)

### dust-plus-vacuum Friedmann equation

*identity · core* · also: eqn 18.11, master equation for dust and Lambda

For a universe containing only pressureless matter and a cosmological constant, the first Friedmann equation multiplied by a^2 becomes adot^2 + k = C/a + (Lambda/3) a^2 with C = (8 pi/3) rho_d a^3, a constant fixed by the conserved amount of dust.

**How introduced:** Slipped in as a footnote while treating flat dust plus Lambda, then promoted to the working equation for curved models and the basis for computing every panel of the summary grid.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann equations, scaling of energy density with the scale factor

$$
\dot a^2+k=\frac{C}{a}+\frac{\Lambda}{3}a^2
$$

$$
C=\frac{8\pi}{3}a_0^3\rho_{\rm d}^{(0)}
$$

**Notes:** Formally identical to an energy equation for a particle in a potential -C/a - Lambda a^2/3 with total energy -k; the chapter exploits this only for k = 1.

**Where:** GA §18.1 p.190 (pdf 207); GA §18.2 p.191 (pdf 208); GA §18.3 p.195 (pdf 212)

### scale factor

*physical-quantity · core* · also: expansion factor, radius function a(t), effective radius of the universe

The function a(t) multiplying the spatial part of the Robertson-Walker metric; ratios a(t2)/a(t1) give how much all comoving proper distances have grown, and for k = +1 it is literally the radius of the 3-sphere.

**How introduced:** Called the expansion factor and informally the effective radius; each model is characterized by its 'radius function' plotted against t.

**Prerequisites:** Robertson-Walker metric

$$
a(t)
$$

$$
l(t)\propto a(t)
$$

**Notes:** For k = 0 and k = -1 space is infinite, so 'radius' is only a figure of speech there.

**Where:** GA p.188 (pdf 205); GA §18.1 p.190 (pdf 207); GA §18.3 p.195 (pdf 212)

### curvature constant k

*convention · developed* · also: spatial curvature index

The constant in the Robertson-Walker metric taking values +1 (spatial 3-spheres), 0 (flat space) or -1 (hyperbolic space) after rescaling coordinates.

**How introduced:** Used as the primary organizing label: flat universes first, then k = +1 and k = -1, and as the column heading of the summary grid.

**Prerequisites:** Robertson-Walker metric

$$
k\in\{-1,0,+1\}
$$

**Where:** GA p.188 (pdf 205); GA §18.2 p.191 (pdf 208); GA §18.3 p.195 (pdf 212)

### Hubble parameter

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: H(t), Hubble constant H0 (present value)

H(t) = adot/a, the fractional expansion rate; the proper distance l between comoving observers obeys ldot = H l.

**How introduced:** Recalled in the margin; then computed for each flat model to show it is constant only for de Sitter and otherwise decays like 1/t, and finally inverted to bound the age of the universe.

**Prerequisites:** scale factor

$$
H=\dot a/a
$$

$$
\dot l=Hl
$$

$$
H_{\rm rad}=\frac{1}{2t}
$$

$$
H_{\rm EdS}=\frac{2}{3t}
$$

**Where:** GA p.188 (pdf 205); GA §18.1 p.189 (pdf 206); GA §18.1 p.190 (pdf 207); GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214)

### scaling of energy density with the scale factor

*law · revisited* · also: dilution laws, equation of state dependence

For conserved pressureless matter rho is proportional to a^-3; for radiation the extra redshift of each quantum gives rho proportional to a^-4; vacuum energy density stays constant.

**How introduced:** Taken from the thermodynamic argument of the previous chapter and substituted into the first Friedmann equation to create the radiation and dust models; the dust law rho a^3 = const also closes the stability calculation.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann equations

$$
\rho_{\rm r}=\rho_{\rm r}^{(0)}(a_0/a)^4
$$

$$
\rho_{\rm d}=\rho_{\rm d}^{(0)}(a_0/a)^3
$$

$$
\rho_{\rm d}a^3={\rm const}
$$

**Where:** GA §18.1 p.189 (pdf 206); GA §18.1 p.190 (pdf 207); GA §18.3 p.193 (pdf 210)

### cosmological constant as vacuum energy

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: vacuum energy, Lambda, cosmological term

A constant Lambda in Einstein's equation equivalent to a fluid of constant energy density Lambda/(8 pi) and pressure equal to minus that density; positive Lambda accelerates expansion, negative Lambda acts attractively.

**How introduced:** Used as an ingredient that can be switched on with either sign; the text reasons that because positive Lambda carries negative pressure, negative Lambda should behave more like ordinary attracting matter.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann equations

$$
\rho_\Lambda=\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi},\quad p_\Lambda=-\rho_\Lambda
$$

**Where:** GA §18.1 p.188 (pdf 205); GA §18.1 p.191 (pdf 208); GA §18.4 p.196 (pdf 213)

### empty flat universe

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: Universe 0

The Robertson-Walker model with k = 0 and no matter or vacuum energy; it is just Minkowski spacetime.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapter 15 as the trivial starting member of the catalogue.

**Prerequisites:** Robertson-Walker metric

**Where:** GA §18.1 p.188 (pdf 205)

### de Sitter universe

*solution-or-model · revisited* · also: Universe 1, flat de Sitter model

The flat Robertson-Walker model containing only positive vacuum energy, expanding as a(t) = a(t0) exp[H(t - t0)] with constant H = sqrt(Lambda/3); it has no Big-Bang singularity.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapter 15 as the constant-H reference; later it is the late-time attractor of Universes 4 and 9, and the exercises reinterpret it as a slicing of a hyperboloid.

**Prerequisites:** cosmological constant as vacuum energy, Hubble parameter

$$
a(t)=a(t_0)e^{H(t-t_0)}
$$

$$
H=\sqrt{\Lambda/3}
$$

**Where:** GA §18.1 p.188 (pdf 205); GA §18.1 p.190 (pdf 207); GA §18.3 p.194 (pdf 211); GA p.199 (pdf 216)

### radiation-dominated universe

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: Universe 2, flat radiation-filled model

The k = Lambda = 0 model filled with radiation (rho proportional to a^-4), for which a(t) = a0 (2 H0 t)^1/2 and H = 1/(2t); it begins at a = 0 with infinite density.

**How introduced:** Built by inserting the radiation dilution law into the flat Friedmann equation, separating variables, and then checking H(t) in Example 18.1.

**Prerequisites:** scaling of energy density with the scale factor, Friedmann equations, Big-Bang singularity

$$
a(t)=a_0\sqrt{2H_0t}
$$

$$
H=1/(2t)
$$

**Where:** GA §18.1 p.189 (pdf 206); GA §18.1 p.190 (pdf 207)

### Einstein-de Sitter universe

*solution-or-model · core* · also: Universe 3, E-dS model, flat dust universe

The flat, Lambda = 0 universe containing only dust, with a(t) = a0 (3 H0 t/2)^2/3 and H = 2/(3t); it starts from a Big Bang and expands forever with ever-decreasing rate, sitting exactly at the critical density.

**How introduced:** Derived like Universe 2 with rho proportional to a^-3; reused as the small-a limit of dust-plus-Lambda models and as the k = 0 member of the Lambda = 0 'standard models'.

**Prerequisites:** scaling of energy density with the scale factor, Friedmann equations

$$
a(t)=a_0\left(\tfrac32H_0t\right)^{2/3}
$$

$$
H=\frac{2}{3t}
$$

**Where:** GA §18.1 p.190 (pdf 207); GA §18.2 p.191 (pdf 208); GA §18.4 p.198 (pdf 215)

### Big-Bang singularity

*phenomenon · developed* · also: initial singularity, Big Bang

An initial moment at which the scale factor goes to zero and the energy density (and H) diverge, so the model's spacetime cannot be continued to earlier times.

**How introduced:** Identified in the radiation model from a(0) = 0 and confirmed through H = 1/(2t) growing without limit; later used as a yes/no column in the comparison table and as an initial condition; its physical meaning is postponed to the next chapter.

**Prerequisites:** scale factor, scaling of energy density with the scale factor

$$
a\to0,\ \rho\to\infty
$$

**Where:** GA §18.1 p.189 (pdf 206); GA §18.1 p.190 (pdf 207); GA §18.4 p.198 (pdf 215)

### Big-Bang initial conditions

*convention · introduced*

The choice of integration constant a = 0 at t = 0 when solving the Friedmann equation, which sets the time origin at the singularity so that t measures the age.

**How introduced:** Named in Example 18.2 when integrating the closed dust model.

**Prerequisites:** Big-Bang singularity

$$
a(0)=0
$$

**Where:** GA §18.2 p.191 (pdf 208)

### Big Crunch

*phenomenon · introduced* · also: final singularity, recollapse

A final singularity in which the scale factor returns to zero after a maximum, the time-reverse of a Big Bang.

**How introduced:** Appears first in the flat negative-Lambda model and then in the closed dust model and one branch of Universe 11.

**Prerequisites:** Big-Bang singularity

**Where:** GA §18.1 p.191 (pdf 208); GA §18.2 p.192 (pdf 209); GA §18.3 p.196 (pdf 213)

### flat dust plus cosmological constant universe

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: Universe 4, flat Lambda-dust model

The k = 0 model with dust and positive Lambda, a(t) = (3C/2Lambda)^1/3 [cosh(sqrt(3 Lambda) t) - 1]^1/3, which behaves like Einstein-de Sitter at early times and like de Sitter at late times.

**How introduced:** Motivated by asking what happens when vacuum energy is added to Universe 3; the authors first argue from which term wins at small and large a, then quote the exact solution (checked in Exercise 18.2).

**Prerequisites:** dust-plus-vacuum Friedmann equation, Einstein-de Sitter universe, de Sitter universe

$$
a(t)=\left(\frac{3C}{2\Lambda}\right)^{1/3}\left[\cosh\sqrt{3\Lambda}\,t-1\right]^{1/3}
$$

**Notes:** This is structurally the flat Lambda-CDM model of present-day cosmology, though the chapter does not say so.

**Where:** GA §18.1 p.190 (pdf 207); GA §18.1 p.191 (pdf 208)

### negative cosmological constant universe

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: Universe 5, flat anti-de Sitter-like dust model

The k = 0 dust model with Lambda < 0: the right-hand side of adot^2 = C/a + Lambda a^2/3 vanishes at a^3 = 3C/(-Lambda), so expansion halts and the model recollapses, a(t) proportional to [1 - cos(sqrt(-3 Lambda) t)]^1/3.

**How introduced:** Posed as an 'intriguing possibility' and motivated by the sign argument that negative vacuum energy acts like attracting positive-pressure matter.

**Prerequisites:** dust-plus-vacuum Friedmann equation, cosmological constant as vacuum energy, Big Crunch

$$
a_{\max}^3=\frac{3C}{-\Lambda}
$$

$$
a(t)=\left[\frac{3C}{2(-\Lambda)}\right]^{1/3}\left[1-\cos\sqrt{3(-\Lambda)}\,t\right]^{1/3}
$$

**Notes:** The prose calls it vacuum-filled but the solution contains the dust constant C.

**Where:** GA §18.1 p.191 (pdf 208)

### closed dust universe

*solution-or-model · developed* · also: Universe 6, closed Friedmann model, k = 1 dust model

The k = +1, Lambda = 0 dust model whose spatial slices are 3-spheres; t(a) = C[arcsin sqrt(a/C) - sqrt(a/C) sqrt(1 - a/C)], so a rises from zero to a maximum C and recollapses.

**How introduced:** Worked out fully in Example 18.2 by substitution u^2 = a/C, then pictured as a stack of 3-spheres growing and shrinking.

**Prerequisites:** dust-plus-vacuum Friedmann equation, Big-Bang initial conditions, Big Crunch

$$
t=C\left[\sin^{-1}\sqrt{a/C}-\sqrt{a/C}\sqrt{1-a/C}\right]
$$

**Notes:** Equivalent to the cycloid a = (C/2)(1 - cos eta), t = (C/2)(eta - sin eta), with maximum radius C at t = pi C/2 and crunch at t = pi C (our reformulation).

**Where:** GA §18.2 p.191 (pdf 208); GA §18.2 p.192 (pdf 209)

### open dust universe

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: Universe 7, k = -1 dust model

The k = -1, Lambda = 0 dust model with hyperbolic spatial slices; t(a) = C[sqrt(a/C) sqrt(1 + a/C) - arsinh sqrt(a/C)], expanding forever and faster than Einstein-de Sitter.

**How introduced:** Stated as the analogue of Example 18.2 with hyperbolic functions, compared graphically with the flat and closed cases.

**Prerequisites:** closed dust universe, curvature constant k

$$
t=C\left[\sqrt{a/C}\sqrt{1+a/C}-\sinh^{-1}\sqrt{a/C}\right]
$$

**Notes:** At late times adot tends to 1, i.e. a grows linearly like the empty Milne model (our remark).

**Where:** GA §18.2 p.192 (pdf 209)

### Einstein static universe

*solution-or-model · core* · also: Universe 8, Einstein Universe, E

The time-independent Robertson-Walker model with dust and a positive cosmological constant Lambda_E: staticity requires k = +1, radius a0 = Lambda_E^-1/2 and dust density rho = Lambda_E/(4 pi).

**How introduced:** Motivated as a way to avoid the 'disturbing' time dependence of all earlier models; Example 18.3 imposes adot = addot = 0 on both Friedmann equations.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann equations, cosmological constant as vacuum energy, curvature constant k

$$
\frac{k}{a_0^2}=\Lambda_{\rm E}
$$

$$
a_0=\Lambda_{\rm E}^{-1/2}
$$

$$
\rho_{\rm d}^{(0)}=\frac{\Lambda_{\rm E}}{4\pi}
$$

**Where:** GA §18.3 p.192 (pdf 209); GA §18.3 p.193 (pdf 210); GA §18.4 p.196 (pdf 213)

### critical cosmological constant Lambda_E

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: Einstein value of Lambda

The value of Lambda that exactly balances a given amount of dust in a closed universe, Lambda_E = 4 pi rho at the static radius; for fixed dust constant C it equals 4/(9 C^2).

**How introduced:** Emerges from Example 18.3 and then serves as the reference against which Lemaitre (Lambda > Lambda_E), Eddington-Lemaitre (Lambda = Lambda_E) and Universe 11 (0 < Lambda < Lambda_E) are defined, and as row labels of the grid.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein static universe

$$
\Lambda_{\rm E}=4\pi\rho_{\rm d}
$$

$$
\Lambda_{\rm E}=\frac{4}{9C^2}\ \text{(our rewriting)}
$$

**Notes:** The formula in terms of C is our derivation from V_eff = C/a + Lambda a^2/3 having minimum value 1.

**Where:** GA §18.3 p.193 (pdf 210); GA §18.3 p.195 (pdf 212)

### instability of the Einstein static universe

*theorem · core*

Small perturbations of the scale factor about the Einstein static radius obey d^2(delta a)/dt^2 = Lambda_E delta a, so they grow exponentially and the static state is an unstable equilibrium.

**How introduced:** Example 18.4 perturbs the acceleration equation (second minus first Friedmann equation) and closes it with dust conservation; historically credited to Eddington in 1930.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein static universe, linear perturbation analysis, scaling of energy density with the scale factor

$$
\ddot{\delta a}=\Lambda_{\rm E}\,\delta a
$$

$$
\delta a\propto e^{\sqrt{\Lambda_{\rm E}}\,t}
$$

**Where:** GA §18.3 p.193 (pdf 210); GA §18.4 p.196 (pdf 213)

### linear perturbation analysis

*technique · introduced* · also: stability analysis

Writing a quantity as equilibrium value plus a small deviation, keeping first-order terms in the equations of motion, and inspecting whether solutions grow or oscillate.

**How introduced:** Applied to the Einstein universe, following a problem-book approach (Lightman et al.).

$$
a=\Lambda_{\rm E}^{-1/2}+\delta a
$$

**Where:** GA §18.3 p.193 (pdf 210)

### effective potential for the scale factor

*technique · developed* · also: V_eff(a)

Rewriting the first Friedmann equation as adot^2 + 1 = V_eff(a) (for k = 1) so that the universe behaves like a particle with fixed 'energy' moving in a potential; regions with V_eff < 1 are forbidden and the minimum of V_eff marks where expansion is slowest.

**How introduced:** Introduced in Example 18.5 to analyse the Lemaitre model with Lambda = (1 + epsilon)^2 Lambda_E, then Taylor-expanded about its minimum in Example 18.6.

**Prerequisites:** dust-plus-vacuum Friedmann equation

$$
V_{\rm eff}(a)=\frac{2(1+\varepsilon)}{3\Lambda^{1/2}a}+\frac{\Lambda}{3}a^2
$$

**Notes:** In the book's sign convention V_eff is really minus a potential (it has a minimum where a mechanical potential would have a hilltop); tutors should state this clearly.

**Where:** GA §18.3 p.194 (pdf 211)

### Lemaitre universe

*solution-or-model · core* · also: Universe 9, Lemaitre solution, loitering universe, hesitating universe, L

The k = +1 model with dust and Lambda slightly larger than Lambda_E: it emerges from a Big Bang, lingers near the Einstein radius for a long coasting phase, then accelerates into de Sitter-like expansion.

**How introduced:** Introduced as a surprise (vacuum energy should give runaway expansion) and analysed with the effective potential; historically identified with Lemaitre's 1931 Primeval Atom proposal.

**Prerequisites:** effective potential for the scale factor, Einstein static universe, critical cosmological constant Lambda_E

$$
\Lambda=(1+\varepsilon)^2\Lambda_{\rm E}
$$

$$
a_{\min}=\frac{(1+\varepsilon)^{1/3}}{\Lambda^{1/2}}
$$

**Where:** GA §18.3 p.193 (pdf 210); GA §18.3 p.194 (pdf 211); GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214)

### coasting period

*phenomenon · developed* · also: loitering phase

An interval during which a Lemaitre-type universe stays close to a fixed size because its expansion rate is nearly zero; its duration grows like ln(1/epsilon)/sqrt(Lambda) as Lambda approaches Lambda_E from above.

**How introduced:** Derived in Example 18.6 by expanding V_eff quadratically about its minimum, obtaining a sinh solution and asking when the growing term becomes of order one.

**Prerequisites:** Lemaitre universe, effective potential for the scale factor

$$
(t-t_{\rm m})\sim\frac{\ln(1/\varepsilon)}{\Lambda^{1/2}}
$$

**Notes:** The printed prefactor in eqn 18.35 is wrong (should be sqrt(2 epsilon/3)); the logarithmic scaling survives.

**Where:** GA §18.3 p.194 (pdf 211)

### Eddington-Lemaitre universe

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: Universe 10, E-L model

The k = +1 model with Lambda exactly Lambda_E that either starts arbitrarily close to the Einstein static state in the infinite past and then expands, or starts from a Big Bang and approaches the static state asymptotically.

**How introduced:** Presented as Eddington's response to the Lemaitre model, avoiding a Big-Bang singularity; shown in both variants distinguished by initial conditions.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein static universe, instability of the Einstein static universe

**Where:** GA §18.3 p.196 (pdf 213); GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214)

### bouncing universe

*solution-or-model · introduced* · also: Universe 11, 0 < Lambda < Lambda_E closed model

A k = +1 dust-plus-Lambda model with 0 < Lambda < Lambda_E that, if started at large radius, contracts exponentially, reaches a minimum size and re-expands exponentially; the other branch starting from a Big Bang recollapses to a Big Crunch.

**How introduced:** Offered as the last case in the k = 1 family, with two branches separated by the forbidden region of the effective potential.

**Prerequisites:** effective potential for the scale factor, Big Crunch

$$
a\propto e^{-Ht}\ (t\to-\infty),\ a\propto e^{Ht}\ (t\to\infty)
$$

**Where:** GA §18.3 p.196 (pdf 213)

### Hubble's law

*law · mention* · also: distance-velocity relation

Galaxies recede with speed proportional to distance, v = H0 d, as expected for a homogeneous expanding universe.

**How introduced:** Given historically: established by Hubble in 1929, implicit in Friedmann's 1922 solutions, and noted by Weyl in 1923 for test particles in de Sitter space.

**Prerequisites:** Hubble parameter

$$
v_{\rm d}=H\times{\rm distance}
$$

**Where:** GA §18.4 p.196 (pdf 213); GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214)

### Hubble time as an age bound

*technique · developed* · also: tangent-line age estimate, 1/H_now

For a universe whose expansion has always decelerated, the tangent to a(t) at the present meets a = 0 at a time 1/H_now before now, which is earlier than the true origin, so the age is less than 1/H_now.

**How introduced:** Example 18.7 with a sketch of a concave a(t) and its extrapolated tangent.

**Prerequisites:** Hubble parameter, scale factor

$$
t_{\rm now}-t_1=\frac{a}{\dot a}=H(t_{\rm now})^{-1}
$$

$$
T<H_{\rm now}^{-1}
$$

**Notes:** With acceleration (positive Lambda) the bound can fail.

**Where:** GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214)

### time-scale problem

*historical-idea · developed* · also: age problem

The pre-1950s conflict in which the Hubble-time upper bound on the age of a decelerating universe (about 1.8 billion years) was shorter than the radiometric age of Earth's rocks (at least 3 billion years).

**How introduced:** Framed via Example 18.7; possible fixes discussed are a large Lambda in Lemaitre's model or an Eddington-Lemaitre start from the static state, and the eventual resolution by revised Hubble-constant measurements.

**Prerequisites:** Hubble time as an age bound

**Notes:** The book also flags a weakness of the Eddington-Lemaitre escape route: it is unclear how stars could condense at the moment expansion begins in a static, homogeneous model.

**Where:** GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214); GA §18.4 p.198 (pdf 215)

### standard models of cosmology

*historical-idea · introduced* · also: Lambda = 0 Friedmann models, Friedmann-Lemaitre standard models (older usage)

The mid-twentieth-century family of Lambda = 0 dust Big-Bang models: Einstein-de Sitter (k = 0), closed (k = 1) and open (k = -1), favoured after the time-scale problem was resolved.

**How introduced:** Named in the history section as the models taken seriously once revised H0 values allowed ages of order 10^10 years without Lambda.

**Prerequisites:** Einstein-de Sitter universe, closed dust universe, open dust universe

**Notes:** Superseded since the late 1990s by Lambda-CDM, which has positive Lambda; the term 'standard model' now refers to that.

**Where:** GA §18.4 p.198 (pdf 215); GA p.199 (pdf 216)

### hot Big Bang

*historical-idea · mention*

The picture in which the early universe was extremely hot because temperature scales as 1/a, allowing nuclear fusion of light elements shortly after the singularity.

**How introduced:** Mentioned as the nuclear-physics support for Big-Bang models promoted by Lemaitre in the 1930s.

**Prerequisites:** Big-Bang singularity

$$
T\propto a^{-1}
$$

**Where:** GA §18.4 p.198 (pdf 215)

### cosmic microwave background

*experiment-or-observation · mention* · also: CMB

The nearly isotropic black-body radiation filling space, left over from the epoch when the early universe became transparent, now at about 2.73 K with faint anisotropies.

**How introduced:** Presented as the compelling evidence for Big-Bang cosmology: predicted by Alpher and Herman in 1948 (about 5 K), found accidentally by Penzias and Wilson in 1964.

**Prerequisites:** hot Big Bang

$$
T_{\rm CMB}\approx2.73\,{\rm K}
$$

**Where:** GA §18.4 p.198 (pdf 215)

### critical density

*physical-quantity · revisited* · also: rho_c

The total energy density that makes a Friedmann universe spatially flat for a given Hubble rate, rho_c = 3 H0^2/(8 pi) in G = c = 1 units, i.e. 3 H0^2/(8 pi G) = 1.88e-26 h^2 kg/m^3.

**How introduced:** Recalled from Chapter 17 to decide which standard model our Universe resembles, and evaluated numerically.

**Prerequisites:** Friedmann equations, dimensionless Hubble constant h

$$
\rho_{\rm c}=\frac{3H_0^2}{8\pi}
$$

$$
\rho_{\rm c}=1.88\times10^{-26}h^2\ {\rm kg\,m^{-3}}
$$

**Where:** GA §18.4 p.198 (pdf 215)

### dimensionless Hubble constant h

*convention · introduced* · also: little h

The number h defined by H0 = 100 h km/s/Mpc, used to carry the uncertainty in H0 through formulas; recent values give h close to 0.7.

**How introduced:** Defined when evaluating the critical density, with a margin note and a timeline plot of H0 measurements.

H_0=100h\ {\rm km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}

**Where:** GA §18.4 p.198 (pdf 215)

### missing mass problem

*historical-idea · mention* · also: dark matter problem (as framed here)

The mismatch between the observed density of visible matter (around 1e-28 kg/m^3, far below critical) and evidence that the Universe is spatially flat, implying unseen contributions to the energy density.

**How introduced:** Stated briefly at the end of the history section as an open question.

**Prerequisites:** critical density

**Notes:** Modern resolution: cold dark matter supplies roughly a quarter and dark energy roughly 70 percent of the critical density; the chapter predates this framing and conflates the two.

**Where:** GA §18.4 p.198 (pdf 215)

### steady-state theory

*historical-idea · mention* · also: C-field cosmology

Hoyle's alternative in which continuous matter creation by a negative-energy creation field keeps the density constant as the universe expands, so there is no beginning.

**How introduced:** Described in a margin note on Fred Hoyle, with its abandonment linked to the discovery of the CMB and the story of Hoyle's 1949 BBC coinage of 'Big Bang'.

**Prerequisites:** cosmic microwave background

**Where:** GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214)

### Milne universe

*solution-or-model · mention* · also: empty open universe

The k = -1 Robertson-Walker model with no matter or vacuum energy, a(t) = t, which is flat Minkowski spacetime in hyperbolic slicing.

**How introduced:** Appears only in Exercise 18.1, where a coordinate change reveals the familiar spacetime.

**Prerequisites:** Robertson-Walker metric, empty flat universe

$$
ds^2=-dt^2+t^2[d\chi^2+\sinh^2\chi\,d\Omega^2]
$$

$$
r=t\sinh\chi,\ T=t\cosh\chi
$$

**Where:** GA p.199 (pdf 216)

### de Sitter hyperboloid

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: de Sitter spacetime as an embedded hyperboloid

De Sitter spacetime realized as the set of points at fixed spacelike 'distance' alpha from the origin of a five-dimensional Minkowski space (one time and four space directions); its induced metric is a vacuum solution with positive Lambda, and toy versions live in 2+1 or 1+1 dimensions.

**How introduced:** Introduced in italic linking text inside the exercises, with a hyperboloid figure and embedding calculations.

**Prerequisites:** de Sitter universe

$$
-T^2+W^2+X^2+Y^2+Z^2=\alpha^2
$$

**Where:** GA p.199 (pdf 216); GA p.200 (pdf 217)

### coordinate slicings of de Sitter spacetime

*technique · introduced* · also: closed, flat and open de Sitter slicings, Poincaré patch

Different choices of time coordinate on the de Sitter hyperboloid produce Robertson-Walker forms with k = +1 (a = cosh t), k = 0 (a = e^t) or k = -1 (a = sinh t); the flat slicing covers only half of the hyperboloid.

**How introduced:** Exercises 18.6 and 18.7 work this out in 1+1 dimensions, noting that the spatial curvature depends on how spacetime is cut into space and time.

**Prerequisites:** de Sitter hyperboloid, curvature constant k

$$
ds^2=-dt^2+e^{2t}dx^2
$$

$$
ds^2=\frac{1}{u^2}(-du^2+dx^2)

**Notes:** Exercise 18.6(c) as printed pairs sinh with spherical and cosh with hyperbolic; the Friedmann equation gives the opposite pairing.

**Where:** GA p.200 (pdf 217)

### static de Sitter coordinates

*technique · mention* · also: static patch

A coordinate system on de Sitter space in which the metric is time independent, ds^2 = -(1 - x^2) dt^2 + dx^2/(1 - x^2) in 1+1 dimensions (units alpha = 1), with a horizon at x = 1 and coverage of only part of the hyperboloid.

**How introduced:** Exercise 18.8, noting that a time-independent Lambda makes a static description unsurprising.

**Prerequisites:** de Sitter hyperboloid

$$
ds^2=-(1-x^2)dt^2+\frac{dx^2}{1-x^2}
$$

**Where:** GA p.200 (pdf 217)

Quadratic expansion about a_min using V(a_min) = (1+epsilon)^(2/3) and V'' = 2 Lambda. Verified.

## Worked examples

### Example 18.1 · intro · GA §18.1 p.189 (pdf 206)

**Problem:** Find the Hubble parameter for the flat radiation universe and decide how H and the energy density behave at early and late times.

**Method:** Differentiate a = a0 sqrt(2 H0 t) and divide by a.

**Key insight:** H = 1/(2t) blows up at t = 0 and decays afterward; because H is proportional to sqrt(rho), the density likewise starts infinite and falls to zero, confirming a genuine Big Bang.

**Result:** H(t) = 1/(2t), so H0 = 1/(2 t0). (The text cites eqn 18.5, but the solution being differentiated is 18.6.)

**Concepts:** radiation-dominated universe, Hubble parameter, Big-Bang singularity

### Example · intro · GA §18.1 p.189 (pdf 206)

**Problem:** Solve the first Friedmann equation for a flat Lambda = 0 universe containing only radiation (embedded derivation leading to Universe 2).

**Method:** Insert rho_r proportional to a^-4 into (adot/a)^2 = 8 pi rho/3, take the square root to get adot = H0 a0^2/a, separate variables a da = H0 a0^2 dt and integrate with a(0) = 0.

**Key insight:** Any density falling faster than a^-2 makes adot diverge as a goes to 0, so the solution must start at zero size in finite past time.

**Result:** a(t) = a0 sqrt(2 H0 t).

**Concepts:** radiation-dominated universe, scaling of energy density with the scale factor

### Example · intro · GA §18.1 p.190 (pdf 207)

**Problem:** Solve the flat dust-only model (Einstein-de Sitter) in the same way.

**Method:** Use rho_d proportional to a^-3 to get adot = H0 a0^(3/2) a^(-1/2), integrate a^(1/2) da.

**Key insight:** Changing the dilution power changes only the exponent of t; slower dilution gives a larger exponent (2/3 rather than 1/2).

**Result:** a(t) = a0 (3 H0 t/2)^(2/3), H = 2/(3t).

**Concepts:** Einstein-de Sitter universe

### Example · standard · GA §18.1 p.190 (pdf 207)

**Problem:** Predict and then state the evolution of a flat universe with dust and positive or negative Lambda (Universes 4 and 5).

**Method:** Compare the two terms of adot^2 = C/a + Lambda a^2/3: for small a the dust term wins (t^2/3 behaviour), for large a the vacuum term wins (exponential); for Lambda < 0 find where the right side vanishes; then quote exact cosh and cos solutions (verified in Exercise 18.2).

**Key insight:** Qualitative behaviour follows from term dominance and zeros of the right-hand side before any integration; negative Lambda guarantees a turning point.

**Result:** a proportional to [cosh(sqrt(3 Lambda) t) - 1]^(1/3) for Lambda > 0; a proportional to [1 - cos(sqrt(-3 Lambda) t)]^(1/3) with a_max^3 = 3C/(-Lambda) for Lambda < 0.

**Concepts:** flat dust plus cosmological constant universe, negative cosmological constant universe, dust-plus-vacuum Friedmann equation

### Example 18.2 · standard · GA §18.2 p.191 (pdf 208)

**Problem:** Integrate the Friedmann equation for a closed (k = 1) dust universe with Lambda = 0 (Universe 6).

**Method:** Write adot^2 = C/a - 1, substitute u^2 = a/C, separate variables with Big-Bang initial conditions u(0) = 0, evaluate the integral of u^2/sqrt(1 - u^2) in terms of arcsin, and substitute back.

**Key insight:** It is easier to find t(a) than a(t); the result shows a maximum radius a = C and a symmetric recollapse.

**Result:** t = C[arcsin sqrt(a/C) - sqrt(a/C) sqrt(1 - a/C)]; maximum radius C, collapse after time pi C (the latter our evaluation).

**Concepts:** closed dust universe, Big-Bang initial conditions, Big Crunch

### Example · standard · GA §18.2 p.192 (pdf 209)

**Problem:** Obtain the open (k = -1) Lambda = 0 dust solution (Universe 7).

**Method:** Repeat the Example 18.2 substitution with adot^2 = C/a + 1; the integral now produces an inverse hyperbolic sine.

**Key insight:** The sign of k flips circular functions into hyperbolic ones and removes the turning point.

**Result:** t = C[sqrt(a/C) sqrt(1 + a/C) - arsinh sqrt(a/C)]; unbounded growth.

**Concepts:** open dust universe

### Example 18.3 · standard · GA §18.3 p.192 (pdf 209)

**Problem:** Find the conditions for a static universe filled with dust and a positive vacuum energy Lambda_E.

**Method:** Set adot = 0, a = a0 and rho = rho_d^(0) in the first Friedmann equation, and additionally set addot = 0 with p = 0 in the second, giving k/a0^2 = Lambda_E; combine.

**Key insight:** The second Friedmann equation alone forces positive k, so a static dust universe must be spatially closed, and dust density and Lambda must be tuned together.

**Result:** k = +1, a0 = Lambda_E^(-1/2), rho_d^(0) = Lambda_E/(4 pi), equivalently a0 = (4 pi rho_d^(0))^(-1/2).

**Concepts:** Einstein static universe, critical cosmological constant Lambda_E

### Example 18.4 · standard · GA §18.3 p.193 (pdf 210)

**Problem:** Test whether the Einstein static universe survives a small perturbation of its radius and density.

**Method:** Subtract the Friedmann equations to get the acceleration equation, perturb a and rho about equilibrium to first order, use dust conservation rho a^3 = const to express delta rho through delta a, and substitute.

**Key insight:** The linearized equation has the form delta a'' = +Lambda_E delta a (a harmonic-oscillator equation whose restoring coefficient has the wrong sign), so small deviations grow exponentially: an expansion lowers density and lets Lambda win, a contraction raises density and lets matter win.

**Result:** delta a proportional to exp(sqrt(Lambda_E) t); the static universe is unstable.

**Concepts:** instability of the Einstein static universe, linear perturbation analysis, scaling of energy density with the scale factor

### Example 18.5 · standard · GA §18.3 p.194 (pdf 211)

**Problem:** Write the closed dust universe with Lambda slightly above the Einstein value, Lambda = (1 + epsilon)^2 Lambda_E, as an effective-potential problem and find where the expansion is slowest.

**Method:** Express the dust density in units set by the Einstein universe, replace Lambda_E by Lambda/(1 + epsilon)^2, rearrange the first Friedmann equation to adot^2 + 1 = V_eff(a), and minimize V_eff (algebra in Exercise 18.4).

**Key insight:** Small a behaves like Einstein-de Sitter, large a like de Sitter; in between V_eff dips only slightly below 1, so adot becomes very small there.

**Result:** V_eff = 2(1 + epsilon)/(3 Lambda^(1/2) a) + Lambda a^2/3, minimum at a_min = (1 + epsilon)^(1/3)/Lambda^(1/2).

**Concepts:** Lemaitre universe, effective potential for the scale factor

### Example 18.6 · challenging · GA §18.3 p.194 (pdf 211)

**Problem:** Estimate how long the Lemaitre universe coasts near a_min.

**Method:** Taylor-expand V_eff to second order about a_min (V = (1 + epsilon)^(2/3), V'' = 2 Lambda), integrate the resulting adot^2 = D^2 + Lambda (a - a_min)^2 to a sinh solution, take epsilon small, and ask when the sinh term becomes of order one.

**Key insight:** Near a hilltop the growth is exponential, so the time to leave a small neighbourhood scales like the logarithm of the initial smallness: coasting can be arbitrarily long but only logarithmically so.

**Result:** Coasting time of order ln(1/epsilon)/Lambda^(1/2). Note: the printed small-epsilon prefactor epsilon/3 in eqn 18.35 should be sqrt(2 epsilon/3), which halves the one-sided estimate but leaves the logarithmic scaling intact.

**Concepts:** coasting period, effective potential for the scale factor, Lemaitre universe

### Example 18.7 · intro · GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214)

**Problem:** Bound the age of a universe whose expansion has always been decelerating, using the present scale factor and expansion rate.

**Method:** Draw the tangent to a(t) at t_now; it crosses a = 0 at t_1 with a(t_now) = adot(t_now)(t_now - t_1); because a concave curve lies below its tangent, the real zero of a occurs later than t_1.

**Key insight:** 1/H_now overestimates the age of a decelerating universe; with 1930s-40s values of H0 this upper bound (about 1.8 billion years) was already below the ages of terrestrial rocks.

**Result:** T < t_now - t_1 = 1/H(t_now).

**Concepts:** Hubble time as an age bound, time-scale problem


## Analogies and intuitions

### The scale factor as a particle rolling in a one-dimensional potential (energy equation adot^2 + 1 = V_eff(a)) → effective potential for the scale factor · strong · GA §18.3 p.194 (pdf 211)

In Examples 18.5-18.6 the Lemaitre model is handled like an energy-conservation problem: the scale factor moves slowly where V_eff is only just above the fixed value 1, which explains coasting. The book does not use the picture for the other k = 1 models, though it extends to them directly.

**Where it breaks down:** The book's V_eff enters with the opposite sign to a mechanical potential (kinetic term equals V_eff - 1), so its minimum plays the role of a hilltop; the 'energy' is fixed by k, and there is no friction or genuine force.

**App idea:** Bead on a wire shaped like -V_eff(a) with energy line at -1; learners tune Lambda and watch the bead crawl over the crest (coasting), balance on it (Einstein), or bounce back (Universe 11).

### Tug-of-war between vacuum push and matter pull → Einstein static universe · useful · GA §18.3 p.193 (pdf 210)

The static universe is described as a balance where vacuum energy tries to inflate the universe and matter tries to collapse it, ending in an unstable standoff.

**Where it breaks down:** Both 'forces' depend on a in different ways (matter weakens as a^-3 while Lambda does not), which is precisely why the balance is unstable; a literal rope tug-of-war would be neutral, not unstable.

**App idea:** Pencil balanced on its tip beside the Einstein cylinder; nudging either shows the same exponential run-away.

### Negative vacuum energy behaves like ordinary attractive matter → negative cosmological constant universe · useful · GA §18.1 p.191 (pdf 208)

Since positive Lambda has negative pressure and repels, the text reasons that negative Lambda should act more like positive-pressure matter and halt expansion.

**Where it breaks down:** Negative Lambda has negative energy density and positive pressure; its effect grows with a (term proportional to a^2) rather than diluting, unlike real matter.

### Stacked slices as a flip-book history of space → scale factor · strong · GA §18.1 p.189 (pdf 206)

Each model is drawn as a solid built from spatial slices (flat grids, circles for 3-spheres, funnel sections for hyperbolic space) whose width tracks a(t), so the outline of the solid is the radius function.

**Where it breaks down:** Flat and hyperbolic slices are infinite, and the apex point wrongly suggests the Big Bang happened at a point in space; embedding in extra dimensions is purely pictorial.

**App idea:** Toggle between the stacked-solid view and the plain a(t) graph, with the slice at the cursor time highlighted in both.

### Extrapolating a slowing car back to its starting point → Hubble time as an age bound · useful · GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214)

Inferred framing of Example 18.7: dividing distance travelled by current speed overestimates travel time if the car has been braking, just as a/adot overestimates the age of a decelerating universe.

**Where it breaks down:** If the car (universe) has been accelerating, the estimate becomes an underestimate; the analogy does not capture that a = 0 is a singularity.

**App idea:** Race-track animation with a car's odometer and speedometer beside the tangent-line diagram.


## Misconceptions addressed

### A static solution must be a stable one, so Einstein's universe could last forever. · GA §18.3 p.193 (pdf 210)

**Correction:** The Einstein universe is an equilibrium but an unstable one: any small change in radius grows exponentially (Example 18.4).

**Why tempting:** In everyday experience things that stay still are usually in a stable state; balance of forces is easily confused with stability.

### Any positive cosmological constant makes a universe expand steadily faster. · GA §18.3 p.193 (pdf 210)

**Correction:** In a closed universe with Lambda just above Lambda_E the expansion can almost stall for an arbitrarily long coasting phase before accelerating.

**Why tempting:** Lambda is introduced as a repulsive ingredient, so intuition expects monotonic acceleration.

### Every expanding universe model necessarily begins with a Big Bang. · GA §18.1 p.188 (pdf 205)

**Correction:** De Sitter expansion, the Eddington-Lemaitre model leaving the static state, and the bouncing Universe 11 all expand without an initial singularity.

**Why tempting:** The popular picture equates expansion with an origin at zero size.

### Closed universes always recollapse and open or flat universes always expand forever. · GA §18.3 p.195 (pdf 212)

**Correction:** That rule holds only for Lambda = 0. With positive Lambda closed models can expand forever (Universes 9, 10), and with negative Lambda even flat and open models recollapse (bottom row of Fig. 18.10).

**Why tempting:** The Lambda = 0 'geometry is destiny' story dominated textbooks for decades.

### The Hubble 'constant' is constant in time. · GA §18.1 p.189 (pdf 206)

**Correction:** H(t) = adot/a generally changes; it falls as 1/(2t) or 2/(3t) in radiation or dust universes and is constant only for pure vacuum energy.

**Why tempting:** The name 'constant' and the single measured number H0.

### The age of the universe equals 1/H0. · GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214)

**Correction:** 1/H0 is only an upper limit if expansion has always decelerated (Einstein-de Sitter age is 2/(3H0)); with a past acceleration phase the age can exceed 1/H0.

**Why tempting:** The Hubble time has units of time and is numerically close to today's accepted age.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The Big Bang was an explosion at one point in pre-existing space, as the apex of the stacked diagrams suggests. · GA §18.1 p.189 (pdf 206)

**Correction:** In flat and open models space is infinite at every time; a = 0 means all distances shrink to zero everywhere, not that matter sat at a location.

**Why tempting:** Figures 18.1, 18.3, 18.6 and 18.8 draw the beginning as a vertex, and a(t) is called the 'radius'.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Spatial curvature k is an intrinsic, slicing-independent property of a spacetime. · GA p.200 (pdf 217)

**Correction:** The exercises show one de Sitter spacetime can be sliced so that spatial sections are closed, flat or open; k characterizes the homogeneous slicing, which is unique only when matter picks out a rest frame.

**Why tempting:** In matter-filled FRW models the comoving slicing is natural and k looks like a fixed label of the universe.

### Hoyle coined 'Big Bang' as an insult. · GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214)

**Correction:** He used the phrase on BBC radio in 1949 and later said he meant only to contrast the models vividly for a general audience.

**Why tempting:** The story is often retold as a put-down, and Hoyle did reject the theory.


## Thought experiments

### Nudging the Einstein universe · GA §18.3 p.193 (pdf 210)

**Setup:** Start with the exactly balanced closed static universe and add or remove a tiny amount of expansion, keeping the total dust fixed.

**Lesson:** Expansion dilutes matter so vacuum repulsion wins and expansion accelerates; contraction concentrates matter so gravity wins; the equilibrium cannot survive.

**App idea:** Nudge button on the static cylinder with log-scale growth plot of delta a.

### Dialling Lambda through Lambda_E · GA §18.3 p.194 (pdf 211)

**Setup:** Hold the dust content of a closed universe fixed and slowly raise Lambda from below Lambda_E, through it, and above it.

**Lesson:** Behaviour changes qualitatively at Lambda_E: recollapse or bounce below, static or asymptotic approach at, loitering then acceleration above; the closer to Lambda_E, the longer the loiter.

**App idea:** Single slider for Lambda/Lambda_E driving both an effective-potential plot and an a(t) plot.

### Running the expansion backward at today's rate · GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214)

**Setup:** Assume the present expansion speed had always applied and extrapolate galaxies back to zero separation.

**Lesson:** This gives 1/H; if expansion has been slowing, the true age is shorter, which produced the historical time-scale crisis.

**App idea:** Tangent-line tool of Fig. 18.13 with selectable histories.

### Flipping the sign of the vacuum energy · GA §18.1 p.191 (pdf 208)

**Setup:** Take the flat dust universe with Lambda added and change Lambda from positive to negative.

**Lesson:** The late-time term that produced exponential runaway now produces a turning point, so even flat space ends in a Big Crunch.


## Applications and observations

- **Radiometric age of Earth's rocks versus Hubble time** (numerical-estimate, GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214)): Uranium dating gave rock ages of at least 3 billion years, while the pre-1950s Hubble time was about 1.8 billion years, so a decelerating Big-Bang universe appeared younger than its rocks. Key numbers: Rock age >= 3e9 yr; 1/H_now about 1.8e9 yr (our check: H0 = 500 km/s/Mpc gives 1/H0 of about 1.96e9 yr).
- **Revised Hubble constant and age of the universe** (data-analysis, GA §18.4 p.198 (pdf 215)): 1950s recalibrations of the extragalactic distance scale lowered H0, increasing the estimated age; a timeline figure shows H0 values from roughly 500-600 in the late 1920s down to a modern cluster near 70 km/s/Mpc. Key numbers: Age estimate about 1.3e10 yr; h about 0.7. Our estimate: 1/H0 about 14 Gyr for h = 0.7, and an Einstein-de Sitter age 2/(3H0) about 9.3 Gyr.
- **Cosmic microwave background prediction and discovery** (detection-event, GA §18.4 p.198 (pdf 215)): Predicted in 1948 as redshifted early-universe radiation with an effective temperature of a few kelvin; detected accidentally in 1964; now measured precisely with small anisotropies. Key numbers: Predicted T about 5 K; measured T = 2.73 K.
- **Critical density versus observed matter density** (numerical-estimate, GA §18.4 p.198 (pdf 215)): Critical density from H0 compared with the density of observed matter, which is far lower even though the universe appears flat (missing mass problem). Key numbers: rho_c = 1.88e-26 h^2 kg/m^3 (about 9.2e-27 kg/m^3 for h = 0.7, our estimate); observed matter about 1e-28 kg/m^3.
- **Big-Bang nucleosynthesis** (observation, GA §18.4 p.198 (pdf 215)): Temperature scaling as 1/a implies extreme early temperatures able to fuse hydrogen into heavier nuclei, making element abundances a consistency check of Big-Bang models.
- **Coasting time of a Lemaitre universe** (numerical-estimate, GA §18.3 p.194 (pdf 211)): Our numerical integration of eqn 18.30-18.31 with Lambda = 1: time spent between 0.9 and 1.1 a_min is about 2.1, 6.4 and 11.0 for epsilon = 1e-2, 1e-4, 1e-6, i.e. growing by about ln(100) per factor of 100 in epsilon, confirming the logarithmic scaling of eqn 18.37 (our own estimate, not in the book). Key numbers: Delta t of about ln(1/epsilon)/sqrt(Lambda) for the full plateau.

## Historical notes

- **Albert Einstein (1917):** Built a spatially closed, uniformly filled static universe and added a cosmological term to prevent gravitational collapse. — First relativistic cosmological model (Universe 8) and origin of Lambda. (GA §18.4 p.196 (pdf 213))
- **Willem de Sitter (1917):** Found a model containing only the Lambda term that could describe expansion (the book also credits him with the repulsion balancing matter). — Source of Universe 1 and of later interest in empty expanding models; the attribution of the static balance to de Sitter in the text is historically garbled. (GA §18.4 p.196 (pdf 213))
- **Alexander Friedmann (1922):** Derived evolving homogeneous isotropic solutions of Einstein's equations, implying a Hubble-type recession law. — Origin of the Friedmann equations and non-static models. (GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214))
- **Hermann Weyl (1923):** Noted that test particles in de Sitter space separate at a rate consistent with a linear distance-velocity law. — Early theoretical anticipation of Hubble's law. (GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214))
- **Georges Lemaitre, Arthur Eddington (1927):** Lemaitre independently rederived Friedmann's models and proposed an expanding model starting from the Einstein state, later promoted by Eddington. — Universe 10, the Eddington-Lemaitre model. (GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214))
- **Edwin Hubble (1929):** Established that galaxy recession speed is proportional to distance. — Observational basis for an expanding universe; ended the case for a static model. (GA §18.4 p.196 (pdf 213))
- **Arthur Eddington (1930):** Showed the Einstein static universe is unstable to perturbation. — Theoretical nail in the static model (Example 18.4). (GA §18.4 p.196 (pdf 213))
- **Albert Einstein (1931):** Recommended removing the cosmological term from general relativity. — Beginning of decades of Lambda = 0 cosmology. (GA §18.4 p.196 (pdf 213))
- **Georges Lemaitre (1931):** Proposed that the universe expanded from a 'Primeval Atom' (identified with his loitering Universe 9); later promoted a hot Big Bang on nuclear-physics grounds. — Conceptual origin of Big-Bang cosmology. (GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214))
- **Howard P. Robertson, Arthur G. Walker (1935):** Independently proved that Lemaitre's line element is the most general one compatible with homogeneity and isotropy. — Justifies the Robertson-Walker metric underlying every model in the chapter. (GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214))
- **Ralph Alpher, Robert Herman, George Gamow (1948):** Alpher and Herman, building on Gamow, predicted relic radiation redshifted into the microwave band with a temperature of about 5 K. — Testable prediction of the hot Big Bang. (GA §18.4 p.198 (pdf 215))
- **Fred Hoyle (1949):** Used the phrase 'Big Bang' in a BBC radio talk while advocating a steady-state cosmology with a creation field. — Naming of the theory; steady state later abandoned partly because it could not explain the CMB. (GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214))
- **History (1950s):** Revised measurements of H0 raised the estimated age to about 1.3e10 years. — Resolved the time-scale problem without Lambda and made the Lambda = 0 models standard. (GA §18.4 p.198 (pdf 215))
- **Arno Penzias, Robert Wilson (1964):** Accidentally discovered the cosmic microwave background, since measured at 2.73 K with faint anisotropies. — Compelling evidence for Big-Bang cosmology. (GA §18.4 p.198 (pdf 215))

## Notation and conventions

- **Units:** Geometrized units G = c = 1 throughout the equations (e.g. rho_c = 3H0^2/8 pi); SI values quoted for the critical density restore G. (GA §18.4 p.198 (pdf 215))
- **Metric signature:** (-,+,+,+), with ds^2 = -dt^2 + a^2(...) for Robertson-Walker and -dT^2 + dX^2 + ... for embedding spaces. (GA p.188 (pdf 205))
- **Universe numbering:** Universe 0 empty flat; 1 de Sitter; 2 flat radiation; 3 Einstein-de Sitter; 4 flat dust + Lambda > 0; 5 flat dust + Lambda < 0; 6 closed dust; 7 open dust; 8 Einstein static; 9 Lemaitre; 10 Eddington-Lemaitre; 11 closed with 0 < Lambda < Lambda_E. Figure labels U3, U6, etc. and E, E-L, L, E-dS abbreviate these. — Book-specific; other texts use names only. (GA §18.3 p.195 (pdf 212))
- **Reference-time quantities:** Subscript or superscript 0 denotes values at a chosen reference time t0: a0 = a(t0), rho^(0), H0. In the history section t_now denotes the present. — In the radiation and dust solutions t0 is tied to the Big-Bang time origin (H0 = 1/(2t0) or 2/(3t0)). (GA §18.1 p.189 (pdf 206))
- **Dust constant C:** C = (8 pi/3) a0^3 rho_d^(0), constant because rho_d a^3 is conserved. (GA §18.1 p.190 (pdf 207))

- **Lambda as vacuum energy:** Lambda is placed on the energy side of the Friedmann equations and called vacuum energy; the table's 'Energy' composition means vacuum energy. (GA §18.1 p.190 (pdf 207))

## Teaching gems

### Number the universes and change one ingredient at a time (content, sign of Lambda, k), then compress everything into a k-by-Lambda grid of a(t) sketches. · GA §18.3 p.195 (pdf 212)

**Why it works:** Turns an intimidating zoo of solutions into a systematic experiment with a memorable lookup table; learners can predict a cell before looking.

**App idea:** Predict-then-reveal game: the app shows a (k, Lambda) cell and asks the learner to sketch a(t) with the mouse before overlaying the integrated curve and scoring the shape.

### Before solving, decide which term of adot^2 = C/a + Lambda a^2/3 dominates at small and large a, then splice the limiting behaviours. · GA §18.1 p.190 (pdf 207)

**Why it works:** Builds physical intuition and a sanity check that transfers to any multi-component universe, instead of relying on exact integrals.

**App idea:** Stacked-bar plot of the dust, curvature and Lambda terms versus log a, with the dominant term highlighted and the corresponding power-law or exponential overlay on a(t).

### Recast the closed-universe Friedmann equation for Lambda slightly above Lambda_E as an energy equation with an effective potential, so that the coasting phase is read off as the region where adot^2 = V_eff - 1 is nearly zero. · GA §18.3 p.194 (pdf 211)

**Why it works:** Leverages mechanics intuition learners already trust; although the book applies it only to the Lemaitre model, the same curve extends naturally to the static, Eddington-Lemaitre and bouncing cases, which a tutor can add.

**App idea:** Effective-potential playground: slider for Lambda/Lambda_E, draggable initial a and direction, bead animation on the potential synchronized with a(t).

### Linearize the acceleration equation about Einstein's static solution and close it with rho a^3 = const to obtain exponential growth. · GA §18.3 p.193 (pdf 210)

**Why it works:** A short, complete stability proof that explains why a historically important model was abandoned and practises a transferable technique.

**App idea:** Step-through derivation with each algebraic step revealed on click, paired with a simulation of the perturbation growing.

### Quantify loitering: the coasting time grows only as ln(1/epsilon) because escape from near an unstable equilibrium is exponential. · GA §18.3 p.194 (pdf 211)

**Why it works:** Connects cosmology to a universal result (time to leave a hilltop), and gives a crisp numerical takeaway.

**App idea:** Log-scale epsilon slider with a live readout of plateau length showing equal increments per decade.

### Use the tangent to a(t) at the present to bound the age by 1/H, then show how that bound created and later dissolved a real historical crisis. · GA §18.4 p.197 (pdf 214)

**Why it works:** An elementary geometric argument with high narrative payoff links abstract models to measurable numbers and the history of H0.

**App idea:** Draggable tangent-line diagram linked to an H0 timeline and a rock-age band.

### Tell the physics in logical order first, then retell it in historical order and point out that the two differ. · GA §18.4 p.196 (pdf 213)

**Why it works:** Learners get a clean conceptual structure without losing the human story; the mismatch itself teaches how science actually progresses.

**App idea:** Toggleable timeline: the same set of universe cards can be sorted by logical parameter or by date proposed.

### In the exercises, one hyperboloid yields closed, flat, open and static metrics depending on how it is sliced. · GA p.199 (pdf 216)

**Why it works:** Shatters the idea that k is intrinsic to a spacetime and previews coordinate patches and horizons in a low-dimensional, drawable setting.

**App idea:** 3D hyperboloid with slicing selector (see Fig. 18.15 redesign).


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Eqn 18.35 prints the small-epsilon coefficient as epsilon/3; expanding [1 - (1 + epsilon)^(-2/3)]^(1/2) actually gives sqrt(2 epsilon/3). Consequently 18.36-18.37 overstate the one-sided coasting time by about a factor of two (the logarithmic scaling is unaffected).** — A careful learner doing Exercise 18.4 or checking 18.34 will not reproduce the book and may doubt their algebra. *Suggestion:* Present the corrected prefactor and emphasise the scaling ln(1/epsilon)/sqrt(Lambda) for the total plateau; verify with a numerical integration.
- **Example 18.1 says it uses 'the solution in eqn 18.5' but differentiates eqn 18.6.** — Minor confusion about which equation is the solution. *Suggestion:* Refer to eqn 18.6 (a = a0 sqrt(2 H0 t)).
- **Universe 5 is described as filled with (negative) vacuum energy, yet its equation and solution contain the dust constant C and start from a Big Bang, so it is really dust plus negative Lambda.** — Learners may think pure negative Lambda alone produces a Big Bang and crunch. *Suggestion:* Label it 'flat dust universe with Lambda < 0' (pure negative Lambda with k = 0 has no real solution for a).
- **The symbol a_min denotes the radius of minimum expansion rate, not a minimum of a; and the book's V_eff has its minimum where a mechanical potential would have a maximum.** — Confusion over whether the universe shrinks to a minimum and over which way the 'potential' pushes. *Suggestion:* Rename it a_* or 'loitering radius' and plot -V_eff as the mechanical potential with total energy -1.
- **The history attributes to de Sitter the idea that the cosmological term exactly cancels gravitational attraction; that balance was Einstein's static model, while de Sitter's 1917 model was empty with Lambda only.** — Garbled history can propagate into tutoring. *Suggestion:* Credit the static balance to Einstein (1917) and the vacuum-only solution to de Sitter (1917).
- **Dated framing: 'standard models of cosmology have Lambda = 0' and the 'missing mass problem' reflect the pre-1998 picture; the chapter never mentions the accelerating expansion discovered with supernovae, dark energy, or Lambda-CDM (which is essentially Universe 4 plus radiation).** — Learners may leave believing current cosmology has no cosmological constant. *Suggestion:* Add a short modern coda: Omega_Lambda about 0.7, Omega_m about 0.3, flat; age 13.8 Gyr close to 1/H0 by coincidence; point to §49.8.
- **Exercise 18.6(c) pairs eqn 18.44 (sinh^2) with a spherical universe and 18.45 (cosh^2) with a hyperbolic one; the Friedmann equation with Lambda/3 = 1 gives k = +1 for a = cosh t and k = -1 for a = sinh t. Also eqn 18.45 is said to be 'from the chapter' but does not appear in it.** — Part (d) will contradict the stated claim, undermining confidence. *Suggestion:* Swap the identifications and state the global de Sitter form explicitly.
- **Exercise 18.8 prints T = (1 - x)^(1/2) sinh t; it must be (1 - x^2)^(1/2) sinh t to lie on the hyperboloid and give eqn 18.51.** — The computation fails as printed. *Suggestion:* Correct the parametrization before assigning.
- **Exercise 18.7 calls ds^2 = (-du^2 + dx^2)/u^2 'a type of Poincaré line element for Minkowski space', which is misleading: it is conformally flat but describes de Sitter (a curved spacetime), not Minkowski space.** — Conflates conformal flatness with flatness. *Suggestion:* Describe it as the Poincaré (conformally flat) patch of de Sitter space.
- **Fig. 18.10's panels are schematic and not to a common scale: e.g. in the Lambda < 0 row the k = 0 arch is drawn smaller than k = +/-1, whereas for fixed C and Lambda the closed model turns around soonest. Lambda_E is only meaningful for k = 1 but labels rows in all columns.** — Learners may read quantitative comparisons into the sketches. *Suggestion:* Generate the grid numerically from eqn 18.11 with shared C, and define Lambda_E = 4/(9C^2) explicitly as a reference value.
- **Stacked-slice figures draw the Big Bang as a single vertex and call a(t) the radius, even for infinite flat and open spaces.** — Reinforces the explosion-at-a-point misconception. *Suggestion:* Caption apex points as 'all distances shrink to zero' and show infinite grids fading out.
- **Several results are quoted without derivation (Universe 4 and 5 exact solutions, the Universe 7 integral, the sinh integral in Example 18.6), and the decaying perturbation mode exp(-sqrt(Lambda_E) t) is not mentioned.** — Gaps between steps can feel like magic; learners may think every perturbation grows. *Suggestion:* Provide short derivations or numerical checks; note that a generic perturbation contains the growing mode.
- **The statement that de Sitter is 'not badly behaved at t = 0' treats t = 0 as special; in flat de Sitter slicing the time origin is arbitrary, and the flat patch is past-incomplete even without a curvature singularity.** — May think the absence of a singularity means the flat coordinates cover all of spacetime. *Suggestion:* Connect to Exercise 18.7(b) and Chapter 19 on coordinate coverage.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'If you can choose what fills a universe and whether space is flat, curved like a sphere, or saddle-like, what futures can you get?' Let the learner guess before building the grid of Fig. 18.10.
- Order of explanation: (1) master equation adot^2 + k = C/a + Lambda a^2/3; (2) term-dominance reasoning; (3) flat models with power laws; (4) Lambda = 0 curvature decides fate; (5) Einstein static universe and its instability; (6) effective potential explaining Lemaitre, Eddington-Lemaitre and bounce; (7) tangent-line age and history; (8) modern update.
- Check for understanding: ask why radiation gives t^1/2 while dust gives t^2/3 (faster dilution means faster early slowdown), and ask the learner to predict the exponent for a general equation of state.
- Check for understanding: 'Why must a static dust universe be closed?' Expected answer: the second Friedmann equation at rest gives k/a^2 = Lambda > 0.
- When teaching the instability, give the physical loop in words (expand, dilute, Lambda wins, expand more) before the algebra, and compare with a pencil balanced on its tip.
- Correct the printed prefactor in eqn 18.35 when the learner reaches Example 18.6; emphasise the logarithmic scaling rather than exact constants.
- Clarify that the book's V_eff should be flipped in sign to act like a mechanical potential; draw -V_eff with energy line -1 to avoid confusion about hilltops versus valleys.
- Common learner question: 'Which universe do we live in?' Answer that data since 1998 favour a flat universe with dust (mostly dark matter) plus positive Lambda, i.e. close to Universe 4 with a small radiation component, not the Lambda = 0 'standard models' named in the chapter; see §49.8.
- Common learner question: 'Is 1/H0 the age of the universe?' Explain the tangent-line bound, the Einstein-de Sitter value 2/(3H0), and why for Lambda-CDM the age (13.8 Gyr) happens to be close to 1/H0.
- Common learner question: 'Did the Big Bang happen at a point?' Use flat stacked grids to explain that every distance shrinks, and flag that the apex in the figures is a drawing convention.
- For advanced learners, use the exercises to show that k depends on slicing in de Sitter space, and correct the swapped sinh/cosh identification in Exercise 18.6(c) and the typo in Exercise 18.8.
- Historical accuracy note: credit Einstein (not de Sitter) with the static balance of 1917; de Sitter found the empty Lambda model.
