# GA ch30 · Classical curvature

> Builds curvature from the ground up with classical calculus in flat 3-space: the osculating circle and Frenet-Serret frame for curves, principal curvatures and the second fundamental form for surfaces, Gauss's intrinsic curvature as det(g^-1 K), and a sketch of how Riemann generalized it to n dimensions.

**Pages:** printed 307–321 · pdf 324–338 · **Difficulty:** math 3/5, conceptual 3/5, novice-friendliness 3/5

Curves (30.1-30.2) are gentle calculus and vector algebra. Surfaces demand comfort with index notation, eigenproblems with a non-trivial metric and many small typos to navigate. Gauss's formula is displayed, not derived; the key claim that det(g^{-1}K) is intrinsic is asserted. Section 30.6 is historical and qualitative. Exercises 30.7-30.8 are a jump in sophistication (covariant derivatives on hypersurfaces). Several printed errors (sphere height, 30.25 sign, 30.64 factors, 30.68-30.69) will frustrate careful readers.

## Role in the book

Opens Part V, the book's second, more rigorous pass through geometry. Having already used the Riemann tensor physically (ch11) and applied it to cosmology and black holes, the authors step back to the pre-tensor, embedding-based picture of curvature so that the coordinate-free machinery of chs 31-36 has a concrete ancestor. It is flagged as optional, but it is where intrinsic versus extrinsic curvature is made computable, and its exercises preview the 3+1 hypersurface language (projection operator, extrinsic curvature tensor, Gauss-Codazzi equations).

## Learning objectives

- Reader can define the curvature of a plane curve as the reciprocal radius of the osculating circle and compute it three ways: tangent turning rate d(theta)/ds, second derivative of height above the tangent line, and |dt/ds|.
- Reader can construct the Frenet-Serret frame (t, p, b) for a space curve, define curvature and torsion, and write and interpret the antisymmetric Frenet-Serret matrix equation.
- Reader can expand a surface's height above its tangent plane as a quadratic form, diagonalize it by rotating axes, and read off the principal curvatures and principal directions.
- Reader can explain why Gauss wanted a curvature measurable from inside the surface and state the theorema egregium.
- Reader can compute Gaussian curvature from the Gauss-map area ratio dOmega/dA using a surface parametrization X(x^1, x^2).
- Reader can decompose the derivative of a basis vector into a tangential part (connection coefficients) and a normal part K_mu nu (Gauss's formula), and compute K_mu nu for a cylinder, paraboloid and sphere.
- Reader can derive that the normal curvature of a curve through a point is kappa_n = K_mu nu t^mu t^nu and, via a Lagrange multiplier, that its extremes are the eigenvalues of g^{-1}K.
- Reader can distinguish intrinsic curvature K = det K / det g = k1 k2 from the trace k1 + k2, and explain with the cylinder and sphere why only the former is visible to surface dwellers.
- Reader can state Riemann's counting argument in outline and relate Gaussian curvature to the Riemann tensor via K = R_1212 / g in two dimensions.
- Reader can, through the exercises, meet Weingarten's equation, projection onto a spacelike hypersurface, the induced metric and derivative, and the Gauss-Codazzi equations.

## Teaching approach

A historically ordered, dimension-climbing build-up. Each new dimension repeats the same three representations: a geometric comparison shape (circle, then ellipse/quadric), a coordinate Taylor expansion of height above a tangent line or plane, and a vector/basis-derivative formalism in flat embedding space. The payoff is reached by pushing the embedding formalism until the embedding drops out (det g^-1 K is intrinsic), then handing off to Riemann in words rather than proofs. Heavy use of boxed Examples keeps the main text short; margin notes carry etymology, history and caveats. The chapter explicitly labels itself skippable and classical, in contrast to the coordinate-free Part V to follow.

**Style:** historical-narrative, components-first, derivation-heavy, example-driven, spiral-revisit, conversational-informal, geometric-first

**Narrative arc**

1. Motivating question: what number should measure how bent a path is? Guitar outline and steering-wheel intuition.
2. Model: compare the curve with a circle; osculating circle from two nearby normals, kappa = 1/rho.
3. Alternative representations: angle swept by unit tangents per arc length; height above the tangent line as a parabola, kappa = y''.
4. Formalism for curves in 3-space: position vector X(s), unit tangent, principal normal, binormal, torsion, Frenet-Serret matrix.
5. Upgrade to surfaces: height above the tangent plane as a symmetric quadratic form; rotate to principal axes (Euler's perpendicularity); sphere check.
6. Problem: all of this uses a surrounding space. Gauss's goal of an insider's curvature; theorema egregium shown via the formidable metric formula; sphere and plane checks.
7. Vector formalism for surfaces: Gauss-map area ratio dOmega/dA and its cross-product formula.
8. Split basis-vector derivatives into in-surface (Gamma) and normal (K_mu nu) parts; compute K_mu nu for a cylinder.
9. Link K_mu nu to curvature: normal curvature of curves through a point, extremize with constraint, eigenvalues of g^{-1}K.
10. Interpretation: determinant is intrinsic (Gaussian) curvature, trace is extrinsic; cylinder versus sphere; paraboloid and sphere examples.
11. Generalization: Riemann's degrees-of-freedom argument, sectional curvature Q(W), the 1861 Paris paper, K = R_1212/g; hand-off to the modern treatment.
12. Exercises extend to Weingarten's equation and to the 3+1 projection formalism with Gauss and Codazzi equations.

**Signature moves**

- Same curvature measured three ways for a curve (circle radius, turning angle per length, second derivative of height) so learners see one invariant through several lenses.
- Dimensional analogy of ratios: angle swept on the unit circle per arc length for curves becomes solid angle swept on the unit sphere per area for surfaces.
- Car steering-wheel intuition for curvature before any formula.
- Physical props for intrinsic vs extrinsic: a tin can wrapped by a flat grid sheet, paper that cannot cover a sphere without cutting, and shoelaces that can always be straightened.
- Splitting a derivative of a basis vector into tangential and normal components, a template reused later for connections and extrinsic curvature.
- Deriving principal curvatures as a constrained eigenvalue problem, then extracting determinant and trace as the two invariants.
- Showing Gauss's full formula in all its ugliness to dramatize why better methods (Riemann tensor, Cartan) are wanted, without asking the reader to use it.
- Etymology and literary asides (osculate = kiss, egregious) used to make terminology memorable.

### §30.1 Curvature of a line — p.308 (pdf 325)

Curvature of a planar path is motivated by how fast tangents turn (steering wheel on a guitar-shaped road). The circle is chosen as the comparison shape: two nearby normals meet at the centre of curvature, giving the osculating circle with radius rho and curvature kappa = 1/rho. Example 30.1 re-expresses this as the angle swept by unit tangents on a unit circle divided by arc length, kappa = d(theta)/ds. A coordinate version measures height y above the tangent line so that kappa = d^2y/dx^2, justified in Example 30.2 by Taylor expansion and by matching to a circle's height x^2/(2 rho).

**Concepts:** curvature of a curve, osculating circle, radius of curvature, arc-length parametrization

**Key moves**
- Pick a reference figure (circle) whose curvature is obviously uniform and scaled by 1/radius.
- Locate the centre of curvature as the limit intersection of two nearby normals.
- Map unit tangents onto a unit circle so curvature becomes swept angle per arc length.
- Use a tangent-aligned coordinate so the linear Taylor term vanishes; the quadratic coefficient is the curvature.
- Match to a circle's small-angle height rho(1 - cos theta) ~ x^2/(2 rho); margin note stresses that locally the comparison is really to a parabola.

### §30.2 Curvature with vectors — p.310 (pdf 327)

Works in flat 3-space with a curve X(s). The unit tangent t = dX/ds is expanded on basis vectors e_mu = dX/dx^mu. Differentiating t.t = 1 shows dt/ds is perpendicular to t, defining the principal normal p and curvature kappa; Example 30.3 checks kappa equals d(theta)/ds via an isosceles-triangle chord. The binormal b = t x p completes an orthonormal triad; Example 30.4 shows db/ds lies along p, defining torsion tau. Differentiating p = b x t yields dp/ds = tau b - kappa t, and the three results assemble into the antisymmetric Frenet-Serret matrix system: two scalar functions kappa and tau describe a space curve.

**Concepts:** unit tangent vector, principal normal vector, osculating plane, binormal vector, torsion, Frenet-Serret equations, plane curve

**Key moves**
- Normalization t.t = 1 differentiated gives orthogonality of t and dt/ds.
- Chord between unit tangents has length 2 sin(dtheta/2) ~ dtheta, proving |dt/ds| = dtheta/ds.
- Unit length of b and orthogonality b.t = 0, differentiated, force db/ds to be along p.
- Cyclic cross-product relations of the triad let dp/ds be computed from the other two.
- Package as a 3x3 antisymmetric matrix acting on the triad.

### §30.3 Two-dimensional surfaces — p.312 (pdf 329)

For surfaces in flat 3-space a single sphere will not do, since bending generally differs by direction. Euler's result that the directions of maximal and minimal bending are perpendicular lets curvature be encoded in a symmetric 2x2 tensor K_ij: the height above the tangent plane is z = (1/2) K_ij x^i x^j. Example 30.5 rotates axes to diagonalize, giving principal curvatures kappa_1, kappa_2 and checks a sphere (both 1/a). The authors then note this depends on an embedding and introduce Gauss's programme of intrinsic measurement: Gaussian curvature K and the theorema egregium. Example 30.6 displays Gauss's explicit formula for K in terms of g_ij and its first and second derivatives, checking K = 1/a^2 for a sphere and 0 for the plane.

**Concepts:** principal curvatures, principal directions, second fundamental form, extrinsic curvature, embedding, Gaussian curvature, theorema egregium, intrinsic curvature, metric tensor

**Key moves**
- Generalize the curve's parabola to a quadric height function with a symmetric coefficient matrix.
- Diagonalize by a rotation of the tangent-plane axes (principal axes).
- Check with a sphere resting on a plane.
- Pose the insider problem: inhabitants cannot step outside to see the embedding.
- Present Gauss's metric-only formula (with locally flat coordinates as the starting assumption) and check it on sphere and plane.

### §30.4 Gauss' equation — p.314 (pdf 331)

By analogy with swept angle per length, Gaussian curvature is defined as the area swept by unit normals on the unit sphere per area on the surface, dOmega/dA. With a parametrization X(x^1, x^2), basis vectors e_1, e_2 and unit normal n = e_1 x e_2 / |e_1 x e_2|, Example 30.7 derives K = n.(dn/dx^1 x dn/dx^2)/|e_1 x e_2|. The more modern route decomposes the derivative of a basis vector into tangential connection terms Gamma^lambda_mu nu e_lambda and a normal part K_mu nu n; projecting on n gives Gauss's equation K_mu nu = (de_nu/dx^mu).n. Example 30.8 computes it for a cylinder: only K_theta theta = -a is non-zero.

**Concepts:** Gauss map, Gaussian curvature, surface basis vectors, unit normal, Gauss's formula for basis-vector derivatives, second fundamental form, connection coefficients

**Key moves**
- Promote the 1D ratio (angle/length) to 2D (solid angle/area).
- Area elements from cross products of coordinate derivatives; triple product with n fixes the sign.
- Normalization factor of n treated as constant when differentiating (margin note 15).
- Decompose de_nu/dx^mu into in-surface and normal pieces; the normal coefficient is K_mu nu.
- Cylinder: straight rulings give zero, circular direction gives -a.

### §30.5 Intrinsic and extrinsic curvature — p.316 (pdf 333)

For a curve in the surface through P, dt/ds splits into an in-surface part kappa_g e and a normal part kappa_n n. Writing d/ds = t^nu d/dx^nu and using Gauss's equation gives kappa_n = K_mu nu t^mu t^nu (margin note introduces first and second fundamental forms). Following Gauss, all curves through P are considered: Example 30.9 extremizes kappa_n under g_mu nu t^mu t^nu = 1 with a Lagrange multiplier, giving the eigenvalue problem for A = g^{-1}K whose eigenvalues are the extremal normal curvatures. Two invariants follow: det A = det K/det g = k1 k2 is the Gaussian (intrinsic) curvature, and Tr A = k1 + k2 is called the extrinsic curvature. A cylinder (tin can, Fig. 30.8) has k1 = -1/a, k2 = 0 so zero intrinsic curvature; a sphere cannot be flattened. Example 30.10 computes both invariants for a paraboloid at its vertex (uv and u+v) and a sphere (1/a^2 and -2/a). A note explains that curves have no intrinsic curvature.

**Concepts:** normal curvature, geodesic curvature, first fundamental form, second fundamental form, principal curvatures, shape operator, Gaussian curvature, extrinsic curvature, intrinsic curvature, developable surface, Lagrange multiplier

**Key moves**
- Project the acceleration of a unit-speed curve onto the surface normal.
- Chain rule converts d/ds into t^nu partial derivatives, giving a quadratic form in t.
- Constrained extremum with the metric as constraint produces the mixed tensor g^{lambda mu} K_mu nu.
- Contract the eigen-equation with t to show eigenvalues equal extremal normal curvatures.
- Identify determinant (intrinsic) and trace (extrinsic) as the basis-independent invariants.
- Cylinder vs sphere as the decisive contrast; shoelaces for 1D.

### §30.6 Riemann's project — p.318 (pdf 335)

Riemann sought an intrinsic curvature for n-dimensional spaces. His counting: a symmetric metric has n(n+1)/2 component functions, coordinate changes use n, so n(n-1)/2 functions carry genuine geometric content. He introduced a quadratic quantity Q(W) on each two-plane W, with -3Q giving the Gaussian curvature of that section, and claimed knowledge of Q on the n(n-1)/2 independent planes fixes the metric. In an 1861 Paris prize essay on heat conduction he wrote down what is now the Riemann tensor and stated flatness as its vanishing; Q(W) is proportional to R(X,Y,X,Y). The chapter gives the two-dimensional link K = R_1212/g and, in Example 30.11, outlines verifying it by combining eqn 11.22, the Christoffel formula and the 2x2 inverse metric with Gauss's formula, leaving the algebra to the reader.

**Concepts:** Riemann tensor, sectional curvature, degrees of freedom of a metric, Gaussian curvature, connection coefficients, flatness criterion

**Key moves**
- Count metric functions minus coordinate freedom to estimate genuine geometric data.
- Reduce higher-dimensional curvature to Gaussian curvatures of 2D sections.
- State flatness as vanishing of the Riemann tensor.
- Close the loop in 2D: K equals the single independent Riemann component divided by det g.

### Chapter summary and exercises — p.320 (pdf 337)

**Key moves**
- Exercises 30.7-30.8 re-express the chapter's embedding ideas covariantly for hypersurfaces in spacetime.

## Concepts

### curvature of a curve

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: kappa, curvature of a line

The rate at which the unit tangent of a curve turns per unit arc length, kappa = |dt/ds| = d(theta)/ds; equivalently the reciprocal of the radius of the circle that best fits the curve at that point.

**How introduced:** From a guitar outline and the steering-wheel intuition that sharper bends turn tangents faster; the circle is chosen as the yardstick and then three equivalent computations are offered.

**Prerequisites:** arc-length parametrization, unit tangent vector

$$
\kappa = 1/\rho = d\theta/ds
$$

$$
\kappa = d^2y/dx^2 \text{ (height above tangent line)}
$$

$$
|\kappa| = |d\mathbf{t}/ds|
$$

**Notes:** The d^2y/dx^2 form holds only in coordinates aligned with the tangent (y' = 0 at the point); the general graph formula is y''/(1+y'^2)^{3/2}, which the book does not give.

**Where:** GA §30.1 p.308 (pdf 325); GA §30.1 p.309 (pdf 326); GA §30.2 p.311 (pdf 328)

### osculating circle

*mathematical-object · introduced* · also: kissing circle, circle of curvature

The circle that touches a curve at a point P to second order: it shares the tangent and the curvature there, and its centre (centre of curvature) lies on the normal at distance rho = 1/kappa.

**How introduced:** Using the fact that two normals of a circle meet at its centre, the limiting intersection of two closely spaced normals of the curve defines the centre; a margin note gives the Latin root meaning to kiss.

**Prerequisites:** curvature of a curve

**Where:** GA §30.1 p.309 (pdf 326)

### radius of curvature

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: rho, centre of curvature

The distance rho from a point on a curve to its centre of curvature; the reciprocal of the curvature.

**How introduced:** As the radius of the osculating circle; later principal radii rho_1, rho_2 for surfaces.

**Prerequisites:** osculating circle

$$
\rho = 1/\kappa
$$

**Where:** GA §30.1 p.309 (pdf 326); GA §30.3 p.313 (pdf 330)

### arc-length parametrization

*convention · revisited* · also: length parametrization, unit-speed parametrization

Parametrizing a curve by the distance s measured along it, so that dX/ds has unit length at every point.

**How introduced:** Recalled from ch9 with a sketch of equally spaced tick marks s1, s2, ... along a curve; used to make tangents unit vectors.

$$
ds^2 = d\mathbf{X}\cdot d\mathbf{X}
$$

$$
\mathbf{t}\cdot\mathbf{t}=1
$$

**Where:** GA §30.1 p.309 (pdf 326); GA §30.2 p.310 (pdf 327)

### unit tangent vector

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: t, tangent

t = dX/ds, the derivative of the position on a curve with respect to arc length; it has unit length and components t^mu = dx^mu/ds on the basis e_mu = dX/dx^mu.

**How introduced:** Defined for a curve X(s) embedded in flat 3-space, with a margin caution that using a position vector relies on the flat embedding and will be abandoned in curved spacetime.

**Prerequisites:** arc-length parametrization

$$
\mathbf{t} = d\mathbf{X}/ds = t^\mu \mathbf{e}_\mu
$$

**Where:** GA §30.2 p.310 (pdf 327)

### principal normal vector

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: p, normal vector of a curve

The unit vector p along dt/ds; it is perpendicular to the tangent because t has constant length, and dt/ds = kappa p.

**How introduced:** Differentiating t.t = 1 shows dt/ds is orthogonal to t, so its direction is named p and its magnitude kappa.

**Prerequisites:** unit tangent vector

$$
d\mathbf{t}/ds = \kappa \mathbf{p}
$$

**Notes:** Often denoted N in other texts.

**Where:** GA §30.2 p.310 (pdf 327)

### osculating plane

*mathematical-object · introduced*

The plane through a point of a curve spanned by the unit tangent t and principal normal p; it is the plane in which the curve locally bends and contains the osculating circle.

**How introduced:** Drawn as a hatched face of a box built on the Frenet triad; for a plane curve it never changes.

**Prerequisites:** principal normal vector

**Where:** GA §30.2 p.310 (pdf 327)

### plane curve

*definition · mention*

A curve lying entirely within one plane; its osculating plane is fixed and its torsion is zero.

**How introduced:** Mentioned to contrast with general space curves before introducing the binormal and torsion.

**Prerequisites:** osculating plane

$$
\tau = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §30.2 p.310 (pdf 327)

### binormal vector

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: b

The unit vector b = t x p, normal to the osculating plane, completing a right-handed orthonormal triad with t and p.

**How introduced:** Introduced to span 3-space for non-planar curves; Example 30.4 then shows its derivative lies along p.

**Prerequisites:** principal normal vector

$$
\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{t}\times\mathbf{p}
$$

**Where:** GA §30.2 p.311 (pdf 328)

### torsion

*physical-quantity · introduced* · also: tau, torsion of a curve

The scalar tau defined by db/ds = -tau p; it measures how fast the osculating plane twists about the tangent, i.e. how quickly the curve leaves being planar.

**How introduced:** After proving db/ds is parallel to p, its coefficient is named torsion.

**Prerequisites:** binormal vector

$$
d\mathbf{b}/ds = -\tau\mathbf{p}
$$

**Notes:** Not the same as the torsion of a connection met later in differential geometry; a tutor should flag the name clash. The book's sentence attributes the twisting measure to b itself; strictly it is db/ds (hence tau).

**Where:** GA §30.2 p.311 (pdf 328)

### Frenet-Serret equations

*theorem · core* · also: Serret-Frenet equations, Frenet frame equations

The linear system giving the arc-length derivatives of the orthonormal triad (t, p, b) of a space curve: t' = kappa p, p' = -kappa t + tau b, b' = -tau p; the coefficient matrix is antisymmetric and a curve is determined up to rigid motion by kappa(s) and tau(s).

**How introduced:** Assembled from the three derivative results into a 3x3 matrix equation, presented as the milestone of classical curve theory.

**Prerequisites:** unit tangent vector, principal normal vector, binormal vector, torsion, curvature of a curve

$$
\frac{d}{ds}(\mathbf t,\mathbf p,\mathbf b)^T = \begin{pmatrix}0&\kappa&0\\-\kappa&0&\tau\\0&-\tau&0\end{pmatrix}(\mathbf t,\mathbf p,\mathbf b)^T
$$

**Notes:** The book states that two parameters suffice but does not state the fundamental theorem of curves (existence and uniqueness up to Euclidean motion).

**Where:** GA §30.2 p.311 (pdf 328); GA p.320 (pdf 337)

### embedding

*definition · developed* · also: embedding space, extrinsic description

Realizing a lower-dimensional space as a subset of a higher-dimensional flat space, so that its points are given by position vectors X in the larger space.

**How introduced:** Used throughout for curves and surfaces in flat 3-space, then questioned: beings confined to a surface cannot use it, motivating intrinsic measures.

**Notes:** Appendix D of GA treats embedding further.

**Where:** GA §30.2 p.310 (pdf 327); GA §30.3 p.313 (pdf 330)

### principal curvatures

*physical-quantity · core* · also: kappa_1, kappa_2, k_1, k_2, extremal normal curvatures

At a point of a surface, the maximum and minimum normal curvatures over all tangent directions; they are the eigenvalues of the shape operator g^{-1}K, with reciprocal principal radii rho_1, rho_2.

**How introduced:** First as the diagonal entries after rotating the height quadratic form to principal axes (30.3), then rederived as constrained extremes of kappa_n (Example 30.9).

**Prerequisites:** second fundamental form, normal curvature

$$
z = \tfrac12\kappa_1\xi^2 + \tfrac12\kappa_2\eta^2
$$

$$
k = K_{\mu\nu}t^\mu t^\nu\big|_{\text{extremal}}
$$

**Notes:** Signs depend on the choice of normal orientation; the book's examples give negative values for a sphere or cylinder with the outward normal.

**Where:** GA §30.3 p.312 (pdf 329); GA §30.3 p.313 (pdf 330); GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

### principal directions

*theorem · introduced* · also: principal axes

The tangent directions in which normal curvature is extremal; when the principal curvatures differ they are perpendicular (Euler), being eigenvectors of a matrix symmetric with respect to the metric.

**How introduced:** Attributed to Euler as the discovery that makes two-direction curvature manageable; shown by rotating axes in Example 30.5.

**Prerequisites:** principal curvatures

**Notes:** Euler's curvature formula kappa_n = kappa_1 cos^2 phi + kappa_2 sin^2 phi is implied but not written.

**Where:** GA §30.3 p.312 (pdf 329); GA §30.3 p.313 (pdf 330)

### second fundamental form

*mathematical-object · core* · also: K_mu nu, K_ij, extrinsic curvature tensor (of a surface), F_2

The symmetric tensor K_mu nu on a surface given by the normal component of the derivative of the basis vectors, K_mu nu = (de_nu/dx^mu).n; in tangent-plane coordinates the height of the surface is z = (1/2)K_ij x^i x^j, and F_2 = K_mu nu dx^mu dx^nu.

**How introduced:** First as the coefficient matrix of the quadratic height function over the tangent plane (30.17), then as the normal part of basis-vector derivatives (Gauss's equation), with the name second fundamental form confined to a margin note.

**Prerequisites:** surface basis vectors, unit normal, embedding

$$
z = \tfrac12 K_{ij}x^ix^j
$$

$$
K_{\mu\nu} = \frac{\partial \mathbf e_\nu}{\partial x^\mu}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}
$$

$$
K_{\mu\nu} = -\mathbf e_\mu\cdot\frac{\partial\hat{\mathbf n}}{\partial x^\nu}
$$

**Notes:** The book uses K for this tensor, for Gaussian curvature, and in Exercise 30.8 for the spacetime extrinsic curvature tensor; learners should track which is meant.

**Where:** GA §30.3 p.312 (pdf 329); GA §30.4 p.315 (pdf 332); GA §30.5 p.316 (pdf 333)

### first fundamental form

*mathematical-object · mention* · also: F_1, induced metric of a surface

The quadratic form g_mu nu dx^mu dx^nu with g_mu nu = e_mu . e_nu, giving lengths on a surface from within; it is the line element ds^2 of the surface.

**How introduced:** In a margin note that identifies the classical name with the familiar line element, noting the terminology will not be used again.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, surface basis vectors

$$
F_1 = g_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu
$$

$$
g_{\mu\nu} = \mathbf e_\mu\cdot\mathbf e_\nu
$$

**Notes:** Printed with dX^mu for the coordinate differentials.

**Where:** GA §30.5 p.316 (pdf 333)

### metric tensor

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: g_ij, line element

The symmetric tensor whose components g_ij give squared distance ds^2 = g_ij dx^i dx^j between nearby points; for an embedded surface g_mu nu = e_mu . e_nu.

**How introduced:** Recalled in Example 30.6 as the object from which Gauss builds an intrinsic curvature; locally flat coordinates with ds^2 = (dxi^1)^2 + (dxi^2)^2 are assumed at a point.

$$
ds^2 = g_{ij}dx^idx^j
$$

**Where:** GA §30.3 p.313 (pdf 330); GA §30.4 p.316 (pdf 333)

### surface basis vectors

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: e_1, e_2, coordinate tangent vectors

For a surface X(x^1, x^2) in flat 3-space, the tangent vectors e_mu = dX/dx^mu along the coordinate lines; they span the tangent plane.

**How introduced:** Defined by analogy with the tangent dX/ds of a curve, the surface coordinates playing the role of s.

**Prerequisites:** embedding

$$
\mathbf e_\mu = \partial\mathbf X/\partial x^\mu
$$

**Where:** GA §30.4 p.314 (pdf 331)

### unit normal

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: n-hat, surface normal

The unit vector perpendicular to a surface, n = (e_1 x e_2)/|e_1 x e_2|; its orientation fixes the signs of K_mu nu and principal curvatures.

**How introduced:** From the cross product of basis vectors in Example 30.7; margin note 15 explains the normalization factor is not differentiated.

**Prerequisites:** surface basis vectors

$$
\hat{\mathbf n} = \frac{\mathbf e_1\times\mathbf e_2}{|\mathbf e_1\times\mathbf e_2|}
$$

**Where:** GA §30.4 p.315 (pdf 332)

### Gauss map

*operation · introduced* · also: spherical image, area swept by normals

The map sending each point of a surface to the tip of its unit normal on the unit sphere; the ratio of image area dOmega to surface area dA at a point is the Gaussian curvature.

**How introduced:** As the 2D analogue of laying unit tangents on a unit circle; the map itself is not named in the book.

**Prerequisites:** unit normal, curvature of a curve

$$
K = d\Omega/dA
$$

$$
K = \frac{\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot(\partial_1\hat{\mathbf n}\times\partial_2\hat{\mathbf n})}{|\mathbf e_1\times\mathbf e_2|}
$$

**Notes:** Signed: a saddle's normals sweep with reversed orientation, giving negative K.

**Where:** GA §30.4 p.314 (pdf 331); GA §30.4 p.315 (pdf 332)

### Gaussian curvature

*physical-quantity · core* · also: K, total curvature, intrinsic curvature of a surface

The scalar K = k1 k2, the product of the principal curvatures, equal to det K_mu nu / det g_mu nu, to the Gauss-map area ratio, and in 2D to R_1212/g; it depends only on the metric.

**How introduced:** As Gauss's answer to the insider problem; shown first via his explicit metric formula, then via normals sweeping area, then as the determinant of g^{-1}K, and finally tied to Riemann's tensor.

**Prerequisites:** principal curvatures, metric tensor, second fundamental form

$$
K = k_1k_2 = \det\underline K/\det\underline g
$$

$$
K = R_{1212}/g
$$

$$
K_{\text{sphere}} = 1/a^2
$$

**Notes:** Exercises add parallel-transport (angle/area) and circumference-deficit definitions.

**Where:** GA §30.3 p.313 (pdf 330); GA §30.4 p.314 (pdf 331); GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334); GA §30.6 p.319 (pdf 336)

### theorema egregium

*theorem · core* · also: Gauss's remarkable theorem

Gauss's theorem that the Gaussian curvature of a surface can be computed from the metric components and their first and second derivatives alone, so it is unchanged by bending without stretching and is measurable by surface dwellers.

**How introduced:** Motivated by beings confined to their space; illustrated by displaying Gauss's explicit formula and checking sphere and plane; the word egregious is glossed in a margin note.

**Prerequisites:** Gaussian curvature, metric tensor

$$
K = K(g_{ij}, \partial g_{ij}, \partial^2 g_{ij})
$$

**Notes:** The chapter never proves that det K/det g equals the metric formula; that link is asserted with 'it turns out' and partly left to exercises and Example 30.11.

**Where:** GA §30.3 p.313 (pdf 330); GA §30.3 p.314 (pdf 331)

### intrinsic curvature

*definition · core* · also: true curvature

Curvature detectable by measurements of lengths and angles made entirely within a space, independent of how it sits in any larger space; for a surface it is the Gaussian curvature.

**How introduced:** Contrasted with extrinsic bending using a tin can (unrollable) and a sphere (cannot be flattened without cutting); a margin note says curves have none.

**Prerequisites:** Gaussian curvature

**Notes:** Revisits GA ch11 §11.1.

**Where:** GA §30.3 p.313 (pdf 330); GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

### extrinsic curvature

*definition · developed* · also: mean curvature (times two), trace of the shape operator

Curvature that describes how a space is bent within a larger embedding space; the book uses the name for the trace k1 + k2 = Tr(g^{-1}K), which is twice the mean curvature, while the full tensor K_mu nu is also called the extrinsic curvature tensor.

**How introduced:** As the second invariant of the eigenvalue problem, illustrated by a cylinder that looks curved but unrolls flat.

**Prerequisites:** second fundamental form, principal curvatures

$$
k_1+k_2 = \mathrm{Tr}(\underline g^{-1}\underline K)
$$

**Notes:** Standard terminology calls (k1+k2)/2 the mean curvature H; in GR 'extrinsic curvature' normally denotes the tensor K_ij and K its trace.

**Where:** GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334); GA p.321 (pdf 338)

### shape operator

*mathematical-object · developed* · also: A, Weingarten map, mixed tensor g^{lambda mu} K_mu nu

The (1,1) tensor A^lambda_nu = g^{lambda mu}K_mu nu mapping tangent vectors to tangent vectors; its eigenvalues are the principal curvatures, its determinant the Gaussian curvature, its trace k1 + k2.

**How introduced:** Emerges from a Lagrange-multiplier extremization of normal curvature subject to unit length measured by the metric.

**Prerequisites:** second fundamental form, metric tensor, Lagrange multiplier

$$
A^\lambda{}_\nu = g^{\lambda\mu}K_{\mu\nu}
$$

**Notes:** Book calls it simply the matrix A; shape operator and Weingarten map are standard names.

**Where:** GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

### normal curvature

*physical-quantity · developed* · also: kappa_n

The component along the surface normal of the curvature vector dt/ds of a curve lying in the surface; it depends only on the tangent direction: kappa_n = K_mu nu t^mu t^nu.

**How introduced:** By splitting dt/ds for a curve through P into tangential and normal parts and projecting onto n.

**Prerequisites:** second fundamental form, unit tangent vector

$$
\kappa_n = K_{\mu\nu}t^\mu t^\nu
$$

**Where:** GA §30.5 p.316 (pdf 333)

### geodesic curvature

*physical-quantity · mention* · also: kappa_g

The component of a curve's curvature vector lying in the tangent plane of the surface; it is intrinsic, and vanishes exactly for geodesics of the surface.

**How introduced:** Appears only as the unnamed coefficient kappa_g in the split of dt/ds; not developed.

**Prerequisites:** normal curvature

$$
d\mathbf t/ds = \kappa_g\mathbf e + \kappa_n\hat{\mathbf n}
$$

**Notes:** The name and geodesic link are our addition; a strong bridge to GA ch8-9.

**Where:** GA §30.5 p.316 (pdf 333)

### Gauss's formula for basis-vector derivatives

*identity · core* · also: Gauss' equation (book's name), Gauss formula

For an embedded surface, the derivative of a basis vector splits into a tangential part given by connection coefficients and a normal part given by the second fundamental form: de_nu/dx^mu = Gamma^lambda_mu nu e_lambda + K_mu nu n.

**How introduced:** Offered as the approach closest to modern differential geometry; the in-surface part previews the connection, the out-of-surface part defines K_mu nu.

**Prerequisites:** surface basis vectors, unit normal, connection coefficients

$$
\partial_\mu\mathbf e_\nu = \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}\mathbf e_\lambda + K_{\mu\nu}\hat{\mathbf n}
$$

**Notes:** A margin note jokes that Gauss has too many equations for this name to be unique; Exercise 30.8 gives the different, higher-dimensional Gauss equation.

**Where:** GA §30.4 p.315 (pdf 332)

### connection coefficients

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: Christoffel symbols, Gamma

The coefficients Gamma^lambda_mu nu giving the in-space part of the change of basis vectors; for a metric-compatible torsion-free connection they are built from first derivatives of the metric.

**How introduced:** Reappear as the tangential part of basis-vector derivatives on an embedded surface, and in Example 30.11 via the standard metric formula.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor

$$
\Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\sigma} = \tfrac12 g^{\rho\lambda}(g_{\lambda\mu,\sigma}+g_{\lambda\sigma,\mu}-g_{\mu\sigma,\lambda})
$$

**Where:** GA §30.4 p.315 (pdf 332); GA §30.6 p.319 (pdf 336)

### developable surface

*definition · mention* · also: surface that unrolls flat

A surface with zero Gaussian curvature everywhere, which can be flattened onto a plane without stretching or tearing, such as a cylinder or cone.

**How introduced:** Through the tin-can picture: a grid sheet wraps the can and lies flat; the term itself is not used.

**Prerequisites:** Gaussian curvature

$$
K = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

### Lagrange multiplier

*technique · mention*

A method for extremizing a function subject to a constraint by extremizing the function minus a multiplier times the constraint.

**How introduced:** Used in Example 30.9 to find extreme normal curvatures with the constraint that the tangent is unit length in the metric; the multiplier becomes the eigenvalue.

**Where:** GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

### Riemann tensor

*mathematical-object · revisited* · also: Riemann curvature tensor, R( , , , )

The (0,4) tensor built from the metric and its first and second derivatives whose vanishing is the condition for flatness; filled with two vectors spanning a plane it gives the Gaussian curvature of that section.

**How introduced:** Historically, as appearing in Riemann's 1861 Paris prize essay; linked to Gauss's K in 2D via R_1212/g.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor, connection coefficients, Gaussian curvature

$$
K = R_{1212}/g
$$

$$
3Q(X,Y) = -R(X,Y,X,Y)
$$

**Notes:** First met in GA ch11; rigorous treatment in ch35.

**Where:** GA §30.6 p.319 (pdf 336)

### sectional curvature

*definition · mention* · also: Riemann's Q(W), curvature of a two-plane

The Gaussian curvature of the two-dimensional surface swept out by geodesics tangent to a two-plane W in a higher-dimensional space; it equals R(X,Y,Y,X)/(|X|^2|Y|^2 - (X.Y)^2) up to sign convention.

**How introduced:** Via Riemann's quadratic function Q(W) with -3Q(W) giving the Gaussian curvature of the section W; the modern name is not used.

**Prerequisites:** Gaussian curvature, Riemann tensor

$$
-3Q(W) = K(W)
$$

**Where:** GA §30.6 p.318 (pdf 335); GA §30.6 p.319 (pdf 336)

### degrees of freedom of a metric

*historical-idea · introduced* · also: Riemann's counting argument

The heuristic count that an n-dimensional metric has n(n+1)/2 component functions, of which n can be altered by a coordinate change, leaving n(n-1)/2 functions of genuine geometric content.

**How introduced:** As Riemann's framing of when two metrics are secretly the same; a margin note derives n(n+1)/2.

**Prerequisites:** metric tensor

$$
n(n+1)/2 - n = n(n-1)/2
$$

**Notes:** At a single point the Riemann tensor has n^2(n^2-1)/12 independent components (1, 6, 20 for n = 2, 3, 4), so this count is about functions, not pointwise components.

**Where:** GA §30.6 p.318 (pdf 335)

### flatness criterion

*theorem · mention* · also: vanishing Riemann tensor

A space is flat (admits coordinates in which the metric is constant and Euclidean or Minkowskian) exactly when its Riemann tensor vanishes everywhere.

**How introduced:** Credited to Riemann's 1861 paper as the answer to what makes a space flat.

**Prerequisites:** Riemann tensor

$$
R_{\mu\nu\alpha\beta} = 0
$$

**Where:** GA §30.6 p.319 (pdf 336)

### Weingarten equation

*identity · mention*

The derivative of the unit normal of a surface is tangent and given by the shape operator: dn/dx^nu = -K^mu_nu e_mu, equivalently K_mu nu = -e_mu . dn/dx^nu.

**How introduced:** Only in Exercise 30.5, by differentiating t.n = 0 along a curve.

**Prerequisites:** second fundamental form, unit normal

$$
\partial_\nu\hat{\mathbf n} = -K^\mu{}_\nu\mathbf e_\mu
$$

**Where:** GA p.320 (pdf 337); GA p.321 (pdf 338)

### projection operator

*mathematical-object · mention* · also: P^mu_nu, spatial projector

For a spacelike hypersurface with unit timelike normal n (n.n = -1), P^mu_nu = delta^mu_nu + n^mu n_nu removes the normal part of a vector, satisfies P^2 = P, and induces the spatial metric h = g + n n.

**How introduced:** Exercise 30.7 only, as a first step toward describing hypersurfaces in spacetime.

**Prerequisites:** unit normal, metric tensor

$$
P^\mu{}_\nu = \delta^\mu{}_\nu + n^\mu n_\nu
$$

$$
h_{\alpha\beta} = g_{\alpha\beta}+n_\alpha n_\beta
$$

**Notes:** Sign relies on signature (-,+,+,+).

**Where:** GA p.321 (pdf 338)

### Gauss-Codazzi equations

*identity · mention* · also: Gauss equation (hypersurface), Codazzi equation, extrinsic curvature tensor K_alpha beta

Relations expressing projections of the spacetime Riemann tensor onto a hypersurface in terms of the hypersurface's own Riemann tensor, its extrinsic curvature tensor K_alpha beta = -P P nabla n, and derivatives of K with the induced derivative D.

**How introduced:** Stated at the end of Exercise 30.8 after building the induced covariant derivative and extrinsic curvature tensor.

**Prerequisites:** projection operator, second fundamental form, Riemann tensor

$$
\nabla_Y X = D_Y X + K(X,Y)\,n
$$

**Notes:** Foundation of 3+1 (ADM) formulations; not followed up in this chapter.

**Where:** GA p.321 (pdf 338)

### Bertrand-Diquet-Puiseux theorem

*theorem · mention* · also: circumference deficit formula

Gaussian curvature equals the limit as epsilon goes to 0 of (6/epsilon^2)(1 - C/(2 pi epsilon)), where C is the circumference of a geodesic circle of radius epsilon; an area version also exists.

**How introduced:** Exercise 30.3, recalling Exercise 3.3, applied to test a metric for flatness.

**Prerequisites:** Gaussian curvature

$$
K = \lim_{\epsilon\to0}\frac{6}{\epsilon^2}\left(1-\frac{C}{2\pi\epsilon}\right)
$$

**Notes:** Printed as 'Bertand'.

**Where:** GA p.320 (pdf 337)

### differential geometry

*historical-idea · mention*

The study of curves, surfaces and higher-dimensional spaces using calculus, including their curvature and metric properties.

**How introduced:** Named in the chapter opening as the field founded by applying calculus to geometry.

**Where:** GA p.307 (pdf 324); GA p.308 (pdf 325)

## Key equations

### (30.1) Curvature as swept angle per length · supporting · GA §30.1 p.309 (pdf 326)

$$
\kappa(s) = \lim_{\Delta s\to0}\frac{\Delta\theta\ \text{(angle swept by unit tangents on the unit circle)}}{\Delta s\ \text{(arc length on the curve)}}
$$

Operational definition of curvature: map tangents to the unit circle and compare the angle they cover with the distance travelled.

**Symbols:** s arc length; theta tangent direction angle

### (30.2) Curvature of a plane curve · central · GA §30.1 p.309 (pdf 326)

$$
\kappa(s) = \frac{1}{\rho(s)} = \frac{d\theta(s)}{ds}
$$

Curvature is the reciprocal radius of the osculating circle and the turning rate of the tangent.

**Symbols:** rho radius of curvature

### (30.3) Curvature from height above tangent line · supporting · GA §30.1 p.309 (pdf 326)

$$
\kappa = \frac{1}{\rho} = \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}
$$

In coordinates where x runs along the tangent at P, the second derivative of the height equals the curvature.

**Symbols:** y height above tangent line; x distance along tangent

### (30.4) Taylor expansion about P · derivation-step · GA §30.1 p.309 (pdf 326)

$$
y(x) = y(x_p) + y'(x_p)(x-x_p) + \tfrac12 y''(x_p)(x-x_p)^2 + \dots
$$

With y(x_p) = 0 and y'(x_p) = 0 at the tangent point, y = kappa x^2/2, matched to a circle's y ~ x^2/(2 rho).

### (30.5) Unit tangent · supporting · GA §30.2 p.310 (pdf 327)

$$
\mathbf t = \frac{d\mathbf X}{ds}
$$

Tangent to a curve given by position vector X(s) in flat 3-space.

**Symbols:** X position vector of the curve

### (30.6) Tangent in a coordinate basis · supporting · GA §30.2 p.310 (pdf 327)

$$
\mathbf t = \frac{d\mathbf X}{ds} = \frac{dx^\mu}{ds}\frac{\partial\mathbf X}{\partial x^\mu} \equiv t^\mu\mathbf e_\mu
$$

Chain rule splits the tangent into components dx^mu/ds and basis vectors dX/dx^mu.

### (30.7) Principal normal and curvature · central · GA §30.2 p.310 (pdf 327)

$$
\frac{d\mathbf t}{ds} = \kappa\,\mathbf p
$$

Because t has unit length its derivative is perpendicular to it; the direction is p and the magnitude is kappa.

**Symbols:** p unit principal normal

### (30.8) Chord between unit tangents · derivation-step · GA §30.2 p.310 (pdf 327)

$$
|\mathbf t(s+\Delta s)-\mathbf t(s)| = 2\sin(\Delta\theta/2) = \Delta\theta + O(\Delta\theta^3)
$$

Isosceles triangle of unit sides links the vector change of the tangent to the turning angle.

### (30.10) kappa equals turning rate · derivation-step · GA §30.2 p.311 (pdf 328)

$$
|\kappa| = \lim_{\Delta s\to0}\frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta s} = \frac{d\theta}{ds}
$$

Confirms the vector definition agrees with the angle definition (30.9 is the intermediate limit).

### (30.11) Cyclic triad relations · supporting · GA §30.2 p.311 (pdf 328)

$$
\mathbf b = \mathbf t\times\mathbf p,\quad \mathbf t = \mathbf p\times\mathbf b,\quad \mathbf p = \mathbf b\times\mathbf t
$$

Right-handed orthonormal frame relations used to differentiate p.

### (30.12) Binormal · supporting · GA §30.2 p.311 (pdf 328)

$$
\mathbf b(s) = \mathbf t(s)\times\mathbf p(s)
$$

Unit normal to the osculating plane.

### (30.13) Binormal derivative is perpendicular to t · derivation-step · GA §30.2 p.311 (pdf 328)

$$
\dot{\mathbf b}\cdot\mathbf t = -\mathbf b\cdot\dot{\mathbf t} = -\kappa\,\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf p = 0
$$

Together with b.(db/ds) = 0 this forces db/ds to point along p.

**Symbols:** dot = d/ds

### (30.14) Torsion · central · GA §30.2 p.311 (pdf 328)

$$
\frac{d\mathbf b}{ds} = -\tau\,\mathbf p
$$

Defines torsion as the rate at which the osculating plane rotates.

**Symbols:** tau torsion

### (30.15) Derivative of principal normal · derivation-step · GA §30.2 p.311 (pdf 328)

$$
\frac{d\mathbf p}{ds} = \frac{d\mathbf b}{ds}\times\mathbf t + \mathbf b\times\frac{d\mathbf t}{ds} = \tau\mathbf b - \kappa\mathbf t
$$

Obtained by differentiating p = b x t.

### (30.16) Frenet-Serret equations · central · GA §30.2 p.311 (pdf 328)

$$
\frac{d}{ds}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf t\\\mathbf p\\\mathbf b\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&\kappa&0\\-\kappa&0&\tau\\0&-\tau&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf t\\\mathbf p\\\mathbf b\end{pmatrix}
$$

A space curve's moving frame rotates with an antisymmetric generator fixed by curvature and torsion alone.

### (30.17) Surface height as a quadratic form · central · GA §30.3 p.312 (pdf 329)

$$
z = \tfrac12 K_{ij}x^ix^j
$$

Near a point, the surface's height above its tangent plane is governed by a symmetric 2x2 curvature tensor.

**Symbols:** x^i coordinates in the tangent plane

### (30.19) Height in matrix form · derivation-step · GA §30.3 p.312 (pdf 329)

$$
z = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}x&y\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}
$$

Same as z = a x^2/2 + b x y + c y^2/2 (30.18).

### (30.20) Rotation of tangent-plane axes · derivation-step · GA §30.3 p.312 (pdf 329)

$$
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha&\sin\alpha\\-\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\xi\\\eta\end{pmatrix}
$$

Choose alpha to diagonalize the symmetric matrix.

### (30.22) Principal-axis form · central · GA §30.3 p.313 (pdf 330)

$$
z = \tfrac12\kappa_1\xi^2 + \tfrac12\kappa_2\eta^2
$$

In principal axes the height separates; the eigenvalues are the principal curvatures kappa_i = 1/rho_i (matrix form is 30.21).

**Symbols:** xi, eta principal-axis coordinates

### (30.24) General 2D line element · supporting · GA §30.3 p.313 (pdf 330)

$$
ds^2 = g_{ij}\,dx^idx^j
$$

A curved surface uses coordinates with a position-dependent metric; locally flat coordinates give ds^2 = (d\xi^1)^2 + (d\xi^2)^2 (30.23).

### (30.25) Gauss's formula for K from the metric · central · GA §30.3 p.314 (pdf 331)

$$
K = \frac{1}{2g}\left[2g_{12,12}-g_{11,22}-g_{22,11}\right] - \frac{g_{22}}{4g^2}\left[g_{11,1}(2g_{12,2}-g_{22,1}) - g_{11,2}^2\right] + \frac{g_{12}}{4g^2}\left[g_{11,1}g_{22,2} - 2g_{11,2}g_{22,1} + (2g_{12,1}-g_{11,2})(2g_{12,2}-g_{22,1})\right] - \frac{g_{11}}{4g^2}\left[g_{22,2}(2g_{12,1}-g_{11,2}) - g_{22,1}^2\right]
$$

Theorema egregium made explicit: Gaussian curvature from metric components and their first and second derivatives only.

**Symbols:** commas denote partial derivatives; g = det g_ij (30.26)

### (30.26) Metric determinant in 2D · supporting · GA §30.3 p.314 (pdf 331)

$$
g = g_{11}g_{22} - g_{12}^2
$$

Determinant used throughout the intrinsic formulas.

### (30.27) Gaussian curvature of a sphere · central · GA §30.3 p.314 (pdf 331)

$$
K(\theta,\phi) = \frac{1}{a^2}
$$

Constant positive curvature of a sphere of radius a; the flat plane gives K = 0 (30.28).

**Symbols:** a sphere radius

### (30.29) Gaussian curvature as Gauss-map area ratio · central · GA §30.4 p.314 (pdf 331)

$$
K(\mathcal P) = \frac{d\Omega}{dA} = \lim_{\Delta A\to0}\frac{\text{area swept on unit sphere by normals}}{\Delta A\ \text{on the surface}}
$$

Two-dimensional analogue of (30.1).

**Symbols:** Omega solid angle

### (30.30) Surface basis vectors · supporting · GA §30.4 p.314 (pdf 331)

$$
\mathbf e_1 = \frac{\partial\mathbf X}{\partial x^1},\quad \mathbf e_2 = \frac{\partial\mathbf X}{\partial x^2}
$$

Coordinate tangent vectors of a parametrized surface.

### (30.31) Unit normal · supporting · GA §30.4 p.315 (pdf 332)

$$
\hat{\mathbf n} = \frac{\mathbf e_1\times\mathbf e_2}{|\mathbf e_1\times\mathbf e_2|}
$$

Normal from the cross product of basis vectors.

### (30.32) Area element · derivation-step · GA §30.4 p.315 (pdf 332)

$$
dA = |\mathbf e_1\times\mathbf e_2|\,dx^1dx^2
$$

Area of the coordinate parallelogram on the surface (printed with partial symbols in place of d on the far right).

### (30.34) Signed area swept by normals · derivation-step · GA §30.4 p.315 (pdf 332)

$$
d\Omega = \hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial\hat{\mathbf n}}{\partial x^1}\times\frac{\partial\hat{\mathbf n}}{\partial x^2}\right)dx^1dx^2
$$

The cross product of normal derivatives is parallel to n; dotting with n gives a signed solid angle.

### (30.35) K from unit normal derivatives · central · GA §30.4 p.315 (pdf 332)

$$
K = \frac{\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\left(\partial_1\hat{\mathbf n}\times\partial_2\hat{\mathbf n}\right)}{|\mathbf e_1\times\mathbf e_2|}
$$

Computable formula for Gaussian curvature of a parametrized surface.

### (30.36) Gauss's formula (tangential plus normal split) · central · GA §30.4 p.315 (pdf 332)

$$
\frac{\partial\mathbf e_\nu}{\partial x^\mu} = \Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}\mathbf e_\lambda + K_{\mu\nu}\hat{\mathbf n}
$$

Changes of basis vectors have an in-surface part (connection) and an out-of-surface part (second fundamental form).

**Symbols:** Gamma connection coefficients; K_mu nu second fundamental form

### (30.37) Gauss' equation (book's name) · central · GA §30.4 p.315 (pdf 332)

$$
K_{\mu\nu} = \frac{\partial\mathbf e_\nu}{\partial x^\mu}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}
$$

Extract the second fundamental form by projecting basis-vector derivatives onto the normal.

### (30.39) Cylinder basis and normal · derivation-step · GA §30.4 p.316 (pdf 333)

$$
\mathbf e_\theta = (-a\sin\theta, a\cos\theta, 0),\ \mathbf e_z = (0,0,1),\ \hat{\mathbf n} = (\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0)
$$

For X = (a cos theta, a sin theta, z) (30.38); only d e_theta/d theta = (-a cos theta, -a sin theta, 0) (30.40) is non-zero, so K_theta theta = -a.

### (30.42) Split of a surface curve's curvature vector · supporting · GA §30.5 p.316 (pdf 333)

$$
\frac{d\mathbf t}{ds} = \frac{dt^\mu}{ds}\mathbf e_\mu + t^\mu\frac{d\mathbf e_\mu}{ds} = \kappa_g\mathbf e + \kappa_n\hat{\mathbf n}
$$

Curvature of a curve in a surface decomposes into an in-surface (geodesic) part and a normal part; (30.41) is the product-rule step.

**Symbols:** e unit tangent-plane vector

### (30.43) Arc-length derivative via chain rule · derivation-step · GA §30.5 p.316 (pdf 333)

$$
\frac{d}{ds} = \frac{dx^\nu}{ds}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\nu} = t^\nu\frac{\partial}{\partial x^\nu}
$$

Converts derivatives along the curve into surface-coordinate derivatives.

### (30.47) Normal curvature · central · GA §30.5 p.316 (pdf 333)

$$
\kappa_n = t^\mu t^\nu K_{\mu\nu}
$$

The normal curvature depends only on the direction t through the second fundamental form; (30.44) is the projection step.

### (30.45)-(30.46) First and second fundamental forms · supporting · GA §30.5 p.316 (pdf 333)

$$
F_1 = g_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu,\qquad F_2 = K_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu
$$

Classical names for the line element and the normal-curvature quadratic form (margin note).

**Symbols:** printed with dX^mu

### (30.48) Constrained extremum of normal curvature · derivation-step · GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial t^\mu}\left[K_{\mu\nu}t^\mu t^\nu - k(g_{\mu\nu}t^\mu t^\nu - 1)\right] = 0
$$

Lagrange multiplier k enforces unit tangent length measured by the metric.

**Symbols:** k Lagrange multiplier

### (30.50) Eigenvalue equation for the shape operator · central · GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

$$
g^{\lambda\mu}K_{\mu\nu}t^\nu - k\,\delta^\lambda{}_\nu t^\nu = 0
$$

Principal directions are eigenvectors of A = g^{-1}K; (30.49) is the lowered form K_mu nu t^nu = k g_mu nu t^nu.

### (30.55) Eigenvalues are extremal normal curvatures · central · GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

$$
k = K_{\mu\nu}t^\mu t^\nu = \kappa_n^{\text{extremal}}
$$

Contracting the eigen-equation with t_lambda (30.54) identifies each eigenvalue with a principal curvature.

### Intrinsic and extrinsic invariants · central · GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

$$
K = \det\underline A = \frac{\det\underline K}{\det\underline g} = k_1k_2,\qquad \mathrm{Tr}\,\underline A = \mathrm{Tr}(\underline g^{-1}\underline K) = k_1+k_2
$$

Determinant is the Gaussian (intrinsic) curvature; trace is what the book calls extrinsic curvature (twice the mean curvature).

### (30.53) Shape operator of a cylinder · supporting · GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

$$
\underline A = \begin{pmatrix}-1/a&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

From K = diag(-a, 0) (30.51) and inverse metric diag(1/a^2, 1) (30.52): K = 0, trace -1/a.

### (30.57) Paraboloid metric · supporting · GA §30.5 p.318 (pdf 335)

$$
g_{\mu\nu} = \begin{pmatrix}1+u^2x^2 & uvxy\\ uvxy & 1+v^2y^2\end{pmatrix}
$$

Induced metric of X = (x, y, u x^2/2 + v y^2/2) (30.56); at the origin it is the identity and K_11 = u, K_22 = v.

**Symbols:** u, v curvature parameters

### (30.64) Sphere basis-vector derivatives (corrected) · supporting · GA §30.5 p.318 (pdf 335)

$$
\partial_\theta\mathbf e_\theta = -a\hat{\mathbf n},\quad \partial_\phi\mathbf e_\theta = \partial_\theta\mathbf e_\phi = \cot\theta\,\mathbf e_\phi,\quad \partial_\phi\mathbf e_\phi = -\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta - a\sin^2\theta\,\hat{\mathbf n}
$$

Normal parts give K_theta theta = -a, K_phi phi = -a sin^2 theta; tangential parts are the familiar sphere Christoffel symbols. The printed version has a spurious factor a in both tangential terms.

**Symbols:** X = a(sin theta cos phi, sin theta sin phi, cos theta) (30.62)

### (30.65) Gaussian curvature from Riemann in 2D · central · GA §30.6 p.319 (pdf 336)

$$
K = \frac{R_{1212}}{g}
$$

In two dimensions the single independent Riemann component divided by the metric determinant is Gauss's curvature.

**Symbols:** g = det g_mu nu

### (30.66) R_1212 from the metric · derivation-step · GA §30.6 p.319 (pdf 336)

$$
R_{1212} = \tfrac12\left(g_{12,21} - g_{11,22} + g_{21,12} - g_{22,11}\right) + g_{\sigma\rho}\left(\Gamma^\sigma{}_{21}\Gamma^\rho{}_{12} - \Gamma^\sigma{}_{22}\Gamma^\rho{}_{11}\right)
$$

Specialization of eqn 11.22 used to check (30.65) against (30.25).

### (30.68) Sample Christoffel symbol (corrected) · derivation-step · GA §30.6 p.319 (pdf 336)

$$
\Gamma^\rho{}_{12} = \tfrac12\left[g^{\rho1}g_{11,2} + g^{\rho2}g_{22,1}\right]
$$

From the standard formula (30.67). The printed version omits the factor 1/2 and writes g_{21,1} where g_{21,2} is needed.

### (30.69) 2x2 inverse metric (corrected) · derivation-step · GA §30.6 p.319 (pdf 336)

$$
g^{11} = g_{22}/g,\quad g^{12} = g^{21} = -g_{12}/g,\quad g^{22} = g_{11}/g
$$

Needed for the verification in Example 30.11. The printed line gives g^22 = g_22/g and appends '= g_21/g' to g^12, both errors.

### (30.72) Circumference-deficit curvature (Exercise 30.3) · supporting · GA p.320 (pdf 337)

$$
K = \lim_{\epsilon\to0}\frac{6}{\epsilon^2}\left(1 - \frac{\text{circumference}}{2\pi\epsilon}\right)
$$

Intrinsic measurement of K from geodesic circles.

### (30.80) Weingarten's equation (Exercise 30.5) · supporting · GA p.320 (pdf 337)

$$
K_{\mu\nu} = -\mathbf e_\mu\cdot\frac{\partial\hat{\mathbf n}}{\partial x^\nu}
$$

Second fundamental form from how the normal tilts; equivalent to (30.37) since e_mu . n = 0.

### (30.87) Extrinsic curvature tensor of a hypersurface (Exercise 30.8) · supporting · GA p.321 (pdf 338)

$$
K_{\alpha\beta} = -P^\mu{}_\alpha P^\nu{}_\beta(\nabla_\mu n)_\nu
$$

Covariant generalization of the second fundamental form for a spacelike hypersurface with unit normal n; (30.83)-(30.84) define P and h.

**Symbols:** P projector; n timelike unit normal

### (30.89) Gauss equation for a hypersurface · supporting · GA p.321 (pdf 338)

$$
P^\mu{}_\alpha P^\nu{}_\beta P^\sigma{}_\gamma P^\rho{}_\delta R_{\mu\nu\sigma\rho} = {}^{(3)}R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} - K_{\alpha\delta}K_{\beta\gamma} + K_{\alpha\gamma}K_{\beta\delta}
$$

Projected spacetime curvature equals intrinsic curvature of the slice plus products of extrinsic curvature: the higher-dimensional heir of K = det K/det g.

### (30.90) Codazzi equation · supporting · GA p.321 (pdf 338)

$$
P^\mu{}_\alpha P^\nu{}_\beta P^\sigma{}_\gamma n^\rho R_{\mu\nu\sigma\rho} = D_\beta K_{\alpha\gamma} - D_\alpha K_{\beta\gamma}
$$

Mixed normal-tangential projection of Riemann is governed by derivatives of extrinsic curvature.

**Symbols:** D induced covariant derivative

## Worked examples

### Example 30.1 · intro · GA §30.1 p.309 (pdf 326)

**Problem:** Find an alternative, parametrization-based definition of curvature and check it on a circle.

**Method:** Parametrize by arc length; transplant unit tangents from a short stretch onto a unit circle; divide the swept angle by the arc length and take the limit. For a circle of radius rho, arc length rho times the angle gives the ratio directly.

**Key insight:** Curvature is a turning rate: angle of tangent change per distance travelled.

**Result:** kappa = 1/rho = d(theta)/ds

**Concepts:** curvature of a curve, arc-length parametrization, unit tangent vector

### Example 30.2 · intro · GA §30.1 p.309 (pdf 326)

**Problem:** Justify that the second derivative of height above the tangent line equals the curvature.

**Method:** Taylor-expand y about x_p with the tangent line as x-axis so the constant and linear terms vanish, giving y = kappa x^2/2. Compare with the height rho(1 - cos theta) of a tangent circle, which for small theta = x/rho is x^2/(2 rho).

**Key insight:** Locally every smooth curve looks like a parabola whose coefficient is half the curvature; the circle and the parabola agree to second order.

**Result:** kappa = 1/rho = y''(x_p) when y'(x_p) = 0

**Concepts:** curvature of a curve, osculating circle

### Example 30.3 · intro · GA §30.2 p.310 (pdf 327)

**Problem:** Show that the coefficient kappa in dt/ds = kappa p is the same curvature as d(theta)/ds.

**Method:** The change between two unit tangents is the base of an isosceles triangle with unit sides, of length 2 sin(Delta theta/2) which is Delta theta to third order; divide by Delta s and take the limit, using |p| = 1.

**Key insight:** For unit vectors, the size of a small change equals the small angle turned.

**Result:** |kappa| = |dt/ds| = d(theta)/ds

**Concepts:** principal normal vector, curvature of a curve

### Example 30.4 · standard · GA §30.2 p.311 (pdf 328)

**Problem:** Determine the direction of the derivative of the binormal.

**Method:** Differentiate b.b = 1 to get b.(db/ds) = 0 (printed with the dot missing). Differentiate b.t = 0 and use dt/ds = kappa p and b.p = 0 to get (db/ds).t = 0. Being perpendicular to both b and t, db/ds lies along p.

**Key insight:** Orthonormality constraints, differentiated, restrict which directions a frame vector can move in; this is why the Frenet matrix is antisymmetric.

**Result:** db/ds is parallel to p, motivating db/ds = -tau p

**Concepts:** binormal vector, torsion, Frenet-Serret equations

### Example 30.5 · standard · GA §30.3 p.312 (pdf 329)

**Problem:** Reduce the general quadratic height z = a x^2/2 + b x y + c y^2/2 of a surface above its tangent plane to principal form, and apply it to a sphere of radius a resting on a plane.

**Method:** Write z as a symmetric matrix quadratic form, rotate axes by an angle alpha chosen to diagonalize, giving z = kappa_1 xi^2/2 + kappa_2 eta^2/2. For the sphere, expand the lower hemisphere's height near the contact point to second order.

**Key insight:** Principal curvatures are eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix and principal directions are its perpendicular eigenvectors.

**Result:** Sphere: both principal curvatures equal 1/a. Printed errors: the height is written with (a^2 + x^2 + y^2)^{1/2} instead of a - (a^2 - x^2 - y^2)^{1/2}, and the expansion coefficient appears as 1/(2a^2) where 1/(2a) is needed for kappa = 1/a.

**Concepts:** principal curvatures, principal directions, second fundamental form

### Example 30.6 · standard · GA §30.3 p.313 (pdf 330)

**Problem:** Exhibit Gauss's intrinsic curvature as a function of the metric and test it on a sphere and a plane.

**Method:** Assume locally Euclidean coordinates exist at a point but not over a region; write the general line element g_ij dx^i dx^j; present Gauss's formula (30.25) involving g, its first and second derivatives and determinant g; substitute g_theta theta = a^2, g_phi phi = a^2 sin^2 theta, and the flat metric.

**Key insight:** Curvature can be computed from distances alone, although the explicit formula is unwieldy, motivating better tools.

**Result:** Sphere K = 1/a^2; plane K = 0.

**Concepts:** theorema egregium, Gaussian curvature, metric tensor

### Example 30.7 · standard · GA §30.4 p.315 (pdf 332)

**Problem:** Turn the area-swept-by-normals definition of Gaussian curvature into a formula in terms of a surface parametrization.

**Method:** Build the unit normal from e_1 x e_2; the surface area element is |e_1 x e_2| dx^1 dx^2; the image area on the unit sphere comes from the cross product of the normal's coordinate derivatives, made signed by dotting with n; divide.

**Key insight:** Gaussian curvature is the Jacobian of the Gauss map. Dotting with n also makes it harmless to ignore the derivative of the normalization factor, since that term is along n and drops out of the triple product.

**Result:** K = n.(d_1 n x d_2 n)/|e_1 x e_2|

**Concepts:** Gauss map, Gaussian curvature, unit normal, surface basis vectors

### Example 30.8 · intro · GA §30.4 p.315 (pdf 332)

**Problem:** Compute the second fundamental form of a cylinder of radius a.

**Method:** Parametrize X = (a cos theta, a sin theta, z); find e_theta, e_z and outward unit normal; differentiate the basis vectors; only d e_theta/d theta is non-zero; project on n.

**Key insight:** Straight generators contribute nothing to K_mu nu; all bending is around the circumference.

**Result:** K_theta theta = -a, other components zero; metric g_theta theta = a^2, g_zz = 1.

**Concepts:** second fundamental form, Gauss's formula for basis-vector derivatives, surface basis vectors

### Example 30.9 · standard · GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

**Problem:** Find the extreme values of the normal curvature K_mu nu t^mu t^nu over all unit tangent directions at a point.

**Method:** Lagrange multiplier k with constraint g_mu nu t^mu t^nu = 1; differentiate with respect to t^mu; raise an index with the inverse metric to get an eigenvalue equation for A = g^{-1}K; contract with t_lambda to identify each eigenvalue with the extremal normal curvature. Margin note: for the cylinder A = diag(-1/a, 0).

**Key insight:** The metric enters because 'unit length' on a curved surface is measured by g, so the relevant matrix is g^{-1}K, not K.

**Result:** Eigenvalues k_1, k_2 of g^{lambda mu}K_mu nu are the principal curvatures; det and trace are invariants.

**Concepts:** shape operator, principal curvatures, Lagrange multiplier, normal curvature

### Example 30.10 · standard · GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

**Problem:** Compute intrinsic (det) and extrinsic (trace) curvatures for (a) the paraboloid z = u x^2/2 + v y^2/2 at its vertex and (b) a sphere of radius a.

**Method:** (a) Find e_x, e_y, metric (30.57), unit normal and second derivatives of X; at the origin g is the identity and K = diag(u, v). (b) Use spherical parametrization, basis vectors, outward normal; decompose second derivatives into tangential and normal parts to read K_11 = -a, K_22 = -a sin^2 theta; combine with g = diag(a^2, a^2 sin^2 theta).

**Key insight:** Determinant of g^{-1}K reproduces Gauss's intrinsic value; the trace depends on the normal orientation and embedding.

**Result:** (a) K = uv, trace u + v. (b) K = 1/a^2, trace -2/a. Printed slips: the origin metric is labelled g_uu, g_vv, g_uv (should be g_xx, g_yy, g_xy); in (30.64) the tangential terms carry a spurious factor a.

**Concepts:** Gaussian curvature, extrinsic curvature, shape operator, second fundamental form

### Example 30.11 · challenging · GA §30.6 p.319 (pdf 336)

**Problem:** Justify K = R_1212/g by checking it against Gauss's metric formula.

**Method:** Write 4gR_1212 = 4g^2 K; expand R_1212 using eqn 11.22; compute Christoffel symbols from the metric formula; use the explicit 2x2 inverse metric; compare term by term with (30.25). The algebra is left to the reader.

**Key insight:** Gauss's ugly formula is just the one independent Riemann component in 2D, divided by det g.

**Result:** Claimed agreement (not carried out). Printed errors in the supporting equations: (30.68) lacks 1/2 and has g_{21,1} for g_{21,2}; (30.69) has g^22 = g_22/g instead of g_11/g and a wrong sign in '= g_21/g'.

**Concepts:** Riemann tensor, Gaussian curvature, connection coefficients

### Example · intro · GA §30.6 p.318 (pdf 335)

**Problem:** Count the number of functions in a metric that carry genuine geometric information (Riemann's argument, with margin note 24).

**Method:** A symmetric n x n matrix has n diagonal entries and half of n^2 - n off-diagonal ones, total n(n+1)/2; subtract the n functions available in choosing coordinates.

**Key insight:** Coordinate freedom removes n functions; what remains must be curvature-like data.

**Result:** n(n-1)/2 functions (1 for a surface, 3 in 3D, 6 in 4D).

**Concepts:** degrees of freedom of a metric, sectional curvature


## Analogies and intuitions

### Driving a car around the bends of a road shaped like a guitar outline: the harder you turn the steering wheel at constant speed, the greater the curvature. → curvature of a curve · strong · GA §30.1 p.308 (pdf 325)

Gives a bodily sense of curvature as turning rate before introducing the circle yardstick.

**Where it breaks down:** Steering angle relates to curvature only at fixed speed and fixed wheelbase geometry; says nothing about surfaces or intrinsic curvature.

**App idea:** Car on an editable road with a steering-wheel dial that rotates in proportion to kappa.

### The osculating circle as the circle that kisses the curve (Latin osculum). → osculating circle · useful · GA §30.1 p.309 (pdf 326)

Etymology in a margin note to fix the image of second-order contact.

**Where it breaks down:** A kiss suggests touching, which a tangent line also does; the circle's special feature is matching curvature too.

### Locally every curve is a parabola and every surface is a quadratic bowl or saddle over its tangent plane. → second fundamental form · strong · GA §30.1 p.310 (pdf 327)

Margin note 5 notes the coordinate method really compares with a parabola; the idea is carried to z = K_ij x^i x^j/2.

**Where it breaks down:** Second-order only; higher terms matter for how curvature varies from point to point.

**App idea:** Zoom slider on a surface patch that progressively overlays the best-fit quadric until they are indistinguishable.

### Ellipse with major and minor axes as the 2D generalization of the circle used to gauge curvature. → principal curvatures · weak · GA §30.3 p.312 (pdf 329)

Motivates two curvature numbers in perpendicular directions via Euler's result.

**Where it breaks down:** The level curve of the quadratic form is an ellipse only when both curvatures share a sign; saddles give hyperbolas; its semi-axes scale as rho_i^{1/2}, not as the curvatures themselves.

**App idea:** Dupin indicatrix drawn on the tangent plane that morphs from circle to ellipse to parabola pair to hyperbola as curvature parameters change.

### Beings confined to a surface who cannot jump out to look at its shape. → intrinsic curvature · strong · GA §30.3 p.313 (pdf 330)

Frames Gauss's goal of an insider-measurable curvature and Riemann's extension to our own space.

**Where it breaks down:** Leaves open how inhabitants actually measure it; the book supplies formulas rather than insider experiments (exercises add loops and circles).

**App idea:** First-person ant view on a hidden surface: the learner pays out string to measure circle circumferences and infers K before the surface is revealed.

### Unit tangents on a circle for curves become unit normals painting a patch on a sphere for surfaces. → Gauss map · strong · GA §30.4 p.314 (pdf 331)

Defines K as swept solid angle per area by direct analogy with swept angle per length.

**Where it breaks down:** Needs an embedding to define normals, so it does not by itself show intrinsicness.

**App idea:** Brush a region on a surface and watch its normal image fill in on a companion sphere, with area ratio readout and orientation reversal on saddles.

### A tin can wrapped in a flat grid sheet: it looks curved but the paper lies flat once unwrapped, whereas paper cannot cover a sphere without cutting. → extrinsic curvature · strong · GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

Separates bending visible from outside from curvature inherent to the surface (Fig. 30.8).

**Where it breaks down:** Suggests extrinsic curvature is illusory; it is physically real (e.g. for embedded hypersurfaces in GR) just not detectable from within.

**App idea:** Morph flat sheet into cylinder while tracking a drawn triangle's angles and edge lengths, then attempt a sphere.

### Shoelaces, however tangled, can be pulled straight. → intrinsic curvature · strong · GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

Explains why a one-dimensional curve has only extrinsic curvature.

**Where it breaks down:** Pulling straight preserves length, which is the point; a learner may wrongly generalize that any surface can be smoothed out likewise.


## Misconceptions addressed

### A curve has intrinsic curvature just as a surface does. · GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

**Correction:** A 1D space can always be laid out straight without stretching; its curvature is purely extrinsic. Intrinsic curvature needs at least two dimensions.

**Why tempting:** The chapter spends two sections on curvature of lines, and it is the same word.

### A cylinder is a curved surface in the same sense as a sphere. · GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

**Correction:** Its Gaussian curvature is zero (k1 = -1/a, k2 = 0); only its extrinsic curvature is non-zero.

**Why tempting:** It visibly bends when seen from outside.

### A surface's curvature can be captured by one number or by one comparison sphere. · GA §30.3 p.312 (pdf 329)

**Correction:** Bending generally differs by direction; two principal curvatures in perpendicular directions are needed, from which K and the trace are formed.

**Why tempting:** The curve story needed only one circle.

### Metric components that vary with position mean the space is curved. · GA §30.3 p.313 (pdf 330)

**Correction:** The g_ij depend on the coordinates chosen; only coordinate-independent combinations such as K or the Riemann tensor signal curvature (e.g. polar coordinates on a flat plane).

**Why tempting:** Curved-space examples always come with non-constant metrics.

### The eigenvalues of K_mu nu itself are the principal curvatures. · GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

**Correction:** Unless coordinates are orthonormal at the point, the relevant matrix is g^{-1}K; e.g. for the cylinder K_theta theta = -a but the curvature is -1/a.

**Why tempting:** In Example 30.5 tangent-plane coordinates are orthonormal, so K alone worked there.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Extrinsic curvature is not physical, so it is irrelevant for general relativity. · GA p.321 (pdf 338)

**Correction:** Spacetime itself is not embedded, but surfaces and hypersurfaces within spacetime (spatial slices, horizons) have extrinsic curvature that enters the Gauss-Codazzi equations and 3+1 formulations, as Exercises 30.7-30.8 preview.

**Why tempting:** The chapter emphasizes that inhabitants cannot see the embedding and that intrinsic curvature is what matters for GR.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### Torsion of a curve is the same torsion as in torsion-free connections. · GA §30.2 p.311 (pdf 328)

**Correction:** Curve torsion measures twisting of the Frenet frame of one curve in flat space; connection torsion is an antisymmetric part of the connection, a different object.

**Why tempting:** Same word, both about twisting, and GA later assumes torsion-free connections.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*

### The sign of principal curvatures of a sphere is intrinsically negative (as Example 30.10 gives -1/a). · GA §30.5 p.318 (pdf 335)

**Correction:** The signs of K_mu nu and k_i flip with the choice of normal direction; their product K = k1 k2 does not.

**Why tempting:** The examples present signed values without comment.

*Inferred: the book guards against this implicitly.*


## Thought experiments

### Flatlanders measuring their own curvature · GA §30.3 p.313 (pdf 330)

**Setup:** Two-dimensional beings confined to a surface can measure lengths and angles but cannot leave the surface to view it from outside.

**Lesson:** A useful notion of curvature for physics must be computable from internal measurements, which Gauss achieved for surfaces and Riemann for any dimension; we are in the same position with respect to spacetime.

**App idea:** Hidden-surface game: learner measures circle circumferences or triangle angle sums from inside and must guess sphere, plane, cylinder or saddle.

### Unwrapping a tin can versus wrapping a ball · GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

**Setup:** Peel the label off a cylinder and lay it flat; then try to cover a sphere with flat paper.

**Lesson:** Surfaces that unroll have no intrinsic curvature; a sphere forces cutting or stretching, revealing intrinsic curvature.

**App idea:** Physics-based cloth sheet that can wrap a cylinder cleanly but wrinkles on a sphere, with a stretch heat map.

### Parallel transport around a loop on a sphere · GA p.320 (pdf 337)

**Setup:** Carry a vector around a closed loop on a 2-sphere and compare its final direction with the initial one (Exercise 30.2).

**Lesson:** The rotation angle divided by enclosed area equals the Gaussian curvature 1/a^2, an internal measurement.

**App idea:** Drag a loop on a sphere; an arrow is parallel-transported around it; angle vs area plotted to show a straight line of slope 1/a^2.


## Applications and observations

- **Numerical checks of curvature for standard shapes** (numerical-estimate, GA §30.5 p.318 (pdf 335)): Sphere radius a: principal curvatures magnitude 1/a, K = 1/a^2, trace magnitude 2/a. Cylinder radius a: k = -1/a and 0, K = 0. Paraboloid vertex: K = uv, trace u + v. Our own illustration: for the Earth (a about 6371 km), K is about 2.5e-14 per square metre, so a geodesic triangle of area 1e6 km^2 has angle excess about 0.025 rad (1.4 degrees). Key numbers: K_sphere = 1/a^2; Earth K ~ 2.5e-14 m^-2 (our estimate)
- **Hypersurfaces in spacetime and 3+1 splitting** (technology, GA p.321 (pdf 338)): Exercises 30.7-30.8 build projection operators, induced metric, induced covariant derivative and extrinsic curvature tensor for a spacelike slice, ending with the Gauss and Codazzi equations. These underpin the constraint equations of numerical relativity (not discussed in the chapter; context added by us).

## Historical notes

- **Jean Frederic Frenet, Joseph Alfred Serret (1847-1852):** The moving-frame equations appear in Frenet's 1847 thesis (abstract published 1852) and were published independently by Serret in 1851. — Explains the dual naming Frenet-Serret or Serret-Frenet. (GA §30.2 p.311 (pdf 328))
- **Leonhard Euler:** Euler showed that the directions of maximal and minimal normal curvature of a surface are perpendicular. — Reduces surface curvature at a point to two numbers; a margin note records Gauss's high praise of Euler's works. (GA §30.3 p.312 (pdf 329))
- **Carl Friedrich Gauss (1827):** Disquisitiones generales circa superficies curvas establishes intrinsic Gaussian curvature and the theorema egregium. — First proof that curvature can be measured from within a surface. (GA §30.3 p.313 (pdf 330))
- **Bernhard Riemann (1854):** Habilitation lecture at Gottingen setting out a programme for curvature of n-dimensional spaces with minimal mathematics. The book dates it 1853 (the year the lecture topic was set); it was delivered in June 1854, as GA ch11 itself states. — Conceptual foundation for the geometry of general relativity. (GA §30.6 p.318 (pdf 335))
- **Bernhard Riemann (1861):** Paris Academy prize essay on heat conduction contains the first explicit curvature tensor in terms of the metric and the flatness criterion; Riemann, in poor health, did not win for lack of detail, and the prize was withdrawn in 1868. — Origin of the Riemann tensor. (GA §30.6 p.319 (pdf 336))

## Notation and conventions

- **Bold 3-vectors:** Bold letters denote vectors in flat three-dimensional embedding space; described as a temporary measure for this chapter. (GA §30.2 p.310 (pdf 327))
- **Dot for arc-length derivative:** From Example 30.3 on, an overdot means d/ds. (GA §30.2 p.311 (pdf 328))
- **Frenet triad letters:** t tangent, p principal normal, b binormal (many texts use T, N, B). (GA §30.2 p.310 (pdf 327))
- **K overloaded:** K_ij or K_mu nu is the second fundamental form (curvature tensor of the height quadric); unsubscripted K is Gaussian curvature; in Exercise 30.8 K_alpha beta is the spacetime extrinsic curvature tensor. — Easy to confuse with the trace K = K^mu_mu common in GR literature. (GA §30.4 p.315 (pdf 332))
- **Underlined matrices:** Underlined bold letters (A, K, g) denote matrices of components; A = g^{-1}K. — In margin note 20 the matrix labelled g holds inverse-metric components g^{lambda mu}, while elsewhere det g is the determinant of the metric itself. (GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334))
- **Comma derivatives:** g_{12,12} means the second partial derivative of g_12 with respect to x^1 and x^2. (GA §30.3 p.314 (pdf 331))
- **Normal orientation:** Unit normal n = e_1 x e_2/|e_1 x e_2|; for the cylinder and sphere this is outward, giving negative K_mu nu and principal curvatures; for the paraboloid it points up into the bowl giving positive values. — Sign of the trace depends on this choice; K does not. (GA §30.4 p.315 (pdf 332))
- **Extrinsic curvature name:** The book calls the trace k1 + k2 the extrinsic curvature (standard: twice the mean curvature). (GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334))
- **Riemann tensor to sectional curvature:** 3Q(X,Y) = -R(X,Y,X,Y) with R the (0,4) tensor; K = R_1212/g in 2D, consistent with GA's MTW-type sign convention (R_{theta phi theta phi} = a^2 sin^2 theta for a sphere). (GA §30.6 p.319 (pdf 336))
- **Signature in hypersurface exercises:** P^mu_nu = delta^mu_nu + n^mu n_nu for a timelike unit normal presumes n.n = -1, i.e. the book's (-,+,+,+) signature. (GA p.321 (pdf 338))

## Teaching gems

### One ratio, two dimensions: curvature of a curve is angle swept by unit tangents per length; Gaussian curvature is solid angle swept by unit normals per area. · GA §30.4 p.314 (pdf 331)

**Why it works:** Learners reuse a picture they already understand, and the definition is visual and computable.

**App idea:** Split screen: curve with tangent circle on the left, surface with normal sphere on the right; brushing a segment or patch paints its image and shows the ratio live.

### Local shape as a Taylor expansion over the tangent line or plane, with curvature read off as the quadratic coefficient. · GA §30.3 p.312 (pdf 329)

**Why it works:** Turns an abstract notion into 'the second derivative', and makes principal curvatures an eigenvalue problem students have met.

**App idea:** Sliders for a, b, c of a quadric patch with its principal axes and Dupin indicatrix updating; saddle regime highlighted.

### Split derivatives of basis vectors into a part inside the surface and a part sticking out. · GA §30.4 p.315 (pdf 332)

**Why it works:** The tangential part previews the connection and the normal part defines extrinsic curvature; the same decomposition returns in hypersurface GR.

**App idea:** Move along a coordinate line on a sphere; the change of e_theta is drawn as an arrow decomposed into coloured tangential (Gamma) and normal (K) components.

### Tin can versus ball: a grid sheet wraps a cylinder and lies flat again, but cannot cover a sphere. · GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

**Why it works:** A kitchen-table demonstration that separates looking curved from being curved.

**App idea:** Roll-and-unroll animation with geodesic triangle angle sums and a wrinkle/stretch map on the sphere attempt.

### Determinant versus trace of g^{-1}K as the intrinsic versus extrinsic invariants, checked on a cylinder (0 and -1/a). · GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

**Why it works:** Compact algebraic criterion that matches the physical intuition; the cylinder is a perfect counterexample to 'looks curved'.

**App idea:** Surface gallery (plane, cylinder, cone, sphere, saddle, torus) with a probe showing k1, k2, product and sum; bend a sheet and watch the sum change while the product stays fixed.

### Showing Gauss's full metric formula to dramatize how hard intrinsic curvature is by brute force. · GA §30.3 p.314 (pdf 331)

**Why it works:** Motivates later efficient machinery (Riemann tensor, Cartan) and makes the theorema egregium feel genuinely remarkable.

**App idea:** Symbolic calculator: learner enters a 2D metric; the app evaluates K by Gauss's formula, by R_1212/g and by Brioschi form, showing they agree.

### Frenet-Serret as an antisymmetric matrix acting on a moving frame. · GA §30.2 p.311 (pdf 328)

**Why it works:** Antisymmetry follows from orthonormality and prepares for rotation generators and connection one-forms in Cartan's method.

**App idea:** Draw kappa(s) and tau(s) as graphs; the app integrates the frame to build the 3D curve in real time.

### Shoelaces can always be pulled straight, so curves have no intrinsic curvature. · GA §30.5 p.317 (pdf 334)

**Why it works:** Instantly explains why intrinsic curvature needs two or more dimensions.

**App idea:** A tangled 3D string that straightens while preserving its length, with tick marks staying equally spaced.


## Gaps and pitfalls

- **Section 30.1 first says the upper bend of the guitar is more curved (consistent with Fig. 30.1, where rho_1 at the top is small), then states the bottom feature has the smaller osculating radius.** — Contradiction on the very first example may shake confidence in kappa = 1/rho. *Suggestion:* State clearly: the tight top bend has smaller radius and larger curvature.
- **Sphere example in 30.3: height printed with a^2 + x^2 + y^2 under the root and expansion coefficient 1/(2a^2); correct is z = a - (a^2 - x^2 - y^2)^{1/2} which is approximately (x^2 + y^2)/(2a).** — The printed coefficient would give curvature 1/a^2, contradicting the stated 1/a. *Suggestion:* Use the corrected expansion and point out dimensional analysis (z must have units of length).
- **The ellipse description of principal curvatures is loose: the book says eigenvalues give the ellipse axes and inverse eigenvalues the curvatures, contradicting eqn 30.22 where eigenvalues are the curvatures; for saddles the level set is a hyperbola.** — Confusion about whether K_ij eigenvalues are curvatures or radii. *Suggestion:* Say eigenvalues are principal curvatures; the level set K_ij x^i x^j = 1 (Dupin indicatrix) has semi-axes 1/sqrt(kappa_i).
- **Eqn 30.25 as printed drops a minus sign inside the second factor of the product in the g_12 bracket: that factor should be the difference 2 g_{12,2} minus g_{22,1}, but it is typeset as a product of the two terms.** — Anyone checking a metric with off-diagonal terms gets a wrong K. *Suggestion:* Use the corrected formula in the dossier, or better, compute K via R_1212/g or Brioschi's formula.
- **The central claim that det(g^{-1}K) is the intrinsic Gaussian curvature is introduced with 'it turns out'; the chain from Gauss-map ratio to det K/det g (Exercise 30.5) and from there to the metric formula (theorema egregium proper) is not shown.** — The key conceptual surprise, that an extrinsically defined product is intrinsic, is accepted on authority. *Suggestion:* Show the Gauss equation R_1212 = K_11 K_22 - K_12^2 for a surface (a 2D instance of eqn 30.89) using Gauss's formula for basis derivatives and commuting partial derivatives, which makes intrinsicness evident.
- **Example 30.4 prints 'b.b = 0' where b.(db/ds) = 0 is meant; the text also says b measures the rate of change of the osculating plane when db/ds (i.e. tau) does.** — Momentary confusion over a contradiction with b.b = 1. *Suggestion:* Write the derivative explicitly and attribute twisting to tau.
- **Errors in worked details: eqn 30.64 tangential terms carry a spurious factor a (should be cot theta e_phi and -sin theta cos theta e_theta); Example 30.10 labels metric components g_uu, g_vv with the parameter names; eqn 30.68 misses 1/2 and has g_{21,1} for g_{21,2}; eqn 30.69 gives g^22 = g_22/g (should be g_11/g) and appends a wrong-sign '= g_21/g'.** — Readers attempting the 'try it and see' verification in Example 30.11 will fail. *Suggestion:* Provide corrected formulas and a worked verification for a diagonal metric such as the sphere.
- **Margin note 15 tells readers not to differentiate the normalization of n without saying why that is legitimate.** — Looks like an unjustified shortcut; applied elsewhere it would be wrong. *Suggestion:* Explain that the extra term is along n and drops out of both n.(d_1 n x d_2 n) and e_mu . d_nu n.
- **kappa_g is introduced but never named or connected to geodesics; Euler's cos^2 formula and Meusnier's theorem are omitted.** — A natural bridge to GA's geodesic equation (ch8-9) is lost. *Suggestion:* Name geodesic curvature and note geodesics are curves with kappa_g = 0, i.e. their acceleration is purely normal to the surface.
- **Terminology: calling the trace k1 + k2 'the extrinsic curvature' conflicts with standard usage (twice mean curvature; in GR 'extrinsic curvature' is the tensor K_ij with trace K). The symbol K is used for three things.** — Confusion when moving to ADM or other GR texts. *Suggestion:* Introduce mean curvature H = (k1 + k2)/2 and keep 'extrinsic curvature' for the tensor.
- **Riemann's counting of n(n-1)/2 functions and the claim that curvature on that many planes fixes the metric are presented without caveat, and the lecture is dated 1853 (delivered 1854).** — Learners may conclude the Riemann tensor has n(n-1)/2 independent components rather than n^2(n^2-1)/12. *Suggestion:* Present counting as a heuristic about functions; tabulate component counts 1, 6, 20 for n = 2, 3, 4.
- **Exercise 30.3 misspells Bertrand; eqn 30.32 writes partial symbols for differentials; F_1 and F_2 use dX^mu for coordinate differentials.** — Minor, but may hamper searching for the theorem. *Suggestion:* Correct spelling and notation in derived materials.
- **Index hygiene slips in section 30.5: the opening sentence writes K_mu nu with de_mu/dx^mu (a repeated index where e_nu is meant), and eqn 30.48 differentiates with respect to t^mu while mu is also a summed dummy index inside the bracket.** — Readers who are still learning the summation convention may think a repeated index can also be free, and then find the Lagrange-multiplier step (30.48 to 30.49) puzzling, including where the factor of 2 goes. *Suggestion:* Rename the free index, e.g. differentiate with respect to t^sigma, and show that d(K_mu nu t^mu t^nu)/dt^sigma = 2 K_sigma nu t^nu, using the symmetry of K. The common factor 2 then cancels against the constraint term.
- **The book calls K_mu nu 'symmetric' without saying why, and in 30.3 it twice says 'curvature of a plane' when it means a two-dimensional surface.** — Symmetry is what makes the principal directions perpendicular (Euler) and the eigenvalues real, so leaving it unexplained hides why the construction works; the 'plane' wording can make learners think a flat plane is being discussed. *Suggestion:* Point out that de_nu/dx^mu is the mixed second derivative of X, which is symmetric in mu and nu, so its normal part is too. Say 'surface' throughout.

## Tutor notes

- Open with: 'Which is more curved, a small circle or a large one? How would an ant living on a ball know it is not on a table?' The first targets kappa = 1/rho; the second sets up intrinsic curvature.
- Order for novices: turning rate of tangents, osculating circle, parabola picture; then surfaces as quadratic bowls or saddles with two principal curvatures; then the cylinder paradox; then Gauss's insider idea. Frenet-Serret can be skipped by learners heading straight to GR.
- For stronger learners emphasize eqn 30.36: the tangential part is the connection, the normal part is K_mu nu. This is the bridge to covariant derivatives and to hypersurface GR.
- Check for understanding: ask for k1, k2, K and k1 + k2 of a cylinder of radius 2 (expect -1/2 or +1/2 depending on normal, 0, 0, magnitude 1/2), and why K vanishes.
- Check: 'A saddle has principal curvatures +1 and -1. Is it intrinsically curved? What sign?' (Yes, K = -1.)
- Ask: 'Why does the metric appear when extremizing normal curvature?' Target answer: unit length of the tangent is measured by g, so the eigenproblem is for g^{-1}K.
- Common learner question: 'If the cylinder is flat, why does it look curved?' Answer with angle sums of triangles and unrolling; extrinsic curvature is about the embedding.
- Common learner question: 'Does spacetime need a higher-dimensional space to curve into?' No; the whole point of Gauss and Riemann is that curvature is defined internally. Embeddings are visual aids only.
- Warn about printed errors before learners attempt checks: sphere height and expansion (p.313), eqn 30.25 minus sign, eqn 30.64 factors of a, eqns 30.68-30.69.
- When relating to GA ch11, connect K = R_1212/g with the sphere: R_{theta phi theta phi} = a^2 sin^2 theta, g = a^4 sin^2 theta, K = 1/a^2.
- Signal the naming clash between curve torsion here and connection torsion later, and between the book's 'extrinsic curvature' (trace) and the tensor K_ij in GR.
- If a learner asks why K_mu nu is symmetric or why the principal directions are perpendicular: de_nu/dx^mu is the mixed second derivative of X, symmetric in mu and nu, so its normal projection K_mu nu is symmetric. A matrix that is symmetric with respect to the metric has real eigenvalues and g-orthogonal eigenvectors, which is Euler's perpendicularity.
