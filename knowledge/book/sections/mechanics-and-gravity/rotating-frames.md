# Rotating frames and fictitious forces

Teaches: `inertial-force`, `centrifugal-and-coriolis-forces`, `driven-damped-oscillator`, `validity-limits-of-newtonian-mechanics`

Builds on: `units-and-dimensions`, `newtons-laws-and-inertial-frames`

**Newton's laws hold only in the frames where a body left alone keeps going straight. In any other frame a rider has to add extra forces, and each one grows in step with the body's mass. A turning floor brings an outward push, and a sideways push on anything moving across it. These added forces are bookkeeping for the frame, yet they shake washing machines and steer storms.**

Go to a playground roundabout, the flat disc that children spin and ride. Set it turning and hold a phone over the middle, filming straight down. Tape a second phone to the floor of the roundabout, so that it turns with it. Now kneel by the middle and roll a tennis ball outward, aimed at a red post on the rim. The floor is slick, like polished metal, so once the ball has left your hand nothing drags it round with the floor.

The first film shows the ball crossing in a straight line at a steady speed, as a body left alone should. The second film shows the same ball curving away from the post, as though a hand kept pushing it sideways. Nothing touched it. *Newton's laws and inertial frames* said that the second law holds in inertial frames; a roundabout is not one. This section works out what a rider on it has to add to keep Newton's laws, and how far those laws can be trusted at all.

## The force a braking bus needs

A city bus is running at 36 kilometres an hour, which is 10 metres every second. The driver brakes smoothly and the bus stops in 4 seconds. So its speed drops by 2 and a half metres a second during each second of the braking.

Inside the bus a backpack of 12 kilograms sits on a shelf. The shelf is so smooth that the friction under the backpack can be ignored. Seen from the pavement, nothing pushes the backpack along the bus. So it keeps its speed of 10 metres a second in a straight line, while the bus slows under it. The backpack slides toward the front.

To a rider sitting in the bus the same thing looks different. The backpack starts at rest, then picks up speed toward the front. A rider who wants to keep Newton's second law must name a force that does that.

The force has to be the backpack's mass times the drop in speed each second: 12 kilograms times 2 and a half, which is 30 newtons. That is about the weight of a 3 kilogram bag. It points toward the front, the opposite way to the bus's change of motion.

A force that a rider has to add for Newton's second law to hold in their own frame is called an inertial force. It is also called a fictitious force, although nothing about its effects is fictitious.

A frame in which a body left alone does not keep a steady speed in a straight line is called a non-inertial frame.

Two marks give an inertial force away. No second body feels an equal push back from it. And its size is always the body's mass times an acceleration. That acceleration is set by the frame, never by the body: in the bus it is the 2 and a half metres a second the bus loses each second. So every body in the frame, heavy or light, picks up the same extra acceleration.

*An inertial force is what a rider in a non-inertial frame must add to keep Newton's second law. No second body feels a push back from it, and its size is the body's mass times an acceleration set by the frame rather than by the body.*

## Sitting still on a turning floor

Back to the roundabout. Take one 3 metres across, so the rim is 1 and a half metres from the middle, turning steadily once every 6 seconds. A child of 30 kilograms sits on the rim and holds the handrail.

Stand on the grass and watch. The child is not going straight: the rim carries her round a circle. Something must pull her toward the middle, and the handrail does it. School mechanics gives that centre-seeking pull as her mass times her speed squared, divided by her distance from the middle. Take that rule on trust here.

The rim is about 9 and 4 tenths metres once round, so her speed is about 1 and 6 tenths metres a second. The pull works out at about 50 newtons, the weight of a 5 kilogram bag. That is about one sixth of the downward pull of gravity on her.

Now ask the child. In her frame she is not moving at all. The handrail still pulls her inward, so to keep Newton's second law she must add an outward force of the same 50 newtons. That added force is called the centrifugal force.

Its size follows the same rule. The speed in that rule is always the speed at which the floor carries round the spot the body is at, measured by someone standing on the grass. A spot twice as far from the middle is carried twice as fast, and the rule then gives a force twice as big. Turning twice as fast doubles the speed at every spot, which makes the force four times as strong.

One more force appears if the turning rate itself changes. While the roundabout is speeding up, a rider sitting still on it is thrown backwards, against the way the floor carries her. A passenger in a bus pulling away meets the same thing: the seat pushes them forward, and in the bus's frame they must add a backward force of the same size. That added force is called the Euler force, and it lasts only while the turning rate is changing.

*A rider sitting still on a turning floor must add an outward centrifugal force: mass times speed squared, divided by distance from the middle. A changing turning rate adds a further Euler force.*

## The sideways push when you move

Kneel by the middle of that same slick roundabout and roll a tennis ball outward at 2 metres a second, aimed at a red post on the rim.

Stand on the grass again. The ball goes straight. It has 1 and a half metres to cover at 2 metres a second, so it reaches the rim three quarters of a second later.

One full turn takes 6 seconds, and three quarters of a second is one eighth of 6 seconds. So in that time the roundabout turns one eighth of a full turn, which is 45 degrees. The post has moved on by that much, and the ball arrives where the post used to be. So in the roundabout's own markings the ball missed its target by an eighth of a turn.

The share of a turn missed is the distance out to the rim, divided by the ball's speed and then by the time for one full turn. Here that is 1 and a half metres, divided by 2 metres a second and then by 6 seconds, which gives an eighth. It grows when the ball is slow and when the roundabout is fast.

To the rider, nothing straight happened, so she has two forces to add. The outward centrifugal force is one of them. It is there for the ball just as it is for anything else out on the turning floor. The other is new. The ball curved sideways, so she must add a force at a right angle to the way the ball is moving in her frame. That added force is called the Coriolis force.

Which way does it push? Seen from above, let the roundabout turn anticlockwise. Then the ball veers to the right of the way it is going. Take right and left as the rider takes them, standing on the floor with her head up and facing the way the ball goes. Roll a ball inward instead, from the rim toward the middle, and it still veers to the right. That is the rule for any motion along the floor: on a floor turning anticlockwise seen from above, the push is to the right of the way the thing is going.

The Coriolis force differs from the other two in one way. It acts only on things moving in the rider's frame, and the faster they move, the harder it pushes. Something parked on the floor gets nothing from it.

*Anything moving across a turning floor needs an added Coriolis force at a right angle to its motion. When the floor turns anticlockwise seen from above, the push is to the right of the way the thing is going.*

## The Earth is a very slow roundabout

The ground under your feet is the floor of a roundabout too. The Earth turns once a day, which is very slow, and that is why nothing like the tennis ball happens on a walk to the shops.

Put a number on it. Take a car on a straight road at 100 kilometres an hour, halfway between the equator and a pole. The sideways Coriolis force a rider in the car must add is about one three-thousandth of the car's weight, whichever way the road runs. The driver never notices, because a breath of wind asks for more steering than that.

Water swirling out of a bath is pushed even less: about one millionth of its weight. Whichever way your bath drains, it was settled by how the water was left moving when the tap went off, and by the shape of the tub and the drain. The story that baths drain one way in the north and the other way in the south is wrong at that size.

Give the push time and room, though, and it wins. A weather system is hundreds of kilometres across and lasts for days. Over days, a push that small still builds a large sideways drift in the moving air. That is why the winds of a storm wrap into a spiral instead of running straight at its middle.

*The Earth turns so slowly that its sideways push is about one three-thousandth of a car's weight and about one millionth of a bath's. It shows itself only where motion has days and hundreds of kilometres to build up.*

## Why a washing machine shudders

Washing never settles evenly in a washing machine's drum. Say a wet lump of half a kilogram ends up on one side. That lump is going round in a circle, so the drum must pull it toward the axis. By Newton's third law the lump pulls the drum outward, and that outward pull points wherever the lump happens to be. So it swings right round once for every turn of the drum.

The drum sits inside a tub, and the tub hangs on springs inside the machine's case. That outward pull therefore shakes the tub. Measured along any one direction, the pull reverses over and over, at exactly the rate the drum turns.

The tub on its springs has a rate of its own. Press it down by hand, let go, and it bounces a couple of times a second before settling. That is called its natural rate.

The free bouncing dies away because the rubber feet and the joints turn the movement into heat. Whatever makes a free bounce die away like that is called damping.

Now start the spin. The drum climbs from rest to 20 turns a second. On the way up it passes 2 turns a second, which is the tub's natural rate, and right there the machine shudders and walks across the floor. Above that rate it goes quiet again, although the outward pull from the lump is a hundred times bigger at full speed.

The reason is timing. Near the natural rate, each push arrives just as the tub is already moving that way, so every push adds to the last. A push that repeats at the natural rate therefore builds a far bigger swing than the same push at any other rate. That holds so long as the damping is light: a free bounce must swing back and forth several times before it dies. That build-up is called resonance.

Two things are worth keeping. Once the spin is steady, the tub shakes at the drum's rate, not at its own natural rate. And the damping sets how bad the peak gets: rubber feet that kill a free bounce in a second or two keep it mild.

*An out-of-balance drum pushes the tub at the rate it spins. The shaking peaks when that rate matches the tub's own bounce rate, however small the push is there, and the damping sets how bad the peak gets.*

## How far Newton's laws can be trusted

Every added force in this section was bookkeeping. Step back into a frame in which a body left alone keeps going straight, and all of them vanish at once, leaving only pushes that come in pairs. That raises a fair question. How far can Newton's laws be trusted before something more than bookkeeping is needed?

A theory is trusted in the range of conditions where its predictions have been checked against measurement and found to agree, and no further. That range is called the theory's range of validity.

Newton's mechanics has been checked over an enormous range, and inside it the agreement is superb. Take a jet at 900 kilometres an hour. The effect of that speed on its clock is so small that it would take about 90 thousand years to put the clock one second behind the clocks on the ground. No bridge and no gearbox needs a correction that small.

Push past that range and the small differences start to matter. The satellites your phone uses to find where it is carry clocks that must be corrected for both their speed and their height. Leave the correction out, and the positions they report drift by about 10 kilometres a day. Mercury's closest point to the Sun creeps round by about 43 arcseconds a century more than Newton's gravity allows.

So relativity does not show that Newton was wrong. It shows where to stop trusting him, and inside the old range it hands back the old answers.

One piece of unfinished business is left. Newton's laws pick out the frames they hold in, but never say what picks those frames out. *Newton's laws and inertial frames* left that open with the spinning bucket, and the centrifugal force gives the question teeth. The child on the rim can tell that she is turning without looking at anything outside. Turning with respect to what?

*A theory is trusted only in the range where it has been checked, and Newton's mechanics is superb for slow motion and weak gravity. Relativity does not refute it; it marks where the range ends.*

## Key equations

**Inertial force in a frame with a straight-line acceleration** (derived-here)

$$\mathbf{F}_{\text{inertial}} = -m\,\mathbf{a}_{\text{frame}}$$

A rider whose frame accelerates in a straight line must add this force to every body of mass $m$. The minus sign means it points the opposite way to the frame's own acceleration, which is why a braking bus throws things toward the front.

- $\mathbf{F}_{\text{inertial}}$: the force the rider has to add
- $m$: the mass of the body
- $\mathbf{a}_{\text{frame}}$: the acceleration of the rider's frame, measured from an inertial frame

Say: The inertial force equals minus the body's mass times the frame's own acceleration.

**Centre-seeking pull on a body going round a circle** (stated)

$$F = \frac{m v^2}{r}$$

School mechanics gives this as the pull toward the middle that keeps a body of mass $m$ moving round a circle of radius $r$ at speed $v$, with the speed measured by someone at rest beside the circle. The centrifugal force a rider on the turning floor adds to any body at that distance has the same size and points outward, with $v$ the speed at which the floor carries that spot round. Taken on trust here.

- $F$: size of the pull toward the middle, and of the centrifugal force the rider adds
- $m$: the mass of the body
- $v$: the body's speed round the circle, measured from the ground
- $r$: the body's distance from the middle

Say: The force equals the mass times the speed squared, divided by the distance from the middle.

Why is the friend wrong about baths and right about storms?

Answer: The size of the push is not the whole story. What matters is how long it acts and how far the water travels while it acts. A bath empties in a minute or two, over about a metre. ## Misconceptions

- **coriolis-really-pushes**: "The Coriolis force is a real sideways push, so a camera fixed above a roundabout would film the ball curving." — The fixed camera films a straight line at a steady speed. The curve shows up only in the markings of the turning floor, which is why a rider has to add the force. (diagnosed by ball-across-a-bigger-roundabout)
- **centrifugal-throws-the-water-out**: "A spin dryer works by flinging the water outward with the centrifugal force." — The drum wall pushes the washing inward, which is what keeps it going round. Water that reaches a hole stops being pushed inward and then simply goes straight, which takes it out. (diagnosed by sock-in-a-spin-dryer)
- **earth-decides-the-bath**: "The Earth's turning decides which way a bath or a sink drains." — The push on bath water is about one millionth of its weight, far too small over a minute and a metre. Leftover swirl from filling and the shape of the tub decide it, while a storm is big and slow enough for the push to win. (diagnosed by does-the-bath-know-the-earth-turns)
- **biggest-push-biggest-shake**: "A machine shakes worst when the push on it is biggest, so the worst moment is top spin speed." — The shaking peaks where the push repeats at the tub's own bounce rate, well below top speed. The push is far smaller there than at full spin, and the damping sets how bad the peak gets. (diagnosed by at-which-spin-rate-does-it-shake)
- **relativity-refuted-newton**: "Relativity showed that Newton's mechanics is wrong, so it should not be used." — Newton's mechanics matches measurement superbly for slow motion and weak gravity, and relativity hands back its answers there. Relativity marks where that range ends rather than deleting what lies inside it. (diagnosed by was-newton-refuted)

## Glossary

- **non-inertial frame**: A frame in which a body left alone does not keep a steady speed in a straight line, such as a braking bus or a turning roundabout. (`inertial-force`)
- **inertial force**: The extra force a rider in a non-inertial frame must add for Newton's second law to hold there. It is also called a fictitious force. (`inertial-force`)
- **centrifugal force**: The outward force a rider on a turning floor must add for every body on it. Its size is the body's mass times the square of the speed at which the floor carries round the spot the body is at, divided by the spot's distance from the middle. That speed is measured by someone standing on the ground. (`centrifugal-and-coriolis-forces`)
- **Coriolis force**: The sideways force a rider on a turning floor must add to anything moving across it, at a right angle to the way it is moving in her frame. (`centrifugal-and-coriolis-forces`)
- **Euler force**: The force a rider on a turning floor must add while the turning rate is changing. It points against the way the floor carries her when the floor is speeding up. (`centrifugal-and-coriolis-forces`)
- **natural rate**: The number of times a second something bounces or swings on its own after a single push, before it settles. (`driven-damped-oscillator`)
- **damping**: Whatever turns the movement of a bouncing or swinging thing into heat, so that a free bounce dies away. (`driven-damped-oscillator`)
- **resonance**: The large swing that builds up when a push repeats at the natural rate of the thing it is pushing. (`driven-damped-oscillator`)
- **range of validity**: The range of conditions in which a theory's predictions have been checked against measurement and found to agree. (`validity-limits-of-newtonian-mechanics`)

## Tutor

Opening question: Picture a playground roundabout three metres across, turning steadily, once every six seconds. You kneel by the middle and roll a tennis ball outward, aimed at a red post on the rim. A friend on the grass films the whole thing from a step ladder, and a second camera is taped to the floor of the roundabout. What does each film show, and why are the two different?

- Q: Is the centrifugal force real or not? A: It depends on which frame you are keeping the books in. Stand on the grass and there is no outward force at all. The handrail pulls the rider inward, and that is the only sideways force in the story. Sit on the roundabout and you have to add an outward force of the same size, or Newton's second law will not balance. The effects are real either way. What depends on the frame is whether you write the force down.
- Q: Why does the Coriolis force not act on someone sitting still on the roundabout? A: Because it acts only on things that are moving in the rider's own frame, and the faster they move, the harder it pushes. Someone sitting still is not moving in that frame, so it gives her nothing. What she does get is the outward centrifugal force, which is there whether she moves or not.
