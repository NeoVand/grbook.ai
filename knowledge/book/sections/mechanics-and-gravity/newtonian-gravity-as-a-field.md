**Every pair of masses pulls along the line joining them, weaker as the square of the distance. The pull is fantastically weak, yet nothing cancels it, so it rules the sky. Divide the pull by the mass you hang there and gravity becomes a map: an arrow and a depth at every point. The mass inside any closed surface fixes what crosses it, and a falling body feels only the differences of pull across itself.**

A light rod hangs from a thin thread, with a small lead ball at each end. Two heavy lead balls are wheeled up close, one to each end of the rod. Nothing seems to happen. But a mirror glued to the thread throws a spot of light onto the far wall, and over the next hour that spot creeps sideways. The rod's ends have moved sideways by less than the width of a hair.

That creeping spot is gravity, caught between objects you could lift. The same pull keeps the Moon on its monthly journey. *Newton's laws and inertial frames* gave the rule that turns a force into a motion. This section gives the force law for gravity, then rebuilds it as something that lives at every point of space.

## One law for the apple and the Moon

Newton's rule is short. Any two lumps of matter pull each other. For round balls whose matter lies in even layers, the pull runs along the line joining their centres. Any two bodies much smaller than the gap between them pull very nearly along that line too. Double either mass and the pull doubles. Double the distance and the pull drops to a quarter, because two squared is four. Falling off as the square of the distance is called an inverse-square law.

No lump of matter is left out, which is why this is Newton's law of universal gravitation. The apple and the Moon obey one rule.

That can be checked. The Moon's centre is about sixty times further from the Earth's centre than an apple lying in the grass. Sixty squared is three thousand six hundred, so each kilogram of the Moon should be pulled that many times more gently.

An apple gains about ten metres a second of speed in each second. Divide by three thousand six hundred, and the Moon should be bent out of a straight path by about three thousandths of a metre a second in each second. The size and timing of the Moon's monthly journey give that same number, to about one part in a hundred.

Distances here run from the centre of each body. The part *What crosses a closed surface* gives the reason.

*Any two masses pull each other along the line joining their centres, more strongly with each mass and weaker as the square of the distance.*

## The weakest pull there is, and why it rules

How strong is that pull? Set two one-kilogram balls on a table, their centres ten centimetres apart. They pull each other with a force equal to the weight of about one hundredth of a grain of table salt. Friction holds them still and you see nothing. That is why nothing in a room ever seems to pull you.

Henry Cavendish measured a pull of that size in 1798, with a rod hung from a thin thread. Heavy lead balls brought up beside the small ones twisted the thread, and the twist can be read off once the thread's stiffness is known. That measurement is Cavendish's experiment, and the instrument is called a torsion balance. It fixes the strength number in Newton's law, which *Units and dimensions* calls Newton's constant. Measure it once in a room, and the fall of an apple gives the Earth's mass: about six thousand billion billion tonnes.

Weak as it is, gravity runs the large world. A toy magnet lifts a pin, beating the pull of the whole Earth. But electric charge comes in two kinds, and ordinary matter carries equal helpings of each, so the pulls and pushes cancel. Mass comes in one kind only. Every extra kilogram adds, and nothing ever subtracts. Across the distances between planets, stars and galaxies, those helpings still cancel, so gravity is the force left standing.

*Gravity is so weak that lead balls on a thread are needed to see it, but mass never cancels, so gravity shapes the sky.*

## An arrow at every point

The pull on a body depends on the body. Hang a two-kilogram bag where a one-kilogram bag hung, and the pull doubles. Divide the pull by the mass you hang there, and both bags give the same answer.

That number, with its direction, is called the gravitational field at the point. At the ground it is about ten metres a second of speed gained in each second, pointing at the Earth's centre.

So draw a short arrow at each point of space around a lone ball of matter. Every arrow points at the ball, and shrinks as the square of the distance. Those arrows are the field, and they are there whether or not anything hangs at the point. The field belongs to the place.

The object you hang there is called a test mass. It has to be light enough not to drag the sources about.

With several sources, work out the arrow each one would make alone, then add the arrows nose to tail. Forces add that way, so fields do too.

*The gravitational field is the pull per kilogram at a point, with its direction; it belongs to the place, and several sources add nose to tail.*

## Three jobs for one word

Count the jobs the word mass is doing here.

The first job is resisting. A body's inertial mass says how hard it is to change the body's motion, and *Newton's laws and inertial frames* reads it off from a push. Gravity is nowhere in that definition.

The second job is answering. Multiply the field at a place by a number belonging to the body sitting there, and you get the pull the body feels. That number is its passive gravitational mass.

The third job is making. The field a body sends out is set by another number belonging to it, its active gravitational mass.

Nothing so far makes the three equal. The third law ties two of them together. The pull of a first body on a second is equal and opposite to the pull of the second on the first. The pull of the first on the second uses the first body's making number and the second body's answering number. The reverse pull swaps them. For the two to match for every pair, each body must have the same ratio of making number to answering number as every other body. The choice of units then makes that ratio one.

Nothing at all ties those two to the resisting number. Only measurement does, and it says they agree to better than one part in a million million. That is why a heavy ball and a light ball, released together, land together.

*Mass resists a push, answers to a field and makes a field. The third law ties making to answering, and only measurement ties them to resisting.*

## A landscape of wells

Lift a brick and you do work on it. Lower it gently and it hands the work back. Around any closed route gravity returns exactly what it took, which is what *Newton's laws and inertial frames* means by a conservative force.

So every point of space can carry one number. Start with the energy needed, for each kilogram, to carry a body from that point out to far away and leave it at rest. Call that the depth of the well at the point. The gravitational potential is that depth written with a minus sign, because it counts how far the point lies below the far-away level. Far from everything the potential is zero, and near a mass it is negative, so the landscape is a set of wells.

Around a single ball the depth goes as one over the distance, not as one over its square. Working the depth out means adding up the pull along the whole climb, so take this shape on trust here. Step out to twice the distance and the pull drops to a quarter, while the well is still half as deep. The field is the steepness of the well, and its arrow points straight down the slope.

Send something up fast enough and it climbs out and never returns. The smallest speed that does it is called the escape speed. It is set by the depth of the well where the journey starts, not by the mass sent up, nor by the direction, as long as nothing is in the way.

*Every point carries a well depth: the energy per kilogram needed to climb out to far away. The potential is that depth written with a minus sign, and the escape speed is set by the depth alone.*

## What crosses a closed surface

Draw an imaginary sphere centred on a lone ball of matter, one whose layers are each alike all over, as a well-made planet nearly is. Nothing then singles out one direction, so at every point of that sphere the field has the same strength and points straight in. Multiply the strength by the sphere's area. The strength falls as the square of the distance, and the area grows as the square of the distance, so the two changes cancel. The product is the same for every sphere: the mass inside, times Newton's constant, times four times pi.

Now a claim we take on trust. The same total comes out for a closed surface of any shape, and it counts only the mass in the region the surface encloses. Empty space neither adds crossings nor takes any away, so denting the surface moves them about without changing their total. This is called Gauss's law for gravity.

Two payoffs follow. Seen from outside such a ball, the counting cannot tell it from a single point holding all the mass at the centre. The evenness leaves the field no other shape to take. That is the shell theorem.

Second, go inside a hollow shell. A surface drawn in the hollow encloses no mass, so its crossings add to zero, and evenness again forces the field to zero throughout the hollow. Float in there beside one wall, and that wall does not win.

*What crosses any closed surface is fixed by the mass inside it. So an even round body pulls from outside like a point at its centre, and not at all inside a hollow.*

## Crumbs in a falling cabin

Ride a sealed cabin down a drop tower with a handful of crumbs, and let the crumbs go. Cabin, crumbs and rider fall together, so no crumb drifts toward the floor. What is left is only the differences in pull from one part of the cabin to another. Those differences are called the tidal pull.

Take two crumbs one above the other. The lower crumb is nearer the Earth's centre, so it is pulled harder, and the pair drifts apart. Move one hundredth nearer. The distance is then ninety-nine hundredths of what it was, and the square of that is about ninety-eight hundredths. The pull goes as one divided by that square, so it grows by about two hundredths.

Take two crumbs side by side instead. Both are pulled toward the same centre, so their pulls lean toward each other and the crumbs close up.

A round cloud of crumbs therefore stretches along the line to the Earth and squeezes the two ways across it. Falling toward one even round body, the stretch is exactly twice each squeeze. So at the moment you let the crumbs go, the cloud changes shape without changing its volume. The volume holds anywhere in empty space, whatever is making the pull, even when the stretch and the squeezes are uneven.

Now let the cloud fall inside matter, deep in a ball of water. A surface drawn around it holds mass, so the crossings no longer balance and the cloud starts to lose volume. How fast it begins to shrink is set by the density of matter right there, and by nothing else. Density means how many kilograms sit in each litre. That statement, written as one line of mathematics, is the Poisson equation for gravity, Newton's field equation. Einstein's equation must give it back wherever gravity is weak and motion is slow.

The Moon's tidal pull on you, standing on the ground, is about one ten-millionth of your weight, far too small to feel. Spread over an ocean, it gives most coasts two high tides a day.

*In a falling cabin only differences of pull remain: a stretch toward the mass and a squeeze across it, and matter inside a crumb cloud is what shrinks its volume.*

## A pull with no messenger

The map of arrows looks like a thing spread through space. In Newton's theory it is not.

Each arrow answers to where the masses are at this very moment, as read on clocks that all agree. Move the Sun sideways and the arrow beside you swings instantly. Nothing crosses the gap, and nothing takes any time to arrive. A pull that works like that is called action at a distance. Newton wrote that he could not say what does the pulling, and he refused to invent a story for it.

Two things break it. An instant pull needs one single now, shared by everybody, which is the absolute time of *Newton's laws and inertial frames*. Relativity throws that away, because people moving differently sort distant events into different nows. And no signal outruns light, so a change at the Sun cannot be known here until light from it arrives, about eight minutes later.

So gravity must be rebuilt as something that lives in space and spreads at a finite speed. Newton's answers have to come back out of it wherever gravity is weak and motion is slow. The rest of this book is that rebuilding.

*In Newton's theory gravity acts instantly across empty space with nothing carrying it, which needs one single now for everybody; relativity denies that.*

## Key equations

**Newton's law of universal gravitation** (stated)

$$F = \frac{G M m}{r^2}$$

The pull between two masses $M$ and $m$ whose centres are a distance $r$ apart, along the line joining them. It is read off from measurement, not from anything deeper.

- $F$: size of the pull, the same on each body
- $M$: mass of one of the two bodies
- $m$: mass of the other body
- $r$: distance between their centres
- $G$: Newton's constant, the strength number

Say: The pull equals Newton's constant times the two masses, divided by the square of the distance between their centres.

**Field of a single mass** (derived-here)

$$g = \frac{G M}{r^2}$$

Divide the pull by the mass hung at the point and that mass drops out, leaving the field: the pull per kilogram, pointing at $M$.

- $g$: field strength, the pull per kilogram
- $M$: mass making the field
- $r$: distance from its centre

Say: The field strength equals Newton's constant times the mass, divided by the square of the distance from its centre.

**Counting what crosses a sphere** (derived-here)

$$g \times 4\pi r^2 = 4\pi G M$$

Field strength times area. The square of the distance cancels, leaving the enclosed mass alone.

- $4\pi r^2$: area of a sphere of radius r
- $M$: mass inside the sphere

Say: The field strength times the area of the sphere equals four times pi times Newton's constant times the mass inside.

**Potential of a single mass** (stated)

$$\Phi = -\frac{G M}{r}$$

Climbing from $r$ out to far away costs $GM/r$ for each kilogram, and the potential is that cost written with a minus sign. Taken on trust here. The distance is not squared, so the well deepens more slowly than the pull grows.

- $\Phi$: gravitational potential, negative near a mass

Say: The potential equals minus Newton's constant times the mass, divided by the distance from its centre.

**Escape speed** (derived-here)

$$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 G M}{r}}$$

Set the moving energy of each kilogram equal to the depth of the well and solve. The mass sent up cancels.

- $v_{\text{esc}}$: escape speed from distance r

Say: The escape speed equals the square root of twice Newton's constant times the mass, divided by the starting distance from its centre.

## Worked examples

**escape-speed-from-the-earth.** How fast must a projectile leave the Earth's surface to climb out of the Earth's well and never come back? Ignore the air and the Earth's spin.

1. At the surface the journey starts 6,371 kilometres from the centre, and the depth of the well per kilogram is Newton's constant times the Earth's mass, divided by that distance.
2. The moving energy of each kilogram is half the speed squared.
3. Escaping with nothing to spare means the moving energy exactly pays for the climb, so half the speed squared equals the depth per kilogram.
4. Then the speed squared is twice Newton's constant times the Earth's mass, divided by the radius. The mass of the projectile has cancelled from both sides.
5. Putting the numbers in gives 11,200 metres a second, which is 11.2 kilometres a second, or about 40,000 kilometres an hour.

Answer: About 11.2 kilometres a second, the same for a pebble and for a loaded rocket, and the same in any direction that is not blocked.

*Escape speed measures the depth of the well, not the thing launched, which is why it is the natural speed to compare with the speed of light.*

## Checks

**pull-at-twice-the-distance** (numeric): A satellite is flying at a height above the ground equal to one Earth radius. Compare the pull on it there with the pull on the same satellite on the ground, and give the field strength at that height. Take the field at the ground as 9.8 metres a second in each second.

Answer: One quarter of the pull, and a field of about 2.5 metres a second in each second. The distance that matters runs from the Earth's centre, not from the ground. On the ground the satellite sits one Earth radius from the centre; one radius up, it sits two. Doubling the distance quarters the pull, because two squared is four, and 9.8 divided by 4 is about 2.5.

Key points: The distance runs from the centre, so it goes from one radius to two; Doubling it quarters the pull: about 2.5 metres a second in each second

Numeric: ratio of the new pull to the old = 0.25 1; field strength at that height = 2.45 m/s^2

**two-balls-released-together** (explain): A 10 kilogram ball and a 1 kilogram ball are released together from a height of two metres, with the air taken away. Which lands first? And which ball pulls the Earth harder?

Answer: They land together, each taking about 0.64 seconds. The Earth pulls the heavy ball ten times harder. But the heavy ball is also ten times harder to get moving, so the two effects cancel. The heavy ball pulls the Earth ten times harder than the light one does. Each ball pulls the Earth exactly as hard as the Earth pulls it, and only the changes in motion differ.

Key points: Same landing time, about 0.64 seconds: ten times the pull on ten times the resistance; Each ball pulls the Earth as hard as the Earth pulls the ball

Numeric: time to fall two metres = 0.64 s

**pull-at-the-space-station** (numeric): The space station circles the Earth about 400 kilometres above the ground, and the Earth's radius is 6,371 kilometres. How strong is the gravitational field there, against its value at the ground? Then say why the crew float.

Answer: The station is 6,771 kilometres from the centre, against 6,371 at the ground. That ratio is 1.063, and its square is 1.13, so the field is about 8.7 metres a second in each second. That is roughly nine tenths of the ground value. The crew do not float because gravity has run out. They float because station, crew and every loose spanner fall together, leaving only the differences of pull across the station.

Key points: About 8.7 metres a second in each second, roughly nine tenths of the ground value; Floating comes from everything falling together, not from a missing pull

Numeric: field strength 400 kilometres up = 8.69 m/s^2

**the-sun-jumps-sideways** (explain): Suppose the Sun were suddenly shifted sideways by a million kilometres. In Newton's theory, when does the pull felt on Earth change direction, and why is that answer trouble?

Answer: The pull here swings the instant the Sun moves, with no waiting. Each arrow answers to where the masses are at that very moment, and nothing travels from there to here. That makes trouble twice over. It needs everybody to agree which distant events count as happening now, and relativity says people moving differently disagree. It also carries news faster than light, which no signal does.

Key points: Instantly, because the law uses where the masses are at that very moment; That needs one single now for everybody, and it beats light; relativity refuses both

**two-pairs-of-crumbs-in-a-dropped-box** (predict): A box is dropped from a great height, with the air taken away. Inside, one pair of crumbs floats one above the other, along the line to the Earth's centre. A second pair floats side by side at the same height. What happens to each pair, and to the volume of a round cloud of crumbs?

Answer: The pair one above the other drifts apart, because the lower crumb is nearer the Earth's centre and so is pulled harder. The side-by-side pair closes up, because both crumbs are pulled toward the same centre, so their pulls lean toward each other. A round cloud therefore stretches along the line to the Earth and squeezes the two ways across it. Falling toward one even round body, the stretch is exactly twice each squeeze. So at the moment the crumbs are let go, the cloud changes shape without changing its volume.

Key points: Along the line to the Earth the pair drifts apart; across it the pair closes up; Stretch equals twice each squeeze, so the cloud's volume holds at the moment the crumbs are let go, anywhere in empty space

## Misconceptions

- **distance-from-the-surface**: "The distance in Newton's law is measured from the surface of the body, so standing on the ground means standing at zero distance." — For a body whose matter lies in even layers, the pull outside is that of a single point at the centre holding all the mass. Distances are counted from that centre, and at the ground the distance is the body's radius. (diagnosed by pull-at-twice-the-distance)
- **heavier-falls-faster**: "The Earth pulls a heavy body harder than a light one, so the heavy body falls faster." — The pull is larger in exactly the same proportion as the resistance to being set moving. The two effects cancel, so bodies released together gain speed together. (diagnosed by two-balls-released-together)
- **earth-pulls-harder**: "The Earth pulls a person far harder than the person pulls the Earth." — The two pulls are equal and opposite, as the third law requires. Only the resulting changes in motion differ, because the Earth is so much harder to move. (diagnosed by two-balls-released-together)
- **no-gravity-up-there**: "Astronauts float because there is no gravity at the height where the space station flies." — The field up there is about nine tenths of its value at the ground. They float because the station and everything in it fall together, so only the small differences of pull across the station are left. (diagnosed by pull-at-the-space-station, two-pairs-of-crumbs-in-a-dropped-box)
- **newtons-field-is-a-messenger**: "The gravitational field is a thing that carries the pull from one mass to another at some speed." — In Newton's theory every arrow answers to where the masses are at that very moment, with no delay and nothing travelling. Making the field a real messenger is the work of a new theory. (diagnosed by the-sun-jumps-sideways)

## Glossary

- **Newton's law of universal gravitation**: The rule that any two masses pull each other along the line joining their centres, more strongly with each mass and weaker as the square of the distance. (`newtons-law-of-universal-gravitation`)
- **torsion balance**: A rod hung from a thin thread, whose slight twist measures a tiny sideways force. Cavendish used one in 1798 on lead balls. (`cavendish-experiment`)
- **gravitational field**: The pull per kilogram at a point, with its direction: a property of the place, not of whatever is put there. (`newtonian-gravitational-field`)
- **test mass**: An object put at a point to read the field there, light enough that it does not move the sources. (`newtonian-gravitational-field`)
- **inertial mass**: The number saying how hard it is to change a body's motion, read off from a push, with no mention of gravity. (`inertial-mass`)
- **passive gravitational mass**: The number belonging to a body that the field is multiplied by to give the pull the body feels. (`passive-gravitational-mass`)
- **active gravitational mass**: The number belonging to a body that sets how strong a field the body sends out. (`active-gravitational-mass`)
- **gravitational potential**: The energy per kilogram needed to carry a body from a point out to far away and leave it at rest, with a minus sign in front. It is zero far from everything and negative near a mass. (`newtonian-gravitational-potential`)
- **escape speed**: The smallest launch speed that lets something climb out of a well and never return, set by the depth of the well where it starts. (`escape-velocity`)
- **Gauss's law for gravity**: The rule that the field added up over any closed surface is fixed by the mass in the region that surface encloses. (`gauss-law-for-gravity`)
- **shell theorem**: A body whose matter lies in even layers pulls anything outside exactly as a point at its centre would, and does not pull at all inside a hollow. (`shell-theorem`)
- **Poisson equation for gravity**: Newton's field equation: the density of matter at a point fixes how fast a small cloud of crumbs, let go there, begins to lose volume. (`poisson-equation-for-gravity`)
- **tidal pull**: The differences in gravitational pull from place to place across a falling body, which stretch it one way and squeeze it across. (`newtonian-tidal-tensor`)
- **action at a distance**: A pull worked by one body on another across empty space instantly, with nothing travelling between them. (`action-at-a-distance`)

## Tutor

Opening question: Picture yourself inside a sealed cabin, dropped from the top of a tall tower, with a handful of crumbs floating beside you. You let the crumbs go. Does anything at all still show you that the Earth is pulling, and if so, what would you watch for?

- Q: Is the gravitational field a real thing in space, or just a picture? A: In Newton's theory it is just a picture, a way of keeping the books. Each arrow answers to where the masses are at that very moment, and nothing travels. In the theory that replaces it the field becomes real: it carries energy, it spreads at the speed of light, and it can go on rippling long after the masses that made it have gone.
- Q: If the Moon pulls the ocean toward itself, why are there two high tides a day and not one? A: Because what counts is the difference in pull across the Earth. The water facing the Moon is pulled harder than the solid Earth, so it rises toward the Moon. The Earth is pulled harder than the water on the far side, so it moves out from under that water and leaves a second bulge. Two bulges, with the Earth turning under them, give most coasts two high tides a day.
