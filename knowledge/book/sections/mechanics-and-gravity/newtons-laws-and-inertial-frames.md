# Newton's laws and inertial frames

**Newton built motion on one time that runs everywhere, and on the frames where a body left alone keeps its speed and direction. Force changes momentum, and every push has an equal push back on the other body. So an isolated group's momentum holds, and moving plus stored energy holds where a force gives back what it took.**

A train runs along straight, level track at a steady one hundred kilometres an hour, blinds down. A passenger sets a full mug of water on her table. She drops a coin from a hand's width above it. The water stays level, and the coin lands right below her fingers, just as it would in the station.

Then the driver brakes steadily, shedding about one metre a second each second. She drops a second coin from the same height. It lands about one centimetre nearer the front of the train, and the water climbs the wall of the mug nearest the front.

Steady motion hid itself completely. The change of motion hid nothing. This section sets out Isaac Newton's rules for motion, and the question that difference raises: what is all this motion measured against?

## One time for everyone

Before any rule about motion, Newton needed a clock.

His answer is the one everybody already uses. Set your wristwatch by the station clock before you board. Carry the watch on a train, a ship or a mountain walk, and it keeps step with every other well-made clock, as closely as any watch can show.

So Newton took it that a single time runs everywhere at once. Every clock, wherever it sits and however it moves, can be set to agree with every other. Then all of them agree about when each event happened. This assumption is called absolute time.

It makes one word safe to use: now. The whole universe shares one present moment.

Everything in this chapter rests on that assumption.

*In Newton's physics one time runs everywhere, so clocks once set together always agree about when each event happened.*

## Left alone, a body keeps going

Push a book across a kitchen table and let go. It slides a hand's length and stops. That looks like the plain truth about motion: to keep something moving, keep pushing it.

Now change only the surface. The same push on a polished floor sends the book much further. An air-hockey puck, held clear of the table by blown air, crosses the whole table with no slowing you could measure.

The rubbing between a body and whatever touches it, the air included, is called friction. Each time we take away more friction, the slowing gets smaller. Nothing was ever added to keep the body going. Something was taken away from what had been stopping it.

Newton took that pattern to its end. A body with nothing acting on it keeps the same speed in the same straight line forever, and a body at rest stays at rest. This is Newton's first law, also called the law of inertia.

Rest is not a separate case. A body at rest has a speed of zero, and zero is a steady speed like any other.

*A body with nothing acting on it keeps the same speed in the same straight line forever, and a body at rest stays at rest.*

## The frames the law holds in

The first law does not hold wherever you might stand to watch. Go back to the train.

While the train runs steadily, the coin lands right below her fingers. Measured against the carriage's own walls and floor, it gained no speed along the carriage as it fell. The first law works inside the carriage.

While the train brakes, the second coin lands one centimetre nearer the front, and nothing touched it on the way down. Measured against those same walls and floor, a body with nothing acting on it changed its speed along the carriage. The first law fails inside the carriage.

Measured from the platform, nothing odd happened. The coin kept the forward speed it had when she let go, and the carriage slowed underneath it.

A set of rulers held in fixed positions relative to one another, with clocks beside them, is called a frame of reference. The carriage is one frame and the platform is another.

A frame in which a body with nothing acting on it keeps a steady speed in a straight line is called an inertial frame. The first law is really the claim that such frames exist.

Any frame moving steadily in a straight line relative to an inertial frame is inertial too, which is why the steady carriage works. A frame that speeds up, slows down or turns relative to an inertial frame is not.

The ground under your feet is good enough for a school laboratory, but Earth turns once a day, and the laboratory turns with it. So the ground is not exactly inertial, and an experiment can be made careful enough to find the difference.

*An inertial frame is one in which a body left alone keeps a steady speed in a straight line, and the first law claims that such frames exist.*

## What the change of motion is measured against

The passenger cannot tell steady motion from standing still, yet she feels the brake at once. Steady motion hides itself; a change of motion does not.

A change relative to what? Newton answered that space itself is there, unmoving, the same from one moment to the next. He called it absolute space. The inertial frames are the ones at rest in it, or drifting steadily through it.

He argued for it with a bucket you can fill yourself. Hang it from a rope, turn it round and round to twist the rope, and let go.

At first the bucket spins and the water does not, and the water's surface stays flat.

Within a few seconds the water is dragged into turning with the bucket. Now its surface dips in the middle and climbs the sides. For a bucket twenty centimetres wide turning once each second, the water at the rim sits about two centimetres above the water at the middle.

Now grab the bucket and hold it still. The water goes on turning for a while, and its surface stays curved.

Compare that last stage with the first. In both, the water and the bucket turn differently from each other, yet the surface is flat in one and curved in the other. So the curve does not follow how the water turns relative to the bucket. It follows the water's own turning, which Newton took to mean its turning relative to absolute space.

Absolute space has a weakness Newton knew about. Nothing in his mechanics finds where you sit in it, or how fast you drift through it. Only changes of motion show. Others answered instead that the water's turning is measured against the distant stars. That answer stayed with Einstein while he built his own theory of gravity.

*Newton measured changes of motion against absolute space; his bucket shows the water's surface follows the water's own turning, not its turning relative to the bucket.*

## How hard a body is to get moving

A shopping trolley starts rolling with a flick of the hand. A car does not. Newton's mechanics makes that comparison exact, without weighing anything.

Put two people on skateboards on flat, smooth ground, facing each other and at rest. They push each other's hands once and let go, and they roll apart. Time each of them over a measured stretch of ground.

Suppose one rolls away at one metre a second and the other at two metres a second. The slower one was the harder one to get moving, by exactly the factor two.

That factor is what we mean by mass. A body's mass says how strongly it resists a change of its motion, and mass measured this way, by pushes and speeds rather than by weighing, is called inertial mass.

*Units and dimensions* gave every measurement a number and a unit. Here one agreed body is given a mass of one kilogram, and every other mass follows from the speeds gained in a push against it. Nothing in that recipe mentions weight, so it works where nothing weighs anything.

Mass alone does not say how hard a moving body is to stop, because speed counts too.

Mass times speed, carried in the direction the body is moving, is called the body's momentum. Its unit is the kilogram metre a second. For a group of bodies, draw each momentum as an arrow. Its length is that body's mass times its speed, and it points the way the body moves. Add the arrows tip to tail.

*Inertial mass, read from the speeds two bodies gain when they push apart, says how strongly a body resists a change of motion. Momentum is mass times speed, in the direction of travel.*

## The law that predicts the motion

A body left alone keeps its momentum. So whatever changes a body's momentum deserves a name, and the name is force.

Newton's second law says how much, and like the first it speaks about inertial frames. Measured in such a frame, the total force on a body equals the rate at which its momentum changes. That means how much the momentum changes each second. When the mass stays the same, that is the mass times the acceleration, and acceleration means how much the speed changes each second.

A force has a direction, and the momentum changes in that direction.

On its own the law looks like an empty definition. If force is only whatever we read off from mass times acceleration, the law cannot be wrong, and it predicts nothing.

What saves it is that forces have laws of their own. Newton's law of gravity gives the pull between two bodies from their masses and the distance between them. Each such rule says what the force is before any motion is watched, and only then does the second law make a prediction.

And the prediction is complete. Give the positions and the speeds of every body now, and the rules for the forces between them, and the second law fixes the whole motion from then on.

Newton's three laws are the starting rules of this mechanics. Nothing simpler proves them. They are kept because what follows from them matches what we measure.

*The total force on a body equals the rate at which its momentum changes, which is mass times acceleration when the mass is fixed. Separate force laws are what let it predict.*

## Every push has a push back

You cannot push without being pushed. Push off a wall on a skateboard and you roll away from it, because the wall pushed you.

Newton's third law states it exactly. When two bodies act on each other, the force on the first from the second is equal in size and opposite in direction to the force on the second from the first.

The two forces of such a pair never act on the same body: one acts on the first, the other on the second. That is why they do not cancel and leave the world frozen.

Now take a group of bodies with nothing outside the group pushing on any of them. Such a group is called an isolated system.

Apply the second law to each member and add the results. The rate at which the group's total momentum changes equals the total of all the forces the members put on each other. Those forces come in equal and opposite pairs, so they add up to nothing.

So the group's total momentum never changes. This is the conservation of momentum.

*Every force has an equal and opposite partner on the other body, so the forces inside an isolated system add to nothing and its total momentum never changes.*

## Energy stored and given back

Throw a ball straight up from your hand and catch it at the same height. With still air it comes back at the speed it left.

Send a marble up a smooth ramp and it comes back to the bottom at the speed it started with. Use a steeper ramp, or a curved track, and it still does. Gravity gave back everything the climb took, whatever the route.

Now drag a box once around a loop on a rough floor and set it down where it began. You get nothing back. All the effort went into warming the floor and the box.

A force that gives back, around every closed route, exactly what it took is called a conservative force. Gravity near the ground is one. Friction is not.

So a conservative force lets you keep a running store of what it has taken from a body. The amount stored is called potential energy, and it depends only on where the body is. Near the ground it is the body's mass, times its height, times the strength of gravity, which speeds a falling body up by about 9.8 metres a second each second.

The energy a body has because it is moving is called kinetic energy: half the mass, times the speed, times the speed again. Both kinds of energy are measured in joules.

Add the potential energy to the kinetic energy. While only conservative forces act, the sum never changes, and that is the conservation of mechanical energy. Take the rule on trust here.

Where potential energy is counted from is your choice. Only differences between two places ever enter the sum, so that choice never changes an answer. The route never mattered because gravity near the ground is conservative, and that is what let us keep the store in the first place.

*A conservative force gives back around every closed route exactly what it took, so it can be given a potential energy. While only such forces act, kinetic plus potential energy stays the same.*

## Key equations

**Momentum of a body** (stated)

$$p = m v$$

A body's momentum $p$ is its mass $m$ times its speed $v$, carried in the direction it moves. A definition, not a result.

- $p$: momentum of the body, in the direction it moves
- $m$: inertial mass of the body
- $v$: speed of the body in an inertial frame

Say: The momentum equals the mass times the speed.

**Newton's second law** (stated)

$$F = m a$$

Measured in an inertial frame, the total force $F$ on a body of fixed mass $m$ equals that mass times its acceleration $a$, and the two point the same way.

- $a$: acceleration: how much the speed changes each second, with its direction

Say: The total force equals the mass times the acceleration.

**Newton's third law** (stated)

$$F_{12} = -F_{21}$$

The force on body one from body two is equal in size and opposite in direction to the force on body two from body one.

- $F_{12}$: force on body one from body two
- $F_{21}$: force on body two from body one

Say: The force on the first body from the second equals minus the force on the second body from the first.

**Conservation of momentum** (derived-here)

$$P_{\text{before}} = P_{\text{after}}$$

The total momentum $P$ of an isolated system, its members' arrows added tip to tail, is the same before and after any interaction.

- $P$: total momentum of the isolated system

Say: The total momentum before equals the total momentum after.

**Conservation of mechanical energy** (stated)

$$\tfrac{1}{2} m v^2 + U = \text{constant}$$

While only conservative forces act, kinetic energy $\tfrac{1}{2} m v^2$ plus potential energy $U$ keeps one value. Near the ground $U = m g h$. Taken on trust here.

- $U$: potential energy stored at the body's place
- $g$: strength of gravity near the ground, about 9.8 metres a second each second
- $h$: height above the level you count from

Say: Half the mass times the speed times the speed, plus the potential energy, stays the same.

## Checks

**the-puck-and-the-blower** (predict): An air-hockey table is 2.0 metres long and level, and its blower is on, so a thin layer of air holds the puck clear of the surface. A flicked puck crosses all 2.0 metres in 4.0 seconds, still moving at the far end.

- How long would that flick take on a table of the same kind 4.0 metres long?
- What would the puck do on such a table with no far end and no air resistance?
- With the blower off the same flick carries it 20 centimetres. A friend says the blower had been pushing it along. What was the blower doing?

Answer: It would take 8.0 seconds. Nothing acts on the puck along the table, so its speed stays at 2.0 metres in 4.0 seconds, which is half a metre each second. At that speed 4.0 metres takes 8.0 seconds.

With no far end it would keep half a metre each second in the same straight line forever. That is Newton's first law.

The blower blows upward, not along. It holds the puck clear so the table cannot rub it. With it off, friction acts backward along the motion and the puck stops.

Key points: Nothing acts along the table, so the speed stays at half a metre each second and 4.0 metres takes 8.0 seconds; the blower lifts the puck, and friction is what stops it

Numeric: time to cross the 4.0 metre table = 8.0 s

**the-ball-on-the-bus-floor** (predict): A bus drives along a flat, straight road at a steady speed, and a ball lies on its flat, smooth floor, at rest relative to the bus. The driver then turns left around a long bend, keeping the same speed. A passenger sitting still, facing the front, watches the ball slide toward the wall on his right. He says a force pushed it that way.

Describe what the ball does as seen from the road. Say whether any sideways force acts on it. Say which frame, the road or the bus, is inertial during the bend.

Answer: Seen from the road, the ball keeps going in the straight line it was already on, at the speed it already had. The floor is smooth, so nothing pushes it sideways. The bus turns left out from under it, and the wall on his right comes across to meet it.

So no sideways force acts. The passenger is measuring a change in the ball's sideways speed against the bus's own floor and walls, then naming a force for it.

During the bend the road is the inertial frame and the bus is not. Turning is a change of motion, just as braking is.

Key points: From the road the ball carries straight on at its old speed with nothing pushing it sideways, so the road is inertial during the bend and the bus is not

**two-astronauts-push-off** (numeric): Two astronauts float at rest beside each other inside a spacecraft coasting with its engines off, far from anything. The first, in her suit, has a mass of 50 kilograms. They push each other's gloves once and drift apart. Measured against the cabin walls, the first drifts away at 1.2 metres a second and the second the other way at 2.0 metres a second.

What is the second astronaut's mass? A friend says neither has any mass, because a kitchen scale in that cabin reads zero. What do you reply?

Answer: The second astronaut's mass is 30 kilograms. The pair touch nothing else, so they are an isolated system. They began at rest, so their total momentum was nothing and still is. The two arrows are therefore equal in length and opposite in direction. The first carries 50 kilograms times 1.2 metres a second, which is 60 kilogram metres a second. So the second carries 60 as well, and 60 divided by 2.0 gives 30 kilograms.

To the friend: a scale measures a pull, and in a coasting spacecraft there is no pull to measure. Mass was measured here by the push itself.

Key points: Starting at rest and touching nothing else, the pair keep momentum arrows equal in length and opposite in direction; Inertial mass is measured by mutual pushes, not by weighing, so it survives where nothing weighs anything

Numeric: mass of the second astronaut = 30.0 kg

**two-trolleys-that-stick** (numeric): On a level, smooth, straight track, a trolley of mass 2.0 kilograms rolls at 3.0 metres a second, measured against the track. It runs into a trolley of mass 4.0 kilograms standing still on the track. They stick together on contact and roll on as one.

How fast do they roll on, and which way? Work out the kinetic energy of the pair just before and just after, and say what fraction is left. Was momentum conserved? Was mechanical energy conserved? Explain the difference.

Answer: They roll on at 1.0 metre a second, the way the first trolley was already going. Nothing outside pushes along the track, so the total momentum holds. Before, it is 2.0 times 3.0, which is 6.0 kilogram metres a second. After, the joined mass is 6.0 kilograms, so its speed is 1.0 metre a second.

The kinetic energy before is half of 2.0 times 3.0 times 3.0, which is 9.0 joules. After it is half of 6.0 times 1.0 times 1.0, which is 3.0 joules. One third is left.

So momentum was conserved and mechanical energy was not. Momentum holds whenever nothing outside pushes. Mechanical energy keeps its total only while every force acting is conservative, and sticking is not. The missing 6.0 joules warmed and dented the buffers.

Key points: Total momentum 6.0 kilogram metres a second before and after, so 6.0 kilograms move on at 1.0 metre a second; Kinetic energy falls from 9.0 joules to 3.0 joules because the sticking forces are not conservative

Numeric: fraction of the kinetic energy left after the crash = 0.3333 1

**two-routes-to-the-same-floor** (numeric): A smooth, curved slide runs from a platform 1.25 metres above a level floor down to the floor. A marble of mass 0.20 kilograms is let go from rest at the top. A second marble, just the same, is dropped straight down from that same height beside the slide. Take gravity to speed a falling body up by 9.8 metres a second each second, and take the slide and the air to rub nothing away.

- How fast is each marble going when it reaches the floor?
- A classmate says the slide marble must arrive slower, because it travels a longer route. What is wrong with that?
- What changes if the slide is rough?

Answer: Both arrive at about 4.9 metres a second.

Each marble starts at rest, so it starts with no kinetic energy. Its stored energy at the top is its mass, times the strength of gravity, times the height: 0.20 times 9.8 times 1.25, which is 2.45 joules. Count the stored energy as nothing at the floor. Only gravity acts on the way down, and gravity is conservative, so the sum of the two energies does not change. All 2.45 joules are kinetic at the floor.

So half of 0.20 times the speed times the speed equals 2.45. The speed times itself is 24.5, and the speed is about 4.9 metres a second.

Nothing in that working asked which route the marble took. Only the drop in height entered it. The two routes differ in the time taken and in the direction the marble is moving when it lands, not in the speed.

A rough slide rubs. Friction is not conservative, so some of the 2.45 joules warms the slide and the marble, and that marble arrives slower than 4.9 metres a second. The dropped marble is unchanged.

Key points: Stored energy at the top is 2.45 joules for each marble, all of it kinetic at the floor, giving about 4.9 metres a second; Gravity near the ground is conservative, so only the drop in height enters the sum and the route does not; A rough slide adds friction, which is not conservative, so that marble arrives slower

Numeric: speed of each marble at the floor = 4.95 m/s

## Misconceptions

- **motion-needs-a-force**: "A moving body needs a continuing push to keep moving, and it slows when the push stops." — It slows because friction and the air push against it. Take those away and it keeps its speed and its straight line with nothing pushing it. (diagnosed by the-puck-and-the-blower)
- **any-frame-will-do**: "Newton's laws hold for a watcher standing anywhere, so a ball that slides sideways across the floor of a turning bus must have been pushed sideways." — The laws hold only in inertial frames, and a turning bus is not one. From the road the ball carries straight on while the bus turns out from under it. (diagnosed by the-ball-on-the-bus-floor)
- **weight-is-mass**: "Mass is what a scale reads, so a body that weighs nothing, such as an astronaut in a coasting spacecraft, has no mass." — Inertial mass is measured by pushing two bodies apart and comparing the speeds they gain. That works in a coasting spacecraft and gives the same masses as on the ground. (diagnosed by two-astronauts-push-off)
- **momentum-means-energy**: "If the total momentum is the same before and after a collision, the total kinetic energy must be the same too." — Momentum holds whenever nothing outside the group pushes. Mechanical energy keeps its total only while every force acting is conservative, and sticking is not. (diagnosed by two-trolleys-that-stick)
- **path-changes-the-energy**: "A body that comes down to the same floor by a longer route must arrive more slowly, because it travelled further." — Gravity near the ground is conservative, so what it gives back depends only on the drop in height, not on the route. Only a force that is not conservative, such as friction along the route, changes the arrival speed. (diagnosed by two-routes-to-the-same-floor)

## Glossary

- **absolute time**: Newton's assumption that one time runs everywhere, so clocks once set together always agree about when each event happened. (`absolute-time`)
- **friction**: The rubbing between a body and whatever touches it, the air included, acting backward along the motion. (`newtons-first-law`)
- **Newton's first law**: A body with nothing acting on it keeps the same speed in the same straight line, and one at rest stays at rest. (`newtons-first-law`)
- **frame of reference**: A set of rulers in fixed positions with clocks beside them, used to say where and when something happened. (`inertial-frame`)
- **inertial frame**: A frame in which a body with nothing acting on it keeps a steady speed in a straight line. (`inertial-frame`)
- **absolute space**: Newton's unmoving space, which he took changes of motion to be measured against. (`absolute-space`)
- **inertial mass**: How strongly a body resists a change of its motion, found from the speeds two bodies gain when they push apart. (`inertial-mass`)
- **momentum**: A body's mass times its speed, in the direction it moves. For a group, the members' arrows added tip to tail. (`linear-momentum`)
- **force**: Whatever changes a body's momentum. It has a size and a direction. (`force`)
- **acceleration**: How much a body's speed changes each second, in the direction the change points. (`newtons-second-law`)
- **Newton's second law**: The total force on a body equals the rate at which its momentum changes, which is mass times acceleration when the mass is fixed. (`newtons-second-law`)
- **Newton's third law**: The force on one body from a second is equal in size and opposite in direction to the force on the second from the first. (`newtons-third-law`)
- **isolated system**: A group of bodies with nothing outside the group pushing or pulling on any of them. (`conservation-of-linear-momentum`)
- **conservation of momentum**: The rule that an isolated system's total momentum is the same after any interaction as before. (`conservation-of-linear-momentum`)
- **conservative force**: A force that gives back, around every closed route, exactly what it took. Friction does not. (`conservative-force`)
- **potential energy**: What a conservative force is holding for a body, set by where the body is. Near the ground, mass times height times the strength of gravity. (`conservative-force`)
- **kinetic energy**: Half a body's mass, times its speed, times its speed again. (`conservation-of-mechanical-energy`)
- **joule**: The unit energy is measured in. Lifting a small apple one metre stores about one joule in it. (`conservation-of-mechanical-energy`)
- **conservation of mechanical energy**: The rule that kinetic plus potential energy keeps one total while only conservative forces act. (`conservation-of-mechanical-energy`)

## Tutor

Opening question: You are on a train running at a steady speed along straight, level track, with the blinds down. You hold a coin a hand's width above your table and let it go. Where does it land? And what changes about where it lands if the driver starts braking just as you let go?

- Q: If a body left alone keeps going forever, why does a ball I roll across the carpet always stop? A: Because the ball is not left alone. The carpet rubs against it and the air pushes back on it, both backward along the motion. That rubbing is friction. Roll the same ball on smooth ice and it goes much further; float it on blown air and it barely slows.
- Q: If the cart pulls back on the horse exactly as hard as the horse pulls the cart, how does the cart ever move? A: Because those two pulls act on two different bodies, so they never turn up in the same body's total. Look at the cart alone: the horse pulls it forward, friction at its wheels holds it back, and it speeds up when the pull wins. On slippery ice the road's push on the hooves goes, and the pair go nowhere.
