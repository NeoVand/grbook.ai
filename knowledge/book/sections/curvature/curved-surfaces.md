Peel a strip of paper label off a tin can and lay it on a table. It lies against the table with no wrinkle. Now peel a piece of skin as big as your palm off an orange and press it down onto the table. However gently you press, its rim splits. Both were bent while they were on the can and on the orange. This section finds the difference between them with a tape measure, a string and a protractor, never leaving the surface. *Gravity as curvature* said that gravity is a curving of space and time; this is what curved means.

## Two friends who walk without steering

Picture a playground as level as a tabletop. Two friends stand on it 10 metres apart, with a straight chalk line drawn between them. Each faces the same way, at a right angle to the chalk line, and both walk ahead at the same pace.

Both follow one rule: never steer to the left or to the right. Walking without ever steering is called walking straight, and the path is called a straight walk.

On the playground, a tape measure stretched between the friends reads 10 metres however far they walk. Two straight walks that begin like theirs are said to start parallel: each leaves the same straight walk, here the chalk line, at a right angle, toward the same side.

Euclid, the ancient Greek who wrote down the rules of school geometry, began from a few starting rules. One of them comes down to this: straight walks that start parallel keep the same gap forever. This rule is called the parallel postulate.

Now repeat the test on a huge, smooth, round ball. Seen from outside, as on a globe, call the top point the North Pole and the circle around the middle the equator. Each line printed on a globe from the equator to the North Pole is a straight walk. The ball on one side of it mirrors the ball on the other side, so a walker on it has no reason to steer, although the path bends over the ball. The equator is a straight walk for the same reason. On the globe, every printed line crosses the equator at a right angle and ends at the North Pole.

Stand the friends on the equator, 10 metres apart, both facing the North Pole. Each leaves the equator at a right angle, so they start parallel, and each walks along one of the printed lines. As they walk, their paths draw together, because both printed lines end at the North Pole. There they meet, a quarter of the way around the ball.

So the parallel postulate holds on the playground and fails on the ball, and only measuring can tell which of the two the space we live in is like.

*Straight walks that start parallel keep their gap on a playground, as the parallel postulate says, but on a ball they meet a quarter of the way around.*

## Draw together, keep the gap, or spread apart

Straight walkers that start parallel can also spread apart. Picture a swim ring lying on a table: a fat rubber tube bent round into a circle around a hole, with two tiny ants living on its surface.

Halfway up the tube's height, a circle runs all the way around the hole, closer to the hole than any other part of the ring. Call it the inner circle. The half of the ring above the inner circle mirrors the half below it, so the inner circle is a straight walk. The ants start side by side on it, a few millimetres apart. Both face away from the hole, at a right angle to the inner circle, so they start parallel.

Each ant walks straight, up over the top of the tube toward its outer side. Seen from above, the two paths lie along two neighbouring knife cuts through the centre of the hole, the way a round cake is sliced: the two edges of one thin slice. The ring on one side of such a cut mirrors the ring on the other side. So an ant on a cut has no reason to steer, just as on the globe's printed lines. A slice is narrow at the hole and wide at the outside, so the ants' gap grows as they walk: they spread apart.

The way a surface makes straight walkers that start parallel draw together, keep their gap, or spread apart is called its curvature.

Where they begin to draw together, as on the ball, the curvature is positive.

Where they begin to spread apart, as around the swim ring's hole, the curvature is negative.

Where they keep their gap, as on the playground, the curvature is zero. A surface with zero curvature everywhere is called flat.

*Curvature is how a surface makes straight walkers that start parallel begin to draw together (positive), keep their gap (zero, called flat) or spread apart (negative).*

## A world you can never leave

Imagine a tiny ant living on a giant, smooth, round ball. She can never leave its surface. What can she still measure?

She can lay a tape measure along the ground between two pebbles, and she takes the length of the shortest route as their distance. Where two paths cross, she lays a tiny protractor against the ground and reads the angle between them.

Some measurements are out of her reach. A rod through the middle of the ball runs where she can never go, so its length is not one of her measurements.

Everything she can work out from her distances and angles along the ground is called the intrinsic geometry of the surface.

Describing a surface through its intrinsic geometry alone is called the intrinsic viewpoint.

The walkers' test used nothing else: never steering is a rule about the walker's own feet, and the gap was read on a tape along the ground. So curvature belongs to the intrinsic geometry. The mirror pictures of the globe and the swim ring only helped us guess where a walker who never steers will go; the walker herself needs no view from outside.

We live inside space and time and cannot step outside them to look. So the curving that *Gravity as curvature* describes is the ant's kind: whatever rulers, clocks and light can reveal from within.

*What someone who never leaves a surface can measure along it is its intrinsic geometry, and curvature belongs to it.*

## The ring test

On a playground, tie a string to a peg and pull it tight along the ground. Walk once around the peg with chalk at the string's far end, so that the chalk draws a ring.

School geometry says the ring's length is 2 times pi times the string's length, about 6.28 times the string. Call this the ring's playground length.

Now do the same on a football 70 centimetres around. Tape one end of a string 17 and a half centimetres long to a spot on the ball. Pull the string tight along the ball's surface, never through the air, draw a ring at its far end, and lay a soft tape measure along the ring.

Take the taped spot as a globe's North Pole. The ball measures 70 centimetres around whichever way you go, so the equator is a quarter of that, 17 and a half centimetres, from the spot in every direction. So the string draws the equator as its ring, and the tape reads 70 centimetres. The playground length of that string is about 110 centimetres, so the ball's ring is short by more than a third.

Comparing a ring's length, measured along the surface, with its playground length is called the ring test.

Why is the ball's ring short? A string pulled tight along the ball lies along a straight walk. If it had a bend, the bend could slide sideways onto a shorter route, so the string was not yet tight. Picture many strings pulled tight from the taped spot in every direction, like the spokes of a wheel. The ring's length is the sum of the gaps between the far ends of neighbouring strings. On the ball, positive curvature acts on neighbouring straight walks the same way whether they start parallel or fan out from one spot: it works to close their gap. Walks that fan out still spread, but their gaps grow more slowly than on the playground, so the ring comes out short. Around the hole of the swim ring, straight walks spread apart faster than on the playground, so a small ring there comes out too long.

A small ring therefore reads the curvature around its centre, as long as the string sweeps over no sharp point like the tip of a paper cone. A cone is made by cutting a wedge out of a flat disc and taping the cut edges together. So a ring around its tip is short by the wedge, while the cone's side is flat everywhere else. Too short means positive curvature, the playground length means zero, and too long means negative. On Earth a ring drawn with a 1 kilometre string comes out short by about 0.026 millimetres, less than half a hair's width.

*A small ring whose string swept over no sharp point reads the curvature around its centre: shorter than 6.28 times the string means positive, equal means zero, longer means negative.*

## A triangle with three right angles

On paper, a triangle's three corners always add up to 180 degrees.

Now walk a triangle of straight walks on the ball. Start at the North Pole and walk to the equator. Turn a right angle to your own left, standing upright on the ball, and walk a quarter of the way around the equator. Turn a right angle to your own left again and walk back to the North Pole. Each corner is the angle between two sides, read with a protractor on the ground, on the piece of the ball that was on your left as you walked.

Each line from the North Pole meets the equator at a right angle, so both corners on the equator are right angles. For the corner at the North Pole, cut an orange from top to bottom into four equal segments. Each segment takes a quarter of the equator, and your walk along the equator was a quarter of it, so your first and last sides run along two neighbouring cuts. Neighbouring cuts meet at the top at 90 degrees, a quarter of 360. This triangle has three right angles, adding up to 270 degrees.

The amount by which a triangle's corners add up to more than 180 degrees is called its angle excess, so this triangle's angle excess is 90 degrees.

The excess is the meeting corner. The two sides that leave the equator start parallel, so their corners already fill 180 degrees. On the playground such walks never meet, but on the ball they meet at the North Pole, and the whole corner there is extra. Around a swim ring's hole the walks spread apart. To meet, each walker must set off tilted toward the other, and that tilt comes off the walker's corner on the base. On the playground the two tilts add up exactly to the meeting corner. Around the hole a bigger tilt is needed for the same meeting corner. So more comes off the base than the meeting corner gives back, and the corners add up to less than 180 degrees.

The excess follows the share of the ball the triangle covers. This triangle is the half of one segment that lies between the equator and the North Pole. So it covers one eighth of the ball, and 8 times 90 degrees is 720 degrees. On a smooth, round ball, a triangle's angle excess is 720 degrees times the share of the ball it covers. Take this rule on trust here. The three sides cut the ball into two pieces, and the corners and the share must be measured on the same piece.

Small triangles cover tiny shares, so their excesses are tiny. A triangle of three towns on Earth, each 100 kilometres from the other two, has an excess of about six thousandths of a degree, too small for any school protractor to read.

*A triangle of straight walks on a ball can have three right angles; its angle excess is 720 degrees times the share of the ball it covers.*

## Bent is not curved

Back to the label and the peel. Roll a sheet of paper into a tube and tape it. From outside, the paper looks bent.

Ask an ant who lives on the paper and measures only along it. Rolling did not stretch or squash the paper anywhere, so every length along it stayed the same. A ring drawn with a 5 centimetre string on a tube 30 centimetres around is 31.4 centimetres long, its playground length, because unrolling turns it into an ordinary circle. A ring of its playground length means zero curvature, and every ring on the tube has it: the tube is flat.

So the word curved has two meanings. Bending seen from the space around a surface is called extrinsic curvature.

Curving that shows up in measurements made along the surface is called intrinsic curvature.

The tube has extrinsic curvature but no intrinsic curvature. The ball has both. In this book, curved on its own means intrinsically curved, and bent means extrinsically curved.

Now the orange peel. Bending a sheet without stretching keeps every length along it, so it keeps every ring's length. A small ring on the peel is shorter than its playground length, because the peel came off a ball and rings on a ball come out short. Every ring lying against the table has its playground length. So the peel cannot lie against the table without stretching, and its rim splits. The label lies down without a wrinkle because it has no intrinsic curvature to lose.

The rubber sheet of *Gravity as curvature* is a drawing of a bend for our eyes; the theory itself uses only intrinsic curvature.

*Extrinsic curvature is bending seen from outside; intrinsic curvature shows in measurements along the surface, and general relativity uses only the intrinsic kind.*

## Key equations

**Playground length of a ring** (stated)

$$L_{\text{playground}} = 2\pi r$$

The length of the ring that a string of length $r$ draws on flat ground, from school geometry. The ring test compares every measured ring with it.

- $L_{\text{playground}}$: playground length of the ring
- $r$: length of the string, the ring's radius along the surface
- $\pi$: pi, about 3.14

Say: The playground length equals two times pi times the string's length.

**Angle excess and share of the ball** (stated)

$$E = 720^\circ \times \frac{A}{A_{\text{ball}}}$$

On a smooth, round ball, a triangle of straight walks has an angle excess $E$ of 720 degrees times its area $A$ divided by the ball's whole surface $A_{\text{ball}}$, with corners and area measured on the same piece. Taken on trust here.

- $E$: angle excess, the corners' sum minus 180 degrees
- $A$: area of the triangle along the surface
- $A_{\text{ball}}$: area of the ball's whole surface

Say: The angle excess equals seven hundred and twenty degrees times the triangle's area divided by the whole surface of the ball.

## Worked examples

**triangle-with-a-120-degree-corner.** Two walkers stand on a ball's equator, one third of the way around the ball apart, both facing the North Pole. Each walks straight to the North Pole. With the piece of equator between them, their paths make a triangle. Find its three corners and its angle excess, then check the excess against the share of the ball it covers.

1. Each walker leaves the equator at a right angle, so the two corners on the equator are 90 degrees each.
2. Cut an orange from top to bottom into three equal segments. The three corners where the cuts meet at the top fill 360 degrees, so neighbouring cuts meet at 120 degrees.
3. The two paths reach the equator a third of the way around apart, so they run along two neighbouring cuts, and the corner at the North Pole is 120 degrees.
4. The corners add up to 90 plus 90 plus 120, which is 300 degrees, so the angle excess is 300 minus 180, which is 120 degrees.
5. The triangle is the half of one segment between the equator and the North Pole. One segment is a third of the ball, so the triangle covers one sixth.
6. 720 degrees times one sixth is 120 degrees, which matches the excess.

Answer: Corners of 90, 90 and 120 degrees, an angle excess of 120 degrees, and a share of one sixth of the ball: 720 degrees times one sixth is 120 degrees.

*The whole corner at the North Pole is the excess, and it equals 720 degrees times the share of the ball the triangle covers.*

## Checks

**ring-on-a-paper-tube** (numeric): Roll a sheet of paper into a tube 40 centimetres around and tape it. Tape one end of an 8 centimetre string to the tube, pull it tight along the paper, and draw a ring at its far end. How long is the ring, measured along the paper? What is the tube's curvature at the ring's centre?

Answer: The ring is about 50.3 centimetres long, its playground length, and the curvature is zero. Unroll the sheet: paper does not stretch, so every point of the ring still sits 8 centimetres from the centre along the paper. On the unrolled sheet that is an ordinary circle, 2 times pi times 8 centimetres long. A ring of its playground length means zero curvature, however bent the tube looks from outside.

Key points: Unrolling changes no length along the paper, so the ring is an ordinary circle of radius 8 centimetres, 50.3 centimetres long; Playground length means zero curvature, whatever the tube looks like from outside

Numeric: ring length = 0.503 m

**ring-on-a-football** (numeric): A beach ball is 80 centimetres around. A string 20 centimetres long is taped to a spot on the ball, pulled tight along the surface, and used to draw a ring. How long is the ring, and how long would the same string's ring be on a playground? A friend says the ring's real radius is the distance through the air to the middle of the ball, so the 6.28 rule still holds. What does the ant living on the ball reply?

Answer: The string reaches a quarter of the way around the ball in every direction, so the ring is the equator, 80 centimetres long. On a playground the same string draws a ring of about 126 centimetres. The ring is short by more than a third, so the curvature is positive. The ant replies that the middle of the ball is not part of her world, so no tape of hers can reach it. Her radius is the string along the surface, and with that radius the 6.28 rule fails. That failure is what curved means for her.

Key points: The ring is the equator, 80 centimetres, against a playground length of about 126 centimetres: positive curvature; The middle of the ball is not on the surface, so the ant's radius is the string along the surface

Numeric: ring length = 0.8 m; playground length = 1.257 m

**near-the-hole-of-a-swim-ring** (predict): An ant stands on a swim ring's inner circle, the circle closest to the hole, halfway up the tube. She walks straight out 1 centimetre in many directions and marks a small ring. Then she walks a small triangle of straight walks there and reads its corners with a protractor on the surface. Is the ring shorter than, equal to, or longer than 6.28 centimetres? Do the corners add up to more than, exactly, or less than 180 degrees?

Answer: The ring is longer than 6.28 centimetres, and the corners add up to less than 180 degrees. Around the hole, straight walks that start parallel spread apart. So the gaps between the far ends of her walks grow faster than on a playground, and the ring comes out too long. For the triangle, two sides that start parallel already fill 180 degrees with their corners, and here they spread apart. To meet, the walkers must set off toward each other by more than on a playground, so the meeting corner is smaller than what they took off.

Key points: Straight walks spread apart around the hole, so the ring is too long; The corners add up to less than 180 degrees; both readings mean negative curvature

**no-view-from-outside** (explain): A friend says that space can only be curved if it bends into a fourth direction we cannot see, so talk of curved space is empty. For each of the section's three tests, the two walkers, the ring and the triangle, say whether it needs a view from outside. What does that say about the claim?

Answer: None of the three needs a view from outside. The walkers' test uses the rule of never steering, which is about the walker's own feet, and a tape laid along the ground. The ring test uses a string pulled tight along the surface and a tape along the ring. The triangle test uses straight walks and a protractor lying on the ground. Each reads curvature from lengths and angles along the surface, so curvature needs no extra direction to bend into. General relativity uses only this intrinsic kind, so the claim does not touch it.

Key points: All three tests use only lengths and angles measured along the surface; Curvature found this way is intrinsic and needs no outside direction, and general relativity uses only that kind

## Misconceptions

- **bent-means-curved**: "Anything that looks bent from outside, like a can's label, is curved." — Curvature is judged by measurements along the surface. A rolled sheet unrolls without stretching, so its walkers keep their gap and its rings have their playground length: it is flat. (diagnosed by ring-on-a-paper-tube)
- **radius-through-the-air**: "The ring's real radius is the distance through the air to the middle of the ball, so the 6.28 rule still holds." — The ant's radius is the string laid along the surface, because the middle of the ball is not part of her world. With that radius the rule fails, and that failure is what curved means. (diagnosed by ring-on-a-football)
- **curvature-only-draws-together**: "Curvature always makes straight walkers draw together, rings come out short and triangle corners add up to more than 180 degrees." — Around a swim ring's hole, walkers that start parallel spread apart, small rings come out too long and small triangles add up to less than 180 degrees. That is negative curvature. (diagnosed by near-the-hole-of-a-swim-ring)
- **curving-needs-an-outside**: "If space is curved, it has to bend into some extra direction outside it." — The walker, ring and triangle tests read curvature from lengths and angles along the surface, with no view from outside, and general relativity uses only that intrinsic kind. (diagnosed by no-view-from-outside)

## Glossary

- **straight walk**: The path of someone who walks without ever steering left or right, even where the path bends over a curved surface. (`geodesic`)
- **start parallel**: Two straight walks start parallel when they leave the same straight walk side by side, each at a right angle to it, toward the same side. (`parallel-postulate`)
- **parallel postulate**: Euclid's starting rule that straight walks that start parallel keep the same gap forever. It holds on a playground but not on a ball. (`parallel-postulate`)
- **curvature**: The way a surface makes straight walkers that start parallel draw together, keep their gap, or spread apart. (`curvature`)
- **positive curvature**: Curvature that makes straight walkers that start parallel begin to draw together, as on a ball. (`curvature`)
- **negative curvature**: Curvature that makes straight walkers that start parallel begin to spread apart, as around a swim ring's hole. (`curvature`)
- **flat**: Having zero curvature everywhere: straight walkers that start parallel keep their gap. A rolled sheet is flat. (`curvature`)
- **intrinsic geometry**: Everything someone living on a surface can work out from distances and angles measured along it, without ever leaving it. (`intrinsic-geometry`)
- **intrinsic viewpoint**: Describing a surface, or space and time, only through measurements made without leaving it. (`intrinsic-geometry`)
- **ring test**: Compare a string ring's length along the surface with 6.28 times the string. Also called the circumference-to-radius test. (`circumference-to-radius-test`)
- **playground length**: The length a ring would have on flat ground: 2 times pi times the string's length, about 6.28 times the string. (`circumference-to-radius-test`)
- **angle excess**: The amount by which the three corners of a triangle add up to more than 180 degrees. On a playground it is zero. (`angular-excess`)
- **extrinsic curvature**: Bending of a surface as seen from the space around it. A rolled sheet of paper has it. (`intrinsic-versus-extrinsic-curvature`)
- **intrinsic curvature**: Curving that shows up in measurements along a surface. A ball has it; a rolled sheet does not. (`intrinsic-versus-extrinsic-curvature`)

## Tutor

Opening question: Picture two friends on a huge, smooth ball, standing on its equator ten metres apart, both facing the North Pole. They walk ahead and never steer left or right. What happens to the gap between them: does it stay ten metres, grow, or shrink, and why?

- Q: How can a can's label be flat when I can see that it is bent? A: Flat here means zero curvature, and curvature is what walkers, rings and triangles measure along the surface. Peel the label off and it unrolls with nothing stretched, so every one of those measurements matches a flat sheet. The bend you see is extrinsic curvature, not what flat is about.
- Q: How can a path on a ball be straight when the ball is round? A: Straight here means the walker never steers left or right. On a ball the path bends over the surface, but the walker does nothing to make it bend. The equator is like that: the ball on one side of it mirrors the ball on the other, so there is no reason to steer either way.
